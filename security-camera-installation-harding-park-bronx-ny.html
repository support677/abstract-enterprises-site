The Neighborhood

Why Harding Park Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Harding Park is a small cottage village at the southwestern tip of Clason Point, and almost nothing about it matches the camera advice written for a city block or a suburban street. It began as a summer place. NYC Parks records that Thomas Higgs, who owned about 100 acres of beachfront, began leasing tents to visitors in the early 1920s, and that his beach and the neighboring Killian's Grove were later cut into small lots for a bungalow colony named for President Warren G. Harding. Nearby stood an amusement park that people called the Coney Island of the Bronx. After World War II, with housing scarce, families turned their summer cottages into year-round homes, and those cottages, extended and rebuilt again and again, are the houses we are asked to put cameras on today. A Queens College paper on the colony says the lots ran from about 25 by 40 feet to 30 by 60 feet, with bungalows and tents set roughly twenty feet apart. That spacing is still the defining fact for any camera plan here.

The houses were built by their owners, not by a developer, and they show it. CityNeighborhoods describes cottages put up by carpenters, dockworkers and tradesmen who pooled their resources, many still dating from the 1920s. CityLore and the Fordham Ram describe bungalows renovated to almost double their original size and some fused together into one sprawling house. The city's own planners say the area has a unique variety of lot sizes and construction types, and its Resilient Neighborhoods study describes one- and two-story light-frame bungalows with additions added over time. City lot records we pulled for another of our Harding Park pages show 232 lots addressed on Harding Park itself, 179 of them one-family houses and 28 two-family, with recorded build years running from 1899 to 2023. For a camera installer, that variety means no two houses are wired the same way, and the walls a camera mounts on may be original wood, a later cement block addition or new siding, sometimes all on one side of the house.

The ground is unusual too. Harding Park residents fought off a plan in the 1950s to clear the colony as a slum, the city took over the land after the last private owner defaulted on taxes, and in the early 1980s the residents bought it back through the Harding Park Homeowners Association. Sources disagree on the exact year, 1981 or 1982, but they agree on the result: a cooperatively owned community where the association holds the common ground and the lanes it calls Private Ways. The Department of City Planning describes those lanes as narrow and paved, with no sidewalks, mostly nine to thirteen feet wide south of Gildersleeve Avenue and even narrower to the north, where lots are smaller and houses sit very close. Addresses are simply a number followed by Harding Park, like 726 Harding Park, and some lanes have no name at all. A camera on a bungalow here looks out at a lane that belongs to the association, past a fence that belongs to the owner, toward a neighbor's house a few strides away. Who owns what changes where a camera can point and where a post or cable can go.

Water is the other big constraint. The city's planners say Harding Park faces significant inundation and wave action from coastal storm surge and ponding in streets and yards from rainfall, made worse by limited stormwater infrastructure. First Street's flood model puts 42.2 percent of Harding Park properties at flood risk this year and 58.8 percent in 30 years. After Hurricane Sandy, Community Board 9 reported basements apparently flooded in Harding Park, Bronx Ink spoke with a resident who lost power on the Monday night and still had none late Tuesday afternoon, and the Bronx Times reported floodwater ripping open a shed. FEMA's later maps added or expanded flood zones across the Bronx shoreline, Harding Park included. A camera system that keeps its recorder in a crawl space, a low basement or a backyard shed can lose both its power and its footage in a single night. In this neighborhood, where the recorder sits matters as much as where the cameras point.

Then there are the reasons people want cameras at all. The 43rd Precinct, which covers Clason Point and Harding Park, appears in Vital City's analysis as one of two Bronx precincts that together accounted for nearly a third of auto accessory thefts, crimes that typically happen overnight. When the Bronx District Attorney charged 16 people with 252 thefts of cars, tires, rims and catalytic converters, eight of those thefts were in the 43rd Precinct. On Harding Park's narrow lanes, residents park where they can, and the car is often the most exposed thing a family owns. Older Bronx Times coverage also recorded quality-of-life complaints about dumping and loitering near the entrance to Clason Point Park at the end of Soundview Avenue. None of this means Harding Park is unsafe; Reader's Digest named it a finalist for the nicest place in America, and residents describe a village where everyone knows each other. It means cameras here are mostly about the car on the lane at 3 a.m., the side path nobody can see from the street and the back fence that faces open shoreline.

The best-known camera story from Harding Park is a gentle one. In the Reader's Digest profile, retired NYPD detective Elbin Mena reviewed footage from his motion-sensor cameras after a snowstorm and found that a neighbor had shoveled his walk, so he could identify the neighbor and thank him. That is what good cameras on a bungalow lot do most of the time: they answer the small question of who came through the gate and when. When the question is bigger, the same well-placed camera is the one police ask for.

Harding Park facts that shape a camera plan

  • Small lots, close houses: original lots of roughly 25 by 40 to 30 by 60 feet, with houses about twenty feet apart or closer, so every outdoor view risks reaching next door.
  • Narrow private lanes: mostly nine to thirteen feet wide with no sidewalks, owned by the homeowners association, so posts and cable stay off the lane.
  • Mixed construction: light-frame wood bungalows with additions in wood, cement and newer materials, which changes the mount and anchor at nearly every camera.
  • A flood-prone peninsula: 42.2 percent of properties at flood risk this year in First Street's model, so recorders and power equipment stay high and dry.
  • Overnight car crime nearby: the 43rd Precinct is one of two precincts behind nearly a third of Bronx auto accessory thefts, which usually happen at night.
  • Numbered addresses: houses are known by a number on Harding Park, and some lanes are unnamed, so camera names and clip notes need to say exactly where each view looks.

What Harding Park households often add next. Families who start with a camera on the front gate usually ask, a few weeks in, about a light that comes on when someone walks up the path after dark, or an alert when the back gate to the shoreline opens. We leave a spare port on the recorder and note where an extra cable could run, so the next addition does not mean drilling the same wall twice.

Short Distances, Close Neighbors: How a Camera Is Framed on a Harding Park Lot

Most camera products are sold with pictures of long driveways and wide lawns, and their default lenses are chosen for that. On a Harding Park lot, the front gate may be ten feet from the door, the side path may be three or four feet wide, and the back fence may be close enough that a wide lens takes in the whole yard and half the neighbor's too. A camera built to see sixty feet will show a face at four feet as a stretched blur at the edge of the frame, and it will happily record the house next door. The right lens for a four-foot view

The most common mistake we see on small lots is a very wide camera placed close to the door. It does catch everything, but a visitor standing on the step is pressed into one corner of the picture and bent by the lens, and a person walking along the side of the house appears for a fraction of a second at the edge. A camera works best when the person it needs to identify walks toward it and fills a fair share of the frame. On a bungalow, that usually means mounting a little farther back than people expect, under the eave at the corner rather than right over the door, and choosing a lens a step narrower than the box default. Varifocal cameras, whose zoom we set on site, help here because we can adjust the view to the exact width of a path or a gate after the camera is on the wall.

Privacy masks, and why we show them to you

In a neighborhood where houses face each other across a lane a dozen feet wide, a front camera will almost always see a neighbor's front door, windows or yard unless it is limited. Most recorders and business-grade cameras let us black out parts of the picture permanently, so those areas are never recorded at all. We set masks on any window, door or yard that belongs to someone else, then show you the masked view on your phone before we leave so you know exactly what the camera keeps. New York's section 52-a gives residential owners and tenants a way to sue over a neighbor's camera aimed at their back yard in certain situations, and beyond the law, Harding Park is a place where people live close and stay for decades. A camera that respects the house next door keeps the peace as well as the record.

Night on a lane without streetlights in front of every door

Many Harding Park lanes are lit unevenly, and the back of a lot facing the shoreline can be very dark. Infrared night vision, the invisible light most cameras use after dark, reaches out a set distance and reflects hard off anything close, including a neighbor's light-colored siding a few feet away. That reflection can wash out a side-path camera completely. We adjust infrared power on close views, use cameras that can switch to color when a porch or motion light comes on, and test every view after dark rather than trusting the daytime picture. Where a household wants more light, a small motion light at the gate or path often improves the picture more than a more expensive camera would, and we can place it so it does not shine into the neighbor's bedroom.

Original bungalows: low eaves, thin walls, short runs

The one-story cottages that started the colony are easy in one way and awkward in another. The runs are short: from the front eave to an interior closet may be only a few yards of cable. But the eaves are low, often just above head height, and the walls are light framing with siding or shingle over it. A camera mounted low can be reached from the lane, so we choose positions at the highest point of the eave, use tamper-resistant domes or turrets on the most exposed corners, and fix each mount into solid framing rather than siding alone, which can loosen in wind. Many of these houses have no real attic, so cable often travels along the outside under the eave in a slim painted raceway, then through one short sealed hole to the inside. That keeps the interior walls, which may hold old household wiring, out of the job.

Additions and joined houses: plan around the seams

When a bungalow has been added onto over decades, the interior walls of the old house and the new rooms rarely line up, and there may be no way to pass cable from one to the other without opening finished walls. In those houses we route around the outside instead, under the eaves and along the corners on supports or in conduit, entering the house once near the recorder. Where two cottages have been joined, there are often two front doors and two roof lines; each door needs its own view, and the cable from the far side crosses at the seam where the two structures meet, which is also where leaks show up first. We seal those crossings carefully and keep cable a little away from the seam so water running down it does not follow the cable inside.

Cement and block: masonry anchors and one sealed opening

Some front rooms and rear additions in Harding Park were built in cement block or poured concrete rather than wood. A camera on block needs masonry anchors set into the solid face of the block, not into the mortar joint, and a cable through it needs one planned opening drilled to size and sealed once the cable is in. Where a house is part wood and part block, we often put the camera on the block section, which holds a mount steadier in wind, and bring the cable into the wood section, where a hole is quicker and easier to seal. A drip loop below every entry point keeps rainwater from running along the cable into the wall.

Rebuilt houses and the grandfathered ones

Newer houses on old lots are usually the simplest camera jobs here: higher eaves, regular framing and sometimes an attic that lets cable reach each corner from inside. If the house was rebuilt to current flood rules, its lowest floor and mechanical equipment may already sit above the design flood elevation, which gives the recorder an obvious safe home. Older houses are different in a way that matters. An architect who renovated a Harding Park bungalow wrote that when the city deeded the land to the association, it essentially grandfathered the noncomplying construction, and that a larger alteration filing could have cost the house those existing conditions. Low-voltage camera work does not normally involve that kind of filing, and for most residential camera jobs no permit is required, but we keep our scope to low-voltage cable, mounts and sealed openings, and if a job would need new electrical circuits or structural changes, we say so in writing and name it as separate work before anything starts.

Small shops where one person minds everything

The businesses along Soundview Avenue and the southern end of White Plains Road are mostly small: a counter, a few aisles or tables, a back room and a back door. In a shop like that, the camera that matters most is the one just inside the entrance, mounted around head height so each customer walks toward it, followed by a camera over the counter that shows the customer, their hands and the register. The back door, which often opens onto a lot or a side street that empties at night, gets a camera with alerts only after closing. A tire or auto shop has a different shape: an open bay, a lot where customers' cars wait and stacks of tires and wheels that are worth stealing. There, cameras go high on the building to look down across the lot, framed on the vehicles and the bay doors, and the recorder goes inside the office on battery backup. Because Clason Point is low-lying near the water, we treat the shop's recorder the same way as a homeowner's: off the floor and away from anything that took water in the last big storm.

Bars, restaurants and the hours after midnight

A bar or restaurant near the water does its busiest business late, and the questions it brings to cameras are usually about the door, the register and the street outside at closing time. The door camera covers who comes in and goes out; the register camera covers cash and card handling without catching card PINs; and an outside camera above the entrance, with the landlord's agreement if the space is leased, covers the sidewalk and the curb in front. Kitchens get cameras only where staff and owners agree on what they show, and restrooms are never covered. Alerts stay quiet during service and come on after the last staff member leaves. We schedule drilling for the hours the place is closed, which for a late business is often late morning.

Association buildings and shared ground

The community center on Gildersleeve Avenue and the common ground around Harding Park belong to the homeowners association, not to any one household. Cameras there are the board's decision, and the questions are about who can see the footage, who holds the main login and what happens when officers change. A good plan names an association account rather than a person's own email as the owner of the system, gives viewing to the officers who need it, and writes down how passwords pass from one board to the next. We put that in a short written proposal the board can discuss at its meeting, with each camera position listed and each view described.

Rules and limits for business cameras near Harding Park

  • Audio is a separate decision. New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, and recording audio without the consent of at least one person in the conversation violates Penal Law 250.05. We leave audio off unless an owner has a clear reason and has taken advice.
  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas and anywhere people expect privacy are never covered, and we test each view to make sure no reflection reaches one.
  • The building owner decides outside the unit. In a leased shop, the facade, the sign band and the sidewalk canopy belong to the landlord, so outside cameras need written agreement first.
  • Association ground needs the association. Posts, cable or cameras on the lanes or common areas the homeowners association owns need its agreement before work starts.
  • Landmark rules apply only where they apply. The LPC Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras on designated buildings; we found no sign that Harding Park is in a historic district, and we check the city's landmarks map for any address before starting.
  • The installer should hold a state license. The NYS Department of State administers the Article 6-D security and fire alarm installer license; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which landlords and associations often ask to see.
Field Notes

Field Notes: A Tape Measure, a Flashlight and a Look at the High-Water Line

We do not publish stories about a particular house or family, so this is our routine for a Harding Park bungalow rather than an anecdote about one.

The first thing out of the bag is a tape measure, not a camera. We measure the lane in front of the gate, the distance from the eave to the gate and the door, the width of the side path and the gap to the neighbor's wall, because those numbers decide every lens. We stand where each camera would go and hold up a phone camera at that height to see what it would take in, then note every window and yard that belongs to someone else. Next we ask where water has stood. Residents here usually know exactly how high it came in the last bad storm, which room took water and where the yard ponds after heavy rain, and that answer decides where the recorder and battery go: off the floor, in an interior room, above anything that has ever been wet. Then we look for power and the internet box, and for a path for cable that stays outside the old walls if the inside of the house has been added onto too many times to follow. After dark, in person or from photos the household sends, we check how the lane is lit and how close the neighbor's siding is to the side camera. Only after that do we write the plan.

Lanes, numbers and the houses next door

Our lane has no name on the map, only house numbers. How should the cameras be named so a clip makes sense to someone outside the family?

Name each camera by the house number and exactly what it sees, for example the house number followed by front gate on lane, side path east, back fence toward water. Forgotten NY and CityLore both note that some Harding Park lanes are so small they have no names, and addresses read simply as a number on Harding Park. When a clip is exported, that camera name travels with it, so a detective, an insurer or a relative knows where it was recorded without a phone call. We set the names, the recorder's clock and the location on the account before we leave.

The camera on the bungalow next door looks straight at our bedroom window. Does New York's backyard camera law cover a window?

Section 52-a is aimed at a device on adjoining property recording a defined backyard recreation area without written consent and with specified intent, so a window may fall outside it. A camera that captures the inside of a bedroom can raise a separate concern under New York's unlawful surveillance law, Penal Law 250.45, which deals with places where people expect privacy. Start by talking to the neighbor, since most cameras can be masked or re-aimed in minutes, and speak to a lawyer if that fails. When we install, we mask neighbors' windows so our cameras never cause this.

Our front wall is only a few feet from the lane. Is a wide-angle camera the right choice at that range?

Usually not. At very close range, a wide lens pushes a person to the edge of the frame, where the lens bends faces and bodies, and it takes in the neighbors' houses across the lane as well. A slightly narrower lens, mounted at the corner of the eave so visitors walk toward it, gives a straighter, larger face. Where the distance is unusual, we use a varifocal camera and set the zoom on site to the exact width of your gate and steps, then show you the picture before the job is finished.

The power and phone lines run on a pole along our lane. Can a camera be mounted on the pole to see the whole lane?

No. Utility poles belong to the power and phone companies, and nobody else may attach equipment to them. A camera on your own house, high under the eave, can usually see your stretch of the lane and your car. If you need more height or a different angle, a short post on your own lot is the alternative, kept off the lane itself, which belongs to the homeowners association. We plan the camera from where you are allowed to mount it, not from the ideal spot.

Inside an old, added-onto bungalow

Our house is really two old bungalows joined together. Can one system cover both halves?

Yes, with one recorder in the half that is driest and easiest to reach. CityLore and the Fordham Ram describe bungalows here that have been fused into one larger house, and those houses often have two front doors, two roof lines and interiors that never fully connected. Each door gets its own camera. Cable from the far half usually travels outside under the eaves and crosses where the two structures meet, which is also where leaks appear first, so we keep it slightly away from the seam, loop it to shed water and seal the entry carefully.

Our bungalow has no attic, just a low roof over the ceiling. How does cable get to a camera at the eave?

Along the outside, in most cases. On a low-roofed cottage, there is no space above the ceiling to pull cable through, and opening interior walls in an old house risks disturbing old household wiring. So the cable runs under the eave in a slim raceway painted to match the trim, or neatly clipped to the fascia, and turns into the house through one short sealed hole near the recorder. It is visible only if you look for it, and it can be reached later without opening a wall.

Our front addition is cement block but the original part of the house is wood. Do the cameras mount the same way on both?

No. On wood, the mount is screwed into solid framing behind the siding, not into the siding alone, which can work loose in wind. On cement block, the mount takes masonry anchors set into the solid face of the block, away from crumbly mortar joints. Block often makes a steadier base for a camera, and wood makes an easier place for cable to enter the house, so on a mixed house we often mount on the block and bring the cable in through the wood, sealing both.

Our bungalow is light wood framing. Do the holes for camera cable make it easier for a fire to spread?

They should not, if each one is sealed properly. The city's planners describe Harding Park's houses as light-frame combustible construction set close together, so we keep openings few and small. Every hole through an exterior wall, a floor or a wall between rooms is sealed after the cable passes, with fire-rated sealant where the wall separates spaces and weatherproof sealant outside. We never pass camera cable through a gap around a chimney, a furnace flue or an electrical panel, and we leave you a note of every place we sealed.

Old household wiring runs all over our house. Does camera cable need to keep away from it?

Yes. Camera cable carries low voltage, and it should not share a box, a hole or a staple line with household electrical wiring, both for safety and because running beside power lines can add interference to the picture. Where the two have to cross, we cross them at right angles and keep them apart. In an older bungalow where wiring has been added over the decades, we trace what is in a wall before drilling near it, and if we find household wiring that looks unsafe, we tell you so you can call an electrician.

Water, power and the long outage

When the power goes out on the peninsula, how long does a battery backup keep the cameras recording?

It depends on how many cameras the battery feeds and how big it is, so we size it to your system and tell you the expected run time in writing. A typical backup on a small bungalow system covers short outages and gives you time to see what is happening; it will not carry a system through a day-long blackout like the one Bronx Ink reported after Sandy. For longer outages, a larger battery or a household generator can be added later. We test the backup by unplugging the power before we leave, so you know it works.

If our house takes water, could we lose all the footage? Should clips also be saved somewhere off the property?

In a flood-prone neighborhood, it is worth having a second copy. First Street's model puts 42.2 percent of Harding Park properties at flood risk this year. Keeping the recorder high in an interior room protects it from most water, but a copy of key cameras sent to a cloud service or a second recorder elsewhere means one flooded room does not take every clip with it. That needs a reliable internet connection, and some cloud services charge a fee, so we explain the options and let you choose.

If we raise our bungalow onto a higher foundation someday, do the cameras have to come down?

Usually they come down for the lift and go back up afterward. Raising a house is a major job, and the contractor will want the outside walls clear. If you are planning to raise or elevate the house in the next year or two, we can install cameras now with cable left long enough and fixed so it can be released and re-hung, and with the recorder in a room that will stay inside the house. After the lift, a short visit reconnects and re-aims each camera for the new height.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Harding Park

These are claims that turn up again and again in quick online answers, cost pages and installer sites about cameras in the Bronx, checked against what the sources say about this neighborhood. Cost pages on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; none of them has stood on a nine-foot private lane.

"Harding Park is a city park, not a place people live."

Search for Harding Park and the results mix the neighborhood with the small city park on Bolton Avenue, and sometimes with Harding Avenue in Throggs Neck, which is a different place entirely.

The park is real: NYC Parks lists a 2.16-acre playground that began as two vacant lots in 1992, now in line for a multimillion-dollar renovation that includes new security lighting. But Harding Park is mainly a cooperative of roughly 230 to 250 homes, depending on the source.

For cameras, that means the typical job is a family's bungalow, its gate and its car, not a public space. City cameras and park lighting, where they exist, do not cover anyone's side path.

"One doorbell camera is enough for a small house."

Quick answers often say a small home needs only a video doorbell, as if the front door were the only way in.

On a Harding Park lot, the front door may face the lane, but the side path between houses and the back yard, which on many lots faces open shoreline, are both out of sight of the street and the neighbors. Those are the places a doorbell cannot see.

A doorbell is a good start for the front step. Most households here end up with three or four cameras: gate and door, side path, back yard and the car on the lane.

"Put the recorder in the basement where nobody will find it."

Hiding the recorder is good advice in most places, and the basement is the usual suggestion.

In Harding Park, the city's planners describe storm surge and regular ponding, and after Sandy basements were apparently flooded across the neighborhood. A recorder on a basement floor, in a crawl space or in a yard shed can lose its footage in the very storm you would want to review.

We hide the recorder well above the floor in an interior room or closet, on battery backup, and offer an off-site copy of key cameras.

"Cloud cameras mean you never need a recorder."

Many quick answers recommend cloud-only cameras because the footage lives off the property and cannot be stolen.

That works only while the internet and power are up. After Sandy, residents nearby went without power for long stretches, and a cloud camera with no internet records nothing to the cloud. Battery cameras also wait to wake up, which can miss the first seconds of someone at the gate.

For a household here, the steadier setup is a local recorder on battery backup that records continuously, plus cloud copies of the most important clips when the connection allows.

"Installing cameras in NYC needs a permit and an electrician."

Some installer blogs say every New York City camera job needs a permit and a licensed electrician.

The sources say something narrower. The NYS Department of State administers a license for businesses that install security systems, and for most residential camera installations no permit is required. Camera cable powered from a recorder or switch is low-voltage work, not new electrical wiring.

Where a job needs a new outlet or circuit, that is an electrician's work and our quote says so. In older grandfathered bungalows, keeping the job to low-voltage work also avoids triggering filings that the household does not need.

"A citywide average tells you what your cameras will cost."

Cost guides publish a New York City average for camera installation, and AI answers repeat it as if it applied to every home.

On a Harding Park bungalow, two houses side by side can be very different jobs. One has a block addition that takes masonry anchors, another has no attic and needs cable run outside under the eaves, a third needs its recorder moved up out of a flood-prone room.

So we do not print one camera price for the neighborhood. Each job is priced on our Bronx rate, quoted line by line after photos or a visit, and booked within 2 hours.

"Your property, your camera: point it anywhere you like."

Plenty of online answers say a homeowner can aim a camera wherever they want as long as it is mounted on their own house.

New York's section 52-a lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera recording their back yard in certain situations, and audio recording has its own rules under Penal Law 250.05. On lots that started about twenty feet apart, almost any outdoor camera can reach next door.

That is why we mask neighbors' windows and yards, leave audio off by default and show you each view before we finish.

FAQ

Harding Park Security Camera Installation FAQ

Car parts get stolen overnight around here. Should our cameras record all night or only when they sense motion?
+

Record all night, and alert on motion. Vital City found that the 43rd Precinct, which covers Harding Park, is one of two Bronx precincts behind nearly a third of auto accessory thefts, and those usually happen overnight. A recorder that runs continuously keeps the seconds before and after a person appears, which motion-only recording can miss. Alerts are then set only for the camera watching the car and only for late hours, so you are not woken by every neighbor walking home. We size the recorder's drive so continuous recording still keeps the number of days you want.

Roosters and cats wander our lane at night. Will they keep setting off the camera alerts?
+

They can on a basic motion setting, which reacts to anything that moves. Writers who visit Harding Park often mention roosters walking in the road, and cats are common on any lane. Most current recorders and cameras can alert only when they detect a person or a vehicle, which filters out most animals. We switch that on, draw the alert area around your gate and path rather than the whole lane, and check a few nights of alerts with you afterward to tune it.

My parents read Spanish more easily than English. Can the camera app be set up in Spanish?
+

Most of the camera apps we install offer Spanish as a language setting, and the phone's own language usually decides it. We confirm the app for your system supports it before we order, set it up on your parents' phones, and name each camera simply so it reads clearly in either language. For relatives who prefer not to use an app at all, a screen in the house showing the gate camera is another option.

Family and neighbors fill our yard for summer gatherings. Should we tell guests the cameras are on?
+

It is a good courtesy, and a simple word or a small sign at the gate is enough for most families. New York's rules on recording conversations are stricter than its rules on silent video, which is one reason we leave audio off by default. If you would rather not record the yard during a party, the back-yard camera can be paused on a schedule or from the app and switched back on afterward. The gate and car cameras can keep recording either way.

We keep a kayak locked against the back fence near the water. Can a camera watch it at night?
+

Yes, if the camera is framed on the kayak and the fence close up rather than on the dark ground beyond. Over open water at night there is nothing for infrared to light, so a wide view shows only black. Mount the camera on the back of the house or a sturdy part of the fence on your own lot, aim it down at the boat and the gate, and add a small motion light if the spot is very dark. Alerts can be limited to that area after dark.

Our bungalow roof is old and soft in places. Will the installer need to walk on it?
+

Almost never. On a one-story bungalow, cameras go under the eave and cable runs along the fascia or through the wall, all reached from a ladder on the ground. We do not need to stand on the roof for that work, and we would not put weight on a roof that might be weak. If the only good position needs roof access, we tell you during the visit and offer another spot instead.

Can a camera be mounted on our chain-link front fence instead of the house?
+

It can, but a fence moves in wind and when the gate swings, and a camera on it will shake in the picture. If a fence spot is truly the best view, we use the heaviest post, usually the gate post set in the ground, fix the camera with a proper clamp and keep the cable protected on its way back to the house. In most cases a camera under the eave aimed down at the gate gives a steadier, harder-to-reach view.

Everyone in our house works during the week. Can the installation happen on a Saturday?
+

Ask when you book, and we will look for a weekend slot. Many households here work weekdays, and someone needs to be home to open the house, show us where the internet box is and choose who gets which login. Much of the planning can be done earlier from photos, so the installation day itself is spent on the work. Requests are booked within 2 hours, and the date is agreed with you before anything is ordered.

Lightning storms come in hard off the water. Can a surge knock out wired cameras?
+

A strong surge can damage any electronics, cameras and recorders included. We plug the recorder, switch and internet equipment into a battery backup with surge protection, keep camera cable apart from household wiring and ground the outdoor equipment where needed. That lowers the risk but cannot remove it entirely, which is why our product warranty, like most, excludes damage from power surges. If a storm takes out a camera, a tested backup and labeled cable make the repair quick.

I run a small trade business from a workshop in my back yard. Can its cameras have a separate login from the house cameras?
+

Yes. One recorder can hold both, with the workshop cameras on one account and the house cameras on another, or with each person's login limited to the cameras they should see. An employee or partner can view the workshop without seeing the family's front door or yard. If the workshop has its own internet line, it can also have its own small recorder. We write down which login sees what before we leave.
