The Neighborhood

Why Windsor Terrace Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Windsor Terrace is small, a narrow neighborhood pressed between Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery, and for most of its history it was a place of modest houses. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's 2013 report on the Prospect Avenue firehouse counts roughly 150 houses here in 1890, most of them small frame buildings, before the Calder company put up about 700 row houses between 1902 and 1919 and investors added apartment houses along Prospect Avenue in the 1920s. Construction of the Prospect Expressway in the 1950s then cut a trench through the middle of it. Every one of those building types reaches the present with the same weak point: none was built with a path for data. The houses have cellars, stairs and closets, plaster and shared brick, and the modern network has to be fitted into whichever of those the owner is willing to open.

The service at the curb is rarely the problem. In speed tests gathered between May 2016 and April 2017, Untapped Cities put Windsor Terrace third among Brooklyn neighborhoods, with a 90th-percentile average of 207.14 Mbps and a fastest recorded test of 803.18 Mbps, and credited "wide access to Fios" for the result. BroadbandNow's data for the whole borough, as of November 11, 2026, says fiber reaches 76.7 percent of Brooklyn, although its own table lists Verizon Fios at 65.6 percent, so take either number as a rough guide rather than a promise for your address. Either way, a household here can usually buy a fast plan. What it cannot buy is a way for that speed to climb from a fiber terminal on the cellar wall to the top-floor bedroom at the back of a row house. A single router on the parlor floor has to push its signal up through wooden floors, across plaster and around chimneys, and every barrier takes something away.

We hear the same pattern on almost every call. The front room is fine. The kitchen is acceptable. The back bedroom on the top floor drops video calls at the worst moment, the extension over the yard gets nothing, and a teenager has moved the game console to the stairs to find a signal. Mesh units help a little and then run out of road, because each one relays the last one's weakened signal. The answer is almost never a bigger router. It is a cable to each floor that ends at an access point or a wall jack where people actually sit, so the top floor gets its connection from a wire rather than from the floor below.

There is a second reason the question comes up so often here: these houses are being worked on. Owners replace systems room by room over years, and when the plaster is open is the cheapest moment to put cable in. Brick Underground described a corner townhouse at 702 Greenwood Avenue in 2018 whose owners had replaced nearly every system over about a decade, plumbing, electrical, the water main and the sewer line, and wired the whole house and the backyard for a music system, with gas, plumbing and electric left in place for a future attic conversion. A house like that has had its walls open more than once. If a network cable had gone in on any of those occasions, it would have cost a fraction of what it costs to fish one through finished rooms afterwards.

The reverse also happens. The author of the Brooklyn Row House blog, writing about a 1903 row house in the borough, described installing fourteen feet of three-inch metal conduit between the cellar ceiling and a second-floor closet in 1999 and pulling Cat5 through it before he knew which room would become the office. Wireless improved, the wire was never connected, and in his words "Their tails remain coiled, labeled and attached to nothing." When he finally wanted cameras and a better signal, the finished woodwork and insulation in the walls meant new cable could not simply be pulled where he needed it. We meet that situation in Windsor Terrace cellars regularly: old conduit, old Cat5, a box of phone wire, coax from two or three providers ago, and nobody sure which run goes where.

  • Most houses are attached. About 700 Calder row houses went up between 1902 and 1919, according to the Landmarks Preservation Commission, so side walls are shared masonry and the way up is through the interior: stair walls, stacked closets and chimney chases.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling for Windsor Terrace's Architecture

Nearly every Windsor Terrace call comes from one of six kinds of building. What separates them for a cabling crew is not the style of the front but four practical questions: where the service comes in, which way is up, how far the cable must go on each floor, and who besides the owner decides. The cards give the short version; the sections after them explain how we work in each.

The attached row house

Two- and three-story brick and limestone rows of the 1900s and 1910s, built close to the sidewalk with a full cellar, shared side walls and a stair along one of them.

The older frame house

Small wood-framed houses and cottages from before the rows, often re-sided and rewired over the years, with lighter walls and shallower cellars.

The house split into apartments

Row and frame houses that hold two or three households, with one cellar, one service entry and several people who each want their own network.

The 1920s apartment house

Walk-up and elevator buildings along Prospect Avenue and toward Fort Hamilton Parkway, with a cellar closet, a superintendent and an owner who controls the building's common spaces.

The park-edge co-op

Prewar co-ops along Prospect Park Southwest such as 135 Prospect Park Southwest, a six-story 1929 building with two elevators, where a board's alteration agreement governs any work.

The new elevator building

Large recent buildings such as 1 Park Point, and the smaller new ones going up on Prospect Avenue, with concrete floors and a telecom room the developer designed.

The attached row house: one stair wall, two party walls and a closet stack

In an attached row, the two long walls belong to you and your neighbors at the same time. They are solid masonry, they carry the floors, and nobody sensible chases a cable into them. That leaves the interior: the stair wall, the partitions between front and back rooms, closets and the chimney breast. Our first task in a row house is to find a vertical route that reaches every floor without crossing a finished room, and in the Calder rows that route is most often a pair or trio of closets stacked one above the other, a plumbing chase beside the bathroom, or an unused chimney flue that a mason has confirmed is no longer in service. We drill down through the closet floor into the cellar, pull every floor's cables up together, and finish each run at a plate in the closet or behind the nearest wall.

On each floor, the cable then has to travel horizontally from that route to the desk, the TV wall or the ceiling point. In a row house the floors usually span from party wall to party wall, so joists run across the width of the house, and a cable going from front to back has to cross every joist. Where the ceiling below is finished plaster, that means either a careful sequence of small openings that are patched afterwards, or a route along the top of a baseboard, behind a door casing or through a closet wall. We show you each option on the walk-through and write the one you choose into the quote, so there are no surprises about which rooms see a hole.

The older frame house: easy framing, layers of history

The small wood-framed houses that predate the rows are, in one sense, the easiest buildings in Windsor Terrace to cable. Their walls are studs and lath rather than masonry, so a cable can often be fished from the cellar straight up an interior wall cavity to a jack on the floor above. What makes them harder is everything that has happened to them since. Many have been re-sided with aluminum or vinyl, insulated by blowing material into the walls, rewired by several electricians in several decades, and extended at the back. Each of those changes affects whether a cavity is open, packed or blocked by a fire stop someone added.

We test before we promise. A small inspection hole inside a closet, a look at the cellar ceiling under the wall in question, and sometimes a borescope tell us whether a cavity is clear. Where blown insulation fills a wall, a cable can sometimes still be pushed through with the right rod, and sometimes it cannot, in which case we look for a closet, a chase or a corner instead. Frame houses also tend to have shallower cellars and lower ceilings, so we plan where the equipment sits around head height and headroom, not just around the service entry.

The house split into apartments: one cellar, several households

Many Windsor Terrace houses hold an owner and one or two tenant households. The building usually has one service entry, often in a cellar the owner controls, and each household may have its own provider account, or the owner may supply internet as part of the rent. Either way, each apartment needs a clean cable path from the cellar and its own jack or access point location, and the shared equipment has to sit where the owner can reach it without going through a tenant's rooms. We plan the cellar as the meeting point: each apartment gets its own labeled runs back to a panel, and each household's equipment is kept separate so one tenant's router never sits on another's network.

Owners who are thinking about including internet in the rent, a question Brooklyn landlords have argued about on Brownstoner's forum for years, need to decide one thing before we cable: whether each apartment will have its own provider line, or whether one connection will be shared and divided inside the building. The cabling is different in each case. With separate accounts, each apartment's cable starts at that provider's equipment. With one shared connection, we build a small switched network in the cellar with a separate segment for each apartment, so tenants cannot see each other's devices, and the owner has a plain way to restart it without entering anyone's home.

The 1920s apartment house: the owner's cellar, the superintendent's keys

The apartment houses built along Prospect Avenue in the 1920s and the walk-ups toward Fort Hamilton Parkway are a different kind of job. The building's service usually arrives in a cellar closet or on a wall beside the boiler room, and from there the building's own systems, cameras, an entry panel, laundry machines, a superintendent's computer, need a network that is not anyone's apartment Wi-Fi. That common network is the owner's responsibility and the owner's decision, and we build it in the cellar and halls, with its own equipment, its own cabinet and its own labels.

Getting cable up a masonry building to a camera on an upper landing or an entry panel at the front door means using what the building already offers: an existing riser closet, a dumbwaiter shaft that has been closed off, a pipe chase or the space behind a stair stringer. Where none of those exists, surface raceway along a hallway ceiling, painted to match, is often the honest answer, and we tell the owner that at the survey instead of promising a hidden route through solid walls. Openings through floors are sealed with listed firestop as the work goes, because in a building with several apartments a hole between floors is a fire path.

The park-edge co-op: board rules first, then the route

The prewar co-ops on Prospect Park Southwest are buildings in which a shareholder owns the apartment's interior use but not the walls, the risers or the cellar. Before we drill, the board's alteration agreement or the managing agent's rules decide what kind of work is allowed, what insurance the contractor must carry, which hours are permitted and whether a plan must be submitted. For most cabling jobs inside an apartment, the paperwork is modest: a scope letter describing each opening, a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the managing agent, and work during the building's permitted hours.

Inside the apartment, the route is usually shorter than in a house, but the obstacles are different. Prewar apartments tend to have one cable outlet where the provider installed it, often in the living room or foyer, and long hallways between rooms. Walls between rooms are often plaster on terra cotta block or gypsum block rather than wood studs, so fishing a cable straight down a wall is not possible. We use closets, door casings, baseboard routes and, where the board allows, small channels in a partition that a plasterer closes afterwards. Nothing leaves the apartment for the public hallway or the building's risers without the board's written agreement.

The new elevator building: the developer's telecom room and your unit

Large new buildings such as 1 Park Point, which New York YIMBY described as 13 stories with 375 rental units averaging about 798 square feet, arrive with their cabling designed in: telecom rooms, a building provider and a jack or two in each unit. A renter in a building like this rarely needs, or is allowed, new cable inside the concrete. What we are asked for instead is a wired connection from the unit's existing outlet to a desk in the second bedroom, a cleaner place for the router than the floor of a closet, or an access point in a long apartment where the provider's single router sits at one end. Those can usually be done with surface-mounted raceway, a door-casing route or a small wireless bridge, all of it removable when the lease ends.

Smaller new buildings are going up along Prospect Avenue too, and there the developer, not the tenant, is our client. The time to bring a cabling contractor into a new building is before the walls close, when conduit to each unit, a riser sleeve between floors and a properly ventilated telecom closet cost little compared with retrofitting them. HPD's broadband guidance for the projects it finances says in-unit broadband is required in all new construction and common-area broadband in all new construction and some preservation projects, so for an HPD-financed building the cabling scope is not optional and belongs in the first drawings.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in a Windsor Terrace Row House Before Drawing a Route

The survey in a Windsor Terrace row house follows the same order every time, because the order prevents expensive mistakes. It starts at the front wall of the cellar, where the provider's line comes in. We note what is there today, a fiber terminal, a cable modem, an old telephone block or all three, and which of the cables leaving it actually carry service. Then we look straight up. If the cellar ceiling is open joists, the cellar becomes the network's main hallway. If someone has finished it with drywall, we look for an access panel, a soffit over a pipe or a run along the edge of the ceiling where it meets the wall.

Last, we test what is already there. Old Cat5, phone wire and coax are toned to find their far ends and, where they might be reused, checked on a certifier. In our experience the old cable worth keeping in a house this age is a run that tests clean end to end and ends somewhere useful; anything that fails is better used as a pull string for its replacement or removed. By the end of the survey we can draw the route on a sketch of each floor, mark every hole, and price the job. That sketch becomes the written quote, and later the port map.

Pathways

From the Front Wall of the Cellar to the Top-Floor Rear Room: Routing Cable Through an Attached Windsor Terrace House

The hardest run in most Windsor Terrace houses is the same one: from the cellar, where service arrives at the front, to the top floor at the back, which is where bedrooms and home offices tend to be. A straight line between those two points crosses two or three floors, a stair, plaster walls and every joist in the house. A good route rarely follows a straight line. It goes straight up somewhere convenient and then across somewhere hidden, and choosing those two places is most of the design work on the job.

Going up, the best routes are, in order: an existing usable conduit from the cellar to an upper floor; a stack of closets; a plumbing chase that has room beside the pipes; an unused chimney flue that a mason has confirmed is out of service and can carry a sleeve; and the stair wall, entered from the cellar below and reached on each landing. Each has its trade-offs. A closet stack loses a few inches of a closet corner on each floor to a small cover. A plumbing chase means coordinating with whoever might open it later. A chimney flue needs a sleeve and a firestop at each floor. The stair wall means small openings on each landing that a plasterer closes. We tell you which of these your house offers and which we recommend.

Going across, the options depend on what is under each floor. On the parlor floor, the cellar below is usually open, so a cable can come up from the cellar exactly under the jack and needs no horizontal run at all. On the upper floors the horizontal run has to happen in the floor or wall between the route and the room. We use a few standard methods: drilling through the top plate from a closet and fishing along an interior wall; running behind a baseboard that is removed and replaced; following a door casing; or, where the household prefers no openings at all, a painted surface raceway along the top of a baseboard or the edge of a ceiling. Surface raceway is often the right answer in a rental or in a room with original woodwork that the owner does not want disturbed.

Openings sealed as the work goes

Every hole we drill between floors is a path for air, smoke, mice and sound. Each one is sealed as soon as the cable is through, with firestop where it passes from one floor to another or from the cellar into the house, and with a grommet or bushing where it passes metal. Where a cable leaves the house, for a yard camera or an access point in the back garden, it passes through a single sealed opening with a drip loop outside, and surge protection is fitted where it enters. We never leave a hole open overnight in a house with children or pets, and any plaster opening is either patched by us or left neat and squared for your plasterer, as the quote says.

Data kept apart from everything else in the walls

Network cable shares the house with electrical wiring, heating pipes, gas lines and, in many Windsor Terrace houses, an older doorbell or intercom wire. We keep data cable away from electrical lines where they run alongside each other, cross them at right angles where they must meet, and never pull data through the same holes as new power wiring an electrician is installing. Cable stays well clear of boiler flues and hot pipes. And where an old system, a music system or an alarm panel already has wires in a closet or a chase, we route around them and label ours, so the next person who opens that closet knows which wires are the network.

Service Entry

Fios, Spectrum or a Building Provider: Where Windsor Terrace Service Arrives and Where the Network Should Begin

Before we plan a single run, we need to know how the internet reaches your building, because that decides where the network starts. In most Windsor Terrace houses, the provider's line comes in at the front of the cellar, either through the foundation wall from the street or down from an aerial drop on the facade. Verizon Fios ends at a fiber terminal on the wall; cable service from Spectrum or another provider ends at a coax splitter and a modem. In newer apartment buildings the building may have its own provider arrangement, and the service reaches each unit over the building's wiring from a telecom room you will never see.

The provider's technician puts the terminal where it is quickest to install, which is usually right where the line enters, and then plugs the router in beside it. That is why so many households here have their router on a cellar shelf or in the front room on the parlor floor, as far as possible from the top-floor rear bedroom. Structured cabling lets you separate the two. The provider's equipment stays where the line comes in, a single cable carries the service from there to a better central spot, and the network spreads from that spot to every floor. Often the best central spot is not a room at all but the bottom of the closet stack in the cellar, directly under the route that goes up.

Switching providers is common here, and a good cabling plan makes it painless. If every room's cable comes back to one panel, changing from cable to fiber, or from one fiber provider to another, only changes the first link between the provider's box and your router. Nothing inside the walls has to move. We also recommend leaving one spare cable, or an empty conduit with a pull string, from the provider's entry point to the panel, so the next installer has a ready path instead of drilling a new hole through the front of the house. Owners of multi-family buildings who have watched each new tenant's provider drill another hole in the facade will understand why.

The building's wiring, the building's rules

In apartment buildings, how service gets to each unit is often governed by an agreement between the owner and a provider. HPD's broadband guidance for projects it finances sets out the rules the Federal Communications Commission now applies to those deals: exclusive revenue-sharing agreements between a landlord and an internet provider are banned, so are graduated revenue-share deals tied to the share of units that subscribe, and so are sale-and-leaseback arrangements for a building's inside wiring. A provider with an exclusive marketing arrangement must disclose it. The same guidance notes that existing wiring must be in good repair to be reused, and points out that owning the building's infrastructure gives an owner more control and leverage in rate negotiations, as long as maintenance is part of the provider's contract.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Licenses, Landmarks and Boards: What Governs a Windsor Terrace Cabling Job

The electrical permit question. Since December 21, 2025, the city's Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, says an electrical permit is not required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. Network cabling falls inside that description. That does not mean anything goes. The cable still has to be rated for the space it passes through, openings between floors still have to be firestopped, and fire alarm wiring is a separate matter with its own licensing and permits. Work on the power side, a new outlet beside the equipment cabinet, a dedicated circuit for a rack, is an electrician's job and is not part of what we install.

Our license. We work under New York State license #12000287431 and carry insurance for low-voltage work. A certificate of insurance goes to the owner, the co-op or condo board or the managing agent before the crew arrives, naming whoever the building asks us to name.

Landmarks. Windsor Terrace is not one of the city's large historic districts. The Prospect Avenue firehouse of Engine 240 and Battalion 48 was designated an individual landmark in 2013, and some blocks at the Park Slope edge that people describe as Windsor Terrace fall inside an extension of the Park Slope Historic District. If your building is a landmark or sits in a district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews most changes to the outside, which includes a new provider line on the facade or a cable running down a front wall. Interior cabling is generally not reviewed. Check your address on the Commission's map before any exterior work, and we will plan the job so that nothing visible appears on a landmarked front without a permit.

Boards and managing agents. In the co-ops on Prospect Park Southwest and the condos at the edges of the neighborhood, the alteration agreement decides what may be opened, when work may happen and what insurance is needed. Rentals are governed by the lease: a tenant needs the landlord's written permission before anything is drilled, and we will not drill a rental apartment without it. Owners of two- and three-family houses need to give tenants proper notice before work inside their apartments.

Party walls and neighbors. On an attached row, the walls between houses are shared. We do not drill into a party wall to bury a cable, and we never route anything through a neighbor's cellar or roof without the neighbor's written consent. If the only practical route for a provider's line runs across a neighbor's facade or roof, that is a conversation for you, the neighbor and the provider before it is a job for us.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Windsor Terrace: Questions Answered

These are the situations Windsor Terrace owners, renters and businesses describe when they call: wiring an earlier owner left behind, rooms with features nobody wants damaged, apartments in big buildings, new construction, shops on the main streets and a few things particular to this neighborhood. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

What earlier owners left in the walls

There is an old metal conduit running from our cellar ceiling up into a closet on the second floor. Can new network cable be pulled through it?

Very often, yes, and it can save most of the work on the job. We first find out what the conduit holds and where it really ends, because an old pipe sometimes carries a doorbell wire or an abandoned phone line and sometimes turns a corner nobody expected. If it is empty or holds only dead wire, we pull a string through, check the bends and the ends for sharp edges, and fit bushings so the new cable is not scraped going in. A three-inch conduit can carry a good number of cables to the second floor at once; the Brooklyn Row House blogger who installed one in his own 1903 house in 1999 later wrote that he probably should have gone with four-inch, which tells you how quickly a single path fills. Before we use it, we confirm it is not carrying live electrical wiring, which data cable must not share.

A previous owner left a bundle of Cat5 coiled in the cellar, each end labeled with a room name but plugged into nothing. Is it worth bringing back to life?

Sometimes. Labeled ends are a good start, because they suggest someone ran each cable to a specific room. We tone each one to find its far end, look at what is printed on the jacket and test it on a certifier. Plain Cat5 from the 1990s may still pass at the speeds most households use today, and if the far end is in a useful place we can terminate it on a panel and put a proper jack on the wall. Where a run fails, is kinked, or ends behind a wall that has since been closed, it becomes a pull string: we tie the new cable to it and pull the replacement through the same path. Either way, you avoid opening the walls the earlier owner already went through.

Our house came with a whole-house music system whose wires run to several rooms and out to the yard. Can the new network share that system's closet and routes?

It can share the closet and often the route, but not the wires. Whole-house music systems like the one Brick Underground described in a renovated Greenwood Avenue house use speaker cable and control wiring that are not suitable for a network, and the system's controller may itself need a network connection. We look at where the system's wires travel, because whoever installed it has already found a way from the closet to each room, and we often run new network cable alongside on the same path. In the closet, the music equipment and the network equipment are kept on separate shelves with their own labels. If the music system is old and no longer used, we can remove its wiring at the same time, with your agreement.

Rooms, features and future plans

The desk in our front bedroom sits under the window, right where the radiator cover is. Can a cable come up behind the radiator?

Not directly behind the radiator itself. A steam or hot-water radiator runs hot for months, and the space behind a radiator cover is the warmest part of the room; network cable is rated for ordinary room temperatures and should not be pressed against a hot surface or a supply pipe. What we usually do is bring the cable up through the floor a short distance to one side of the radiator, outside the cover, and finish it at a jack on the wall beside it, or at a floor box if the floor allows. If the cover is a built-in cabinet with space at one end away from the heat, the jack can sometimes sit inside that end. The pipes that feed the radiator from the cellar also mark a route we avoid.

We plan to turn our attic into a bedroom next year. Should a cable be run up there now, before the room exists?

If we are already cabling the floor below, yes, and it costs very little extra. A cable or two, plus an empty conduit with a pull string, can be left coiled at the top of the closet stack or the attic hatch, labeled and tested, ready for the attic's builder to bring to the right wall once the room is framed. The alternative is to fish cable into a finished attic room next year through a ceiling that has just been closed. Tell your architect or contractor where the cable is waiting, so it is not covered with insulation or cut when the attic floor goes down. When the room is built, we come back to terminate the jack and fit the access point.

Apartments in large and new buildings

Our apartment is in a building with hundreds of units, and our Wi-Fi competes with dozens of neighbors' networks. Would a cable to an access point in our own apartment help?

Often a great deal. In a large building every apartment has its own router and the air is crowded, so a single router at one end of a long apartment has to shout over its neighbors to reach the far bedroom. A wired access point placed in the middle of the apartment, at lower power, serves your rooms well without fighting the whole floor. It also lets you put the desk computer and the TV on cables, which takes them off the wireless network entirely. In a rental we do it without opening walls: a surface raceway from the unit's existing outlet, a cable along the top of a baseboard or a door casing, and an access point that can be taken down when you move out.

Our small new building on Prospect Avenue is being financed through an HPD program. Does that mean every apartment must have a network connection?

According to HPD's residential broadband guidance, broadband in each unit is required in all new construction it finances, and common-area broadband is required in all new construction and some preservation projects. The installation cost is treated as a construction cost and the service cost as part of the building's operations. In practice that means the cabling scope belongs in the first set of drawings, not in a change order after the walls close: a sleeve or conduit from the provider's entry to a telecom closet, a riser to each floor, a cable to each apartment and a plan for common-area Wi-Fi. HPD's own design guidelines and its staff set the exact requirements for your program, and we build to them.

Studios and storefront fit-outs

Our new studio leads classes from a screen at the front of the room and plays music in every room. What should be cabled before the mirrors go up?

Everything that sits behind a mirror or on the front wall. The screen needs a network jack and power behind it, plus an empty conduit to wherever the source device or the instructor's station sits, so the cords do not cross the floor. The music system needs a cable to each room's speakers or amplifier, depending on the system you choose, and a network connection for its controller. The check-in desk needs wired connections for its computer and card reader. A ceiling access point covers members' phones and any wireless equipment. Once mirrors are hung on the walls, adding any of this means taking them down, so we plan the room before the fit-out crew starts.

Questions particular to Windsor Terrace

We live a block from the Windsor Terrace Library and can pick up its free Wi-Fi from our front steps. Could it serve as our backup when our own internet goes down?

We would not build it into your network. The Brooklyn Public Library says the branch's rooftop antenna extends free wireless internet about 300 feet into the neighborhood, which is a real help to a neighbor with a phone or a laptop outdoors. But it is a public network shared with strangers, it is not designed to carry a household's traffic, and bridging a public network into your home network would expose your devices to it. If you need a backup connection for working from home, a cellular backup on your own router is the right tool, and it switches over by itself when your main line drops.

Our neighborhood tests near the top in Brooklyn for internet speed. Why does our back bedroom still feel slow?

Because the speed rankings measure the line, and your back bedroom is measuring the house. Untapped Cities ranked Windsor Terrace third in Brooklyn using tests that reached a 90th-percentile average of 207.14 Mbps, but a speed test on a phone in a top-floor rear bedroom measures the Wi-Fi between that phone and a router two floors and several walls away. To tell the difference, test with a laptop plugged into the router by cable, then test in the problem room. If the first number is good and the second is poor, the line is fine and the house needs a cable to that floor. If both are poor, the problem is the service or the router, and we will say so rather than sell you cabling.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Windsor Terrace

"Faster internet will fix slow Wi-Fi"

This is the most common advice and the least useful one here. Windsor Terrace already has fast service available; Untapped Cities ranked it third in Brooklyn for speed and attributed that to wide access to Fios. A household that upgrades from a fast plan to a faster plan usually sees the improvement in the front room and nowhere else.

The slowdown happens inside the building. A router on the parlor floor or in the cellar has to reach the top-floor rear bedroom through wooden floors, plaster and the stair wall, and the signal that arrives there is a fraction of what left the router, whatever the plan says.

The fix is to carry the speed to the floor by cable and let a local access point do the last few feet. Before you pay for a bigger plan, test with a laptop plugged into the router, then test in the slow room, and compare.

"You can run cable through any wall"

In a frame house with open stud cavities, a cable can often be fished from the cellar up an interior wall. In an attached row house it cannot go into the side walls at all, because they are shared masonry party walls that carry the floors and belong to two owners.

The prewar co-op apartments on Prospect Park Southwest add another obstacle: partitions between rooms are often plaster on block, not hollow drywall, so there is no cavity to fish through.

That is why our survey in these buildings starts by looking for closets that stack, old conduit, chases and stair walls, and why we sometimes recommend painted surface raceway instead of a hidden route that would mean opening finished plaster in several rooms.

"Old Cat5 should always be ripped out"

Many guides treat any Cat5 as obsolete. In practice, an old run that tests clean end to end may still carry the speeds a household uses, and even a failed run has value: it is a pull string already sitting in exactly the path the new cable needs.

The Brooklyn Row House blogger described the opposite problem in his 1903 house: Cat5 that had been run, labeled and coiled in 1999, and never connected. Removing it without testing it would have thrown away the one route through his finished walls.

We tone and certify old cable before deciding. What passes can be terminated; what fails becomes the way to pull its replacement; what goes nowhere is removed so it stops confusing the next person.

"A national price per jack tells you what your job will cost"

National cost guides quote averages across every kind of house in the country. Angi, for example, gives a national range for Ethernet installation and notes that most projects fall within a broad band. Those figures describe the average house, not a three-story attached row in Brooklyn.

UniFi Nerds, writing about New York City, makes the point directly: a multi-level townhouse with finished areas, limited pathways and strict scheduling can cost much more than a simpler build with the same number of drops. That is exactly the kind of house Windsor Terrace is full of.

The reliable way to price a job here is a survey of the actual route. That is why we quote per job, in writing, and list every jack, hole and route so the number can be compared fairly with anyone else's.

"Low-voltage cabling always needs a permit," or "it is completely unregulated"

Both are wrong for New York City since December 21, 2025. Under Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, an electrical permit is not required for wiring that operates at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data.

That exemption does not remove the other rules. Cable still has to carry the right rating for the space it runs through, openings between floors still need firestop, fire alarm wiring is handled separately, and a co-op board's alteration agreement or a landlord's lease still decides what can be drilled.

Anyone who tells you a network job in a co-op needs no paperwork, or that it needs a full electrical permit, is skipping a step. The paperwork that matters is usually the board's, not the city's.

"The internet provider will wire the rooms for you"

A provider's installation brings service to one point, usually where the line enters the building, and connects a modem or terminal there. Some will add a second outlet. None will plan a route to every floor of a row house, test old cable or build separate networks for a shop.

In apartment buildings, the provider's agreement with the owner sets what it may do inside, and HPD's broadband guidance notes that the FCC now bans sale-and-leaseback deals for a building's inside wiring, so an owner who wants control of that wiring should own it outright.

The right division of work is simple. The provider brings service to the building; a cabling contractor builds the network inside it; and the two connect at one documented point.

"Mesh Wi-Fi replaces the need for cable"

Mesh systems are good products, and in a single-floor apartment or a small frame house they may be enough. Each satellite, though, has to receive its signal wirelessly from the previous one before it can pass it on, so every hop through a floor or a plaster wall costs speed.

In a three-story attached house, a satellite on the top floor is relaying a signal that has already crossed two floors. Families notice it as calls that drop in the back bedroom while the kitchen is fine.

Most mesh systems can use a cable between satellites instead of the air. Wiring the satellites back to the main router, which is called wired backhaul, turns the same equipment into something much closer to a structured network, and it is often the cheapest upgrade a household already owning mesh can make.

FAQ

Windsor Terrace Structured Cabling FAQ

Who should I hire to put ethernet jacks in a Windsor Terrace house, an electrician or a cabling installer?
+

A low-voltage cabling installer. Since December 21, 2025, New York City does not require an electrical permit for data wiring under 50 volts, and network cabling is specialist work: planning the route, terminating, labeling and certifying each run. An electrician is still needed for any new outlet or circuit for the equipment.

Is it hard to run ethernet cable through a Windsor Terrace row house yourself?
+

The cellar part is often manageable for a handy owner. The hard part is getting from the cellar to the upper floors without opening plaster, because the side walls are shared masonry and the way up is through closets, chases or the stair wall. Terminating and testing each run properly also takes equipment most households do not own.

Is it cheaper to cable during a renovation than after we move back in?
+

Almost always. When walls, floors or ceilings are already open, cable goes in with little extra labor and no patching. Once rooms are finished and furnished, the same runs need careful routing, small openings that must be repaired and more time on site.

Does choosing Cat6A instead of Cat6 add much to a Windsor Terrace job?
+

It adds some material and labor, because Cat6A is thicker and stiffer and fills a closet stack or an old conduit faster. In most houses Cat6 serves every room well. Cat6A makes most sense for long runs, access points expected to need very high speeds later, and business backbones.

What should a written cabling quote for our house list?
+

Every jack and access point by room, the cable type, the route floor by floor, every hole and how it will be sealed or patched, how each cable is terminated and labeled, the certification test, the port map you will receive and any equipment priced separately.

How can we keep the cost down without ending up with a weak network?
+

Wire the things that matter most first: an access point on each floor, the home office desk and the main TV. Leave an empty conduit or a spare cable up the closet stack for later. Supply your own equipment if you already own good access points or a mesh kit that can use a cable.

What should we ask any cabling company before hiring it for a Windsor Terrace job?
