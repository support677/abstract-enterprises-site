The Neighborhood

Why Dutchess County Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Built Around Turnover

Most smart lock advice assumes one household that never changes. Dutchess County rarely works like that. The county is mostly owner-occupied — the Census Bureau’s QuickFacts put the owner-occupied rate at 69.2 percent for 2020–2024, across roughly 124,843 housing units and about 300,708 residents in its July 2025 estimates — yet a striking share of the calls we get from here are about doors that somebody else uses. A landlord in the City of Poughkeepsie re-letting a two-family. Parents who bought a house near campus for their own child and rent the other rooms. A couple in Beacon who rent their home to visitors while they travel. An estate owner in Rhinebeck or Staatsburg whose caretaker, gardener and housekeeper each carry a copy of a key nobody has counted in years. The lock is the easy part. The hard part is knowing, at any moment, exactly who can open each door — and being able to change that without a locksmith.

The colleges set one of the county’s clocks. Marist University reports 6,382 students on a campus that sits on Route 9 in the Town of Poughkeepsie, and its own housing pages tell graduate students that they must live off campus, in apartment complexes or in private homes around Poughkeepsie and the nearby towns. Vassar sits on roughly a thousand acres on the other side of the city, with the Arlington neighbourhood’s houses and apartments around it. Up the river, Bard’s residence-life office says the majority of its off-campus students live close to the college in Red Hook, Tivoli or Rhinebeck. Every one of those leases ends in late spring and begins again in late summer, often with five or six unrelated young adults in one house, and each of them used to be handed a key that could be copied at any hardware store on Route 9. A keypad lock with individual codes changes that turnover from a re-keying job into a few minutes in an app — if it is planned properly.

Short-term rentals set the second clock, and in Dutchess the rules change at every town line. The City of Beacon’s 2020 law, as The Highlands Current summarised it in February 2026, lets homeowners and tenants rent their own primary residence for up to 30 days at a time and no more than 100 days a year, and does not allow accessory dwelling units to be rented that way; the same report found 133 Beacon units listed on Airbnb in July 2025 against only 33 registered with the city. The Town of Rhinebeck rewrote its short-term rental law in 2025: an applicant must submit a floor plan of every level showing bedrooms, windows and exits, authorise the Code Enforcement Officer and the Fire Inspector to inspect, and name a contact who will answer the town within 24 hours. Red Hook’s chapter on short-term rentals asks for a designated local contact whenever the owner is not staying within 15 air miles. None of those laws mentions locks. All of them assume that the right person can get into the house quickly, and that guests can get out of it.

Then there is the age of the doors. Dutchess is not the oldest county in the valley — a Stacker analysis of Census Bureau data puts the median home here at 1971 — but about 18 percent of the county’s homes, some 22,056 of them, were built in 1939 or earlier, concentrated in exactly the places where rentals cluster: the City of Poughkeepsie, Beacon, and the old village centres of Rhinebeck, Red Hook, Millbrook and Pawling. Old doors mean old paint. Dutchess County’s health department tells owners of two-or-more-unit rental properties in the City of Poughkeepsie’s 12601 zip code, built before 1980, that they must register with the New York State rental registry and obtain a lead safety certificate after an inspection that includes dust-wipe sampling. Drilling a new bore through a painted door and frame is a small job, but it is exactly the kind of job that makes dust, and we treat it that way.

And because this is New York State rather than New York City, the tenant rules that apply here are the state’s. Since the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act of 2019, section 768 of the Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law makes it unlawful anywhere in the state to change the lock on an occupant’s entrance door without supplying them with a key — and the state Bar Association notes the act made unlawful eviction a Class A misdemeanor statewide. A smart lock gives a Dutchess landlord a very fast way to break that law by accident, simply by deleting a code. Part of every rental job we do here is setting up the access so that the owner keeps control without ever being one tap away from a lockout.

Door details we check on every Dutchess job

Paint, and what is under it. On any house that looks older than about 1978 we assume the door and frame carry old paint unless the owner has a lead certificate or test that says otherwise. We choose locks that can use the existing cross bore and edge bore where possible, so the new work is mostly in screw holes and strike mortises rather than fresh large holes. Where more cutting is unavoidable we mask the floor, catch dust at the drill, and clean with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes rather than a broom. The EPA’s renovation rule treats disturbing six square feet or less of interior painted surface, or twenty square feet or less outside, as minor maintenance and repair, as long as no prohibited practice is used; a lock change is normally far below that. If a job would grow beyond it — replacing a whole door, say — we tell you before we start.

Doors that are thicker, older or out of square. Many Dutchess front doors are heavier than the 1-3/8 to 1-3/4 inch range a typical smart deadbolt is listed for, or are bored at an unusual backset, or are fitted with an old mortise lock. Before recommending a model we measure thickness, backset and bore size, and check the frame for rot. Where a door is outside the lock’s range, the answer is usually an interior retrofit unit over a compatible deadbolt, a lock line with a thick-door kit, or a carpenter’s repair first.

Frames that move with the seasons. An old wooden jamb is a different width in August than in February. Someone turning a key by hand never notices a sixteenth of an inch; a small motor driving the bolt does, stalling and draining batteries. We test the bolt with the door pulled shut by hand, closed by its own weight and closed by any storm door, and we adjust or enlarge the strike so the bolt throws cleanly in both seasons.

One plain motion out. Every lock we put on a Dutchess house door opens from inside with a thumb-turn or lever, no key, code or app. That matters most in rentals and hosted homes, where the person inside is a stranger to the house, and where a town’s permit file already shows where the exits are.

Compliance callouts for Dutchess business doors

  • Exit without knowledge or effort. A door that serves as a way out must open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. Keypads go on the outside; inside, it is a lever, a thumb-turn, panic hardware or a push-to-exit control.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure, door by door. When power drops, an electric strike or magnetic lock either releases or stays locked. Which is right depends on whether the door is on the exit route and what the building code and fire marshal expect, so we specify it per door.

We prefer readers and keypads that mount on the frame or inside the vestibule, and we tell owners to check with the municipality before anything visible changes.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Dutchess County: Questions Answered

Five kinds of Dutchess owner call us most: landlords letting to students, people hosting guests in their own homes, owners of old houses and estates, families reacting to a police warning, and businesses with staff who change every season. Their questions are answered below in those five groups; money, scheduling and travel sit in the FAQ near the end of the page. We are lock installers, not lawyers, so wherever a statute or town code comes up we name it and you can check it, or have your attorney check it, for yourself.

Student houses near Marist, Vassar and Bard

We let a house near Marist to a new group of students every August — how do we wipe every old code between May move-out and move-in?

Treat the turnover as a checklist rather than a scramble, and build it into the lock from the first day. When we set up a student house, each tenant gets their own code or fob, created with an end date that matches the lease, and the owner keeps the only administrator account. In May, when the lease ends, those codes stop working on their own; the owner doesn’t have to remember to delete anything. Over the summer, the house can run on a short list of owner, painter, cleaner and contractor codes, each with its own dates. In August, the new group is enrolled, preferably in person on move-in day so each student tests their own code at the door while someone watches.

Three things go wrong if this isn’t planned. A tenant is made an administrator “to help” and quietly creates codes for friends that the owner can’t see. A shared house code is used instead of individual codes, so nobody knows who let whom in, and changing it means telling every tenant. And the mechanical key cylinder is forgotten: the students had copies of the old key, and the new lock’s cylinder was never re-keyed. We fix all three at installation by keeping administration with the owner, using one code per person, and re-keying or replacing the cylinder whenever a lock outlasts a group of tenants.

Keep a short record of each turnover: which codes were removed, which were added, and the date the cylinder was last changed. If a dispute about a missing item or a late move-out ever comes up, the lock’s log and that record together tell a clear story.

Can each student in a shared Poughkeepsie rental have a keypad lock on their own bedroom door?

It can be done, and students often ask for it, but it needs more thought than the front door. A bedroom-door lock in a house let by the room is mainly about privacy and belongings, so a simple keypad lever or deadbolt with the student’s own code is usually enough. What it must never do is make the room harder to leave. The inside has to open with a plain turn or push, and there should be nothing — no second key-only bolt, no code needed from inside — that could slow a student down if the hall is full of smoke at 3 a.m.

The owner also needs a way in. Leases usually give the landlord a right of access with notice, and in an emergency someone has to be able to open the room without breaking the door. That means the owner keeps an override code or a master key for every bedroom lock, held securely and logged when used. It also means thinking about the state’s lockout rule: once a student has their own lockable room under a lease, deleting their bedroom code to force them out is no different, legally, from changing the front-door lock on them.

Before you fit locks to bedrooms in a City of Poughkeepsie rental, check with the city’s building department about how the house is classified and whether bedroom locks affect its occupancy or its rental registration. If the house is a pre-1980 multi-unit rental in 12601, remember that interior doors are painted surfaces too, and the lead-safe precautions in the next group of answers apply to them as well.

A student tenant in our Arlington house is behind on rent — can we switch off their code?

No. In New York State the lockout rule is not limited to New York City. Section 768 of the Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law, strengthened by the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act of 2019, makes it unlawful to remove or disable the lock on an occupant’s entrance door, or to change it without giving them a key, except under a court’s warrant of eviction. Disabling a tenant’s keypad code is, in practice, changing the lock without supplying a key. The state Bar Association notes that the 2019 act made unlawful eviction a Class A misdemeanor statewide, and the Attorney General’s guidance to police treats it as a criminal matter.

What a smart lock can legitimately do in a rent dispute is document. The log shows when the tenant was using the house, which can matter if they claim to have moved out. Scheduled codes for the tenant’s guests or a subletter that the lease doesn’t allow can be removed, because those people are not the occupant. But the occupant’s own code stays live until a court says otherwise or the tenant hands back possession.

This is the main reason we set up rental locks with the owner as the only administrator and the tenants as ordinary users: it puts the owner in control of guests and contractors without putting a lockout button in an angry moment’s reach. For the dispute itself, talk to a landlord-tenant lawyer, not to the lock’s app.

Hosting under Dutchess town rules

Beacon only lets us host in the home we actually live in — how do we give guests the house and still lock away our own things?

That is the everyday problem of a primary-residence rental, and it has a tidy answer. Beacon’s 2020 short-term rental law, as The Highlands Current described it, allows homeowners and tenants to rent their own primary residence for up to 30 days at a time, up to 100 days a year, and the city code says no one may operate a short-term rental without first obtaining a permit. So the house is full of your clothes, papers, tools and wine. Guests get a dated code on the front door; the owner’s room, a closet, a home office or the basement gets its own smart lock or keyed lock that the guest code doesn’t open.

Choose the inner lock for the room’s purpose. For a closet or storage room, a keypad or keyed lever that opens only for the owner is enough. For a whole bedroom you keep for yourself, use a lock with a plain inside turn as well, so nobody can be shut in by accident. Never lock a door that is part of a guest’s way out of the house, and never lock a door that hides the electrical panel, the water shut-off or the boiler from the person you have left in charge of the house.

Then set up the front door so you can run it from wherever you are travelling: a connected lock or a bridge near the door, guest codes that start at check-in time and end at check-out, a separate code for the cleaner limited to turnover days, and a notification when each code is first used, so you know the guests arrived.

Rhinebeck’s short-term rental application asks for a floor plan showing every exit — should the lock plan follow it?

Yes, and it is the most useful document you will have for the job. The Town of Rhinebeck’s 2025 short-term rental law asks applicants for a floor plan of every occupiable level — basement, first floor, second floor and any attic rooms — showing bedrooms, windows and exits, and asks them to authorise the town’s Code Enforcement Officer and Fire Inspector to inspect. Once you have drawn that plan, every door marked as an exit has to open from inside, for a guest who has never seen the house, without a code, key or app.

We use the plan to decide which doors get which hardware. The main entrance gets the smart lock that guests use. Secondary exits marked on the plan — a kitchen door, a door to a deck, a basement walk-out — get locks with a simple inside thumb-turn and are kept on the same code list or left keyed, but never fitted with anything that needs a code from inside. Doors not on the exit route, like an owner’s closet, can be locked off. The result is a lock layout that matches what the inspector will be looking at on paper.

Remember that the plan, the permit and New York’s new state short-term rental registry, Chapter 99 of the Laws of 2025, are separate obligations, and that the Village of Rhinebeck takes its own permit applications at Village Hall. If your house is in the Rhinecliff hamlet districts, the town law also requires a special use permit from the Planning Board. We fit locks; we don’t file permits.

Red Hook wants a local contact within 15 air miles when we’re away — what does that person need from the lock?

Their own way in, that works even if your phone is off. Red Hook’s short-term rental chapter asks for a designated local contact when the owner is not staying within 15 air miles of the town while the property is rented. That person exists to deal with problems quickly, and most problems at a rental start at a door: a guest who can’t get the keypad to work, a code that was mistyped when it was created, a door left open in the rain, a noise complaint that needs someone on the doorstep.

So the local contact gets a permanent personal code, separate from the cleaner’s and the guests’, and a physical key kept somewhere they can reach at night. If they are comfortable with apps, they can also be given a limited role in the lock’s app so they can see whether the door is locked and issue a replacement guest code; if not, a code and a key are enough. Either way, they should know where the spare batteries are kept and how the external battery terminals or emergency key work on your particular lock.

Test it once before the first booking: the contact opens the door with their own credential while you watch the notification arrive, then locks up again.

Older houses, estates and historic villages

Our Poughkeepsie two-family was built before 1980 — does drilling the door for a smart lock raise a lead-paint problem?

It can, so we work as if it does. The Dutchess County health department says owners of rental properties with two or more units in the City of Poughkeepsie’s 12601 zip code, built before 1980, must register with the New York State rental registry and obtain a lead safety certificate, after an inspection that includes a visual check for deteriorated paint and dust-wipe sampling. Doors and frames are among the most painted, most rubbed surfaces in any old house, and drilling, chiselling a strike mortise or sanding a sticking edge all make fine dust.

The good news is that a lock change is a very small amount of disturbed paint. The EPA’s renovation, repair and painting rule treats work that disturbs six square feet or less of interior painted surface, or twenty square feet or less of exterior surface, as minor maintenance and repair rather than a renovation, so long as no prohibited work practice, window replacement or demolition is involved. We keep it that way: we choose locks that fit the existing bores where possible, cut only what the new hardware needs, never dry-sand or use a heat gun on old paint, catch dust at the bit, lay plastic under the work and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes.

If the door is in poor condition — peeling, chipped, or binding so badly it needs planing — that is a bigger job, and on a registered rental it is one the owner should plan with a lead-safe certified contractor. We will tell you at the walkthrough if we think a door has crossed that line, before any tool touches it.

Which smart locks look right on a Victorian or Federal-style front door in Rhinebeck or Poughkeepsie’s historic districts?

The ones you can’t see from the street. On a historic front door the most discreet option is usually an interior retrofit unit that mounts over a compatible single-cylinder deadbolt on the inside face and leaves the outside of the door exactly as it was, keyhole and all. Where an outside keypad is wanted, a traditional-style handleset with an integrated keypad, or a slim keypad mounted on the frame or inside a vestibule, changes the face of the door far less than a large modern touchscreen escutcheon.

Finish matters as much as form. Unlacquered or oil-rubbed bronze, aged brass and black iron read as period on a Victorian or Greek Revival door; satin nickel and glossy black glass read as twenty-first century. We bring finish samples to the walkthrough and check the lock against the hinges, knocker and mail slot already on the door.

Whether a change needs approval depends on where you are. The Village of Rhinebeck’s historic district overlay sends new construction and major exterior modifications to the Village Planning Board; the City of Poughkeepsie’s Historic District and Landmark Preservation Commission reviews exterior alterations, repairs and changes in exterior appearance within its districts. Neither body’s published summary mentions lock hardware specifically, so ask the village or city before you change anything visible, and keep the original hardware in case you or a future owner want it back.

The caretaker who has looked after our river estate for years is leaving — how do we take back every key, code and app login?

Start with an inventory, because on an estate nobody usually has one. Walk every building — the main house, gatehouse, carriage house, cottages, greenhouse, pool house, barns — and list every exterior door, what kind of lock it has, which keys open it and who holds them. Then list every electronic credential: lock codes, alarm codes, gate codes, garage-opener remotes, and the email address each smart device’s account is registered to. On properties that have been run by one trusted person for years, a surprising number of those accounts are registered to the caretaker’s own email.

Then move control, not just keys. Every app account should belong to an owner’s or estate office’s email, with the new caretaker added as a user with the access they need. Codes the departing caretaker knew are retired; mechanical cylinders on the doors they had keys for are re-keyed, ideally to a restricted keyway whose blanks can’t be cut at a hardware counter. Gate and alarm codes change the same day.

Smart locks make the next handover much easier. With each staff member on their own code, a departure becomes a single deletion and a log entry instead of a weekend of re-keying — which makes a caretaker’s departure a sensible moment to convert the most-used doors on the property.

Break-ins and the habits police warn about

The State Police told Dutchess residents to lock second-floor entry points too — does an upstairs deck or balcony door need a smart lock?

It needs to be locked, which is not quite the same thing. In January 2025 the New York State Police issued a public-safety alert about a series of targeted residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam counties, and the first piece of advice was to make sure all doors and windows are securely locked, “including second-floor entry points.” Upstairs balcony doors, doors onto a deck over the garage, and porch-roof doors are easy to forget, because nobody walks through them every day and they feel out of reach.

For most of those doors, a good mechanical deadbolt or the door’s own multipoint lock is perfectly adequate — the problem is remembering to use it. That is where a door contact tied to an alarm or a smart-home hub helps: it can tell you, at bedtime or when you set the alarm, that the upstairs deck door is still open. A smart lock makes sense on an upper door that is used regularly, such as a deck off a kitchen on a hillside lot, because auto-lock removes the remembering altogether.

One caution: sliding glass doors and some French doors can’t take a standard smart deadbolt at all. For those we look at secondary bars and pins, door contacts and, if the family wants it, a camera covering the deck.

Should we stop keeping a house key or garage remote in the car parked in our Millbrook or Amenia driveway?

Yes. The Dutchess County Sheriff’s Office has reported thefts from and of vehicles in the north-east of the county — in April 2021, for example, it described several larcenies from vehicles and three vehicle thefts in the Amenia and Millerton area, alongside an attempted break-in at a jewelry store on Route 44 in Millerton that ended when the alarm sounded. Its advice was to lock vehicles and never leave valuables or keys or fobs inside. A house key in the glovebox or a garage remote clipped to the visor turns a car break-in into a way into the house.

A keypad on the door from the garage or the side door removes the reason to keep a spare key in the car. The family uses codes; the garage remote lives in the house or on a keyring; and if a car is stolen or broken into, nothing in it opens the house. Where the garage door opener itself is app-connected, set it to close automatically after dark and send a notification when it opens.

On farms and large properties east of the Taconic, the same thinking applies to barns and equipment sheds: keys hanging inside the barn door, or a combination written on the frame, are as good as no lock. A keypad or a code-only padlock is a small change that keeps working when nobody is around.

Shops and restaurants

Our shop on Beacon’s Main Street shares a back stair with the apartment upstairs — how do we keep shop codes off the tenant’s door?

Put the two on separate locks and, ideally, separate systems. In many of the brick buildings along Beacon’s Main Street, the shop’s back door and the apartment’s entrance open onto the same rear stair or yard. If both doors run on one keypad system with one administrator, a shop employee’s code can end up opening the tenant’s door, or the tenant can end up with a code to the stockroom. Neither is acceptable, and the first is a privacy problem for the tenant and a liability for the owner.

The cleanest setup is a business keypad on the shop’s door administered by the business, and a residential lock on the apartment door administered by the landlord or the tenant. Where the shared stair has its own street door, that door can carry a reader or keypad that both the shop and the tenant use, but with separate credentials on each side so either can be removed without affecting the other. The tenant’s door carries the state’s tenant protections, so it is never part of the shop’s schedule.

If the shop is a tenant of the building too, put the arrangement in writing with the landlord before we install: who owns each lock, who holds the administrator accounts, and what happens to codes when the lease ends.

Our Hyde Park restaurant hires culinary students for the season — how should kitchen-door codes work when they leave in the fall?

Give each person their own code with an end date on the day you hire them. The Culinary Institute of America’s Hyde Park campus runs its own student restaurants, and if your kitchen takes on students or seasonal cooks for the busy months, the same turnover arrives every fall. The worst arrangement is the one most kitchens have: a single back-door code that everyone knows, that was last changed two summers ago, and that a dozen former employees could still use.

With per-person codes, the schedule does the work. A cook who works dinner service has a code that opens the kitchen door from mid-afternoon to close; a baker’s code works from before dawn; the season’s students have codes that expire on the date the season ends. The manager sees in the log who opened the delivery door at 5:40 a.m., and when someone leaves early, one deletion closes the door to them.

Keep the inside simple: a kitchen’s back door is usually an exit, so the inside opens with a lever or panic bar, never a code. If the walk-in or the liquor storage needs its own control, a separate keypad lock on that door, with a shorter list of codes, keeps the high-value stock behind a second layer.

“Give your Airbnb guests a code and you’re done”

Hosting blogs often treat keyless check-in as the finish line. In Dutchess it is the first step of a longer list. “The lock is the main safety feature of a rental”

A lock keeps the wrong people out. It does nothing about the dangers inside. In October 2024 a mother and her daughter died in a fire at an Airbnb in Salt Point; Dutchess investigators allege, according to CBS New York, that the listing falsely claimed a smoke alarm and that the property lacked a required local permit mandating smoke and carbon monoxide detectors and extinguishers on each floor. Those are allegations in a criminal case, not findings.

The lesson for hosts doesn’t depend on how that case ends. Working smoke and CO alarms, extinguishers and clear exits come before any keyless convenience.

When we fit a rental’s locks we walk the exits with the owner, and we will point out a missing or chirping alarm on the way. Our fire alarm installation team in Dutchess County can take that further.

“Landlords can just change the code when a tenant stops paying”

Property-management articles sometimes present keyless entry as a way to “enforce” leases. In New York State it is not. RPAPL 768 makes it unlawful to change or disable the lock on an occupant’s entrance door without supplying a key, outside a court-ordered eviction, and it applies in Poughkeepsie and Red Hook exactly as it does in the city.

Deleting a tenant’s code is changing the lock. A smart lock makes it faster, not lawful.

Use the lock for what it is good at in a rental — separate codes, dated access for contractors, a record of when the door was used — and leave possession disputes to the courts.

“Installing a smart lock is a 20-minute DIY job on any door”

On a modern pre-bored door with a standard backset, a confident homeowner can often fit a smart deadbolt with a screwdriver. Many Dutchess doors are not that door: thick old slabs, odd backsets, mortise locks, frames that have moved, and multiple coats of old paint.

On a pre-1978 house the paint is the part a how-to video never mentions. Drilling or chiselling old paint makes dust, and in a rental that may already hold a lead safety certificate, that dust is the owner’s problem.

We measure first, reuse bores where we can, keep the disturbance within minor-repair scale and clean up with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes. If a door needs more than that, we say so before starting.

“Smart locks are only for new homes”

Lock marketing photographs new doors in new houses. Yet about 18 percent of Dutchess homes were built in 1939 or earlier, and many of the people who most need controlled access — landlords near the colleges, hosts in village houses, estate owners — own exactly those buildings.

Interior retrofit units, traditional handlesets with keypads and thick-door kits exist precisely for older doors. The skill is in choosing the one the door can take without damage.

On a historic front we start with the least visible option and keep the original hardware, so the change can be undone.

“The app keeps everything safe, so who holds it doesn’t matter”

An app is only as safe as the account behind it. On estates and in rentals we regularly find locks registered to a former caretaker’s email, a property manager who has moved on, or a tenant who was made an administrator to be helpful.

Whoever holds the administrator account controls every code. If that person leaves, the owner may not be able to change anything without resetting the lock.

We set up every Dutchess lock with the owner, or an estate office address the owner controls, as administrator, with a second administrator for backup, and everyone else as ordinary users.

A lock change is small work, but on a painted door in an old house it should still be done carefully. This is what we go through before drilling any door that may carry pre-1978 paint.

  • How old is the house, and is it a registered rental? In the City of Poughkeepsie’s 12601 zip code, pre-1980 rentals with two or more units fall under the state rental registry and its lead safety certificate. We ask, and we plan the work so we don’t undo it.
  • Can the existing bores be reused? We choose locks whose cross bore, edge bore and backset match what is already in the door, so new cutting is limited to screw holes and small mortises.
  • Is the work minor repair? The EPA treats disturbing six square feet or less of interior painted surface, or twenty square feet or less outside, as minor maintenance and repair when no prohibited practice is used. A lock change is normally well inside that.
  • What practices are off the table? No open-flame or heat-gun paint removal, no power sanding of old paint, no dry scraping that spreads dust.
  • How is dust contained? Plastic sheeting under the door, dust caught at the bit, and cleanup with a HEPA vacuum and wet wipes before anyone walks through.
  • Is the door sound? Peeling paint, a split stile or a frame that binds is a carpentry and lead-safe painting job first; we say so and stop rather than drill into it.
Connectivity

Keeping Remote Codes Working When the Owner Is Far Away

Many Dutchess rental and estate owners manage their locks from somewhere else — a weekday apartment in the city, an office in Westchester, a winter away. Remote code changes only work if the lock can reach the internet, and the house’s internet only works if it has power.

  • Wi-Fi built into the lock: simplest where the door has a strong signal. In thick-walled old houses and estates it often means adding an access point near the entrance.
  • Bluetooth lock with a bridge: the bridge plugs in near the door and relays to the router; August, for example, recommends its Connect bridge within 10–15 feet of the lock. We test signal at the bridge position, not at the router.
  • Z-Wave or Thread with a hub: better where several locks and sensors share one system, such as an estate’s outbuildings, and kinder to batteries.
  • Codes that work offline: codes already stored in the lock keep working when the internet or power is down; only new codes and remote unlocks wait for the connection to come back.

Power is the other half. When storms crossed the region on June 27, 2024, the Times Union reported roughly 10,000 Central Hudson outages in Dutchess alone. Battery locks keep working through an outage; the router and bridge do not. For hosted homes and estates we often put the router, bridge and hub on a small battery backup so the owner can still see the door during a short outage. In houses where Wi-Fi doesn’t reach the door, a cable run to an access point is the permanent fix; our Dutchess County network installation team and structured cabling in Dutchess County handle that work.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Dutchess Rental and Estate Doors

General working practice on Dutchess jobs where keys have passed through many hands — how we sequence the visit, not an account of any single property

Before touching a door we ask for the key ring. Every key the owner has, every spare from the kitchen drawer, and the names of everyone who might still hold one: past tenants, the last property manager, a cleaner, a contractor from three summers ago. Nobody ever has the full list, and that is the point of the exercise — it tells us which cylinders need changing as well as which doors need smart locks.

Then we ask who owns the accounts. On older smart installations the lock, the bridge and the garage opener are often registered to someone who is no longer around. We get those accounts moved to the owner first, or plan a factory reset, before any new hardware goes in.

Then the paint and the doors. We look at the age of the house, ask about any lead certificate, and check thickness, backset, bores and frames. Old painted doors get the dust-control routine; doors that need a carpenter get a note, not a drill.

Last, the exits. If there is a town floor plan, we walk it door by door. If there isn’t, we draw a rough one with the owner. Every exit is opened from inside, in the dark, by hand, before we leave.
