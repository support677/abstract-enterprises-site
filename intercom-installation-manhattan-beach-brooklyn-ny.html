The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Beach Properties Need Intercom Installation

Manhattan Beach was planned to keep people at a distance. Austin Corbin created it in 1877, according to NYC Parks, as a self-contained summer resort on 500 acres of salt marsh at the eastern end of what had been Coney Island, the farming area once called Sedge Bank. His Manhattan Beach Hotel opened in 1877 and the grander Oriental Hotel in 1880; both were gone by 1916. Joseph Day, who bought out Corbin's interests in 1908, divided the land north of the old hotels into building lots, and the streets were laid out in alphabetical order from Amherst to Pembroke, named after places in England. What grew on those lots is a neighborhood of houses that sit back from the street. Brick Underground describes Cape Cod bungalows on the smaller lots and larger two-story brick houses with broad lawns on the blocks closest to Shore Boulevard; the average detached house, it reports, sells for about $2 million. When a house is set behind a lawn and a gate, a knock on the front door is not something you hear from the back kitchen or the second floor, and a visitor at the gate cannot be seen at all without walking out. That gap between the street and the people inside is what an intercom closes.

The second reason is the door itself and who is standing at it. Manhattan Beach residents have spent money on security for a long time. A history of resident patrols published by Cambridge University Press records that in 1977, families here each paid $100 a year to hire the Pinkerton Agency to watch the neighborhood. Years later the Brooklyn Paper reported police advice to Manhattan Beach neighbors after a run of thefts by people posing as utility workers: always ask for proper identification when someone shows up at the door, and even call the utility company to check whether it really sent an inspector. That advice is hard to follow through a closed wooden door, and it is exactly what a video intercom makes easy. You can see the person, ask for a badge to be held up to the camera, keep the conversation going while you call the company, and never unlock anything until you are satisfied. The same Brooklyn Paper coverage described a failed effort to persuade residents to put surveillance cameras throughout the neighborhood. A door station is different. It watches your own doorstep, not the street, and only the household decides who comes in.

The third reason is water. Sandy pushed eight-foot waves through the neighborhood, the Forward reported, and the City Limits account from December 2012 describes damage ranging from a foot of water to total destruction, with the community group's president saying that every house was flooded. StreetEasy lists buildings on Oriental Boulevard and West End Avenue in hurricane evacuation Zone 1, the first zone the city evacuates, and the National Weather Service's coastal flood table lists minor flooding on Shore Boulevard and Amherst Street at lower water levels. In nearby Gerritsen Beach, the Daily News reported, the damage after Sandy was especially severe in homes whose electrical boxes and critical wiring were in the basement. For an intercom, that changes the plan. The old habit of hanging the transformer on a cellar wall and splicing everything nearby is exactly wrong on this peninsula. Power supplies, network switches and joints belong above the level the water has reached, and cable that has to run low or underground belongs in sealed conduit.

The fourth reason is the households themselves. Some Manhattan Beach houses are large enough to hold a second household: one listing on Oriental Boulevard describes a custom manor of more than 5,000 square feet on an 8,000-square-foot lot, one of only 21 lots that size in the neighborhood, with a separate two-bedroom apartment. Many residents are retirees, Niche notes, and older people living alone or with family need a door system they can answer without hurrying to the door. Then there are the apartment buildings near the entrance on West End Avenue: a six-story co-op of 96 units completed in 1951 at 150 West End Avenue, a 121-unit co-op from 1953 at 2 West End Avenue, a 24-unit condo completed in 2013 at 35 West End Avenue, and The Terraces at 65 West End Avenue, a seven-unit condo with a virtual doorman. Those buildings need lobby panels, apartment stations and a board's approval, and the state's intercom law reaches some of them and not others.

Same-Day Repair in Manhattan Beach

Need Repair in Manhattan Beach: A Gate Station That Went Quiet After High Water, a Monitor Showing Only Snow, or a Lobby Panel Ringing the Wrong Apartment

Repair calls from Manhattan Beach follow the shape of the property and the history of the water. On detached houses, the most common fault is at the far end of the longest run: the gate station that worked for years and then began to crackle, cut out or stop calling the house. Gate cable usually runs underground or along a fence, and if it was laid without conduit, or in conduit that took on water, the copper corrodes slowly until the signal cannot get through. A second pattern is a system that was put back together after flooding without anyone testing what had been underwater. The stations may light up, but a splice in the cellar is green with corrosion, and on damp days the release works only on the second or third press. A third is a video monitor that shows a snowy or rolling picture, which on a two-wire system usually points to a bad joint or a cable that is too long or too thin for the load. In the West End Avenue co-ops, calls more often come from crossed apartments after a renovation, dead stations on one line of the building, or a lobby panel whose parts are no longer made.

If the gate or front door will not latch, deal with that first: lock it with the key and a deadbolt while the intercom is down. If a breaker feeding the intercom trips again after you reset it, leave it off; in a house that has taken water, a repeated trip can mean moisture in a box, and that should be looked at before anything is switched back on. Then call (347) 934-8335. Many single faults, such as a corroded joint, a failed power supply or a strike that has drifted out of line, are found and fixed in one visit, and we tell you on the phone what the photos let us promise and what they do not. Service calls and tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we estimate the tracing time before we start. Where water has damaged a large part of an older system, or the maker no longer supplies parts, we put the repair and a replacement side by side in writing, so the owner or the board can decide with the numbers in front of them.

Tudor and traditional houses: heavy doors and a first owner's wiring

The 1920s and 1930s houses that give many Manhattan Beach blocks their look tend to have deep entries: a recessed porch, a stone or brick arch, a solid wood door, sometimes a vestibule with a second door behind it. Listings show houses of that era still standing, such as a three-story house on Oriental Boulevard built in 1925. If an intercom was ever installed, it was probably a simple audio system, with a thin pair of wires running from a small panel by the door to a transformer in the cellar. The first decision on these houses is whether any of that wire is still worth using. We test each run for continuity and for leakage between the conductors, and we look closely at any length that sat in the cellar during 2012. Wire that passes can often carry a modern two-wire video system to an indoor monitor. Wire that spent hours in salt water usually does not pass, and we would rather replace it now than have a new station fail in the first damp autumn.

The heavy door matters as much as the wire. A solid wood door that has hung in the same frame for close to a century may have settled, so the latch no longer meets the strike plate squarely. An electric strike fitted to the old position can leave the door swinging shut without catching. We set the strike to where the latch actually lands, adjust or replace the closer so the door pulls itself fully closed, and test from fully open several times. On an arched or stone entry, we look for a place to mount the station where it is sheltered from rain, visible to a visitor and reachable at the heights the city's rule sets, and we use anchors suited to stone or brick rather than the mortar joints between them.

Mediterranean and Colonial Revival houses: the long walk from gate to door

CityNeighborhoods describes many Manhattan Beach houses as detached brick and stucco houses, Mediterranean or Colonial Revival in style, set back behind gardens. On these properties the visitor's first stop is often a gate at the sidewalk, and the front door is a walk across a planted yard away. That raises the central question for the whole system: where does the visitor ring, and what opens? Some owners want the gate to be the only call point, with a release that opens the gate and leaves the front door to be answered in person. Others want stations at both, so a guest let through the gate can be spoken to again at the door. We draw the property, mark where a stranger can reach, and agree with the owner which doors are controlled and which are not before choosing hardware.

Stucco needs care. Drilling through a stucco face without a plan can crack the finish around the hole and let water behind it, and once water is behind stucco it is slow to come out. We prefer to bring cable up from below through a sealed entry, or to mount the station on a gate pier or a solid part of the wall rather than directly through thin stucco, and every entry point is sealed with a material made for exterior masonry. The cable between gate and house goes in conduit, and the conduit route is chosen with the owner to avoid planting beds, irrigation lines and tree roots.

Cape Cod bungalows: a closer door and a lower floor

The Cape Cod houses on the smaller lots are simpler in one way: the front door is closer to the sidewalk, and often there is no gate at all, so a single station at the door may be enough. But the ground floor of a small house sits low, and if the house took water in 2012, the cellar and sometimes the first floor did too. That is the part of the plan that needs thought. We place the power supply in a dry spot on the main floor or higher, keep splices out of the cellar, and run the cable from the door station up through a wall or closet instead of down through the basement and back up. A small house also means the indoor monitor is never far away, so an owner who lives alone often does well with one monitor in the kitchen and a phone app for the rest of the house.

Large houses on big lots: distance, several doors and a second household

The larger houses near Shore Boulevard, with what Brick Underground calls broad lawns, and the few manor-sized properties on the biggest lots bring three problems together. The gate may be a long way from the house, so the signal has to travel farther than a typical door system is designed for. There are often several doors a visitor could choose: the front, the side off the driveway, a door from the garage, sometimes a back gate. And some of these houses have a second household, such as the separate two-bedroom apartment described in the Oriental Boulevard listing. We handle distance with the right cable and, where needed, a system built for longer runs or a networked system with a switch in the house. Several doors become several stations on one system, each identified on the monitor. A second household gets its own button and its own indoor station or phone, with the camera feeds and calls divided so that each household sees only what belongs to it.

On a large house the indoor side matters too. A monitor by the front door is useless to someone in a kitchen at the back or an office on the second floor. We plan monitors where people actually spend their time, add a phone app for the people who move around, and set the call so it reaches every one of them at once, then stops ringing everywhere as soon as one answers. A family that wants the gate to ring quietly in bedrooms after a certain hour can have that too, and an apartment tenant's calls never reach the main house at all.

Rebuilt and newer houses: putting the intercom on the drawings

Some Manhattan Beach houses are newer than the blocks around them, built on the sites of older houses or rebuilt after the 2012 flood. For an owner building or rebuilding, the intercom is easiest and cheapest to get right before anything is poured or closed. That means conduit to the gate laid before the walk and the driveway, a box for each outdoor station framed into the wall at the right height, a cable from every station location to one equipment spot, and that equipment spot placed above the design flood level with power nearby. We can provide a cable schedule for the architect or general contractor, pull the cable ourselves while the framing is open, or both. Each cable is labeled at both ends and left with slack in a capped box. The stations themselves go on once the house is closed in and the finishes are done, so nothing gets painted over or knocked off by trades.

The common mistake on a rebuild is treating the intercom as a finish item, like a light fixture, and ordering a wireless doorbell at the end. On a house with a gate and a long walk, a wireless doorbell often cannot reach, cannot open the gate, and needs Wi-Fi at a spot where the signal is weak. A single conduit laid at the right moment costs very little compared with cutting a finished walk or a new stucco face later.

West End Avenue co-ops, condos and small buildings: boards, lobbies and shops

The apartment buildings near the entrance to Manhattan Beach are a separate kind of job. The co-op at 150 West End Avenue was completed in 1951, six stories and 96 units, and a rental listing names an intercom among its features. The co-op at 2 West End Avenue has 121 units and dates from 1953; listings describe it as a doorman elevator building and disagree on whether it has six or seven stories. Buildings of that age were built of masonry, with block or brick walls between corridors and equipment rooms, and their intercom risers may be original or may have been replaced more than once. Work there runs through the board and the managing agent, the lobby panel has to find one name among about a hundred quickly, and every apartment station has to be reached without disrupting residents for days. Newer condos are different again: 35 West End Avenue is a six-story, 24-unit building completed in 2013, and The Terraces at 65 West End Avenue is a seven-unit condo whose listing mentions a lobby virtual doorman and video security in the common and parking areas. Those buildings usually have networked systems already, and the questions are about extending, repairing or replacing what the developer installed.

Near the same stretch, StreetEasy lists small two-story buildings with three units each, such as 89 West End Avenue from 1927 and 181 West End Avenue from 1930. Buildings of that size on a commercial stretch can combine a shop with apartments, and where they do, the apartments need their own station and release, powered and wired separately from the business. We explain the legal side of these buildings in the rules section below, and the commercial side in the business section.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Manhattan Beach Property From the Sidewalk to the Water Line

Start where a stranger starts. The technician begins on the sidewalk, not at the front door, and walks every route a visitor could take: through the gate, up the driveway, along the side of the house. Every door that route reaches is a door the plan has to answer for, either with a station or with a decision to leave it locked and unlisted.

Find the water mark. In the cellar or utility room the technician looks for the line the water left in 2012, or asks the owner where it reached. Everything electrical that the new system needs, from the power supply to the last splice, is planned to sit above that line, with margin.

Pull the old station off its wall. Behind an old intercom or bell plate, the wire shows its history. Green-crusted copper, a splice wrapped in tape, or wire that crumbles when bent means the run gets tested before anyone decides to keep it. Clean jacketed cable usually means the house was rewired after the flood.

Pace the distance to the gate. The length of the gate run decides what kind of system can reach it. The technician measures it and checks the route for walks, beds and roots before promising anything.

Ask who answers. Who is home most of the day, on which floor, how well they hear, and whether a second household lives in the house: those answers decide how many stations, how loud, how simple, and who gets which calls.

Water, Salt and Wire

Zone 1 Wiring: Building a Manhattan Beach Intercom That Survives the Next High Water

Most of what makes an intercom last on this peninsula happens out of sight: where the power lives, where the joints are, how cable leaves the house and crosses the yard, and what it is protected by. A brand-new station on the gate post is only as reliable as the run behind it. This section explains how we handle each part, so you can see what the survey checks and why.

Power above the line. The old way was to mount a transformer on a cellar joist beside the electrical panel. In a house where the cellar has flooded, that puts the heart of the system where water arrives first. We mount the power supply, and any network switch or distribution module, in a dry enclosure on a main floor or higher, on a circuit that is labeled at the panel. If the panel itself is still in the cellar, we keep the intercom's circuit easy to identify, and if a new circuit or outlet is needed upstairs, a licensed electrician does that part. The Daily News account of Gerritsen Beach after Sandy, where homes with critical wiring in the basement suffered the worst damage, is the plainest argument for this rule.

No joints in the cellar. Every splice is a place for water to get in. Where a run has to pass through a low space, we run it continuously, without joints, and make any necessary connections in labeled junction boxes above the flood level. When we take over a system that was repaired after 2012, we cut out old low splices and remake them higher up, even if the wire itself tests well.

Sealed conduit outdoors and underground. Gate cable buried without protection fails slowly; cable in open-ended conduit fills with water and fails faster. We use conduit rated for burial, seal the ends where it enters the house and the gate post, and slope it so water drains away from the house rather than toward it. Where cable runs along an exterior wall, it goes in sealed conduit or raceway, never stapled bare to brick or stucco. A National Weather Service table lists minor flooding on Shore Boulevard and Amherst Street at lower water levels than a hurricane, so even an ordinary nor'easter can put water around a gate post on some blocks.

Salt air at the station. A restoration company that works in Brooklyn describes long-term wood rot and salt corrosion after Sandy and places parts of this shoreline in the city's highest-risk flood zones. That is a contractor's description rather than an independent study, but it matches what anyone sees on metal fixtures near the water. We choose outdoor stations with sealed housings and corrosion-resistant faceplates, mount them where an overhang or a recess gives some shelter, and use stainless fasteners. A station on the beach side of Oriental Boulevard faces harder weather than one on the bay side, and the choice reflects that.

Testing what survived. Wire that sat in salt water can look fine on the outside and leak between its conductors inside. Before keeping any run in a house that flooded, we test it with an instrument rather than trusting a visual check. If it passes, it stays. If it fails or is marginal, it is replaced, and the written quote says which runs are being kept and which are not.

Planning for the next evacuation. Zone 1 is the first zone the city evacuates. A system designed with that in mind lets the owner see and answer the gate from a phone while away, keeps its power supply where water is least likely to reach, and uses door hardware that keeps the door locked if power fails. When the family comes home, a quick check of each button and release confirms that everything still works.

The Rules at the Door

Houses, Co-ops and Condos: What New York Rules Mean for a Manhattan Beach Intercom

Manhattan Beach has two very different kinds of building under one ZIP code, and the rules for an intercom differ sharply between them. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources we reviewed say, so you can ask your attorney, board or managing agent the right questions.

Detached and semi-detached houses. The state's intercom requirement is written for buildings with many apartments. A one-family house has no legal obligation to have an intercom, and the reason Manhattan Beach families install them is practical: the door is far from the people inside, and they want to see a visitor before opening it. A house with a separate rented apartment is still a small building, well below the eight-apartment line in the state law, but once a tenant relies on a door system, keeping it working is part of keeping the apartment in good order.

Buildings with eight or more apartments. The New York Attorney General's tenants' rights guide explains that a building with eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door, and tenants must be able to buzz open the entrance door. For buildings built before 1968, the guide says, an intercom is required if a majority of the apartments request it. The West End Avenue co-ops completed in 1951 and 1953 fall into that older group. The 2013 condo at 35 West End Avenue, with 24 units, falls into the newer one. The seven-unit condo at 65 West End Avenue is below the threshold.

Keeping an existing intercom working. Habitat Magazine, which covers co-op and condo boards, reported in 2024 that the city's housing maintenance code requires a functioning intercom, and that boards must keep it repaired. Parker Custom Security, a security company, describes a broken intercom as a Class B hazardous violation that HPD enforces on tenant complaint. In practice, a co-op that already has an intercom should treat repairs as an obligation, not an upgrade to schedule when the budget allows. Brick Underground reported in 2014 that one renter waited three months for an inspector to respond to a 311 complaint about a broken intercom, which is one more reason for a board to fix problems before residents have to report them.

Heights at the door. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. We follow that in lobbies and apply the same sense at house doors and gates, measured from where the visitor stands. Where the public uses an entrance, such as an office or a congregation's door, accessibility reach rules usually put buttons lower than that maximum.

Permits. A press release on building security upgrades in New York City says most low-voltage installations, including intercoms, do not require a building permit, while electrical work above low-voltage thresholds does. We treat that as a reasonable summary rather than an official ruling. If a project needs a new electrical circuit, a licensed electrician handles it and any filing it requires.

Installer licensing. New York's Article 6-D licensing, as summarized by the NSCA's guide to state licensing, covers people who install electrical systems that detect or give notice of intrusion, break-in, theft or movement. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, and a board or owner should expect any installer to show a license number.

Getting out. Any door fitted with an electric strike must still open freely from inside, without a key, code or button. Every system we install controls the way in only.

Landmarks. We found no historic district covering Manhattan Beach. The Manhattan Beach Jewish Center at 60 West End Avenue is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, which is a different program from city landmark designation. Before mounting anything on a building you believe might be designated, check the address on the Landmarks Preservation Commission's map.

Compliance notes for Manhattan Beach institutions and apartment buildings

  • Free exit at every controlled door. A door with an electric strike opens from inside without a key, code or button. The intercom controls entry, never exit.
  • Reachable controls. 1 RCNY 42-01 caps push buttons at six feet and places the speaking device between four and five feet; doors the public uses also follow accessibility reach rules, which usually means lower buttons.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Manhattan Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Manhattan Beach owners, families and boards bring to us: houses rebuilt above the flood line, strangers at the door claiming to be from the utility, homes with a second household or an aging parent, co-ops and condos with doormen real and virtual, and gates a long way from the house. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Rebuilt houses and the flood line

Our rebuilt house sits above the flood elevation, with the entry a full story up. Which parts of the intercom can safely go below that line?

Only the parts built to be outside and wet. The outdoor stations at the gate and at street level are weather-sealed and designed for rain, so a gate station below the flood elevation is normal; if water ever reaches it, it may need replacing, but it is a small, inexpensive part of the system. The cable to it should run in sealed, burial-rated conduit with no joints along the low stretch. Everything else belongs above the line: the power supply, any network switch or distribution module, every splice and junction box, and the indoor monitors. If the ground floor under your raised house is a garage or storage area, treat it as the cellar was treated before: nothing electrical for the intercom lives there except the conduit passing through it. We mark the design flood elevation on the wiring record we leave with you, so anyone who works on the system later knows where the line is.

Checking who is at the door

A man at the gate says he is from the utility company and needs to come in. How does a video intercom help us check him before we open anything?

It lets you follow the advice police gave Manhattan Beach neighbors years ago, without ever opening the door: ask for identification and call the utility to confirm an inspector was really sent. With a video station you see his face and clothing on the monitor, ask him through the speaker to hold his badge up to the camera, and read it on the screen. While he waits at the gate, you call the company's published number, not a number he gives you, and ask whether anyone was sent to your address. If the answer is no, you tell him through the intercom that you are not letting him in, and call 911 if he will not leave. At no point is the gate or door released. Most stations can also keep a still image or short clip of each caller if the owner enables it, which is useful if the same person turns up at other houses.

When we answer the gate from our phones while we are away, can the visitor tell that nobody is home?

Not from the call itself. When the gate station forwards to an app or a phone, the visitor hears your voice from the station's speaker exactly as if you were answering from the kitchen; nothing on the station says where you are. What can give it away is what you say and how long you take. Answering promptly and saying something like "leave it at the gate, thanks" reveals nothing, while "we're away until Sunday" reveals everything. Delays matter too: if the call goes through several phones before anyone answers, a visitor waits longer than usual. We set the call to ring all chosen phones at once and stop ringing the others as soon as one person picks up. Some systems also let you send a short recorded reply instead of talking, which we cover in the FAQ.

One house, more than one household

Our house includes a separate two-bedroom apartment with a tenant. Can the tenant have their own button without being able to watch our camera or answer our calls?

Yes, and that separation should be built in from the start rather than added as a setting later. Each household gets its own button on the outdoor station, its own indoor station or app account, and its own release. The system is programmed so that a call to the main house reaches only the main house's monitors and phones, and a call to the apartment reaches only the apartment's. Camera viewing is divided the same way: the tenant's station can show the visitor who rang the tenant, but it cannot pull up the gate or front-door camera at other times. The owner keeps the administrator login and can see everything; the tenant's account cannot change the programming. If the apartment has its own entrance, it gets its own outdoor station, and its release opens only its own door. Before we leave, both households press their buttons while the other household watches its monitor to confirm nothing crosses over.

My father has started getting up at night and wandering. Can the door system let us know if the front door opens while everyone is asleep?

Many systems can. A small door position switch in the front door frame tells the intercom when the door opens. With a schedule set, an opening during the night can send an alert to the phones of family members, sound a chime on the monitor in a parent's or caregiver's bedroom, or both, while daytime openings stay silent. A video station at the door can also save a short clip when the door opens, so you can see which way he went. This is not a medical alarm or a monitoring service, and it does not replace a plan with his doctor, but it does turn an opening no one would notice into a message someone gets within seconds. News coverage this year of a Silver Alert for a 98-year-old last seen near Cass Place and Shore Boulevard is a reminder that this happens here. If the house has more than one outside door, each one needs its own switch.

The co-ops and condos on West End Avenue

Our co-op has a doorman at the desk. Does that let the building go without a working intercom?

Not if the building already has one, and having a doorman is not an exception written into the rules we reviewed. The Attorney General's guide says buildings of eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door with a working door release, and that for buildings built before 1968, like the 1951 and 1953 co-ops on West End Avenue, an intercom is required when a majority of the apartments ask for one. Habitat Magazine reported that the housing maintenance code requires a functioning intercom and that boards must keep it repaired. A doorman covers hours when someone is at the desk, but residents still need to be reached and to buzz in guests at other times, and many buildings have desk coverage only part of the day. If your building has an intercom, the safe course is to keep it working; your board's attorney can confirm the details for your building.

Our small condo's listing mentions a "virtual doorman." What does that service do that a plain video intercom does not?

A virtual doorman puts a remote person between the visitor and the residents. When someone rings, a staff member at a remote center sees and speaks to them through the lobby camera, checks them against the building's instructions, and then calls the resident or lets in an approved delivery or service worker. A plain video intercom sends the visitor straight to the resident, who decides. The service adds judgment when residents are out and can manage deliveries and contractors, but it is a monthly service run by an outside company, and it depends on that company's connection and staff. The hardware underneath is still an intercom: a lobby station with a camera, door hardware, and a network. When we work on a building like The Terraces at 65 West End Avenue, the useful questions are what happens if the service is unavailable, whether residents can still be called directly, and who owns the lobby equipment if the building changes providers.

Can one shareholder in a West End Avenue co-op replace just the station inside their own apartment?

Usually, yes, but only with the building's approval and with a station that works on the building's system. The apartment station is wired to the building's riser and talks to the lobby panel in that system's own language, so it has to be a compatible model from the same family, or the building has to approve an adapter that the maker supports. A shareholder cannot simply buy any video monitor and expect it to show the lobby camera. The first step is to ask the managing agent which system the building has and whether replacement stations are still available; the second is written approval, since the work touches building wiring. We can identify the system from a photo of the station and the lobby panel, tell the shareholder and the agent what is compatible, and install it so nothing else in the building is disturbed. If the building's system is too old for any compatible station, that is useful information for the board.

Big lots and long runs

On an 8,000-square-foot lot the gate is a long way from the house. How far can an intercom signal travel, and what carries it?

Farther than most people expect, as long as the system matches the distance. A two-wire video system carries video, audio and the release over a single pair, and each maker publishes a maximum distance for a given cable size; a well-chosen cable reaches across even the largest Manhattan Beach lots. A networked system uses network cable, which has its own length limit per run, so on very long distances a small switch can be placed partway, in a weatherproof enclosure at a garage or outbuilding, to extend it. Wireless links between gate and house exist, but they depend on a clear line between two antennas, and trees, metal gates and brick walls all weaken the signal, so we treat wireless as a last resort for a gate. On the survey we measure the actual route, not the straight line, choose the system and cable for that length with margin, and test the signal before anything is mounted.

FAQ

Manhattan Beach Intercom Installation FAQ

Does whoever installs our intercom need a New York State license?
+

Yes. New York's Article 6-D licensing covers people who install electrical systems that detect or give notice of intrusion, break-in, theft or movement, and door entry systems fall within that work. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431. Ask any installer for their license number before work starts, and a co-op or condo board should also ask for proof of insurance.

For a detached house, what is the practical difference between a two-wire system and a networked one?
+

A two-wire system sends video, audio and the release over one pair, so it can reuse a sound existing pair and is simple to power from one supply. A networked system uses network cable to each station, which makes it easier to add phone answering, more doors, a second household and access control later. Houses with one or two doors and good wire often suit two-wire; large houses with several entries usually suit networked.

We are gutting the house after water damage. Can our existing intercom be taken down, stored and put back afterward?
+

Often, yes, for the stations and monitors themselves, as long as they were not underwater and the model is still supported. We remove and label each piece, note where it came from, and store it safely or leave it with you. The wire is a different matter: anything that sat in water gets tested, and the rebuild is the best time to replace failed runs and move power above the flood line.

Can the gate station play a short recorded reply when we cannot talk, such as asking a courier to leave a package?
+

Many current systems can. From the app you choose a short pre-recorded message, such as asking a driver to leave the package at the gate, and the station plays it to the visitor. Some models let you record your own messages. It is useful in a meeting, at the beach or late at night, and it avoids saying anything that reveals whether the house is empty.

My mother moves slowly. Can she answer the gate from a cordless handset she keeps beside her chair?
+

Yes. Several systems offer a portable handset or a tablet-style station that works anywhere in the house, so she can see the visitor, talk and release the gate without getting up. We set the ring loud enough for her to hear, keep the buttons to the essentials, and show her and whoever helps her how it works before we leave.

I live alone in a single-family house. Is an intercom worth it when there is nobody else in the house to call?
+

For many people living alone it is worth more, not less. The point is not calling between rooms; it is seeing and speaking to whoever is at the gate or door before you open it, and answering from your phone when you are out. On a house set back behind a lawn, it saves walking out to a stranger, which matters most when nobody else is home.

Could a call from our gate be forwarded to a neighborhood security patrol if nobody answers?
+

Technically, yes: many systems can forward an unanswered call to another phone number after a set time. Whether a patrol will take those calls is up to the patrol and the people who pay for it. Manhattan Beach residents have funded private patrols in the past, but we have no current information on any service, so arrange it with them first and we will program the forwarding.

A family member uses a wheelchair. Can the intercom release trigger a powered door opener so the door swings open by itself?
+

Yes, if the door has an automatic operator. The operator itself is installed by a door contractor, and we wire the intercom so that a press of the release unlocks the strike and signals the operator to open the door, then lets it close and latch. We coordinate the timing with the door contractor so the lock releases before the operator pushes.

How do we stop the app from sending us a notification every time a car or a jogger passes the gate?
+

Most stations with motion detection let you turn motion alerts off entirely, shrink the detection area so it covers only the gate itself, or lower the sensitivity. Many owners keep alerts only for button presses, which removes nearly all the noise. We set this up during programming and adjust it after a week if the alerts are still too frequent.

Can the gate station take a family code, so the kids can come back from the beach without carrying a key?
+

Many stations include or accept a keypad, so family members enter a code to open the gate while visitors still ring. Each person can have their own code, and a code can be removed at any time without changing the others. The keypad uses the same strike or gate lock and the same cable as the intercom, so it adds little if it is planned from the start.
