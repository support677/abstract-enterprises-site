The Neighborhood

Why Mott Haven Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

Mott Haven is, first of all, a residential neighbourhood. It fills the southwestern corner of the Bronx, roughly from East 149th Street down to the Bruckner Expressway and west to the Harlem River, with East 138th Street as its main street and the 6 train running underneath it. About 54,000 people lived there at the 2020 count, on a little over a square mile. Wikipedia's summary of the neighbourhood's land use is blunt and useful: it is dominated by tenement-style apartment buildings and large public-housing campuses, with three small historic districts of brownstone row houses and a recent wave of two- and three-unit houses and mid-rise apartment buildings.

Each of those building types asks a different question of a fiber installer. A landmarked row house asks how the provider's line gets in without new conduit on a protected front. A five-storey walk-up asks where a riser can climb when the only vertical space is a single public stair and a stack of kitchens. A new affordable building asks whether its cabling meets the City's broadband guidelines, and whether the clinic or pharmacy on its ground floor can be kept apart from the residents' network. A school leasing space above a store, or a church with a parish hall across a churchyard, asks how a few buildings with small budgets can share a link that is documented well enough to survive the next volunteer or IT vendor.

The need behind all of this is not abstract. The City Council's internet-access data puts Melrose, Mott Haven and Port Morris — Bronx Community District 1 — at 44 percent of households without a broadband subscription, the highest share of any district in the city. A 2021 reporting project by CUNY's NYCity News Service, The Bronx is Buffering, noted that the borough already has a large system of telecom conduit running to it from Manhattan, and that the expensive part is the last stretch into homes and businesses. That last stretch, inside the property line, is where owners and managing agents can act: a tested, labelled path from the cellar to every floor makes it cheaper for any provider, free programme or community network to serve the building.

We work on the private side of that line — owner-held fiber from where service lands to where it is used. When the same visit needs copper drops, door entry or cameras, our Mott Haven structured cabling and Mott Haven intercom teams walk the building with us, so one set of holes serves every system.

Mott Haven facts that shape a fiber plan

  • Three landmark districts in one square mile. The Mott Haven Historic District on Alexander Avenue (designated 1969), and the Mott Haven East and Bertine Block districts on East 139th–140th and East 136th streets (both designated April 5, 1994). Front and rear façade work in all three goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Seventeen NYCHA developments are listed for the neighbourhood, from the twenty- and twenty-two-storey towers of Mott Haven Houses to Betances developments made of rehabilitated five- and six-storey tenements — the same walk-up form that lines the private side streets.
  • 44 percent of households without a broadband subscription in Community District 1, according to the City Council — the highest district share in New York City.
  • One of the busiest emergency departments in the country sits at the northern edge: NYC Health + Hospitals/Lincoln at 234 East 149th Street, dedicated in 1976, with more than 144,000 emergency visits a year, and with clinics, pharmacies and social-service offices spread through the blocks around it.
  • Dozens of schools — district, charter and parochial — appear in the neighbourhood's school list, many of them in leased or shared buildings rather than purpose-built campuses.
  • Twenty-five bodegas for every supermarket in Mott Haven and Melrose, according to the City Health Department's 2018 community profile: small storefronts, many with apartments upstairs, sharing one cellar and one provider entry.

What We Install

Fiber Optic Installation Services in Mott Haven

Most Mott Haven jobs are small in footage and large in care. The runs are measured in tens of feet, not thousands, but they pass through homes, landmark fabric and buildings that cannot close for a day. These are the services we are asked for most often between the Harlem River and St. Ann's Avenue.

Row-House Service Entries

A concealed path from the street or the provider's box into the cellar of a brownstone or rowhouse, planned so any exterior part meets landmark rules and the inside run stays out of sight.

Floor-by-Floor House Links

Single-mode drops from the cellar to the parlour floor, the upper floors and the top-floor rental, each ending in a small box so every floor or apartment can take its own service.

Walk-Up and Tenement Risers

A strand count from the cellar to a small enclosure on each landing of a five- or six-storey walk-up, routed through a chase, a dumbwaiter shaft or a new sleeved path instead of the exit stair.

Affordable-Housing Backbones

Riser and horizontal fiber for HPD-assisted and other affordable buildings, laid out so the owner keeps the in-building network and any provider can connect to it later.

Clinic and Pharmacy Suites

School and Childcare Links

Fiber between floors or annexes for charter schools, childcare centres and after-school programmes in leased space, installed in the hours the owner and the school allow.

Church, Hall and Rectory Links

Links between a sanctuary, parish hall, office and rectory on one lot, routed through the buildings or overhead so nothing is dug where there may be burials.

Storefront and Upstairs Splits

Fiber from a shared cellar to a corner store and to the apartments above, with each party's strands kept and labelled separately in the same enclosure.

Tracing, Testing and Repair

Building Specialists

Fiber Optic Installation for Mott Haven's Architecture

Nineteenth-Century Row Houses

Brownstone and brick houses of three to five storeys on Alexander Avenue, East 139th and 140th streets and the Bertine Block, with stoops, iron fences, plaster interiors and a cellar under the parlour floor. Every visible exterior change is a landmark question; inside, routes follow closets and old service shafts.

Walk-Up Tenement Buildings

Five- and six-storey brick walk-ups with one public stair, narrow halls and apartments stacked kitchen over kitchen. There is rarely a riser closet. The cellar is the hub, and a sleeved path up a chase or a corner of the stairwell wall becomes the backbone.

Rehabilitated Tenement Clusters

Groups of renovated walk-ups held by one owner or non-profit across several neighbouring addresses. One cellar room can feed the group where the buildings genuinely connect below grade; otherwise each address needs its own entry and its own small enclosure.

Postwar Towers on Campuses

Towers of up to twenty-two storeys set in open grounds. Their own in-building service is arranged by their owner; our work around them is in community-facility spaces, neighbouring private buildings and the institutions that serve their residents.

New Affordable and Mixed-Use Buildings

Recent concrete mid-rises along East 138th Street, Third Avenue and the side streets, often with senior or supportive housing above and a clinic, pharmacy or shop below. The telecom room and risers are drawn in from the start, and the question is how well.

Civic and Religious Buildings

Churches, a Carnegie library, a police station house and older school buildings, from St. Ann's Church of 1841 to the library of 1905, built of stone and brick with high ceilings and ornate rooms. Cable stays in cellars, attics, sacristies and service rooms, never across the decorated spaces.

The craft rules are the same everywhere: the cable is pulled by its strength members, never past its bend radius, with a service loop at each end and protection where it leaves a closet. What changes in Mott Haven is who is affected while we work. Code and approval points that come up on Mott Haven jobs

  • Landmarks. In the three historic districts, most exterior changes to front and rear façades need Landmarks Preservation Commission review. Interior work needs an LPC permit only if it requires a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or touches a designated interior landmark.
  • Lead-safe work. In housing built before 1978, HPD's safe-work-practice rules apply once work disturbs two square feet or more of paint in a room, with independent dust-clearance testing afterwards. Fiber cores are usually far smaller than that, and we say in writing when a job might not be.
  • City broadband guidelines. HPD-assisted new construction and preservation projects must deliver at least 100 Mbps up and down per apartment and keep the in-building network in the owner's hands.
  • Exit routes. Public stairs and fire escapes are means of escape. Our practice is never to fasten cable to a fire escape or leave anything in a stair that narrows it or can come loose.
  • Sealed openings. Any sleeve we cut through a floor or a fire-rated wall is closed with a listed firestop material, and a photo of it, taken before anything covers it, is added to the owner's records.
Historic Districts

Landmark Row Houses: Getting Fiber In Without a Mark on the Front

The Mott Haven Historic District runs along Alexander Avenue between East 138th and East 141st streets. Its four- and five-storey row houses, the stretch once called Doctors' Row and the Irish Fifth Avenue, were built in the second half of the nineteenth century, and the Historic Districts Council notes that one of the Bronx's earliest row-house series went up here between 1863 and 1865. Two smaller districts were added on April 5, 1994: Mott Haven East, on East 139th and 140th streets between Brook and Willis avenues, where houses of 1883 combine Dutch and Flemish detail with neo-Grec fronts; and the Bertine Block on East 136th Street, where developer Edward D. Bertine built ten Queen Anne houses between 1891 and 1895 beside three earlier brick houses from 1877 and 1878.

For fiber, the rule that matters is simple to state. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says most exterior changes to the front and rear façades of buildings in a historic district require its review. A new conduit up a brownstone front, a box beside the door or a fresh hole through the areaway wall is exterior work. The Commission's permit guidebook, in its chapter on utility equipment, sets out how conduit tied to an installation should be handled: concealed by running through plain masonry behind the equipment, with staff able to approve roughly ten inches of visible conduit where that is impossible; surface runs painted to match and fixed into mortar joints rather than brick faces; and nothing attached to cast iron or decorative surfaces.

In practice that steers a good plan away from the front entirely. Many houses already have an underground service entry into the cellar, used by the phone or cable company decades ago; if it can take another cable, the façade is never touched. If not, the rear wall below the yard line, an existing opening for a vent or meter, or a sleeve under the areaway paving is usually a better path than anything on the street side. Where an exterior change is unavoidable, the permit application describes exactly where, how big and what colour, and staff-level permits exist for work that meets the Commission's rules. Work that falls outside them goes to a Certificate of Appropriateness and a public hearing — something most owners prefer to avoid for a cable.

Inside, the landmark rules usually step back. The Commission says an interior permit is only needed when the work needs a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself has been designated. That still leaves the house's own fabric to respect: original plaster, cornices, pocket doors and stair rails that owners have spent years restoring. Fiber is thin and flexible enough to follow closets stacked floor over floor, old service shafts, the back of built-in cabinetry or a corner behind a door, and each floor can end in a small, paintable box rather than a rack. We photograph the route before any opening and agree it with the owner floor by floor.

Walk-Up Buildings

Five Storeys and One Stair: Finding a Riser in a Mott Haven Walk-Up

Most Mott Haven residents live in tenement-style apartment buildings: brick walk-ups of five or six storeys with a single public stair, a cellar under the whole footprint and apartments stacked so that kitchens and bathrooms line up floor over floor. They were built long before anyone thought about telecom rooms, and many have been renovated more than once, sometimes by a non-profit, sometimes as part of a public programme. The NYCHA list for the neighbourhood includes several Betances developments that are exactly this kind of building, rehabilitated five- and six-storey tenements, which shows how much of the housing stock shares the same shape.

That shape decides the backbone. The cellar is the natural hub, because the provider's lines already land there and there is usually room for a small wall-mounted enclosure near the service entrance. The hard question is the vertical path. A walk-up rarely has a closet on each landing, and the public stair is the building's escape route, so it cannot be cluttered with cable, boxes or anything that might come loose. The candidates, in rough order of preference, are an abandoned dumbwaiter or other old shaft, a plumbing or mechanical chase that has spare space, a corner of the stair hall where a slim painted raceway can run without narrowing the stair, and finally a new sleeved path cored floor by floor through a closet in each apartment of one line.

Each option has trade-offs we explain on the walk. A chase is hidden but means coordinating with the plumber's next visit; a stair-hall raceway is quick and always accessible but visible; a cored path through apartments is the neatest result but needs every tenant in that line to agree to a short visit, and in a building older than 1978 it brings the City's lead-safe rules into play if paint is disturbed beyond small amounts. The owner chooses with the trade-offs in front of them, and the proposal prices the chosen route line by line.

On each landing, a small enclosure takes the strands for that floor. From there, either fiber continues to each apartment or the provider's own equipment does, depending on how the building wants service delivered. The value of the owner's backbone is that it is neutral: any provider, a free programme or a building-wide Wi-Fi system can use it without drilling again, and the next super inherits a labelled diagram instead of a mystery.

Affordable Housing

The City's Broadband Rules for New and Preserved Affordable Buildings

A lot of Mott Haven's newer housing is affordable housing, financed or assisted through the City's Department of Housing Preservation and Development. HPD's broadband design guidelines, last updated in March 2026, apply to HPD-assisted multifamily new construction, supportive housing, senior housing and preservation projects in its pipeline. They define "fast" as at least 100 Mbps upload and download per apartment, "reliable" as 99.9 percent uptime, and require that the service come at no additional cost to tenants, with each household getting secured access through its own profile.

Two of the requirements are about cable rather than service, and they are the ones an installer is responsible for. The guidelines say the building owner should keep ownership of the network infrastructure inside the building, with maintenance written into the contract with the provider, and that every vertical and horizontal cabling pathway should be easy to reach and leave room for more cable in the future. For new construction and preservation projects alike, the standard supply option is fiber brought from the nearest telecom vault in the street to the basement, a switch there, and new fiber or cable up vertical risers to each floor. HPD notes it may approve other plans for properties with fewer than 50 units, or for groups of buildings close together.

Since 2025 there has also been a Bronx-specific route. Neighborhood Internet, operated by the New York Public Library and funded by HPD, can be chosen by developers and sponsors of HPD-assisted projects in the Bronx west of White Plains Road and in Manhattan north of 96th Street as their installer and provider, with installation and service underwritten by HPD. The guidelines also allow a sponsor to give other providers access to install their own equipment and sell paid service alongside the free one. The Bronx Times reported in May 2026 that the programme was being expanded with $2 million, covering microtrenching, rooftop equipment and in-unit connectivity across about 50 buildings.

What that means for an owner is that the City's programme and the owner's own cabling are not alternatives. A building still needs clean, documented pathways, an entry that can take more than one provider, and the separate links a building runs for itself: cameras, door entry, the super's office, a community room, and the commercial tenant downstairs. The Tres Puentes campus on East 138th Street is a good picture of the building type: according to its developer's partners, two new buildings added 175 affordable senior apartments to the existing Borinquen Court building, with a 4,500-square-foot health clinic run by Urban Health Plan, a 1,300-square-foot pharmacy, a social-services suite and shops along Third Avenue. Every one of those occupants needs a different network from the residents. We design for that from the cellar up, and our structured cabling crew finishes the copper side in the same visit.

Health & Social Services

Clinics, Pharmacies and Case-Management Offices Below the Apartments

Health care is one of the neighbourhood's biggest employers and its most visible institution. Lincoln Hospital, municipally owned by NYC Health + Hospitals, traces its history to 1839 and moved into its current building, dedicated in 1976, at 234 East 149th Street. It is an adult Level I trauma centre and handles more than 144,000 emergency visits a year. Around it, and through the blocks to the south, are smaller places that serve the same residents: community health centres such as the one in the Tres Puentes buildings, private practices in converted storefronts, pharmacies, and the social-service offices of supportive-housing operators.

Those tenants share a pattern. They sit on the ground floor or the first floor of a residential building, they handle sensitive records, and they depend on a connection that does not slow down when two hundred households upstairs start streaming in the evening. The right design gives them their own strands from the building's entry room, in their own locked enclosure or a separately keyed compartment, and a route that does not pass through any apartment. If the clinic later needs a second provider for redundancy, the spare strands and the extra space in the entry sleeve are already there.

Medical tenants also care about timing. A clinic cannot lose its network in the middle of a session, so cutovers are planned for the hours they choose, with the old connection left running until the new one has tested clean. Before any work starts we ask what else rides on the network — card readers, phone systems, medication refrigerators with alarms that report over the internet — so nothing is surprised when a cable is moved. And because these suites change hands more often than the buildings above them, the closeout file is written for the next tenant as much as for this one.

Institutions

Schools, Churches and a Carnegie Library: Old Institutions on Small Budgets

The neighbourhood's list of schools is long: district elementary and middle schools such as P.S. 30, P.S. 40, P.S. 49 and P.S. 154, charter schools including Mott Haven Academy and South Bronx Classical II, parochial schools, and Hostos Community College on the western edge. Where a school works out of leased floors, a converted building or space shared with another school, it does not control the building's risers and cannot drill on its own say-so. A clean fiber plan for a school like that starts with the landlord's or the building owner's approval, works in summer or after hours, and leaves the school's network separate from everyone else's.

The churches are older still. St. Ann's Episcopal Church on St. Ann's Avenue, built in 1841 and described as the Bronx's oldest church, is the resting place of members of the Morris family, including Gouverneur Morris and Lewis Morris. St. Jerome's Roman Catholic Church anchors the historic district on Alexander Avenue. Congregations like these often have a sanctuary, a parish hall, an office and a rectory on one lot, a volunteer looking after the network and a growing need to stream services. Fiber between those buildings is a sensible investment, but routes across old church grounds are never dug on assumption: where burials are known or possible, the link goes through connected cellars, along walls or overhead.

The Mott Haven branch of the New York Public Library at 321 East 140th Street, a Carnegie library opened in 1905 and a designated City landmark, is a reminder of what these buildings share: thick masonry, high plaster ceilings, ornate public rooms and back-of-house spaces where cable can run unseen. We do not claim work at the library or at any particular church or school. We describe them because they show the building type, and because the same methods — cellar and attic routes, small enclosures in service rooms, careful sealing and photographing of every opening — apply to every civic building of that age in the neighbourhood.

Public Housing

Next to the Campuses: What a Private Fiber Installer Can and Can't Do

Mott Haven has one of the highest concentrations of public housing in the Bronx. The neighbourhood's NYCHA list runs from Mott Haven Houses, eight buildings of twenty and twenty-two storeys, and Mitchel Houses, ten buildings of seventeen to twenty storeys, to the fifteen buildings of Patterson Houses and the nine sixteen-storey towers of Millbrook Houses. Free in-building internet for residents of many NYCHA developments comes through the City's Big Apple Connect programme, which the Mayor's Office said in September 2025 covers about 220 developments and has been extended through June 2028, delivered by the cable companies serving each area.

It is worth being plain about our role. The wiring inside NYCHA buildings belongs to NYCHA and is arranged through its own programmes and contractors; a private installer cannot be hired by a resident to run a riser up a public-housing tower. Where we do work around the campuses is on the private side: the community-facility spaces leased to non-profits and health providers, the churches, schools and stores across the street, and the many privately owned walk-ups and newer buildings that sit between the developments. Those owners face the same question the City's programmes answer for NYCHA — how to get service from the curb to every apartment — but they have to solve it themselves.

For a tenant association or community group asking what can be done in a private building, the honest answer is that the owner decides, and the owner's cheapest lasting step is usually a building backbone: a cellar entry with room for several providers and a labelled riser to every floor. Once that exists, a free programme, a low-cost provider or a community network can serve the building without new drilling. We are happy to walk a building with an owner and a tenant representative together, and to put the options in writing so both can see them.

The Hub

The Hub and the East 149th Street Edge

At Mott Haven's northern border, East 149th Street, Third Avenue, Willis Avenue and Melrose Avenue converge at the Hub — "the Hub of the Bronx," also called the Broadway of the Bronx, and the oldest major shopping district in the borough. Most of it lies in Melrose, along Mott Haven's northern edge. The HUB Third Avenue Business Improvement District, established in 1990, was the first BID in the Bronx. Department stores, theatres and later chain stores and family businesses have filled its buildings over a century; the old Bronx Opera House is now a boutique hotel, and newer buildings such as the Hub Retail and Office Center and the Triangle Plaza Hub have added offices and shops since 2007.

Fiber work at the Hub looks different from the row-house and walk-up jobs further south. Buildings are larger and older, often with several retail tenants sharing a cellar, offices or classrooms on the floors above and a provider entry that has been patched for decades. The usual requests are a tested link from the shared cellar to one store's back room, a riser for a building whose upper floors have been re-let to clinics or training programmes, and a survey of what a departed tenant left behind. Sidewalks here are often crowded with shoppers, so deliveries and any cellar-hatch work are timed for early morning, and the area outside a hatch is coned off while it is open.

Your Questions Answered

Fiber Optic Installation in Mott Haven: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from Mott Haven owners, boards and institutions, grouped by the kind of building they come from: row houses, walk-ups and affordable buildings, and the churches, schools and community spaces between them.

Row houses on the landmark blocks

Our row house already has a Landmarks permit application in for façade restoration — can the fiber entry be added to it?

Often it can, and it is usually the easiest moment to do it. An application that already describes work on the façade, areaway or rear wall can be amended to show one more small opening or a concealed conduit, and the Commission's staff look at the whole package once. Ask your architect or expediter before the application is approved; once a permit is issued, a new element generally means an amendment. We provide a marked-up photo and a written description of the entry — size, position, finish and how it is sealed — in the form the applicant needs.

Our row house has a rented garden apartment that takes up most of the cellar level — where can the fiber equipment go?

Not inside the tenant's apartment, if it can be avoided. Equipment that serves the whole house should sit somewhere the owner can reach without knocking on a tenant's door: a boiler room, a meter closet, a corner of a shared vestibule or a small closet on the parlour floor. If the provider's line has to land inside the rental, we bring it straight through to a common space in a protected sleeve and put the enclosure there. Each apartment then gets its own labelled strand from that point.

Can the old gas-light pipes inside the walls of an 1880s house be used as conduit for fiber?

No. Old gas piping is not a listed raceway, its joints and fittings were never meant to have cable pulled through them, and nobody can be certain from the outside which pieces were capped and which are still connected somewhere. Cutting into or relying on it is a job for a licensed plumber and the gas company, not a cabler. The cavities that old pipes run through can sometimes help, because they show where a wall is open from floor to floor, but our cable goes in its own sleeve or innerduct.

Walk-ups and affordable buildings

Our tenement shares a light well with the building next door — whose permission does a cable up that well need?

That depends on where the property line runs, and in many shared light wells it runs straight down the middle. If any part of the cable, its supports or its entry holes would sit on the neighbour's wall or above their side, their written consent is needed; if everything stays on your own wall, it is your decision, subject to landmark rules where they apply. On the walk we look at the survey or the tax map with you, and if consent is needed we prepare a one-page description of the work for the neighbour to sign.

Our building is HPD-financed — what do the City's broadband guidelines require of the cabling itself?

Two things above all: that the owner keeps ownership of the in-building network, with maintenance covered in the provider contract, and that every vertical and horizontal pathway is easy to reach and has room for more cable later. The guidelines describe fiber from the street vault to a basement switch and then risers to each floor as the standard option, require Wi-Fi in common areas for new construction, and let HPD approve alternatives for buildings under 50 units. We design the pathways and backbone to those points and hand over drawings your project manager can check.

If we choose the Library-run Neighborhood Internet option for an HPD project, is there any cabling left for us to do?

Yes, usually. Neighborhood Internet can be your installer and provider for residential service, with HPD underwriting the cost, but the building still has its own systems — cameras, door entry, the management office, laundry card readers, a community room — and any commercial tenant needs its own service. HPD's guidelines also let a sponsor give other providers access to sell paid service. Pathways sized for all of that, and separate strands for the building's own systems, are the owner's part. We coordinate with whoever installs the residential network so nothing is drilled twice.

Some of our senior residents depend on medical-alert units at home — how is a cutover handled so those keep working?

By finding them before the cutover rather than after. Many personal emergency response units use a cellular connection and are unaffected, but some still use a landline or the home internet. Before any service is moved we ask management for a list of residents who use alert devices, check with each how theirs connects, and schedule the change for a time when staff or family can test the unit straight afterwards. Nothing is cut over in a building like this without the old service left in place until the new one is proven.

Churches, schools and community spaces

Our church's buildings are separated by an old churchyard — can fiber cross it?

Yes, but not by digging where there may be graves. The usual routes are a connected cellar or passage between the buildings, a run fixed along a boundary or building wall, or a short overhead span on a supporting messenger between two buildings. If underground work is the only option, the congregation should first find out from its records and its diocese or denomination what lies there; we would only trench where everyone is satisfied there are no burials, and we keep the route as short as possible.

Our charter school shares a city-owned school building with a district school — who approves new cabling?

The building's owner and operator, not either school on its own. In a public school building that means the Department of Education and the building's facilities staff, and their technology and construction rules apply to any contractor. We work only with written approval from whoever controls the building, in the hours they set, and we keep the school's network physically and logically separate from the other occupants'. If approval is not possible, we can often still help inside leased space the school controls directly.

Types of Fiber Optic Cabling We Install

The cable and hardware below cover nearly every Mott Haven job. In homes and walk-ups the deciding factors are size, flexibility and how neatly a box can sit on a painted wall; in newer buildings they are fire rating and room for growth.

Paintable Surface Raceway

Low-profile channel for the places cable has to show, such as a stair-hall corner, finished to blend with the wall it sits on.

Media Converters and SFP Optics

Small units or switch modules at each end that convert the light signal for ordinary network gear, picked to suit what is already installed.

Which cable we choose for a brownstone, a walk-up and a new affordable building

In a row house we usually run a slim single-mode drop cable to each floor from one cellar enclosure: it bends around tight corners, it is easy to hide, and a provider can use it later without new holes. In a walk-up the backbone is riser-rated cable with enough strands for every apartment on the line plus spares, landing on a small box on each floor. In a new affordable building the risers are often already designed; our job there is to make sure they are sized for more than one provider, that plenum cable goes wherever ceilings carry return air, and that the clinic, the shop and the building's own systems each have their own path.

Why we lean toward single-mode even on short runs

Multimode can be cheaper on paper for very short links, but most providers deliver service over single-mode, and single-mode cable has no practical speed ceiling inside a building. In a house or a small walk-up where the owner will not want to open walls twice, that future-proofing is worth more than the small difference in optics. Where a school or office has multimode switches already, we match them rather than force a change.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Handle the Things Mott Haven Buildings Throw at Us

These are standing practices rather than stories about particular customers. They come from the building types described on this page and from the rules that govern them.

An entry that is already there is worth more than a new one. In the historic districts especially, we spend time finding and testing the old underground phone or cable entry before proposing anything new. If it has room, the façade never comes into the conversation.

A stair is for people. Cable in a stair hall is kept in a slim, painted raceway fixed to the wall at the corner, never on the treads, never on the handrail side where it narrows the stair, and never on a fire escape.

Old pipes are not conduit. Abandoned gas, water or steam piping is left alone. We use the cavities they reveal, never the pipes themselves.

Separate means separate. When a clinic or shop shares a building with homes, its strands land in their own enclosure or a locked compartment, and its route avoids every apartment.

Every hole gets a photo. Each penetration is photographed open and again sealed, and the photos go in the closeout file, because in a building that has been rewired by five different companies, proof of who did what saves arguments.

Test before you blame. When residents complain about speed, we test the building's cable before recommending new fiber. If the fault is the provider's, the owner hears that first.

Mott Haven Fiber Optic Installation FAQ

Do you also take fiber work in Melrose, the Hub and the Grand Concourse blocks next to Mott Haven?
+

Yes. The Hub at East 149th Street is mostly in Melrose and we work there regularly, along with the rest of Melrose, the Concourse blocks to the north-west and Port Morris to the south, which has its own page. The same Bronx rates and crew cover all of them, and anywhere else in the borough.

Does the Landmarks Commission have to approve fiber work that stays entirely inside a historic-district house?
+

Usually not. The Commission says interior work needs an LPC permit only when it also needs a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior is a designated interior landmark. A fiber run that stays inside, with no new exterior opening, normally falls outside all three, but the Commission's preservation staff can confirm for a specific house.

Our house is in the historic district but is not one of the notable buildings — do the landmark rules still cover it?
+

Yes. Designation covers every building inside a historic district's boundary, not only the best-known ones, and the Commission says most exterior changes to front and rear façades in a district need its review. We check the district maps for your address on the site walk and plan any exterior work accordingly.

What happens if one tenant along the planned route won't let us into their apartment?
+

We do not enter, and the route changes. Usually the cable can move to a different line of apartments, a chase reached from the hall, or a stair-hall raceway, and the proposal is revised with any price difference shown. Arranging access with tenants is between the owner and the tenant; we only work in apartments where access has been agreed.

Our building is older than 1978 — do lead-paint rules affect drilling for fiber?
+

They can. HPD's safe-work-practice rules apply in pre-1978 housing when work disturbs two square feet or more of painted surface in a room, or 20 square feet outside, and they require dust-clearance testing afterwards. Fiber sleeves are only a few inches across, so most jobs stay below that, but if a plan would open more, the proposal says so and lead-safe work is arranged first.

How many fiber drops does a typical three-storey row house need?
+

Most need one drop per floor that will have its own network equipment or its own apartment, all fed from one enclosure in the cellar — so three for a three-storey house with a rental on top. A fourth is worth adding if there is a garden-level unit or a home office that wants a wired link separate from the family network.

Does a single-family row house pay the same fiber rates as a commercial building?
+

Yes. We use the same published Bronx lines for every kind of building: $447 per single-mode drop, $391 per multimode drop, $35 per termination on existing fiber and $40 per strand for an OTDR test. A house simply needs fewer of them, and occupied-home precautions are listed in the proposal.

Our bodega's cameras record to a box in the cellar and the picture keeps freezing — is it the cable or the recorder?
+

Either is possible, and we test before guessing. We check the link between the cellar and the cameras end to end, look at the recorder's storage and network settings, and see whether the freezes line up with a particular camera or time of day. If the cable is the fault we fix it; if it is the recorder, our camera team can advise on that separately.

The store on our ground floor controls the cellar where the provider's line comes in — how do the upstairs owners get access for fiber work?
+

Through the building owner and the store's lease, which normally lets the landlord reach building systems. We agree access times outside the store's trading hours, protect its stock, and keep the apartments' enclosure separate from the store's. Where it is practical, we also give the upstairs network a box the owner can reach without entering the store.

The provider will run its line to my house for free — when does a homeowner need a private installer as well?
+

When the service has to reach more than the spot where the provider puts its box. The provider's technician usually stops at its own equipment; getting a wired connection to an upper floor, a rented apartment or a home office, without visible cable, is the part we do. If one connection near the entry is all you need, you may not need us at all.

How many days will a crew be working inside a lived-in walk-up?
+

For a riser in a five- or six-storey walk-up, usually two to four working days on site, depending on the vertical path and how many apartments we have to visit. A row house with three or four floors is often a single day. The proposal gives the planned number of visits, and the noisy steps are grouped together.

Will the new cable be visible in our building's public hallway, and what will it look like?
+

Only where there is no hidden path. Where it has to show, it runs in a slim surface raceway at the corner of the wall or along the ceiling line, painted to match, with a small box on each landing. Nothing is fixed to the stair treads, the handrail side or a fire escape, and we agree the exact line with the owner before fixing it.
