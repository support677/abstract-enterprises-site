The Neighborhood

Why Cadman Plaza Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Most of the people who live around Cadman Plaza get through their front door because someone at a desk lets them in, and that changes what a broken buzzer looks like. In a walk-up, a dead buzzer is obvious within a day: a visitor rings, nothing happens, and somebody walks down four flights. In a staffed tower, the doorman covers for the failure. He waves the courier in, phones the apartment from the desk, or walks a guest to the elevator. The apartment station on the kitchen wall can sit dead for months before anyone notices, and the first sign is often a night when the desk is short-handed, or a new resident who finds out the handset in the apartment they just bought has never worked for them. Our job in these buildings is as much about finding what quietly stopped working as about fixing the thing that just broke.

The towers came out of a single plan. The Center for Brooklyn History describes the Cadman Plaza complex as apartment buildings and townhouses between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street, built in the late 1960s on the site of the old Brooklyn Bridge trolley terminal: Cadman Plaza North at 140 Cadman Plaza West, Whitman Close at 75 Henry Street, and Cadman Towers at 101 Clark Street and 10 Clinton Street. They were Mitchell-Lama middle-income co-ops. StreetEasy lists 140 Cadman Plaza West as a 250-unit high-rise, many apartments with balconies, and CityRealty dates it to 1967 with 27 floors. NYC Urbanism's history of Cadman Towers describes a 32-story tower and a 13-story tower with 420 co-op apartments, 50 duplex townhouses built to keep the street at a human scale, garages for about half the units, an elevated plaza with a bridge over Clark Street, and towers and townhouses set behind six-foot concrete walls. Brownstoner's 2012 look at Whitman Close counts roughly sixteen two-story townhouses beside a 33-story tower. Each of those features adds an entrance, and every entrance is a door that has to call someone and then lock again.

That is the first reason Cadman Plaza buildings need a buzzer technician who thinks in systems rather than parts. A single co-op here may have a main lobby on the plaza, a street-level door below it, a garage door into the elevator lobby, a service entrance, a package room, and a row of townhouse doors with their own bells, all installed at different times by different people. When one of them fails, the cause can be a part at that door, or it can be something they share: a power supply in a basement room, a desk console, a cable that runs the length of the garage ceiling. We start by drawing that map with the super or the managing agent, door by door, before we open anything.

The second reason is turnover. In April 2024 THE CITY reported that Cadman Towers had become the first Mitchell-Lama co-op approved to convert to an HDFC, with more than three quarters of shareholders in favor, resale prices capped for buyers earning up to 125 percent of area median income, and the old waitlist replaced by a lottery on Housing Connect. Habitat Magazine put the vote at 77 percent. NYC Housing Connect now lists a Cadman Towers HDFC lottery. A building that is selling apartments to new households is also a building where apartments are emptied, painted, rewired and renovated between owners. Every one of those jobs is a chance for an intercom wire inside a wall to be cut, a station to be taken down and hung back on the wrong pair, or a handset to leave with the previous owner's furniture.

The third reason is the mix just outside the towers. The small brick buildings on Henry Street by Cadman Plaza are distinctive enough that a Reddit user on r/AskNYC asked what it is like to live in one. Those buildings, and the walk-ups along Pineapple, Middagh and Clark Streets, have no doorman to hide a failure. A resident of a roughly 20-unit Brooklyn Heights building wrote on r/nyc that the front door "hydraulically locks when pushed open," and still found it propped open late at night. That is the pattern we see in small buildings with a slow or unreliable release: residents stop trusting the buzzer, prop the door for a delivery, and the lock that cost good money stops protecting anyone. On the commercial side, the offices around Borough Hall and Court Street depend on a buzzer at the suite door the way the towers depend on the doorman.

We also want to be straight about what the records show and do not show, because it shapes how we read a call from this part of Brooklyn. In NYC Open Data, as of November 11, 2026, we found no 311 buzzer complaints and no HPD intercom violations at the four Cadman Plaza complex addresses or at One Clinton. We read that as a sign that staffed co-ops and condos solve these problems through management rather than through the city, not as proof that their systems never fail. The complaints that do appear nearby come from walk-ups and mid-size rental buildings, and we cover them in their own section below.

Many complaints close without an inspection. The most common outcome in the 2026 records is some version of HPD's note that it "attempted to conduct an inspection in response to this complaint, but was unable to complete the inspection." Others close because an inspector could not gain access, or because someone told HPD by phone that the condition was corrected. A closed complaint is not the same as a working buzzer. If you live in one of the walk-ups near the park and your complaint closed that way, the building's duty to repair has not gone anywhere, and the fault is still in the wall.

The closest records are in the small buildings. The towers are absent from the files. We found no 311 buzzer complaint and no HPD intercom violation at 140 Cadman Plaza West, 75 Henry Street, 101 Clark Street, 10 Clinton Street or 280 Cadman Plaza West. Our reading, and it is only a reading, is that residents of staffed co-ops and condos take a dead handset to the management office or the board long before they call 311, and that the doorman covers the gap in the meantime. That does not make those systems healthier. It makes their failures less visible, which is why a full test of every apartment, rather than a fix of the one that complained, is often the most useful thing a tower's board can order.

What the HPD text says. When HPD does write a buzzer violation in Brooklyn, it is typically under section 27-2005 of the Housing Maintenance Code, with wording ordering the owner to properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell, buzzer or intercom system from the building entrance door to the apartment. In 11201 the classes recorded run from A to B. A violation at 24 Joralemon Street in July 2026 carries the status "invalid certification," which usually means an owner told HPD the work was done and HPD did not accept it. A repair that will be certified to HPD needs a written record of what was tested at that apartment, and we provide one with every job.

From the Lobby Desk to the Top Floor: What a Doorman, Shareholder or Office Manager Can Notice First

In a Cadman Plaza tower, three different people hold three different pieces of the story. The doorman knows which apartments he can't reach from the desk. The shareholder knows that her handset rings but she can't hear anyone. The managing agent knows which contractor last worked in the building. A few minutes of noticing, with nobody opening a panel, a desk console or a basement box, tells us what to bring and saves a second trip. Please don't unscrew anything. Ask the doorman to list the apartments he can't ring

The single most useful thing in a staffed building is a list, made at the desk, of every apartment the desk console can't reach, can reach but can't hear, or can hear but can't release for. Doormen often know this list by heart and have been working around it for months. Written down by floor and apartment letter, it shows us at a glance whether the failures are scattered or stacked in one line. Stacked failures point to the riser. Scattered ones point to individual stations, and often to the apartments that have been renovated.

Call the desk from your own handset, then have the desk call you

A resident can do one simple test. Pick up the apartment station and call the desk, then have the desk call you back. Note whether each direction rings, whether each side can hear the other, and whether your release button opens the entrance while the doorman watches. Intercoms have separate paths for ringing, talking, listening and releasing, and a fault on one does not mean the others are gone. Four short answers save us a lot of time.

Listen at the entrance frame while someone presses release

At a townhouse door, a walk-up on Henry Street or a suite door off Court Street, stand by the frame where the latch meets it while someone else presses release. A hum means power is reaching the strike. If you hear it and the door stays shut, push the door gently toward its stop while the button is held. A door that only opens when pushed first is pressing its latch too hard against the strike, which is a door adjustment rather than an electronics fault. Silence at the frame sends us to the power and the release circuit instead.

Find the date of the last job in the building

Buzzer faults in a co-op often start right after something unrelated to the buzzer. An apartment sold and gut-renovated. A plumber opened a wall at a riser. A cable or fiber installer worked in the hall closets. The garage was resurfaced. The lobby was repainted and the panel taken off the wall. Ask the managing agent for the dates of recent alteration agreements and contractor work. In our experience the last job's date and the date the buzzer failed line up more often than anyone expects.

Photograph the panel, the desk console and any notice

Take a straight-on photo of the entrance panel, a photo of the desk console showing its make and buttons, and a photo of the door edge where the latch is. If there is an HPD notice, a 311 complaint number or a management work order, photograph that too. Send them with the form or by text. They tell us what system we are likely to meet and which parts to bring on the first visit.

Inside a poured-concrete tower. The Cadman towers are concrete buildings, and NYC Urbanism describes Cadman Towers' walls as saw-toothed poured concrete with large windowless sections. That changes how a repair is done. In a brick walk-up, a damaged run can sometimes be fished through a hollow wall. In a concrete tower, the cable lives where the builders left room for it, in risers, sleeves, conduit and closets, and there is rarely a new path to be had. So we repair what is there. We find the junction that failed, remake it properly, and protect it. When a run inside a concrete wall is truly damaged, the honest options are a repair at the nearest accessible point or a short new run in surface raceway, and the quote says which, and where it would show, before the board decides.

On a tower riser. In a building of 250 or more apartments, the wire from the lobby climbs the building in one or more risers and branches at each floor to reach the apartments. A failed junction at one floor can silence every apartment above it in that line while the floors below keep working perfectly. Two shareholders in the same tower can describe two different buildings, one where the buzzer works and one where it never has. We test at the desk, at the panel, at the base of the riser and at the lowest silent floor before we decide where the loss is, and we leave every box we open labeled with the apartments it serves.

On a townhouse at a tower's foot. The duplex townhouses were built so the complex would meet the street at the scale of a house, and many of them have their own door, bell and button. Some are tied into the tower's system and some never were. We find out which before we quote, because the answer decides who approves the work and what the repair can touch. The co-op's exterior rules still apply to anything visible from the street or the garden, so a new button or station goes in the existing opening unless the board agrees to something else.

  • Doors held by a magnet. Some suite and shop doors use a magnetic lock instead of a strike. Code conditions for electrically locked egress doors require that people inside can always get out, typically through a sensor or a release that cuts the lock's power. We test the way out before the way in.
  • What a door does when power fails. Allegion explains that a fail-secure strike stays locked when it loses power and a fail-safe strike unlocks, and that with a strike people inside can still leave by the lever. Which one suits a file room door or a shop's back door depends on what the door protects and who must be able to leave through it.
  • Reach heights. The 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach for a control at no more than 48 inches. A replacement call button or reception release goes within reach, which matters for clients and visitors who use wheelchairs.
  • Self-closing residents' doors. Where a building with shops also has three or more apartments, HPD treats a defective self-closing door as a Class C immediately hazardous violation, to be corrected within 14 days of receiving the notice. A delivery routine that leaves the residents' door propped never fits that rule.
  • Line-voltage work. The Department of Buildings requires a licensed electrician and a permit for electrical work at line voltage. Our work stays on the low-voltage side; if a door supply needs a new outlet or circuit, the quote names it as an electrician's job.

Towers with more than one way in

Our tower's main lobby is up on the plaza, and there is a second entrance down at street level. Why does one door call apartments and the other doesn't?

Because in a complex built with an elevated plaza, the two doors are often wired as two separate entrances, sometimes installed years apart. Each one may have its own panel, its own power supply and its own path into the system, and they only meet at a shared controller or at the desk. When the plaza door works and the street door doesn't, the shared parts and the apartment stations are almost certainly fine, and the fault sits in the street door's panel, its supply or the stretch of cable between that door and the point where it joins the rest. We test the working entrance first to prove the shared parts, then follow the failing door's own path. If the street door was never meant to call apartments and only releases from the desk, we will tell you that too, because it changes what "fixed" means.

Cadman Towers has a 32-story building and a 13-story building in the same complex. If only the shorter one loses calls, what does that point to?

It points away from anything the two buildings share and toward something only the shorter one has. In most two-building complexes each tower has its own riser and often its own power supply, even when one desk or one panel calls both. If every apartment in one building goes quiet at once while the other is fine, we look first at that building's supply, at the cable that links it to the shared equipment, and at the junction where that cable enters the building. If only some apartments in the shorter building are silent, the riser in that building becomes the suspect. Either way, the healthy tower is useful: it lets us prove the shared equipment works before we spend time on it.

The door from our garage into the elevator lobby releases for some apartments and not for others. How can one door be picky?

A door can't choose, but the system deciding who may release it can. In many large buildings the garage door is set up as a separate output, released only from certain stations, from the desk or by a card or code, and the list of who may release it lives in programming or in how the stations are wired. When some apartments can release it and others can't, the cause is usually one of three things: a programming list that was never updated after apartments changed hands, a group of stations wired to a different output, or a fault on the release line that only affects some floors. We test the door from a working station and a failing one, check the programming if there is any, and then trace the release wiring only if the first two come back clean.

Sales, renovations and new owners

Apartments in our co-op are being resold and gut-renovated one after another. What should the alteration agreement say about the intercom?

It should say who disconnects the station, who protects the wire while walls are open, and who tests the station before the renovation is signed off. We suggest three plain lines: the station is removed and its wires labeled before demolition, the cable is protected and not cut or relocated without the managing agent's written approval, and the station is reconnected and tested from the desk and the entrance panel before the walls close. Habitat Magazine, reporting on a New York Times reader question, quoted attorney Andrew Freedland of Herrick Feinstein saying that if a neighbor's renovation caused an intercom problem, the neighbor may be responsible depending on the lease terms or the alteration agreement. Writing the test into the agreement up front avoids having to prove it later. This is general information, not legal advice.

A household that bought through the Housing Connect lottery moves in next month, and the handset in that apartment has been dead since the last owner left. Who should arrange the repair before move-in?

Usually the co-op, through its managing agent, because the station is part of the building's entry system even when it hangs inside an apartment, and the new household needs it working on day one. Proprietary leases differ on how repairs inside an apartment are split, so the board should check its own lease. Cooperator News notes that some co-ops perform such repairs at no charge to the shareholder as a matter of policy even where the lease would allow otherwise. Practically, the best time to fix it is while the apartment is empty. We can test the station, the wire to the riser and the release in one short visit, and the managing agent gets a dated note showing the apartment was working when the keys changed hands.

Townhouses, walk-ups and the records

We own a duplex townhouse at the foot of the towers with our own street door. Is our bell on the tower's system or a separate one?

It depends on the townhouse, and it is the first thing we find out. Some townhouses in large co-op complexes have a simple bell or door station of their own, powered by a transformer inside the house, that never touched the tower's system. Others were tied into the main entry system so the desk can reach them and visitors at the main panel can call them. A few have both. The clues are on the door station and inside the house: a small transformer on its own circuit means a separate system, while a station matching the ones in the tower's apartments suggests a shared one. Which it is decides who approves the repair and who pays for it, so we confirm it with the managing agent before quoting.

The city's records show buzzer complaints at walk-ups on Pineapple and Clark Streets but none at the big co-ops across Cadman Plaza West. Does that mean nobody in the towers has complained?

No. It means nobody there complained to 311, as far as the open records show. In a staffed co-op, a shareholder with a dead handset calls the management office or tells the doorman, and the problem either gets fixed or gets worked around at the desk without the city ever hearing about it. In a walk-up with an absentee owner there is no desk and often no quick answer, so tenants file. The records are a good picture of where complaints go unanswered, and a poor picture of where systems fail. For a tower's board, the practical takeaway is that the only way to know how many stations work is to test them.

Offices and shops

On court mornings our suite door near the Supreme Court is buzzed open dozens of times, and by afternoon the release is weak. Is the strike wearing out?

It may be, but the power supply is just as likely. A strike that is energized over and over in a short time runs hot, and some strikes, especially older ones on alternating current, are not rated for that much use. As the coil heats, it pulls less firmly, and the door starts to need a push. A small supply feeding that strike can also sag under the load, so the release gets weaker as the morning goes on and recovers by evening. We measure the supply's output while the strike is working, check the strike's rating against how often the door is buzzed, and replace whichever part is the weak link with one suited to a busy suite door.

Our suite's reception desk can buzz the suite door, but late clients can't get past the building's locked lobby after hours. Can the two be tied together?

Sometimes, but only with the landlord's agreement, because the lobby door and its entry system belong to the building, not the tenant. In many multi-tenant office towers near Borough Hall the after-hours lobby runs on the landlord's panel, card readers and staff, and a tenant's suite buzzer has no connection to it. Options that can work, with approval, include an after-hours call point that reaches the suite, a visitor procedure through the building's security, or temporary credentials issued by the building. We can repair and improve the suite side on our own; anything touching the lobby we quote only after the landlord's property manager says yes in writing.

The back door of our shop on Pineapple Walk opens toward the co-op's service area. Who controls its buzzer, the store or the co-op?

That depends on the store's lease with the co-op that owns the strip, and it is worth settling before anyone is called. Typically a tenant controls hardware it installed for its own use, while anything wired into the co-op's systems, powered from the co-op's equipment or opening into a space the co-op controls belongs to the landlord. A back door buzzer that the store added, powered from inside the store, is usually the store's to repair. A door that releases from the co-op's desk or service entrance system is usually the co-op's. We ask for the lease language or the managing agent's answer before the visit, so the repair is billed to the right party and nobody's system is changed without permission.

Who Answers for a Broken Buzzer in a Cadman Plaza Co-op, Condo or Walk-Up

Around Cadman Plaza most of the people asking "who fixes this?" are not renters with a landlord. They are shareholders in a co-op, owners in a condo, or tenants of a professional suite. The rules that make a building keep its intercom working still apply to them, but the route from "my handset is dead" to "the handset works" runs through a board, a managing agent and a proprietary lease. This section lays out how those layers fit, as general information and not legal advice.

The state rule. According to the New York Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide, multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 must have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at every entrance, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom that lets an occupant buzz a visitor in. The Cadman Plaza towers went up between 1967 and 1973 and hold hundreds of apartments each, so most of them sit squarely in the class of buildings that rule describes. Older buildings, such as some of the walk-ups at the edge of the complex, must also have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request one, the guide says.

The city rule. New York City's Housing Maintenance Code adds its own duty to maintain a working intercom where one is required or provided, and HPD writes violations under it. In ZIP 11201 those violations appear in HPD's records against walk-ups and mid-size rental buildings, not against the towers, but the code itself makes no exception for co-ops or condos.

The co-op rule. In February 2024 Habitat Magazine, drawing on a New York Times Ask Real Estate column, put it plainly. "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom," said Nancy Kourland, a partner who handles real estate litigation at Lasser Law Group. The same article explains that courts treat a working intercom as an essential service, so failing to maintain one breaches the warranty of habitability under state law because it affects the habitability and security of an apartment. Andrew Bart, a senior litigation counsel at Kagan Lubic Lepper Finkelstein & Gold, added that if a co-op sues a shareholder for nonpayment, a broken intercom could be a defense. The article lists the shareholder's steps as notifying the board in writing, complaining to 311, or bringing an HP proceeding in housing court, where no lawyer is required.

The proprietary lease. Inside that framework, a co-op's own proprietary lease decides who handles what. Many leases make the corporation responsible for building systems and the shareholder responsible for the interior of the apartment, which leaves a gray zone for a station that is part of a building system but hangs on an apartment wall. Cooperator News notes that some cooperatives handle such repairs at no charge to the shareholder as a matter of policy, even where the lease would let them charge. For a board in a Cadman Plaza tower, the cleanest answer is a written policy: the building repairs entry-system parts, including apartment stations, and charges a shareholder only when the shareholder's own work caused the damage.

The condo version. At One Clinton, or any condo, the entry system is normally a common element maintained by the condo board through its managing agent, paid from common charges. Unit owners report a failed station to management, and the board decides whether a repair is a common expense or charged back to an owner whose renovation caused it. The bylaws, not the proprietary lease, are the document to check.

The HDFC layer. Cadman Towers' 2024 conversion into an HDFC co-op, reported by THE CITY and Habitat, does not change the basic duty. It does change the money. THE CITY reported $62 million of debt, and an HDFC board working within regulated resale prices may want to phase repairs. A phased plan is reasonable as long as every apartment can call and release now, even if the longer-term upgrade waits. We write quotes that separate "make every station work" from "improve the system," so a board can approve the first immediately and budget for the second.

The walk-up layer. In the brick rental buildings around the edge of the complex, the duty sits with the owner, and the 311 route is the one tenants use. If HPD closed your complaint because it couldn't get in, the condition hasn't changed and you can file again. When an owner calls us about an open violation, we test the cited apartment and the entrance, repair what failed, and give a written record the owner can use to certify the correction.
