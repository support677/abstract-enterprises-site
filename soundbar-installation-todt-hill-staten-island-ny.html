The Neighborhood

Why Todt Hill Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Todt Hill is the high ground of Staten Island, and the houses on it were built to match. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s 2007 designation report on a house on Circle Road puts the summit at 409.8 feet and repeats the long-standing claim that it is the highest natural point on the eastern seaboard from Maine to Florida. Wikipedia’s figure is 401 feet, and it calls the hill the highest natural point in the five boroughs; Atlas Obscura goes further and calls it the highest point on the entire Atlantic coastal plain. Whichever number you prefer, the summit sits near Todt Hill Road and Merrick Avenue, the hill itself is serpentine rock, and most of its crown is woodland inside the Staten Island Greenbelt. Everything else on the hill is private houses, and the way those houses were built is what this page is about.

The LPC report tells the story briefly. In the nineteenth century the hill “became the location of country homes on large estates.” Two families shaped what came next. George Cromwell, the first borough president of Staten Island, began selling off acreage of his property for development in the early twentieth century. The architect Ernest Flagg, who designed the Singer Building in Manhattan, bought land on the hill from 1897 onward until his estate held more than 300 acres by the mid-1940s; it too was eventually subdivided and sold. Flagg left behind his own mansion, Stone Court, and a group of small stone cottages built by a method of his own invention. The subdivided land became the streets you know now: Flagg Place, Benedict Road, Circle Road, Willow Pond Road and the lanes off Todt Hill Road and Ocean Terrace. Forgotten New York describes Benedict, Circle and Willow Pond Roads as the “exclusive, walled-off part of Todt Hill.”

What does that mean for a soundbar? Three things. First, scale. A 2017 Brick Underground guide to the neighbourhood quoted a broker describing “mostly massive mansions with over 3,000 square feet of space,” pictured a house of more than 13,000 square feet, and noted “gargantuan cast iron gates” and private roads. Rooms in houses of that size are often deeper, wider and taller than the room a soundbar’s engineers had in mind, and the bar that sounds full in a showroom can sound thin from a sofa twenty feet back. Second, the ground. The city’s Special Natural Area District rules describe the “central, serpentine, hilly spine” of Emerson Hill, Dongan Hills, Todt Hill and Lighthouse Hill as “richly endowed with steep slopes, rock outcrops, erratic boulders and ponds.” Houses on slopes like that step down: a main floor at the street, a lower level that is underground at the back and opens to the garden at the front, sometimes a third level below that. Third, the walls themselves, which range from Flagg’s stone-and-concrete to post-war plaster to modern drywall over steel in a new build.

Then there is the other Todt Hill. At the bottom of the north-western slope, in the part of the neighbourhood NY1 places in Manor Heights, stand the Todt Hill Houses: seven six-storey buildings with 502 apartments and about a thousand residents, completed in 1950 as the first public housing development on Staten Island. NYCHA awarded a contract worth more than $150 million in January 2024 to modernise the whole development, with new kitchens and bathrooms, full plumbing and electrical upgrades, new floors and windows, and the removal of lead-based paint. NY1 reported in December 2025 that residents are moved temporarily to other NYCHA apartments while their building is done, with the right to return, and that the work is expected to finish by mid-2027. The census tract that holds the hill, Todt Hill–Emerson Hill–Lighthouse Hill–Manor Heights, is 30.3 percent renter-occupied, and many of those renters live here and in the apartment buildings near Richmond Road.

Finally, getting here. Wikipedia notes that no public transit serves the hill itself and that most streets, Todt Hill Road included, have no sidewalks; Brick Underground adds that most have no street lights either, and that “no buses climb Todt Hill.” Buses run along Richmond Road, which marks the border between Todt Hill and Dongan Hills. None of that changes a price on this page, but it does change how a visit runs: we drive to the door, we bring everything in one trip, and on a winter evening the technician arrives with a torch because the lane outside will be dark.

  • 32,822 residents and 10,623 households in census tract area SI0203 (Todt Hill–Emerson Hill–Lighthouse Hill–Manor Heights) at the 2020 census, up 6.4% from 2010; 32.7% of households have children under 18 and the largest age group is 55–74 (Wikipedia, citing NYC Planning).
  • 30.3% renter-occupied in that tract, so roughly seven homes in ten are lived in by their owners (Wikipedia).

An owner-occupied estate house, a 1960s split near Richmond Road and a renovated NYCHA apartment share a ZIP code and a hill, and almost nothing else. Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Todt Hill’s Architecture

The hill was built in waves, and each wave left a different kind of wall. Estate houses of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Flagg’s experimental stone cottages, mid-century houses on the first subdivisions, large modern houses on the last of the estate land, and the brick apartment blocks at the foot of the slope. Here is what each usually means for a soundbar.

Flagg’s Stone Cottages

Ernest Flagg built a group of small houses on the grounds of Stone Court using what he called mosaic rubble: flat-faced stones set in reusable timber forms with concrete poured behind them. Bowcot on West Entry Road (1916–17), Wallcot at 285 Flagg Place (1918–21) and the McCall’s Demonstration House at 1929 Richmond Road (1924–25) are designated landmarks. The stone, the LPC records, was serpentine from a quarry on the estate. For a soundbar that means a wall of hard stone faces backed by concrete, and anchors chosen for both.

Older Estate Houses

The country houses that survive from the nineteenth-century estates, and houses moved or rebuilt on the hill, such as the Gillett-Tyler House on Circle Road, built around 1846 and re-erected here in 1931. Plaster walls, deep window reveals and original trim that nobody wants scarred.

Mid-Century Houses

Colonials, ranches and splits from the first subdivisions of the estates. Mostly plaster or early drywall on wood studs, with family rooms added later and lower levels finished in the 1970s and 1980s.

Large Modern Houses

The big houses on private lanes and cul-de-sacs: two-storey great rooms, open kitchens, stone fireplaces, glass walls toward the trees, and in many cases a finished walk-out lower level. Drywall on studs for the most part, with stone veneer and large-format tile as feature finishes.

Lower Levels Cut Into the Slope

Whatever the age of the house, a lower level on this hill often has a poured or block wall on the uphill side and windows or doors on the downhill side. The TV usually wants the solid wall; the solid wall is usually concrete.

The Todt Hill Houses

Seven six-storey brick buildings from 1950, now being modernised with new kitchens, bathrooms, floors, windows and electrical work. Apartment rules and the condition of newly finished walls come before any bracket.

What Flagg’s method means for a drill bit

Flagg published his building system in a book called Small Houses, and the LPC’s designation report on the McCall’s Demonstration House describes it in detail. Timber uprights and cross-bars stood on footing sleepers to make a trough. Stones went into the trough with their flat faces against the outer form, and concrete was poured in behind them as a backing. When that course set, the forms were raised and the process repeated. Only the joints were pointed afterwards. The corners at Bowcot, according to the Historic Districts Council’s 6 to Celebrate guide, were made of concrete blocks with vertical reinforcing rods, filled with concrete — an idea about twenty years ahead of general use.

Inside, that gives a TV wall with no stud and no cavity. The drill meets either a stone face, a pointing joint or concrete, sometimes all three within the width of a bracket. Serpentine is a dense, often slippery rock; a hammer drill and a sharp masonry bit go through it, but slowly, and the bit must be kept cool. We mark every hole, decide whether it lands in stone, joint or concrete, and choose anchors to suit each one rather than one type for all. Where the inside face of the wall has been plastered, we treat the plaster as a finish only and anchor through it into the masonry.

The same report notes that Flagg’s method for interior partitions in Small Houses used plaster applied to a jute or burlap screen rather than to wooden lath, while saying it can’t confirm that every house used it. If your TV is going on an interior partition in one of these houses, we test-drill before promising anything: a fabric-backed plaster partition has nothing for a hollow-wall anchor to grip, and the bracket has to reach framing, the masonry at either end, or a backer board spanning between them.

Landmarked houses and the inside of the room

Several houses on and around the hill are individual landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says an LPC permit for interior work is required when the work needs a Department of Buildings permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark; ordinary repairs and maintenance need no permit. A soundbar fixed to an inside wall, wired inside the room, normally needs no DOB permit and touches nothing outside. That is our reading of the LPC’s published guidance, not legal advice. If you are unsure, or if any part of the job would touch the outside of the house — a speaker on a terrace wall, a cable through a stone sill — check with the Commission before we come, and we will hold that part of the job until you have its answer.

Compliance notes for business rooms

Two rules come up regularly. The first is the reach of wall-mounted objects in public circulation space under the ADA standards: things fixed to a wall between 27 and 80 inches above the floor should not project more than 4 inches into a walkway, which is why a TV and soundbar in a corridor or lobby usually go above 80 inches or into a recess. The second is noise. New York City’s noise code limits sound from commercial music that carries outside the premises, and a bar in a club room or office is very unlikely to approach that, but we set a sensible maximum volume where staff or members can reach the remote. We also supply a certificate of insurance free of charge when a club, school or landlord asks for one.

We are not the right people for a public-address system, a hearing loop or a full sound-reinforcement install in a hall. When a room needs one of those, the conversation moves to the AV team and a design visit.

Stone, rubble and fabric-backed plaster

Our TV wall is Flagg-style stone with concrete poured behind it — what does the drill actually go into, and which anchor holds?

A wall built by Flagg’s mosaic-rubble method is three materials in one: hard, flat stone faces on the room side, mortar pointing between them, and a concrete backing that fills every gap behind the stones. Where the inside face has been plastered over, there is a fourth layer that is decoration only. Within the width of a typical soundbar bracket, the four or six holes may land in any of those, and each behaves differently. Serpentine and similar hard stone drill slowly and cleanly; pointing is softer and can crumble around a fast bit; the concrete backing is consistent and is usually the best material to anchor into.

So we map before we drill. The bracket is held up level, the hole positions are marked, and each mark is judged: stone, joint or concrete. Where we can move the bracket a few centimetres to put every hole in stone or concrete, we do. Where a hole has to fall in a joint, we go deeper so the anchor engages the concrete behind, and we use an anchor that doesn’t rely on expanding against crumbly mortar. The bit is new, sharp and kept cool, the hammer action is used gently, and dust is caught at the hole.

A soundbar is light compared with a TV, so the load is not the worry; the worry is a chipped stone face that you will see for the next fifty years. That is why the stone add on the price list (+$65) is there, and why, if a stone face looks fragile, we will suggest the TV-attached bracket so that the bar hangs from the TV mount and the wall is drilled only once.

A partition in our older house rang hollow when we tapped it, and the stud finder found nothing — could it be plaster on a fabric screen, and what holds a bracket then?

It could. The LPC records that Flagg’s published method for interior partitions used plaster on a jute or burlap screen instead of wooden lath, though it doesn’t say every house followed it, and other houses of that age used metal lath or early gypsum board. All three can fool an electronic stud finder. What they share is that the plaster is thin and the space behind it may have no wood where you’d expect a stud.

We find out with a small test hole behind where the bracket will sit, so the hole is hidden afterwards. If there is framing, we fix into it. If the partition is fabric- or mesh-backed with framing too far apart, a hollow-wall toggle won’t grip reliably and we don’t pretend it will. The answer is a plywood backer board ($85) screwed across the nearest framing members and painted to match, with the bar bracket fixed to that, or the TV-attached bracket, or console placement if the TV is on its stand.

Either way, you hear which one before we drill a second hole, and the price you agree is made of the published lines: $129 for the mount, $45 for plaster, $85 for a backer board if one is needed.

Very large rooms

Our great room has a conversation area near the TV and a second sofa across the room by the kitchen — can one bar serve both groups?

It can serve one of them properly and the other acceptably, and it is worth deciding in advance which is which. A soundbar’s room calibration measures from one place, usually where you hold the phone or the bar’s microphone picks up its test tones, and it tunes the bar for that seat. Sit twice as far away and you hear the same bar with more of the room mixed in: voices get softer relative to the music and effects, and bass from a subwoofer placed for the near seats may boom or vanish at the far ones.

Our usual approach is to calibrate for the main seating group, then walk to the second group and adjust the dialogue or voice setting until speech is clear there without being harsh up close. Many bars let you raise the centre channel or voice band independently of the overall volume, and that single control does more for a far sofa than turning everything up. Where the second group is used for watching as often as the first, a second pair of rear speakers is not an option on most bars, so the real fix is a speaker near that group — an in-ceiling or in-wall pair fed from a receiver — and that is a design job for our AV team rather than a soundbar job.

Tell us how the room is actually used. If the far sofa is for reading while the game is on, one bar tuned for the near seats is right. If it’s the family’s main seat on movie night, we plan around it instead.

One side of our great room is floor-to-ceiling glass and the other is a stone fireplace wall — will the bar sound lopsided?

It can, a little, and it is one of the more common complaints in big modern rooms. Glass and stone are both hard, but they sit at different distances from the bar and reflect differently, and an open kitchen on one side swallows sound that a solid wall on the other side throws back. The result is a sound image that leans: voices seem to come from slightly left or right of the screen, and effects that should travel across the room bunch up on one side.

Most of the cure is in setup rather than equipment. The bar should sit centred on the screen, not on the wall, and the main seat centred on the bar where the furniture allows. Room calibration from that seat corrects much of the imbalance, because the bar measures what actually reaches you. If the bar has rear speakers, we place them symmetrically about the seat rather than about the room, and set their levels separately. Soft furnishings help more than people expect: a large rug between the bar and the sofa and curtains that can close over part of the glass take the edge off the reflections.

What we can’t do is make a room with one glass wall sound like a room without it. If the imbalance still bothers you after calibration, the next step is acoustic treatment or a speaker layout designed for the room, which our AV team can quote separately.

We’re buying a large flagship bar to hang from the TV mount — is there a weight limit we should know about?

Yes, two of them. The TV-attached soundbar bracket has its own rated load, printed on its box and in its manual, and flagship bars with up-firing drivers and several woofers weigh noticeably more than compact ones. The second limit is the TV mount: whatever hangs from the TV is added to the TV’s weight, and on an articulating arm it also adds leverage, because the bar sits further from the wall when the arm is extended.

Before we book we check the bar’s weight against the bracket’s rating, and the TV plus bar against the mount’s rating. If either is close, we fix the bar to the wall on its own bracket instead, which takes it off the TV mount altogether. On a full-motion arm we also check that the bar clears the wall and furniture through the whole range of movement and that its cables have enough slack to follow.

The Sanus bracket we supply is $84.99. If you’ve bought another TV-attached bracket, we’ll use it if it’s rated for your bar and fits your TV’s mounting pattern; if it isn’t, we’ll tell you before the bar goes on it.

All our boxes live in a closet more than 25 feet from the TV — what happens beyond the 25-foot line on your price list?

The published cable-run line is $99 for up to 25 feet, and that is all the price list covers. Beyond 25 feet two things change. The route usually needs more work — through a basement ceiling, along a different floor, around a stair — and an ordinary passive HDMI cable may not carry a 4K signal reliably that far. Longer runs normally need an active or fibre-optic HDMI cable, which is designed for distance and has a direction marked on it.

Because none of that is on the published list, we don’t guess a price on the phone. We ask for photos of both ends and a description of the route, and send you in writing either a plan that uses the published lines (for example two runs to an intermediate cabinet) or a statement that this part of the job needs a site visit. Nothing is charged that you haven’t seen in writing first.

Often there is a simpler answer: move only the streaming box to the TV wall and leave the rest in the closet. The bar needs one HDMI connection, to the TV’s eARC or ARC port; everything else can feed the TV wherever it sits.

We have TVs in six rooms — which of them actually need a soundbar, and which are fine on their own speakers?

Most houses don’t need six bars. A useful test is how the room is watched. Rooms where people sit for films, sport or long evenings — the family room, the lower-level den, often the primary bedroom — repay a good bar, because thin TV speakers are what makes dialogue hard to follow. Rooms where the TV is background — a kitchen, a gym, a guest room used a few nights a year — often do fine with the TV’s own speakers, or with a compact bar on the console for $79 if speech is hard to hear there.

Two further questions decide it. Does anyone in the household have trouble following speech? Then the rooms that person uses come first, and a bar with a good voice-enhancement mode is worth more than one with the biggest bass. And does the household want music in several rooms at once? Then it may be better to choose bars from one maker’s system so they can be grouped, and to plan the rooms together rather than one at a time.

We price each room on its own line, so you can do the important rooms now and the others later without paying for a plan you won’t use.

Houses that step down the slope

Our walk-out lower level is underground on the uphill side and opens to the garden on the downhill side — which wall should carry the TV and bar?

Usually the uphill wall, but for reasons of light as much as sound. The downhill side is where the windows and doors are, and a TV facing them will reflect the garden all afternoon; a TV on that side faces the dark end of the room and the viewers face the glare. The uphill wall is solid, faces the light, and puts the seating with its back to the view, which most families accept for a TV room.

The catch is what the uphill wall is made of. On a slope like this it is often poured concrete or block, holding back the hill, sometimes with a framed and drywalled finish wall a few inches in front of it. If there is a finish wall, the bracket goes into its framing and we stay clear of the concrete. If the TV wall is bare or painted masonry, we anchor into it (+$75) and run the cables on the surface in a paintable cord cover ($59), because there is no cavity to hide them in. We don’t drill deep into a wall holding back earth just to bury a cable.

For sound, the uphill wall is also the better choice: hard concrete behind the bar gives a solid, even base, and the open garden side of the room behind the seats lets the sound out rather than bouncing it back.

Our subwoofer seems to boom in the upstairs bedrooms through the heating ducts — is that possible, and what can be done?

It is possible. Forced-air ducts are long sheet-metal tubes that connect rooms on different floors, and low bass travels along them with little loss. A subwoofer placed close to a floor or wall register, or in a lower-level room whose return duct runs straight up to the bedrooms, can make the bedrooms hear more bass than the rooms in between.

The first step is placement. We move the subwoofer away from any register or return grille and try two or three spots, listening upstairs as well as in the room. The second step is level: subwoofers are very often set too high after unboxing, and turning the sub down a few steps relative to the bar, together with the bar’s night mode in the evening, solves most cases. The third step, if you want it, is a heating contractor’s job — flexible duct sections or duct liners can reduce sound travelling through the system — and we’ll tell you if we think it’s worth asking about.

What we don’t recommend is blocking a register with the subwoofer or with furniture to stop the sound. That stops the heating and cooling too.

The lower-level TV hangs on the end wall of the games room, past the pool table — will the bar get knocked by cues, and will anyone at the sofa hear it?

Both are fair worries. A pool cue on the backswing reaches well beyond the end of the table, and a bar mounted at ordinary height under a TV on the end wall can sit right in its path. We measure from the cushion to the wall before deciding the height. Where the gap is tight, the bar goes higher, tight under the TV on its own bracket, or on a TV-attached bracket so it sits within the TV’s footprint; the screen itself usually needs to go up anyway so players can see over the table.

Raising the bar helps the cue problem and hurts the sound a little, because the drivers point over the heads of seated viewers. Most bars cope with a modest height if the calibration is run from the sofa, and a bar with a good voice mode keeps speech clear. If the seating is at the far end of the room, past the table, we can place the subwoofer and any rear speakers near the sofa so the sound doesn’t have to cross the table at all.

The price is the ordinary mount ($129) plus whatever the wall is — lower-level end walls on the hill are often concrete (+$75) — and the high-mount line ($75) only if the bar ends up above about 8 feet.

Networks in large houses

Our mesh Wi-Fi has a node in the TV cabinet — can it sit right next to the soundbar and subwoofer, or will they fight each other?

They can share the cabinet, but placement inside it matters. Many soundbars link to their subwoofer and wireless rear speakers by their own radio connection, and mesh nodes are busy radios too. Packed tightly behind a cabinet door, with a cable box and a games console adding heat and their own electronics, the links can drop out when everything is busy.

We keep the node at the front or top of the cabinet with its antennas clear, at least a hand’s width from the bar’s wireless hub if it has one, and we leave the cabinet ventilated. If the house has wired network points, the bar and the node can both use them; a wired connection to the bar takes its streaming off the wireless network entirely and leaves more room for the sub and rears. Where the node can sit on a shelf outside the cabinet, that is better still.

If your network itself is the weak point — dead spots at the far end of a long house, a lower level the signal never reaches — our Staten Island network installers can run cable to the TV wall or add access points, and the soundbar visit can follow.

Apartments at the foot of the hill

We’ve just moved back into our modernised Todt Hill Houses apartment — how do we choose the TV wall now that the outlets and layout have changed?

Start with the outlets, not the furniture. NYCHA’s presentation to Todt Hill residents recorded requests for more outlets and lights in the apartments, and the modernisation includes full electrical upgrades, so the outlets in a renovated apartment may not be where they were. A TV and soundbar want one outlet close behind or below the screen; a wall without one means a long cord across the floor, and moving an outlet is an electrician’s job, not ours.

Next, light. The renovation includes new windows, and a TV facing a window will show reflections all afternoon. The best TV wall in most of these living rooms is the one the window faces, with the outlet nearest to it. Then ask management whether wall mounting is allowed on the new finishes before anything is drilled; we don’t drill a NYCHA wall without the resident confirming that. If the answer is no, or not yet, the bar goes on the TV stand for $79 and works exactly the same.

If the wall is allowed and turns out to be masonry, the mount is $129 plus $75, with the cable in a $59 cord cover so nothing is cut into the new walls. Keep the bar’s box and bracket from before the move; a missing bracket is the most common snag after a relocation.

HDMI eARC, ARC and optical: which connection your TV offers

The best connection is HDMI eARC: the TV sends the sound from every source — its own apps, the cable box, a streamer or a console — to the bar over one cable, including lossless and object-based formats, and the TV remote controls the bar’s volume through HDMI-CEC. Ordinary ARC does nearly the same job but carries compressed formats only. Optical is the fallback for older TVs: it carries sound reliably but not the newest formats, and usually needs the bar’s own remote or a learning setting for volume. We check which port is which before connecting, because on many TVs only one of the HDMI inputs supports eARC, and plugging the bar into the wrong one is the most common reason a new bar sits silent.

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation on Todt Hill

Search summaries and cost sites are useful for the basics, but they average out the whole country. Here are seven claims that turn up in AI answers and national cost guides, and how they look from a Todt Hill living room. Where a claim comes from a named site, we say so; we couldn’t capture the exact AI Overview text in our build environment, so the claims below are paraphrased as they commonly appear.

“A soundbar is plug and play — anyone can install one in fifteen minutes”

On a console in front of a TV on its feet, that is close to true, and we say so: the $79 console line exists because it is a short job. The claim stops being true the moment the bar goes on a wall, and on Todt Hill the wall is often the problem. A drill that meets a serpentine face backed by concrete, or a plaster partition with fabric behind it, is not a fifteen-minute task for anyone.

The national guides that quote short install times are describing drywall over studs, which is the common case across the country. On the hill it is common too, but not universal, and the uncommon walls are exactly the ones where a rushed hole becomes a chipped stone or a bracket that pulls out of plaster in a year.

Our price list separates the two. The labour line covers the ordinary wall; the add-on lines for stone (+$65), concrete or block (+$75), plaster (+$45) and backer board ($85) cover the walls that take longer, and you see them before the job starts.

“Bigger room? Just buy a bigger soundbar”

A bigger bar helps up to a point. A flagship bar plays louder, wider and lower than a compact one, and in a family room of ordinary size it makes a real difference. But in a great room where the seats are fifteen or twenty feet back, the limit is not the bar’s size; it is that all the sound starts from one spot under the screen and reaches you after bouncing around a large, hard room.

That is why the answer for the biggest Todt Hill rooms is usually not “bigger” but “closer”: rear speakers on stands near the seats, a subwoofer placed for the listening position, and the voice setting raised. In the very largest rooms, or rooms with two seating zones, the answer is speakers in the ceiling over the far seats, which is no longer a soundbar job at all.

We would rather tell you that before you buy than after. Send us the room’s rough dimensions and the bar you’re considering, and we’ll say plainly whether it will carry.

“Soundbar installation costs $100 to $300”

National cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish ranges in that region for a basic wall mount, and for drywall over studs our own line, $129, sits inside it. The range quietly assumes the cheapest wall and no extras. It doesn’t include a bracket, a stone or concrete wall, cable concealment, a long run to a closet, rear speakers or calibration.

On Todt Hill, where many jobs involve at least one of those, an honest total is the sum of several lines. A bar on Flagg-style stone on the Sanus bracket is $129 + $84.99 + $65 = $278.99. A great-room system with in-wall wiring, full home theater hookup and a universal remote adds up to $831.99, shown line by line in the packages below.

The useful thing about a published line-by-line list is that you can do the arithmetic yourself, and you can see which lines you could skip. A cost site’s range can’t tell you that.

“Hide the wires by running them inside the wall — it’s always possible”

In a drywall stud bay with nothing in the way, an in-wall kit is a tidy job, and we fit plenty of them on the hill for $169. But “always” is wrong. A mosaic-rubble wall has no cavity at all. A poured lower-level wall holding back the slope has no cavity either, and drilling deep channels into it to bury a cable is not something we’ll do. Some older partitions are full of blocking, old wiring or insulation that shouldn’t be disturbed.

For those walls the tidy answer is on the surface: a slim paintable cord cover ($59) painted to the wall colour, run into the corner of a reveal or down beside a trim line, or a cable run along the baseboard to a cabinet ($99 up to 25 feet). Done carefully, it’s barely visible from the sofa.

We tell you which kind of wall you have before booking, from your photos, and confirm it with a look and sometimes a test hole when we arrive.

“Ring, ADT or SimpliSafe can add the TV sound when they install your smart home”

National security brands sell alarms, cameras and doorbells, and some bundle smart-home devices. They don’t mount soundbars, and their installers aren’t sent to configure eARC or calibrate a subwoofer. If you already have one of those systems, nothing about it changes when a bar goes in; the bar sits on your Wi-Fi like any other device.

Where integration is genuinely useful — the TV and bar switching on with a scene, or a doorbell chime announced in the family room — it depends on the bar’s own app and voice assistant, not on the alarm company. We set up what the bar supports and leave the security system alone.

For households who want one company for both, AESS also installs cameras, access control and smart locks; those are separate services with their own pages and prices.

“Every room in a big house should get the same soundbar”

Some summaries recommend picking one model and repeating it in every room for simplicity. Matching makes sense where you want multi-room music from one app, because bars from the same maker group together easily. It makes less sense as a sound decision: the bar that suits a 25-foot great room is wasted, and often too big physically, under a 50-inch bedroom TV, and the compact bar that suits the bedroom will struggle downstairs.

Our advice is to choose a family of products if multi-room music matters, then choose the size within that family for each room. A house might have a flagship bar with rears in the family room, a mid-size bar in the lower level and compact bars in two bedrooms, all in one app.

Each room is priced on its own line, so mixing sizes doesn’t change the arithmetic.

“A landmarked house means the Commission must sign off on any drilling at all”

Not quite. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says a permit for interior work is needed when the work requires a Department of Buildings permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior is a designated interior landmark. Ordinary repairs and maintenance need no LPC permit. A soundbar fixed to an inside wall of an individually landmarked house typically touches none of those triggers.

Where the claim is right is on the outside. A speaker on a terrace wall, a cable through a stone wall to a garden TV or anything fixed to a landmarked facade needs checking with the Commission first. If any part of your job goes outside, we hold that part until you have the Commission’s answer.

None of this is legal advice; it is our reading of the LPC’s own published guidance, and the Commission’s staff are the people to ask in a doubtful case.

FAQ

Todt Hill Soundbar Installation FAQ

Most streets on the hill have no street lights or sidewalks — can we still book an evening window?
+

Yes. Windows run until 8 PM every day, and the technician drives to the door and carries a torch for the walk from the van. An evening visit takes the same time as a daytime one, and the room lights are all we need once inside.

Does a big house cost more per room than a small one?
+

No. Each room is priced from the same published lines whatever the size of the house, and there is no charge for the distance between rooms or for the climb up the hill. What changes the total is the wall, the extras and how many rooms you choose, all of which you see in writing first.

Can you work while our general contractor's crew is still finishing the house?
+

Yes, once the TV wall is finished and painted and the outlet behind the TV is live. We coordinate with the contractor so we are not drilling while they are sanding or painting in the same room, and we ask that the room is clear enough to set up a ladder and a blanket.

Should we put the alarm's motion sensor in the TV room on bypass during the visit?
+

It helps. A technician moving around the room, a ladder and drill dust can all trip a motion zone. Bypassing that zone, or telling your monitoring company that work is under way, avoids a false alarm. We never need your alarm code; someone in the household sets the bypass and restores it when we leave.

Our TV has ARC but not eARC — will a new Dolby Atmos bar still play Atmos from our streaming apps?
+

Often, yes. Streaming apps usually deliver Atmos inside Dolby Digital Plus, a compressed format that ordinary ARC can carry. What ARC can't carry is lossless Atmos from a Blu-ray player or some consoles; that needs eARC, or a bar with its own HDMI input that the source plugs into first. We check what your TV passes through and show you the bar's format indicator before we leave.

The wall behind our TV backs onto an indoor pool or spa room — is the humidity a problem?
+

The bar itself sits in the TV room, so what matters is that room's air, not the pool's. Where a cable has to pass through a shared wall, we seal around it so damp air doesn't travel through the hole, and we keep electronics out of the pool room itself unless they are rated for it.

Mobile signal is weak inside our stone house — will that stop the soundbar's app setup?
+

No. The bar's app sets it up over your home Wi-Fi, not the mobile network, so a phone with no bars of signal can still finish the job as long as it is on the house Wi-Fi. If you need to receive a sign-in code by text, stand near a window or the door for that step.

Will the technician need to get into our electrical panel or the basement?
+

Usually not. A soundbar plugs into an existing outlet, so the panel only matters if the outlet behind the TV turns out to be dead and we need to find which breaker feeds it. The basement matters only if a cable run is planned through its ceiling. If either applies, we say so when we quote, so someone can show the technician the way.
