The Borough

Why Queens Warehouses Need Security Camera Installation

Queens holds more of New York City’s industrial land in active use than most people realize. NYCEDC lists 21 Industrial Business Zones across the five boroughs, and seven of them are in Queens: Jamaica, JFK, Long Island City, Maspeth, Ridgewood, Steinway and Woodside. These zones are made up entirely of manufacturing-zoned land and exist to keep industrial firms in the city. The Maspeth zone alone, according to the Maspeth Industrial Business Association, is home to more than 850 industrial businesses with more than 16,000 jobs, 80 percent of them industrial: construction firms, wholesalers, transportation and warehousing companies, food and beverage makers, printers, metal fabricators, box plants, building-material suppliers, perishable-food distributors, shipping and logistics firms, and waste and recycling haulers. Almost every one of those businesses stores something worth stealing, and many of them share walls, yards and driveways with neighbors they do not control.

The borough’s second freight economy runs on airplanes. The Port Authority reports that Kennedy Airport handled 1.67 million tons of cargo in 2024, about 5 percent more than the year before, and in April 2025 a $270 million consolidated cargo handling center opened as the first step in rebuilding the airport’s north cargo area. Much of the work that follows the planes happens off the airport. The city’s own Springfield Gardens/JFK Transportation Study describes Springfield Gardens as having the highest off-airport concentration of air cargo facilities in New York City, with industrial and warehousing uses south of North Conduit Avenue and west of Rockaway Boulevard that depend on trucks, and with Rockaway Boulevard designated as a through truck route. That cluster of forwarders, customs brokers and trucking firms moves freight that is small, valuable and easy to resell, which makes it a different security problem from a bulk distribution building.

The third economy is the parcel. The NYC Comptroller’s November 2025 report on last-mile delivery, as summarized by the Queens Daily Eagle, counted 19 last-mile warehouses in Queens operated by companies such as Amazon, FedEx and UPS. The report’s appendix lists Amazon facilities at 26-15 Boody Street and 66-26 Metropolitan Avenue. In Maspeth, crashes near FedEx and Amazon facilities rose 53 percent and 48 percent respectively, and the Eagle reported that crashes increased near six of the eight newest Queens facilities after they opened. Those numbers are about traffic safety, not theft, but they describe the operating reality that a camera system has to cover: hundreds of vans and their drivers cycling through a building in a few hours, every one of them handling parcels that have to be accounted for when they come back.

Cargo crime follows the paperwork. In October 2020, ABC7 reported that six people, including former airport workers, were charged over two heists from the receiving office of an air cargo importer at JFK that together netted about $6 million in designer goods. According to the report, the suspects used forged documents listing the correct airway bill and flight details, relied on inside knowledge of how releases worked, and loaded pallets onto a tractor-trailer. Nobody had to break a door. Nationally the pattern has only grown: Verisk CargoNet recorded 2,646 confirmed cargo thefts in the United States and Canada in 2025, up 18 percent, with estimated losses near $725 million and an average theft of $273,990. For a Queens forwarder, the camera that matters most is not on the roof; it is the one that shows the face, the document and the truck at the moment freight is released.

Then there is the ground itself. The NYC Hazard Mitigation Plan records that on September 29, 2023, a flash flood dropped around eight inches of rain at the JFK Airport weather station, the most for any September day there since 1960, with 28 rescues across the city and just over 600 damage reports of flooded basements and first floors. Rebuild by Design’s analysis of 311 data found that Queens Community District 12 had 7,250 flood reports, the highest of any community board in the city. The Army Corps of Engineers notes that Howard Beach saw record flooding from Sandy’s storm surge in 2012 and has been susceptible to surge and nuisance flooding since. And in July 2006, equipment failures in Con Edison’s Long Island City network left Queens customers without reliable power for about eight days, from July 17 to July 25; an estimated 175,000 people were affected, although the utility put the number of customers far lower. A warehouse camera system in Queens that keeps its recorder on the floor beside the modem, with no battery behind it, is designed for a borough that does not exist.

Those facts set the order in which we design. First, the release points: the counter, window or dock where goods are signed out to someone else, because that is where documents can be forged and loads can go to the wrong truck. Second, the openings: dock doors, roll-up gates, man doors and the shared walls or yard gates that neighbors also use. Third, the high-value areas: cages, bonded or segregated stock, overnight staging. Fourth, the overview cameras that tie everything together. And underneath all of it, from the first sketch, the infrastructure question Queens forces on you: where the recorder, switch and internet equipment can sit so that a cloudburst or a long outage does not take the system down when it is most needed.

Same-Day Repair

Recorder or Dock Camera Down at Your Queens Warehouse?

A warehouse usually learns its recorder has failed at the worst possible moment: the morning a forwarder’s customer reports a short shipment, or the day a van comes back missing a bag, when someone goes to pull the clip and finds the screen frozen on last week. Repair calls go to the front of the queue for that reason. The faults we see most often in Queens buildings are ordinary ones: a recorder that never restarted after a power dip, a dock camera knocked out of aim by a forklift mast or a swinging trailer door, a switch in an un-air-conditioned mezzanine closet that cooked over the summer, a hard drive at the end of its life, water in a junction box on an exterior wall after a heavy storm, or remote viewing that stopped the day the internet provider replaced the router. Most single faults are found and fixed within one to two hours of arrival.

Service and repair visits are billed at our standard rate of $195 an hour with a 3-hour minimum ($585). That covers diagnosis, re-terminating and testing cable, re-aiming and refocusing cameras, recorder configuration and recovering access where you can show the system is yours; replacement cameras, drives and switches are quoted once we know what failed. If someone else installed the system, send a photo of the recorder’s label and of one camera before we come, so the right parts are on the truck and nobody waits on a second trip across the borough.

If something has already gone missing and the footage matters, do one thing first: stop anyone from restarting, resetting or unplugging the recorder. Most recorders overwrite their oldest video when the drive fills, and a well-meaning reset can wipe settings or, on some models, recent recordings. Tell us the date and time window you need and we will start by exporting that clip, then fix whatever failed. If water has reached the recorder, unplug it at the outlet only if you can do so without standing in water, and do not power it back on until it has been opened and dried; a drive that is spun up wet is far harder to recover. Where a camera stopped recording before the incident, we say so plainly rather than promise footage that does not exist, and we check every other camera that might have caught the same minute from another angle.

What We Install

Warehouse Security Camera Services in Queens

Every service below starts from the same three questions: where does freight arrive, where is it released to someone else, and who else has a key to the building, the yard or the wall next door. The answers decide the cameras.

Air cargo release and receiving

For forwarders, brokers and handlers in Springfield Gardens, Jamaica and South Ozone Park: a face-height camera at the release window or desk, a document camera over the counter where paperwork is checked, and a camera on each truck door that shows the vehicle, its markings and, where the bay allows, its plate.

Last-mile station coverage

Van lanes, sort lines, cage and bag staging, the returns table, driver check-in and the exit door. Laid out so a supervisor can follow one route bag from the sort line to the van and back again, which is the question a parcel station asks every single day.

Multi-tenant industrial buildings

Older one- and two-story buildings in Maspeth, Ridgewood, Glendale, Woodside and Long Island City, often split between several tenants. Cameras on your doors, your share of the yard and any wall you share with a vacant unit, with clear lines on what belongs to you and what belongs to the landlord.

Wholesale and cash-and-carry

Warehouses in College Point, Flushing and Jamaica that also sell from a counter or will-call window. A camera over the cash point and the counter, a second on the pickup door, and a view of the aisle that leads to both, so a disputed drawer or a disputed pickup can be settled from the recording.

Food and beverage distribution

Coolers, freezers, dry storage and receiving doors for distributors and producers in the industrial zones. Cameras chosen for cold, condensation and wash-down, aimed to see deliveries counted in and orders loaded out, and kept away from restrooms and changing areas as state law requires.

Contractor and equipment yards

Fenced lots for builders, roofers and equipment renters, common along the industrial edges of Maspeth, Long Island City and southeast Queens. Pole or wall-mounted weather-rated cameras, a camera on the gate that sees every vehicle, and lighting checks so night footage is usable.

Flood- and outage-ready head-ends

The recorder, switch, modem and battery backup raised off the floor, on a shelf or wall bracket above the level water has reached in that building, with a backup sized to keep recording through ordinary outages and an off-site copy of key clips where the owner wants one.

Old DVR replacement and takeovers

Systems installed years ago that nobody can log into, that record at low resolution or that are out of disk. We document what is there, keep cable that tests good, and replace the recorder and cameras that cannot do the job.

Warehouse Camera Installation for Queens’ Buildings and Districts

Queens storage comes in a handful of building types, each tied to a part of the borough. The type decides where a camera can hang, how cable reaches it and whose permission the work needs.

Forwarder buildings near JFK

One-story warehouses with a few dock doors, an office up front and a release window between them, clustered in Springfield Gardens, Rosedale and along Rockaway Boulevard and the Conduit. Many are leased on shorter terms, with landlords who limit drilling into block walls, and many run nights and weekends around flight schedules.

Maspeth and Ridgewood industrial blocks

Low brick and block buildings, some subdivided, some with open yards, in the Maspeth and Ridgewood Industrial Business Zones. Heavy truck traffic, shared driveways and neighbors on three sides. Cable usually runs exposed along bar joists or block walls rather than above a drop ceiling.

Long Island City and Steinway loft-industrial

Multi-story concrete industrial buildings, some still holding manufacturers and distributors, others partly converted to studios and offices. Concrete ceilings mean surface conduit or raceway, freight elevators may be shared, and a building manager often wants to approve the cable route.

Last-mile stations

Large single-floor buildings and converted industrial sites in Maspeth, Woodside, Middle Village and near the airport, used to sort parcels and dispatch vans. High ceilings, many doors, constant motion and a shift pattern that peaks before dawn and again in the evening.

College Point and Flushing logistics

The College Point Corporate Park and the warehouses and factories around it, described by the city’s small-business agency as part of the area’s commercial mix, plus newer purpose-built space such as the College Point Logistics Center reported in 2023. Modern ceilings and slab, but large yards and long cable runs.

Low-lying southern Queens

Buildings in Howard Beach, the Rockaways and southeast Queens where surge or stormwater has reached the ground floor. The design starts with where water has been, and moves the recorder, network equipment and battery above it before a single camera is placed.

The airport ring and the western zones: two different first cameras

A forwarder in Springfield Gardens and a distributor in Maspeth may buy similar equipment, but their first cameras are different. The forwarder’s biggest risk is a confident stranger with good paperwork, so the first cameras go on the release window, the document counter and the truck door, set up so one export shows all three for the same minute. The Maspeth distributor’s biggest risks are usually after-hours entry through a gate, a roll-up door or a shared wall, and loads that leave with more than the order, so the first cameras go on the yard gate, each roll-up door and the loading lane. Both end up with overview cameras and a clean recorder. What changes is the order of work and the questions we ask on the walkthrough: for the forwarder, how releases are authorized; for the distributor, who else has access to the yard and the building.

What a Queens landlord usually asks for before the first visit

Most Queens industrial landlords are less formal than a Manhattan managing agent, but they still have rules. Expect to be asked for a certificate of insurance naming the owner, a description of where holes will be drilled and cable will run, assurance that nothing will be fixed to the roof or the outside of the building without approval, and sometimes a promise that cameras will be removed and holes patched at the end of the lease. In shared buildings, the landlord may also want to know whether any camera will see common areas or another tenant’s door. We ask these questions on the first call and put the answers in the written proposal, so the work is not stopped at the gate on installation day.

Questions Answered

Queens Warehouse Cameras: Questions Answered

These are questions Queens warehouse owners, forwarders and station managers put to us on walkthroughs. They are specific to how freight moves in this borough, so the answers are too.

Our JFK-area forwarder keeps the paperwork desk and the truck bays in different rooms. How do the cameras tie a release to the truck that loaded?

With time, naming and grouping rather than with one heroic camera. Put a face-height camera behind the release desk so it sees the driver across the counter, and a second camera looking down at the counter surface itself, close enough to show that a document was presented and stamped, even if it cannot read every line. Then put a camera on each truck door that shows the back of the trailer, the shipment going in and the side of the cab. On the recorder, those cameras go into one group named for the release process, so a supervisor who searches a release time pulls the desk, the counter and every bay for that minute in a single export.

The link between the rooms is the clock. Every camera records against the recorder’s time, and that time has to be correct to the second, synchronized to an internet time source and checked on every service visit. When your release log says a shipment was handed over at 22:14, the desk camera at 22:14 shows the person and the bay cameras from 22:14 onward show the truck. If the clock drifts by ten minutes, the evidence is still there but it takes an hour to find and invites argument. We also write the release log’s time zone and format into the handover notes, so nobody has to reconcile a 12-hour log with a 24-hour recorder in the middle of an investigation.

The 2020 JFK heists reported by ABC7 turned on forged documents carrying the correct airway bill and flight details. Cameras cannot judge whether a document is genuine; your release procedure does that. What cameras can do is make sure that, if a bad release happens, you have a clear face, a clear document handover and a clear truck, all tied together, before anyone leaves the building. That combination is what investigators and insurers ask for first.

Delivery vans come back to our Maspeth station after dark with parcels that were not delivered. How do cameras help account for what came back?

By covering the return path as carefully as the dispatch path. Most stations put their best cameras on the morning load-out and treat the evening return as an afterthought, yet the return is where undelivered parcels are counted back in, handed across a table and placed into a cage or a re-delivery area, often by tired people in a hurry. Map that path on the walkthrough: where vans pull in, where drivers carry returns, the table or scanner where they are checked in, and the cage where they wait. Each of those points gets a camera close enough to see hands and parcels, not just people walking past.

The returns table deserves the closest camera in the building. Mount it above and slightly behind the person checking parcels in, so it sees the parcel, the scanner and the driver’s hands at once. A second camera at the van lane entrance records each van arriving, ideally showing its number on the side or rear. When a route comes back short, the supervisor searches the van’s arrival time, follows the driver to the table, and sees exactly which parcels were handed over. If the van arrived with fewer parcels than the scan sheet says, the lane camera shows that too.

Lighting changes after dark, and the return lane is often where it changes most: the bay door is open to a dark street and vans arrive with headlights on. We set these cameras up for that mix, using wide dynamic range so a bright doorway does not black out the person in front of it, and we test them during an actual evening return before handover, not at noon. The Comptroller’s figures on van traffic around Maspeth stations describe how busy those lanes are; a camera aimed for a quiet afternoon will not hold up to the evening rush.

Our crew speaks several languages across three shifts. How do we make the camera system usable for every shift lead?

Make it simple enough that language barely matters. Census QuickFacts reports that 55.4 percent of Queens residents aged five and over speak a language other than English at home, and a warehouse crew in this borough often reflects that. A camera system with cameras named “CH07” and a twenty-step export process will only ever be used by the one person who set it up. We name cameras with short, plain place names — “Dock 3,” “Gate,” “Release desk,” “Cooler door” — and pair each name with a number painted or labeled near the camera itself, so a shift lead can match the screen to the building by pointing.

Most commercial recorders and their phone apps can display their menus in several languages, set per user. Where that is available, each shift lead’s account can be set to the language they read best, without changing what anyone else sees. We also leave a one-page, picture-based guide by the recorder: a screenshot of the playback screen with arrows to the three buttons that matter (pick the camera, pick the time, export), and a photo of where the USB port is. A guide made mostly of pictures works for everyone, including the person who joins next month.

The handover matters more than the paperwork. We train at least one lead from each shift, on site, on their own phones, and have each of them export a real clip before we leave. That way the night shift is not waiting for the day manager to come in before footage can be pulled, and nobody is learning the system for the first time during an incident.

Our building sits inside the Maspeth Industrial Business Zone. Does that change anything about cameras?

Not the rules, but it can change the help available. An Industrial Business Zone is an economic development designation: NYCEDC describes the 21 zones as areas made up entirely of manufacturing-zoned land, and the city uses them to support industrial firms with services and programs, such as a relocation tax credit for firms that move into one. Being in an IBZ does not create a separate rule for security cameras. The same city and state rules apply as anywhere else: low-voltage camera cabling falls under the city’s exception for signaling and data wiring under 50 volts in Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, employers may not record in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms under Labor Law §203-c, and the installer should hold the state license for security system work.

What an IBZ does bring is a local business organization that knows the area. In Maspeth that is the Maspeth Industrial Business Association; in Long Island City, the Long Island City Partnership serves as the Industrial Business Service Provider for zip codes including 11101 and 11106. These groups track issues that affect member businesses, such as truck routes, street conditions and local crime concerns, and they can be a useful source of context when you are deciding what to protect first. We do not represent any of them, but we ask clients in these zones what they have heard locally, because neighbors often know which blocks have had problems.

The zone also tells us something about your neighbors, and that does shape a camera plan. In an IBZ you are surrounded by other industrial users with trucks, yards and early starts, which means busy shared streets, more vehicles parked overnight and fewer residential neighbors to notice something odd at 3 a.m. That usually argues for a gate camera that sees every vehicle, good lighting on the yard, and alerts on the doors after hours.

Our JFK-area crew starts at 2 a.m. to meet overnight flight arrivals. How should cameras be set up for a staffed night shift?

Treat the night shift as the main shift, not as after-hours. Many systems are tuned in daylight, set to send alerts all night and checked by a manager who works days, which means a building full of people at 3 a.m. triggers a stream of meaningless alerts and the cameras that matter are exposed for an empty room. For a forwarder or handler working around overnight arrivals, we set the recorder’s schedules around your real hours: full-quality recording and no door alerts while the crew is in, alerts on the doors and the yard only in the gaps when nobody should be there.

Lighting is the bigger question. Under warehouse lighting at night, a camera can stay in color with a clean picture; out in a dark truck court it switches to infrared and loses color, which matters when you need to describe a vehicle or a jacket. We look at every exterior camera at night during the walkthrough. Where the yard, the dock apron or the release door is dark, a modest amount of added light, or cameras designed to keep color in low light, gives you usable footage of the trucks and drivers that come and go during the shift. We also check that the light does not shine straight into a camera across the yard.

Finally, put the people who are there at night in charge of what happens at night. A night lead who can open the app, find the release desk and export a clip means a short shipment discovered at 4 a.m. can be documented before the truck has left the area, rather than at 9 a.m. when the day manager arrives. We train that person on site, during their shift, before we sign off.

Our Maspeth distributor’s racking runs nearly to the ceiling with stock on every level. Where do cameras go so the racks don’t block them?

Down the aisles, not across them. Full-height pallet racking turns an open floor into a set of corridors, and a camera mounted on an outside wall looking across the racks sees only the end of the first row. The cameras that work in a racked building are mounted at the end of each aisle or pair of aisles, high enough to clear forklift masts and the top load beam, and aimed straight down the aisle with a lens chosen for its length. They see every pick face on both sides and every forklift that enters.

The cross-aisles and the ends of the racks need their own coverage, because that is where pallets are staged, wrapped and moved toward the docks. A camera over each main cross-aisle, plus cameras on the dock doors, completes the path from rack to truck. Top-level stock is the hardest to see; if high-value goods are stored up high, we either put a camera on the opposite side of the aisle angled upward, or suggest moving those goods to a lower, better-covered location, which is often cheaper than chasing them with cameras.

Racking moves. Distributors in Maspeth and Long Island City change layouts as customers and seasons change, and a new rack run can blind a camera overnight. We leave a little slack and a spare labeled cable at the end of each aisle run, document every camera’s view on handover, and re-aim cameras as part of a service visit when the layout changes.

Our station’s busiest hours are the pre-dawn sort and the evening return. Can the system record differently at those times?

Yes, and it should. Most commercial recorders let each camera follow a schedule, and a parcel station has an unusually clear one: a heavy sort before dawn, van load-out, a quiet midday, the evening return, and a near-empty building overnight. During the sort and the return we record the sort line, returns table, cages and van lanes continuously at full quality and a higher frame rate, so hands and parcels are clear when someone has to follow a single bag. In the quiet hours, the same cameras can record on motion or at a lower frame rate, which stretches the recorder’s retention without losing anything that matters.

Alerts follow the same schedule. Door and yard alerts during the sort would be useless noise, because people and vans are everywhere; overnight, a door opening is exactly what a manager wants to know about. We set the alert windows to the station’s actual shift times, and adjust them if the schedule changes for peak season. The Comptroller’s report on last-mile stations describes how heavily they are used at peak times, and that is when the recording quality has to be highest.

Scheduling only works if the recorder’s clock is right and the schedules are written down. We record the schedule in the handover notes, so a new station manager can see why the cameras behave differently at 4 a.m. and at noon, and change them without guessing.

Our metal building shakes when trucks pass on Rockaway Boulevard. Will that blur the cameras?

It can, if the cameras are mounted to the wrong thing. Light-gauge wall panels and long, unbraced brackets amplify vibration, and a camera zoomed in tight on a distant door will show that movement as a shake or a smear, especially at night when exposures are longer. The fix is mostly about mounting: fix cameras to structural steel or to a braced point rather than to a thin panel, keep brackets short, and avoid hanging a heavy camera at the end of a long arm.

Lens choice helps too. A camera set to a moderate field of view is far less sensitive to small movements than one zoomed all the way in, so for a long view we often use two cameras with moderate lenses rather than one at maximum zoom. Many commercial cameras also offer electronic stabilization; it helps with light vibration but cannot rescue a camera bolted to a panel that flexes every time a truck goes by. Infrared at night adds another issue: if the mount lets the camera move against its housing or a nearby surface, the IR light reflects and flickers, so we check night images on site before we leave.

Rockaway Boulevard is one of the designated through truck routes in the city’s Springfield Gardens/JFK study, so buildings along it live with steady heavy traffic. We look at existing camera footage, if there is any, during the walkthrough; if it already shakes, the mounting plan for the new system starts there.

Our College Point wholesaler takes cash at a will-call window. Can the cameras settle a drawer that comes up short?

They can show what happened at the drawer, which is usually enough to settle it. The camera needs to be above and slightly behind the cashier, so it sees the cash drawer, the counter surface and the customer’s hands, and close enough that bills and change are visible as objects. A camera across the room that shows the cashier’s back is useless for this. We usually add a second camera facing the customer side of the window, at face height, so each transaction has both the money and the person.

The recorder’s clock matters here as much as anywhere. When your point-of-sale system shows a transaction at 14:32:10, the drawer camera at 14:32:10 should show that sale. Some point-of-sale systems can send transaction text to the recorder so it appears over the video; that is useful in busy wholesale counters but depends on the specific systems involved, so we check compatibility before promising it. Without it, an accurate clock and a camera that clearly sees the drawer still let a manager walk through a shift sale by sale.

Keep the cash camera’s purpose narrow. It should see the counter and drawer, not a back office where staff change or take breaks, and access to its footage should be limited to the managers who reconcile the drawer. A short written note to staff explaining that the counter is recorded, and why, prevents most of the friction a cash camera can cause.

Pallets wait overnight on our Springfield Gardens floor between the airline handler and the morning truck. How do we prove what was on the floor at closing?

Record a deliberate closing view. Choose a camera position, or two, that sees the whole overnight staging area from high up, with each pallet position marked on the floor. At closing, the last person out walks the area so the camera records every pallet, and the recorder can be set to take a high-resolution snapshot of that view at a fixed time each night. The next morning’s opening view is compared with it. If a pallet is missing or has been broken down, the recordings in between show when.

Staging cameras should be complemented by door cameras, because a pallet that leaves overnight leaves through a door. Every dock door and man door that opens onto the staging area needs a camera that sees anyone using it, and the recorder should be set to raise an alert when those doors move outside working hours. That gives you a real-time warning as well as a record. Motion search on the staging camera lets a supervisor find any activity in the area overnight in a few minutes instead of scrolling through eight hours.

Air cargo customers sometimes ask how you protect freight between the handler and the truck. A floor plan showing the staging camera, the door cameras and the alert schedule, plus a sample closing snapshot, is a clear way to answer them.

We run two Queens warehouses a few blocks apart, one in Maspeth and one in Ridgewood. Should each have its own recorder?

Yes, in almost every case. Each building should record locally, so that losing the internet link between them never stops either building from recording. Two recorders of the same brand can then be viewed together in one app, with both buildings’ cameras on one screen, and managers can be given access to one building or both. That arrangement is simpler and more resilient than trying to pull video from one building to a recorder in the other.

Linking two buildings physically, with fiber or a wireless bridge across the street, only makes sense when you want them to act as one network for other reasons, such as shared phones or a shared inventory system, and when you have rights to cross the space between them. Even then, we would still keep a recorder in each building. The app does the joining; the recorders do the safeguarding.

Use the same camera naming scheme in both buildings, with a short building prefix (“MAS Dock 2,” “RID Gate”), so nobody confuses the two sites in the middle of a search. And keep the two systems on the same firmware and the same retention, so managers can expect the same thing at either address.

The unit next to ours in a Glendale building has been empty for months. Should cameras watch the shared wall?

Watch the space on your side of it. In subdivided industrial buildings, a vacant neighbor is a quiet weakness: someone with access to the empty unit can work on a shared wall, a shared mezzanine or a shared roof hatch without being seen from the street. A camera on your side that views the length of the shared wall, ideally low enough to see the base of the wall where block or drywall is easiest to open, records any attempt and lets an alert fire if motion appears there after hours.

Check the other shared features too. Many subdivided buildings have one roof hatch, one fire door between units, a shared electric room or a mezzanine that runs over more than one tenant. Each of those is a route into your space from the vacant one, and each deserves either a camera or a contact on the door tied to the recorder’s alerts. Ask the landlord how the vacant unit is secured and who has keys to it; it is a reasonable question, and the answer often tells you how much to worry.

Do not point cameras into the vacant unit, even through a gap or a window. It is someone else’s space. Cover your own side well, light it, and set the alerts. If the unit is re-let, adjust the masks and alerts so the new neighbor’s ordinary work does not trigger them.

Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Warehouse Cameras in Queens

Ask an AI assistant how to protect a warehouse and you will get a generic plan for a suburban distribution center. Here is where that advice goes wrong in Queens.

“Thieves break in at night, so focus on perimeter cameras and alarms.”

For air cargo near JFK, the biggest losses often happen in daylight at the release window. The 2020 heists ABC7 reported involved forged documents with the correct airway bill and flight details, and freight that was loaded onto a truck rather than carried through a broken door. Perimeter cameras matter, but a forwarder’s first cameras belong at the release desk, the document counter and the truck door, tied together by time.

“Put the recorder in the server closet next to the modem.”

In much of Queens, that closet is on the ground floor or in the cellar, and the modem is where the provider’s line came in, near the floor. After the September 2023 storm and Ida in 2021, plenty of Queens businesses found water in exactly those rooms. The recorder, switch, modem and battery should sit above the highest water the building has seen, with a margin. Moving the modem up a wall costs very little; replacing a drowned recorder and losing the footage on it costs a great deal.

“Wireless cameras avoid the cost of cabling.”

Block walls, steel racking full of stock, metal buildings and busy radio environments near an airport all work against Wi-Fi. A wireless camera that drops its signal every time a trailer backs into a bay is a camera with gaps in its recording. Wired cameras powered over the network cable record continuously and survive outages on the recorder’s battery. We use wireless links only where cable genuinely cannot go, such as a bridge to a detached building, and design them as point-to-point links with clear line of sight.

“Use facial recognition to flag known thieves.”

Facial recognition raises legal and practical problems for a warehouse and is rarely the tool that solves its losses. New York City requires commercial establishments such as retailers, food and drink businesses and entertainment venues that collect customers’ biometric identifier information to post a clear sign at customer entrances, and it bars selling that information. A warehouse with a will-call counter open to the public should take that seriously. In practice, a clear face-height image at the counter and an accurate clock solve most warehouse cases without any biometric matching.

“Cameras in the break room and locker area will stop staff theft.”

New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording employees in a restroom, locker room or room designated for changing clothes unless a court authorizes it, and such recordings cannot be used for any purpose. Food and beverage distributors in particular often have changing areas for uniforms and gear. Cover the doors of those rooms from outside, and cover the stock, the docks and the release points, which is where goods actually go missing.

“One 4K camera on the gate will read every plate.”

A wide overview camera on a busy Queens truck gate sees the whole scene but rarely reads a moving plate, especially at night with headlights pointed at it. Plate reading needs its own camera, aimed at a narrow lane where vehicles slow or stop, at a shallow angle, with a fast shutter. The overview camera and the plate camera do different jobs, and a gate that matters needs both.

“More cameras means more security.”

Forty cameras that nobody watches, named “Camera 1” through “Camera 40,” with a recorder that nobody on the night shift can operate, are less useful than twelve well-placed cameras with plain names and a lead on every shift trained to export a clip. In a borough where crews often work in several languages, simplicity is a security feature. We size systems to the release points, openings and high-value areas the building actually has.

“A camera system needs a monthly cloud subscription.”

It does not. Commercial systems record to a recorder in your building and can be viewed remotely without a monthly fee. An off-site copy of selected clips can be valuable for buildings that have flooded or that store high-value freight, and it can be set up with storage you control. What you should avoid is a system where the footage exists only in someone else’s cloud and disappears when the subscription lapses.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Decide Where the Recorder Lives Before Anything Else

This is a note on method rather than a story about a particular client. Most camera proposals start with the cameras: how many, which model, where each one points. In Queens we have learned to start with a less exciting question — where the recorder, switch, modem and battery will live — because that decision is the one most likely to make the whole system useless at the worst possible moment.

A recorder is the only place the footage exists unless you have made a copy. If it sits on the floor of a ground-floor office in a building that took water in a cloudburst, the cameras on the walls will survive the storm and the evidence will not. If it sits in a mezzanine closet with no ventilation above a dock, the summer heat will shorten the life of its drive. If it is plugged into the same outlet as a compressor, it will reboot every time the compressor starts. None of these problems shows up on the day of installation; all of them show up later. The 2006 Long Island City outage, which lasted about eight days, and the September 2023 storm that flooded basements and first floors across the borough are reminders of how long and how badly things can go wrong.

So the head-end location is settled on the walkthrough, before we draw a single camera. We look for a wall or cabinet above any known water line, a clean outlet on a circuit that does not feed motors, enough air to keep the equipment cool, and a lock. We size the battery to ride through ordinary outages and to shut the recorder down cleanly in a long one. Where the building has flooded before or holds high-value freight, we set up an off-site copy of the clips that matter. It costs very little next to the cameras, and it is the difference between a system that looked good on the quote and one that still has the footage the morning after.

FAQ

Queens Warehouse Security Camera FAQ

Our Long Island City building has poured-concrete ceilings and no drop ceiling. How does camera cable get run?
+

In surface raceway or conduit fixed to the concrete, along routes agreed with the building owner before work starts. We follow beams and wall edges so the runs are neat and short, use anchors sized for concrete, and bring several cameras’ cables together in one larger raceway where they share a path. It takes longer than pulling cable above tiles, which is reflected in the quote, but the result is tidy and easy to service.

Remote viewing stopped the week the landlord switched internet providers. Is the system broken?
+

Probably not. A new provider usually means a new router with different settings, and the recorder loses its path to the internet even though it is still recording. Check that the recorder shows live cameras on its own monitor; if it does, the footage is safe. Reconnecting it to the new router, and checking its network settings and the phone app, is normally a short service visit.

We bought a building in Jamaica that came with someone else’s camera system. Who controls the footage and the passwords now?
+

Ask the seller for the recorder’s administrator password and app account at closing, along with any manuals. If they cannot provide them, the recorder can usually be reset to factory settings with proof that you own the building, which clears the old accounts and, on many models, the stored footage. We document what is there, test the cameras and cable, set new owner passwords that only you hold, and tell you plainly which parts are worth keeping.

New LED high-bay lights went in and now the camera picture rolls with dark bands. Can that be fixed?
+

Usually, yes. Many LED fixtures flicker faster than the eye can see, and a camera’s shutter can pick that up as rolling bands. Changing the camera’s anti-flicker setting to match the local 60 Hz power, or adjusting its shutter speed, often cures it. Where a particular fixture flickers badly, the lighting contractor may need to look at its driver. We test each affected camera after the change.

Can the recorder keep a copy of key footage away from the building in case of flood or fire?
+

Yes. Selected cameras, or clips flagged by motion or alerts, can be copied automatically to storage outside the building, such as a second recorder at another site or cloud storage you control. Copying everything continuously needs a lot of upload bandwidth, so most Queens clients copy the dock, gate and release cameras only. That way a total loss of the building does not mean a total loss of evidence.

An airline or shipper near JFK wants to see its freight being handled in our building. Can we send clips without giving them access to the system?
+

Yes. Export the cameras and time window that show their shipment — arrival at the dock, staging, and loading onto the outbound truck — as a clip or a short set of clips, and send it as a file or a time-limited download link. Nobody outside your company needs a login to your recorder. If a customer wants regular proof of handling, we can set up a camera group for their freight area so the export takes a few minutes, and keep their view separate from everyone else’s.

A forklift keeps knocking the camera we mounted low beside a dock door. Where should it go?
+

Higher, and out of the swing of masts, trailer doors and pallets. We typically mount dock cameras above the door header or on the wall well to one side, with a lens chosen so the view still reaches the trailer bed. Where a camera has to stay low, a steel guard or a recessed mount protects it. Check that any new mounting point is clear of the door’s track and springs.

What should we tell our staff about the cameras?
+

Tell them plainly where the cameras are and why, that no cameras are in restrooms, locker rooms or changing rooms, and who is allowed to view the footage. A short written policy, translated where your crew needs it, prevents most misunderstandings. Cameras that staff know about deter theft from outside and inside the company, and they protect workers too when a dispute or injury needs to be reviewed.

Our recorder sits in a hot mezzanine closet above the docks. Is that a problem?
+

It can be. Recorders and switches have an operating temperature range printed on their spec sheets, and a closed closet under a summer roof can exceed it, which shortens drive life and causes freezes and reboots. We check the closet temperature on the walkthrough, add ventilation or move the equipment to a cooler wall, and choose a recorder rated for the conditions where moving it is not possible.

Can a camera see inside a 53-foot trailer while it is being loaded at our door?
+

Partly. A camera mounted above and slightly to one side of the door, looking into the trailer, can see the front portion of the load and every pallet as it goes in, which is usually what matters. It will not see the nose of a fully loaded trailer. For high-value loads, recording each pallet crossing the dock plate, with the trailer number visible, gives a clear record of what was loaded and when.

Do we need our own electrician for a warehouse camera installation?
+

Usually not for the cameras. Network cameras are powered over their data cable from the switch, and the city’s Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 sets out an exception from electrical permits for signaling and data wiring under 50 volts done by qualified people. What the head-end does need is a suitable outlet. If there is none where the recorder should go, or if you want a dedicated circuit, that part is work for a licensed electrician, which we can coordinate.

Can the system count trucks or show how long they sit at our doors?
+

Many commercial cameras and recorders can count objects crossing a line or report how long something stays in a zone, and that can give a useful picture of door activity. The counts are estimates, not a substitute for your yard or dock management records, and they depend on good camera placement and lighting. We can enable these features where the equipment supports them and show you how reliable they are in your building.
