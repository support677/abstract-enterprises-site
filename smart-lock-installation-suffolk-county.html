The Neighborhood

Why Suffolk County Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Matched to Town and Shoreline

Suffolk is not one market; it is ten towns strung along eighty-six miles of island. The state lists them as Babylon, Brookhaven, East Hampton, Huntington, Islip, Riverhead — the county seat — Shelter Island, Smithtown, Southampton and Southold, and inside them sit dozens of incorporated villages with codes of their own. Each town writes its own rental-permit law, runs its own fire marshal's office and draws its own flood-zone maps from the federal ones. A keypad deadbolt is the same product in Huntington Station and in Hampton Bays; what the town expects of the door it goes on, and what the weather will do to it, are not. That is why our Suffolk walkthroughs start with an address and a question about the town before anybody looks at a lock.

Most of the doors we see belong to owners, not tenants. Census Bureau QuickFacts put Suffolk's owner-occupied housing rate at a little over 82 percent across roughly 584,000 housing units. Owners make different decisions from renters: they can drill, rewire and replace the slab, and they are the ones who answer to the town when a rental permit comes up for renewal or an accessory apartment is licensed. A growing share of those owners are also landlords of a sort — renting a basement or an upstairs unit, or a whole second house — and the moment a stranger lives behind one of their doors, the lock on it stops being a matter of taste. The model property maintenance code that New York builds on asks that a deadbolt on a rented unit open from the inside without a key, special knowledge or effort, and that it throw at least an inch. Those are small requirements, and they rule out more consumer locks than people expect.

Then there is the water. Suffolk has more of it than land — Wikipedia's county figures, drawn from the Census Bureau, give 912 square miles of land and 1,461 of water — and the South Shore bays, the barrier beaches and the two forks put a large number of homes within reach of a storm surge. When Newsday totalled FEMA's damage assessments in January 2013, Superstorm Sandy had flooded, damaged or destroyed 20,798 structures in Suffolk, more than 8,000 of them by more than half their value. Thousands of South Shore houses were later raised onto new foundations, and in places like Lindenhurst the Long Island Advocate still describes empty lots where state buyouts removed homes altogether. A raised house changes the job completely: the front door moves to the top of a stair, the old ground floor becomes an enclosure that federal flood rules limit to parking, storage and access, and anything electronic left down there is the first thing the next surge reaches.

Behind the shoreline is a county of backyards. In-ground pools are ordinary from Commack to Manorville, and New York's residential pool code treats the house wall as part of the pool barrier when a door opens straight onto the pool area, with a listed alarm required on that door. Older ranches and colonials still have steel bulkhead doors over the cellar stairs. And east of the Long Island Expressway's end, plenty of houses are reached by roads that turn to sand — or, on Fire Island, by no road at all.

Suffolk is also where Long Island goes to work. The Long Island Innovation Park at Hauppauge, which its business association describes as the second-largest industrial park in the nation, holds about 1,350 companies and 55,000 employees. Route 110 in Melville, the Ronkonkoma station area, Riverhead's Route 58 and the village main streets from Bay Shore to Port Jefferson add offices, practices, shops and flex bays. Commercial doors here answer to the fire marshal as much as to the owner: in the Town of Islip, for example, any building with a fire protection system must keep a Knox-Box key vault in service, and the keys in it have to be swapped whenever the locks change.

Methods and materials we check on Suffolk doors

The stair-top door on a raised house. When a house is lifted, the front door is often replaced and re-hung on a new landing, sometimes ten feet or more above grade. New doors are usually steel or fibreglass slabs that arrive pre-bored for a standard deadbolt, which suits most smart locks. What changes is exposure: the landing catches wind-driven rain that the old porch roof used to stop. We favour locks whose makers publish an outdoor temperature and water rating, mount the keypad side under whatever overhang the landing has, and check that the door's weatherstripping isn't so tight that a motorised bolt has to fight it.

The enclosure door below. FEMA's technical bulletins describe the National Flood Insurance Program's rule that enclosures under elevated buildings may be used only for vehicle parking, building access and storage, and its guidance on utility systems expects electrical components below the required elevation to be designed to resist flood loads or moved up. We treat the door into that space as a door that will one day be underwater. It gets a sturdy mechanical lock or a battery-free push-button keypad — nothing that needs power, nothing that will be ruined by salt water, and nothing the flood-plain administrator would read as a sign the space is being lived in.

Sliders onto decks and pools. Sliding patio doors are the weak point of many Suffolk ranches, and very few true smart locks are made for them. Where the slider opens onto a pool area and the house wall forms part of the pool barrier, New York's pool code expects a listed door alarm on it. We pair an auxiliary foot bolt or a keyed slider lock with a door sensor that reports open and closed to the same app as the other locks, and we leave the code-required alarm doing its own job.

Bulkheads and cellar stairs. A steel bulkhead locks from inside with a slide bar. Bilco, which makes many of them, sells a keyed lock kit that lets the door be locked and unlocked from outside while the slide bar still works from inside. A smart lock rarely belongs on the bulkhead leaf itself; it belongs on the door at the bottom of the stairs, if there is one, and only where the inside can still be opened without a key.

Hollow-metal frames on industrial doors. Flex-bay man doors usually hang in steel frames, often filled with grout. Fitting a strike or running a cable through them is slower than in a wood frame and is planned in advance, and on a fire-rated assembly any cutting has to stay within what the door's listing allows. Where that is in doubt we fit a lock that works through the door's existing preparation instead.

Compliance callouts for Suffolk business doors

  • The Knox-Box comes first. Islip's Fire Prevention Bureau says the New York State Fire Code and the Town Code let the fire marshal require a Knox-Box wherever access is restricted, and that every building with a fire protection system must have one in service. When the locks change, the keys inside must be changed by appointment with the fire marshal. Other towns run their own programs; we check which one covers your building before the old cylinders come out.
  • A way out that needs nothing. Any door on an exit route opens from inside with one motion, no key, code, card or phone. Keypads and readers go on the outside; electrified locks get a push-to-exit button or sensor inside, and the release does not depend on the network.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure, decided per door. When power drops, an electric strike or magnetic lock either releases or stays locked. Which is right depends on the door's place in the exit route and on fire-alarm interface, so it is specified door by door, never by default.
  • Fire-rated assemblies. Stairwell and corridor doors in offices and plants are often labelled fire doors. Locks and strikes on them must suit the listing, and field cutting beyond what the label allows is avoided.
  • Accessible hardware. Where a business door must be accessible, operable parts should work without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist and sit within the reach range the 2010 ADA Standards set out, which is why lever trims and large keypads are our default on public doors.
  • Who did the work. Suffolk's Office of Consumer Affairs licenses more than 15,000 businesses, and says over half of the licences it issues each year are home improvement licences. Commercial-only work and residential work are treated differently; ask any installer which licences their job needs, and check them on the county's search page.

Smart Lock Installation in Suffolk County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Suffolk owners, landlords, practice managers and building operators ask before a smart lock goes on a door, grouped by the situation behind them: town rental rules and second units, flood zones and barrier beaches, backyards and cellars, and commercial doors. Price, scheduling and door-type questions are in the FAQ near the end of the page. Where a law, code or program is mentioned we name it so you can read it yourself; none of this is legal advice.

Town rental rules and second units

Our Islip rental permit is up for renewal — will the town look at the lock on the tenant's door?

Not directly, going by the town's own paperwork. The Town of Islip's rental occupancy permit application for one- and two-family dwellings asks the owner to swear to a short list of things: that there are no outstanding violations on the property, that the unit won't be used as a transient rental, that smoke alarms are installed outside and inside every sleeping area and on every storey including basements, and that carbon monoxide alarms are in place where the code requires them. Door hardware isn't on that list. A permit can also be suspended if any federal, state or town violation is issued later, which is the part that should make an owner think about the lock.

The standard the lock has to meet comes from the property maintenance code. The model version New York's code is built on says that where a door into a rented dwelling unit has a deadbolt, the deadbolt must open from the side people leave by without keys, special knowledge or effort, must throw at least an inch, must be installed to the maker's instructions and must be kept in working order — and it says a sliding bolt doesn't count. A keypad deadbolt with a plain thumb-turn inside and a full one-inch bolt meets that comfortably. A double-cylinder deadbolt, a lock that needs a code to leave, or a cheap retrofit whose bolt throws short does not.

So before a renewal we check three things on every rented door: the inside turn, the bolt throw, and the strike, because a bolt that only half-enters a loose strike is a lock in name only. Then we set the codes up so the tenant has their own, the owner can retire a former tenant's code the day a lease ends, and the tenant keeps a working key. If you rent in Babylon, Brookhaven, Huntington or another town, its permit form will differ in detail; send it to us with your request and we will read it with you.

We are licensing an accessory apartment in Brookhaven — how should its entrance be locked compared with ours?

As a separate home with its own front door, because that is what it is. The Town of Brookhaven's code ties an accessory apartment to owner occupancy — the owner has to live in the house, in either the main unit or the apartment — and requires a provisional accessory apartment licence before anyone moves in. That means two households under one roof, each entitled to a door that the other can't simply open, and an owner who is both neighbour and landlord.

In practice we give the apartment's entrance its own lock with its own administrator account, held by the tenant, rather than adding it as one more door in the owner's app. The owner keeps a mechanical key for the access the lease allows, kept somewhere sensible rather than on a hook by the kitchen door. If the apartment and the main house share an interior door — a basement stair, a door off a shared laundry — that door is locked from both sides with ordinary keyed hardware or a privacy set, never with a code only one household knows, and it must still open from the apartment side if it is part of the tenant's way out.

Two details save arguments later. Keep the tenant's entrance on a separate Wi-Fi network or at least a separate app account, so a change to the owner's router doesn't knock the tenant's lock offline. And put the arrangement in the lease: which doors the tenant controls, how the owner gets in for repairs, and what happens to the tenant's codes on move-out. The lock then enforces an agreement both sides have already signed.

Flood zones, barrier beaches and islands

Our Lindenhurst house was lifted after Sandy — can the door into the space under it have a smart lock?

It can have a lock; it shouldn't have electronics that depend on staying dry. FEMA's technical bulletins describe the flood-insurance rule that an enclosure below an elevated building may be used only for parking vehicles, getting into the building and storage, with flood openings so water can pass through rather than push the walls in. FEMA's guidance on building utility systems expects electrical components below the required elevation to be designed to resist flood loads or be moved above it. A powered smart lock and its bridge, sitting at grade on the South Shore, are exactly the kind of thing that guidance is about.

Our usual answer is a strong mechanical deadbolt or a battery-free push-button keypad on the enclosure door, with the house's smart locks on the doors at the top of the stair. If you want to know when the enclosure door opens, a wireless door contact can report to the same app; it is inexpensive, and if salt water ruins it the loss is small. The hub, bridge or router that the upstairs locks rely on goes upstairs too, on an interior wall above the flood line.

It is also worth checking your elevation certificate and any flood-plain conditions attached to the work permit before adding anything to the enclosure, because finishing or equipping that space as if it were living area can affect both compliance and the flood insurance premium. We stay out of that decision and fit hardware that keeps the enclosure looking and working like what it is supposed to be: parking, storage and a way in.

Can a smart lock be installed at a Fire Island house that is reached only by ferry or water taxi?

Yes, but it is planned as a one-trip job, because a forgotten part is a lost day. Driving on Fire Island is by federal permit only. The National Park Service's permit FAQs say contractor permits authorise driving only to communities where the holder has work, that summer residents and renters can't get permits because ferry service is considered adequate in season, and that in winter people reach their houses by ferry, water taxi or private boat. For most car-free communities, that means the tools and the lock go over the way the owner does, and nothing is driven to the door.

So the walkthrough is done from photographs and a call: the door's thickness, backset and condition, the strike, the cottage's Wi-Fi or lack of it, and how exposed the door is to wind-blown sand and spray. We bring the lock, a second set of fixings, a spare strike, batteries and the tools to re-drill if the old preparation is rotten, and we take the old lock away or leave it with you as a spare. Locks with published outdoor ratings and a mechanical key are the default, because a cottage door on a boardwalk sees more salt than almost any door on the mainland.

Plan the codes for the way the house is actually used: an owner's code, codes for family and guests with end dates, and one for whoever checks the house out of season. If the cottage has no reliable internet, choose a lock that can create time-limited codes without a connection, and keep a key with a neighbour or caretaker in the community, because getting a locksmith across the bay after the last ferry isn't realistic.

Backyards, pools and cellars

Our back slider opens straight onto the pool deck — does a smart lock on it take care of the pool alarm rule?

No. They do different jobs, and the pool rule is about the alarm. The New York State Department of State's summary of the residential pool requirements says that where a wall of the house is part of the pool barrier, every door with direct access to the pool through that wall needs an alarm listed to UL 2017, with a touch pad or switch that silences it for a single opening for no more than 15 seconds; where the house sits wholly inside the pool enclosure, every door with direct access needs one. Other approved means, such as self-closing and self-latching devices, are allowed only if they protect at least as well.

A smart lock can't stand in for that alarm. It secures the door against someone outside; the alarm warns that a child inside has opened it. We fit the two together: a slider lock or a hinged-door smart lock that reports locked and unlocked, plus the listed door alarm mounted where the code wants it and where adults can reach the pass-through switch but a small child can't. If the pool door is a hinged French door, the smart lock's auto-lock can be set so the door relocks soon after it closes, which helps, but it is still not the alarm.

The pool gate has its own rules in the same summary: self-closing, self-latching, opening outward away from the pool for a pedestrian gate, with the latch handle inside the enclosure and at least 40 inches above grade, and a lock to keep it shut when the pool is not supervised. A keypad lock can be fitted to many gates, but only one that doesn't defeat the self-latching hardware; the town inspects the barrier, not the app. Pool installers handle the barrier itself; we fit the lock and the alarm and coordinate with them.

Our cellar has an outside bulkhead door — can it go keyless, and what if someone sleeps down there?

The bulkhead itself is a poor place for a smart lock, and if anyone sleeps in the basement it is also part of their way out. Steel bulkheads normally lock from inside with a slide bar. Bilco sells a keyed lock kit for its basement doors that adds an outside key while keeping the slide bar for locking from inside, and that is usually the right level of hardware for the bulkhead leaf. Motorised locks struggle with doors that move as much as a bulkhead does with heat, frost and settling, and the leaf is exposed to rain and snow lying on it.

If there is a door at the foot of the bulkhead stairs into the basement, that door is the better candidate for a keypad, since it sits sheltered under the steel. Whatever goes on it must open from the basement side with a single turn and no key. The model residential code requires a basement, and every sleeping room, to have an emergency escape and rescue opening that works from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge, and many Suffolk basements rely on that stair for it.

So our usual plan is a keyed kit on the bulkhead, a keypad deadbolt with a plain thumb-turn on the inner door if there is one, and a door contact on the bulkhead so the app tells you when it opens. If the basement is, or will be, a bedroom or an accessory apartment, the town's building department decides what the escape opening must be; we make sure the lock never gets in its way.

If Dad falls at home and can't reach the door, how does the ambulance crew get in?

Plan it before it happens, because a responder at a locked smart door has the same options as at any locked door: someone opens it, or they force it. There is no master code that fire or EMS crews carry for consumer smart locks. What a smart lock does offer is a way for a family member to open the door remotely, from anywhere, the moment they know help is at the step — which only works if the lock is connected, the family member has the app and the notifications reach them.

The most reliable set-up we fit for an older parent living alone is layered. The front door gets a connected smart lock with a doorbell camera beside it, so the person who gets the call can see the crew and unlock. A neighbour or nearby relative holds a code of their own. And a mechanical key sits in a key safe or with that neighbour, because a phone, an app and a home network are three things that can fail on a bad night. Ask your local fire district or ambulance company whether it offers or recommends a residential key-box arrangement, and follow its advice on where to mount one.

Two settings matter. Turn on notifications for the door being unlocked or opened, sent to two family members rather than one. And make sure the inside of the door opens with a single, easy turn, so your parent can let people in on a good day and nobody is held up by the lock on a bad one.

Industrial parks, practices and congregations

We are moving our Hauppauge building to keyless entry — what happens to the keys in our Knox-Box?

They have to change too, and you can't do it yourself. The Town of Islip's Fire Prevention Bureau explains that property owners are not given keys to their own Knox-Box: the fire marshal's office schedules a visit to lock the keys inside, keeps a master list of every box and its key inventory, and only the marshal's office and the local responding fire department can open it. Its bulletin is explicit that whenever you change your locks, you must call the fire marshal to swap the keys, or responders may have to force entry and cause damage. Generally, keys to all spaces in the building go in the box.

Going keyless doesn't mean going keyless for the fire department. Every door we convert keeps a mechanical override — a key cylinder on the lock or a keyed bypass on the strike — and the new keys are what go into the box. Where the system uses cards or fobs, a credential can go in too if the marshal asks for it, but a key that works with the power off is the one that matters. We plan the cylinder change, hand you a labelled key set for the marshal's visit, and keep the old keys working until the box has been updated.

Hauppauge straddles Islip and Smithtown, and the rest of Suffolk's industrial streets fall under other towns and fire districts with their own procedures. We check which office covers your address before the old cylinders come out, so the building is never left with a box full of keys that no longer fit.

Our flex unit has a roll-up bay door and a steel man door — which one gets the keypad?

The man door, almost always. It is the door staff use to arrive, leave and step out for a break, it is usually the unit's required exit, and it hangs in a steel frame that takes a commercial keypad lever or a strike and reader well. A coded lever that relocks automatically means the last person out can't leave it on the latch, and each employee's code shows up in the log.

The roll-up door is a different machine. A motorised bay door is opened by its operator, and controlling it means working with the operator's own inputs and safety devices, which is a job for the door company. A manual roll-up is secured with its own slide locks or padlock hasps from inside. What we can add is a door contact on the bay door, so the same app that logs the man door also reports when the bay goes up after hours. That gives an owner one timeline for both openings without touching the operator.

If the unit has an office front with an aluminium storefront door as well, that door gets its own keypad or reader and its own schedule, often a later unlock than the shop floor. And whatever you choose, the new keys go into the Knox-Box, as described above.

Does a keypad with an audit trail on our records room help a Suffolk dental practice with HIPAA?

It helps you do what the rule asks, but it is not compliance on its own. The HIPAA Security Rule's physical safeguards, at 45 CFR 164.310, include a facility access controls standard: limit physical access to electronic information systems and the facilities that house them, while making sure authorised access is allowed. Its implementation specifications cover a facility security plan, procedures to control and validate a person's access based on role, and maintenance records documenting repairs and changes to security-related parts of the facility, which the regulation itself illustrates with hardware, walls, doors and locks.

A keypad or card lock with individual codes and a log fits that well. Each staff member gets a credential tied to their role, the server or records room opens only for the people who need it, and when someone leaves you delete one code instead of re-keying the room — the example HHS's own guidance gives of a physical change worth documenting. The lock's log, kept with your maintenance records, shows who had access and when it changed.

What it doesn't do is write your policies, train your staff or cover the technical safeguards on the computers themselves. A practice is better off treating the lock as one line in its security plan. We supply a written description of each door, the hardware and the credential list for that plan, and we never need access to patient information to do the work.

Can a Suffolk church, synagogue or mosque use the state's security grant toward keyless doors?

Possibly; check with the program before you count on it. New York's Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes program, administered by the Division of Criminal Justice Services, gives grants to nonprofit organisations at risk of hate crimes or attacks because of their ideology, beliefs or mission. The Governor's application page says grants can fund interior or exterior security improvements, such as alarms, panic buttons, fences, shatter-resistant glass and public address systems, among other items. Door locks and access control aren't named on that page, so confirm with DCJS whether they are eligible in the round you apply to.

If they are, the doors that matter most for a congregation are usually the ones used outside service times: the office entrance, the school or hall door, the kitchen and delivery door. Coded or card access there lets clergy, staff and volunteers in on a schedule, while the main doors stay on ordinary keyed hardware with panic exit devices for crowds. Every exit still opens from inside with one push.

For an application, we can provide a written scope and itemised quote for each door, product data sheets, and a description of how exits stay free, which is the kind of documentation grant reviewers tend to ask for. We don't prepare applications or advise on eligibility; the program office does.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Suffolk County

A note on method: the tools used to research this page could not open Google's AI Overview, so the seven headings below are not quotations from it. They are claims that recur in product listings, cost-guide sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and how-to articles, which answer engines tend to repeat. After each one is what we find on Suffolk doors.

“A smart lock will fit any exterior door”

Product pages list the door thicknesses and backsets a lock fits, and most ordinary front doors qualify. Fitting the door's preparation is not the same as suiting the door.

In Suffolk, plenty of exterior doors are poor homes for electronics: the enclosure door under a raised South Shore house, a steel bulkhead over cellar stairs, a slider onto a pool deck, a cottage door facing the ocean on Fire Island. Each has a better answer, and it is usually mechanical.

We sort the doors before choosing locks: which ones get a smart lock, which get a battery-free keypad, which get a keyed kit and a sensor, and which should be left alone.

“Add a keypad and the rental is ready”

Landlord blogs present keyless entry as the finishing touch on a rental. Suffolk's towns see it differently. Islip's permit form, for instance, is about smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms, open violations and a ban on transient rentals.

A keypad doesn't touch any of those, and the wrong keypad can create a problem: a lock that needs a code to get out, or a bolt that throws short of the inch the model maintenance code calls for, is a violation in waiting.

Get the permit basics right first, then fit a lock that opens freely from inside and lets you retire a former tenant's code the day they leave.

“A smart lock makes the pool door safe for children”

Child-safety roundups sometimes list smart locks and auto-lock among pool precautions. New York's residential pool code asks for something else on a house door that opens onto the pool: a UL 2017-listed alarm, or an approved alternative that protects at least as well.

The difference is direction. A lock keeps people out; the alarm tells the adults that someone inside has opened the door. A toddler who can work a thumb-turn will open a smart-locked door from inside as easily as any other.

We fit both and explain which does what, and we leave the barrier and gate to the pool contractor and the town inspector.

“Go keyless and you can throw the keys away”

Commercial access-control marketing promises an end to key management. The fire marshal disagrees. Islip's Knox-Box bulletin requires keys to all spaces in the box and an appointment to change them whenever the locks change.

A card or a phone credential is useless to a crew at a dark building with the power off, which is exactly when they need to get in.

Every Suffolk business door we convert keeps a mechanical override, and the new keys go to the marshal's office before the old ones stop working.

“An audit log makes the practice HIPAA compliant”

Some access-control sellers describe their logs as a HIPAA feature. The Security Rule's facility access controls do ask for access to be limited and changes to doors and locks to be documented, and a log helps with both.

But the rule is a set of policies and procedures across a whole practice, from workstations to backups. No lock satisfies it on its own, and a log nobody reviews protects nothing.

We set up role-based codes, give you the door and credential records for your security plan, and leave compliance decisions to your privacy officer.

“A keypad on the door secures the whole unit”

Small-business guides picture a single front door. A Suffolk flex unit has two openings, and the bigger one is the roll-up bay.

A keypad on the man door does nothing about a bay door left up after a delivery, or a manual roll-up whose slide locks were never thrown.

We put the keypad on the man door, a contact on the bay door, and both on one timeline, so the owner sees every opening.

“Emergency crews can get through any smart lock”

It is widely assumed that police, fire and EMS have some way past consumer smart locks. There is no master code they carry for them.

A crew at a locked house door has the options it has always had: someone opens it, a key is found, or the door is forced. For an older person living alone, that is a reason to plan, not to avoid smart locks.

Remote unlock by a family member, a camera at the door, a neighbour's code and a key in safe keeping together give responders a quick way in without anyone breaking the door.

Shoreline Doors

A Flood-Line Checklist for Smart Locks on Raised and Bay-Front Houses

On the South Shore and the barrier beaches, the most important measurement on a lock job isn't the backset; it is the height above the flood line. This is what we go through before recommending hardware for a house that has been raised or sits near the water.

  • Which doors are above the flood line? Doors at the top of the stair get the smart locks. Doors into the enclosure below get mechanical or battery-free hardware, because the National Flood Insurance Program limits that space to parking, access and storage, and FEMA expects anything electrical down there to resist flooding or be moved up.
  • Where does the hub live? Bridges, hubs and routers go upstairs on an inside wall, never on a shelf in the enclosure where they would be the first thing lost.
  • Is the stair-top door exposed? A new landing catches wind-driven rain. We pick locks with published outdoor ratings, mount keypads under whatever cover exists, and check the weatherstripping doesn't fight the bolt.
  • Does the house still have a key? After a storm, power and internet can be out for days. A mechanical key that works without either goes to every household member and one trusted neighbour.
  • Is anything telling you about water? A flood sensor in the enclosure and a contact on its door report to the same app as the locks, so you know the space has flooded, or been opened, before you get home.
  • What happens before a storm? Check the batteries, make sure everyone who might need to get in has a key, and photograph the enclosure. Electronics can't be protected from a surge; they can only be kept above it.

Owners who raised their houses with state or federal help may have conditions attached to the work. Read them, or ask the program, before adding equipment below the elevated floor.

Rented Doors

Running Smart Locks on a Suffolk Rental Between Tenants

A lock on a rented door changes hands with every lease. Whether it is a basement unit in Babylon, an accessory apartment in Brookhaven or a whole house in Islip, the same routine keeps the door secure, keeps the tenant in control of their own home and keeps the owner on the right side of the town's paperwork.

  • Before a new tenant arrives: delete every code, fob and phone credential left from the last lease, including any temporary codes given to movers, cleaners or contractors. Change the mechanical cylinder or re-key it if keys were not all returned, because a smart lock with an old key still working is only half changed.
  • On move-in day: the tenant sets their own code in front of the owner or our technician, receives at least two mechanical keys, and, where the unit has its own lock account, becomes its administrator. The owner keeps the access the lease allows and nothing more.
  • Check the hardware, not just the codes: the inside thumb-turn should open with one easy motion, the bolt should throw its full inch into a tight strike, and the batteries should be new. These are the same checks we make before a rental permit renewal.
  • Write it down: keep a one-page record of the lock model, the date codes were cleared, who holds keys and how the owner gets in for repairs. It answers most disputes before they start and is useful if a town inspector or insurer asks.
  • At move-out: remove the tenant's credentials the day the keys come back, not at the start of the next lease, and check the door for damage while the unit is empty.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Suffolk Doors

The order our installers work through a Suffolk property — a routine we follow everywhere in the county, not the story of a single visit

We start outside, at the back, because that is where Suffolk doors get complicated. Is there a pool, and does a door open straight onto it? Is there a bulkhead, and does anybody sleep in the basement? Is the house raised, and what is behind the door under it? Those answers rule some doors out before we have looked at a single lock.

Then the doors that people actually use. On a ranch it is usually the side door from the driveway; on a raised house, the door at the top of the stair; in a plant, the man door by the time clock. We open each from inside with the lights off and check the bolt goes fully home into its strike.

Then the paperwork. If a door is rented, we ask for the permit or licence and read what it covers. If it is a business, we look for the Knox-Box and ask when the fire marshal last updated it.

Last, the people: who holds the administrator account, who gets a code, who keeps a key, and who gets the alert when a door opens at 3 a.m. A smart lock is only as good as that list.

Suffolk County Smart Lock Installation FAQ

What does a keypad and electric strike on a Suffolk business door cost?
+

A single-door keypad or fob reader with an electric strike is $1,800 to $3,000 installed at our Suffolk County rate, which is our published Brooklyn base multiplied by 1.20. A new electric strike on an entrance door is $300 to $540, a failed strike repair is $354 to $582, and a visit to change codes or schedules without new hardware is $250 for the first hour. Smart locks on house, rental and cottage doors are quoted flat after a free walkthrough.

We are building a new house in Suffolk — at what stage should the smart locks go in?
+

After the exterior doors are hung, trimmed and weatherstripped, and once the house can be secured, but before you move furniture in. By then the doors have settled in their frames, the builder's construction cylinders can be retired, and the Wi-Fi the locks will use is usually working. If you want a doorbell camera or door contacts as well, tell the builder early so a power supply or cable can be left at the door.

Can a smart lock go on a fibreglass or insulated steel entry door?
+

Yes. Most fibreglass and steel entry doors sold for houses arrive bored for a standard deadbolt, which is what most smart deadbolts need. We check the door's thickness and backset against the lock, and we use the maker's reinforcement or a backing plate where the skin is thin, so the lock body is held firmly rather than by the outer skin alone. We avoid cutting new holes in a door's foam core unless the maker allows it.

Can the narrow aluminium door on our strip-centre shop take a keypad?
+

Usually, with the right hardware. Narrow-stile aluminium storefront doors use a slim deadlock in the stile rather than a household deadbolt, so they need a keypad or reader made for that preparation, or a reader on the wall with an electric strike in the frame. We confirm the stile width, the existing lock and the landlord's rules for the storefront before quoting, and the door always opens from inside without a key.

Our front door opens outward — can it take a smart deadbolt?
+

In most cases. A deadbolt works the same way whichever way the door swings, but on an out-swinging door the hinges are often on the outside and the keypad side of the lock faces the weather more directly. We check that the hinge pins can't simply be lifted out, add security hinge studs where needed, and choose a lock whose maker publishes an outdoor rating for the exposed side.

Our house is on the bay and the salt air pits everything outside — which smart lock will hold up?
+

Choose a lock the maker rates for exterior doors and read what its warranty says about coastal or salt-air exposure before you buy. Finishes that already resist corrosion on your other hardware are a sensible guide, and a keyed override matters more on the water, where a corroded keypad or a flat battery should never leave you locked out. We favour models with sealed keypads, mount any bridge or hub indoors, and suggest rinsing and checking the outside escutcheon each spring.

Can a sliding patio door be locked and checked from an app?
+

Partly. Very few genuine smart locks are made for sliders, so we usually fit a strong keyed slider lock or a foot bolt to do the locking, and a door sensor that reports open and closed to the same app as your other locks. You can then see from your phone whether the slider is shut, and set an alert if it opens late at night, without relying on a lock that wasn't designed for that door.

Can the owner see which employee unlocked the shop each morning?
+

Yes, if each employee has their own code, fob or phone credential. The lock or system records each unlock against the person's name, and the owner can view that history in the app or have it sent as a notification. Shared codes defeat the purpose, so we set up one credential per person and show the owner how to delete it when someone leaves.

What should we do with the smart locks before a coastal storm?
+

Check that the batteries are fresh, make sure everyone who may need to get in has a mechanical key, and charge the phones that run the app. If you have a hub or bridge, it should already be above the flood line; don't move electronics into an enclosure or garage below it. After the storm, test each lock by hand before relying on the keypad, because wind-driven rain and salt can affect keypads even on doors above the water.

How do you protect our floors and door finish while you work?
+

We put down drop cloths at each door, tape the door face around the lock while drilling or re-fitting, and vacuum the shavings before we leave. Old locks are removed carefully so the paint around them isn't torn, and if old screw holes or bore holes are left exposed we tell you before we start and fill what can be filled.
