The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Properties Need Alarm Installation

The Brooklyn Navy Yard sits on Wallabout Bay in northwest Brooklyn, bounded by Navy Street on the west, Flushing Avenue on the south, Kent Avenue on the east and the East River on the north. It was a U.S. Navy shipyard from 1801 until the Navy decommissioned it on June 30, 1966; the city bought it, and since 1981 the nonprofit Brooklyn Navy Yard Development Corporation has run it as the city’s real estate developer and property manager. BNYDC describes a 300-acre campus with more than 550 businesses employing more than 13,000 people. The National Register historic district covers 225.15 acres, which is the figure Wikipedia gives; we quote both rather than pick one.

Almost nobody lives here, so the alarm questions are commercial ones. The tenants BNYDC lists run from food and beverage, fashion and visual design to manufacturing, transportation and clean energy, and its Industry Development Council names medtech, robotics, wearables, life science and sustainable design-build as the sectors it wants to grow. In practice that means a woodshop next to a medical-device startup, a brewery under a design studio, a film stage down the road from a dry dock. What they have in common is expensive tools, valuable work in progress, and spaces that sit empty overnight and over weekends in buildings full of other people’s businesses.

The campus itself is closed. BNYDC’s 2026 Tenant Manual says access is for tenants, their employees and registered visitors; every employee needs a BNYDC-issued ID card; visitors come in on a single-day QR code; and the Security Department handles IDs, parking permits and after-hours emergencies. The same manual says otherwise in plain words: tenants install their own door locks, BNYDC does not keep or duplicate keys to leased spaces, and tenants are responsible for the security within their leased space. A guard at the Sands Street gate is not watching your second-floor door in Building 3 at 2 a.m., and has no key to it if he were.

The thefts that do get reported show what people go after. In February 2024 Brooklyn Paper’s 88th Precinct blotter described sixteen spools of Cat 5E cable, about $20,000 worth, taken from an equipment-rental company’s locked vehicle at Steiner Studios; the company identified the man on security cameras and police made an arrest. In January 2025 Williamsburg365 reported two December arrests at the shipyard on the Yard’s waterfront, one for about $10,000 of copper wiring cut from a crane and one after a security guard saw a man cutting copper wiring at about 8 a.m. Materials, cable, metal and equipment are the targets, and the people taking them are already inside the fence or come in with it.

Then there is the water. Brooklyn Paper, citing the Navy Yard’s own resilience strategy, reported in 2025 that Sandy put up to six feet of water across the campus in October 2012 and caused about $100 million of damage; the Yard flooded again in Hurricane Ida in 2021, and Brooklyn Paper’s coverage of the September 2023 storms showed water inside a Yard business. For an alarm that matters in one simple way: the panel, the battery and the communicator of a ground-floor unit should not live at floor level.

Put together, an alarm at the Navy Yard is a different job from a house or a storefront. BNYDC has the fence, the gates, the roads and the common areas. You have your unit, your door and whatever is inside. A good alarm plan draws that line clearly, stays on your side of it, and makes sure that when something happens on your side, the call goes to someone who can open your door. When you also want recordings, our security cameras for Navy Yard units can be tied to the same alarm events.

Repairs

Alarm Faulting or Chirping in a Navy Yard Unit? Same-Day Repair on Weekdays

A false alarm in a multi-tenant building costs more than it does in a house: the station calls, nobody answers, and the next time the siren goes the people next door assume it is nothing. The faults we trace in industrial units are predictable — a roll-up door contact knocked out of line by a pallet jack, a motion detector that now sees a new heater or a fan, a backup battery past its life, a communicator that lost its signal when racks were moved in front of it. What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Every system below uses the same parts — a control panel, door and window contacts, motion and glass-break detectors, a siren, keypads or fobs, and a communicator to a monitoring station or your phones. At the Yard the order of planning is different: we start from your lease, your door, your hours and the people who can respond, and only then choose hardware.

Tenant-unit intrusion alarms

Contacts on your own doors, motion and glass-break inside your unit, a panel and keypad in your office, and nothing installed in the common corridor, lobby or dock.

Roll-up and loading doors

Heavy-duty overhead-door contacts that survive forklifts and pallet jacks, plus an alert when the door is left open after the last person leaves.

High-value areas inside a shop

Tool cribs, material cages, server closets and finished-goods rooms on their own alarm area, so they stay armed while the floor is in use.

Studios and small units

Compact wireless systems for a single studio on a shared floor: one door, a motion detector, a glass-break and alerts to your phone.

Counters and food-hall stands

Hold-up buttons under a counter, a stockroom zone and opening and closing reports for businesses that take payment on site.

Monitoring and call lists

Central station monitoring with a call order you choose — your staff first, BNYDC Security where your agreement with them allows, police when a second detector confirms.

Approval paperwork

A device list, a marked-up floor plan and a description of every fixing point and cable route, in the form your Property Manager asks for.

Takeovers and moves

Existing panels from a previous tenant tested and reprogrammed or replaced, and systems moved when you take a bigger space elsewhere in the Yard.

Inside the Fence

The Fence Is BNYDC’s. The Door Is Yours.

Most alarm pages are about a house or a shop with its own street door. At the Navy Yard nearly every job sits inside three layers that belong to someone else — the campus fence and gates, the building and its corridors, and then your lease and the rules that come with it. We are not BNYDC’s contractor and we do not speak for it. What follows is how we plan a tenant alarm around the rules BNYDC publishes in its 2026 Tenant Manual, which itself says that the lease controls wherever the two disagree. Read your lease first; we work to whatever it requires.

What the landlord’s security does, and what it does not

BNYDC’s Security Department runs the gates, issues ID cards and parking permits, manages the visitor system and handles after-hours and weekend emergencies on the campus. That is real security and it is the reason the Yard does not see the street crime a commercial strip does. But the manual is explicit that tenants put in their own locks, that BNYDC does not hold keys to leased spaces, and that tenants are responsible for security within the space they lease. Its section on events even repeats the point: BNYDC does not automatically provide security for things happening inside your unit.

So we design as if nobody else will notice a forced door on your side of the line, because in most buildings nobody will until morning. The alarm is the only thing that knows your door opened at 1:40 a.m.; the plan decides who learns about it, how fast, and whether that person can get onto the Yard and into your space.

Approval before installation

The manual says that, generally, plans of any proposed alteration must be submitted to and approved by BNYDC before work starts, that licensees may not make any alteration without approval, and that some minor non-structural work — painting is the example it gives — may be treated as a “Non-Material Alteration” under a lease. It tells tenants to check with Property Management before assuming any small job is exempt. Electrical work is stricter: plans must be submitted and approved, and the work must be done by a licensed electrician. BNYDC also has separate Tenant Alteration Guidelines, which it gives tenants through Leasing or Property Management; that document is not public and we have not seen it, so we do not guess at its contents.

In practice we prepare an approval package for every Yard job, even when the system is wireless and plugs into an existing outlet: a floor plan of your unit with each device marked, the make and model of each part, how each one is fixed and to what, the cable route if there is one, where the panel and transformer go, and confirmation that nothing is installed outside your demised space. If the panel needs a new dedicated circuit, that part goes to a licensed electrician under BNYDC’s electrical rules. Your Property Manager decides whether the alarm counts as material; our job is to make the answer quick.

Getting a crew through the gate and up to your floor

Every person on the Yard needs either a BNYDC ID or a registered visit. For a one-day install you register our technicians in the visitor system and each of us receives a QR code to scan at the gate. The manual says recurring visitors and consultants on long-term projects should not be registered that way; they should get temporary ID cards from BNYDC Security. A two-day install is usually fine as two visits; a phased job across several weeks is where a temporary ID makes sense, and you arrange it.

Then there is the elevator. In buildings with dedicated freight cars, the manual says freight service shuts down from 8 p.m. to 5 a.m. on weekdays and does not run at all on Saturdays, Sundays or federal holidays. After-hours freight can be booked two business days ahead for a fee: $50 an hour per car between 8 and 11 p.m., and a four-hour minimum, $200, between 11 p.m. and 5 a.m. or at any time on a weekend. If you want us to work when your floor is empty, we plan the tool load around those hours and tell you in advance whether a booking is needed.

Gate hours matter for the response plan as well as the install. BNYDC’s Directions + Map page, updated in September 2026, lists Sands Street and Clinton Avenue as open around the clock, Cumberland Street from 5 a.m. to 11 p.m. on weekdays and 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. on weekends, and Clymer Street with shorter hours. The June 2026 Tenant Manual gives different hours and lists the Clinton Avenue gate as closed for critical repairs from May 2026. Gate hours clearly change; whoever is on your call list should know which gate will be open at night before they need it.

The signal path out of your unit

An alarm is only as good as its route to the outside world. The Yard has four telecom providers with infrastructure on campus, according to the manual — Brooklyn Fiber, Crown Castle, Spectrum and Verizon — and access to a building’s telecom closets is coordinated through your Property Manager. If your unit already has a business internet line, the alarm can report over it, but that path depends on your router, your provider and the building’s closet staying up.

We prefer a cellular communicator as the primary path and the internet line as a backup, and we test the signal before we choose where the panel goes. That test matters more here than almost anywhere in Brooklyn. Wikipedia’s account of Building 77 says its lowest eleven stories were built in 1941 with walls about 25 inches thick and no windows, likely for storing ammunition; windows were only added in the 2017 renovation. Building 3 is an eleven-story reinforced-concrete storehouse from 1917. Thick concrete, steel and deep floor plates can weaken a cellular signal badly in the middle of a floor. Where it is weak we move the communicator toward a window or an outside wall, use a remote antenna inside your unit, or make the wired internet path primary — and we never mount anything on the roof, which the manual says tenants may not use except in emergencies unless their lease includes roof rights.

Who gets the call

The manual tells tenants to contact BNYDC Security for after-hours or weekend emergencies, and it asks every company to name people in its ID Portal for roles such as emergency contact and “Fire Safety & Security,” which it defines as the local fire-alarm contact for FDNY response. An intrusion alarm needs its own call list on top of that. We build it with you in order: the person who locks up, a second key-holder who lives close enough to come in, then — only if you have agreed it with BNYDC — a call to its security desk to look at your door, and police dispatch when two detectors confirm or a glass-break follows a door. The station gets written instructions and a gate to give responders.

The siren is part of the same plan. New York City’s Administrative Code §24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop on its own within fifteen minutes. In a building full of other businesses a siren mostly tells an intruder he has been noticed; it rarely brings help on its own. We set the cutoff inside the legal limit, place interior sounders inside your unit where they will be heard on the floor, and put the weight of the plan on the monitoring call rather than on noise.

People, codes and the day someone leaves

BNYDC asks tenants to revoke a departing employee’s Yard ID immediately, through its ID Portal or by emailing Security, and it notes that the change takes effect at once. The alarm needs the same discipline. Every person gets their own user code or fob, the panel logs who armed and disarmed, and the code is deleted the same day their Yard ID is turned off. For companies with a lot of turnover we set the system up so that a manager can delete a user from a phone app without calling us, and we put “delete alarm code” on the same offboarding list as “revoke Yard ID.”

Building Specialists

Alarm Systems for the Brooklyn Navy Yard’s Architecture

The Yard’s buildings were put up over more than a century for a navy, then refitted one by one for private tenants, so two units a few hundred yards apart can need very different alarms. These are the building types we plan for, with the facts we used about each. We name buildings to describe their construction, not because any business in them is a customer of ours.

Building 77

Sixteen stories and about 952,000 square feet, built in 1941; its lower eleven floors had 25-inch walls and no windows until the 2017 renovation. Thick walls mean a signal test before the communicator is placed, and long corridors mean doors are the main protection.

Building 3

An eleven-story reinforced-concrete supply storehouse finished in 1917, with a loading platform around its base and a rooftop farm above. Concrete floors and columns suit wired zones; big window bays suit glass-break detectors.

Building 128

A one-story L-shaped machine and erecting shop from about 1900, steel, masonry and glass under a high gable-monitor roof, now the Green Manufacturing Center and Newlab. High roofs need detectors chosen for mounting height, not standard ceiling units.

Dock 72

A 675,000-square-foot office building that topped out in 2017 at the northeast corner, beside the ferry stop. Modern glass and drop ceilings make for a conventional office alarm, inside a building whose lobby and floors are not yours.

Older brick warehouses

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century brick buildings with large multi-pane windows, heavy doors and freight access. Window glass and ground-floor openings carry the risk; basements and ground floors also carry flood history.

New manufacturing buildings

Recent construction such as 399 Sands Street on the former Admiral’s Row, finished in 2020, and the newer multi-tenant floors such as Building 303. Clean ceilings and pathways make wired systems easy, if the approval allows it.

Thick walls and deep floors

In Building 77, Building 3 and the older concrete buildings we test cellular strength at the planned panel spot before anything is fixed. Where it is poor we use a remote antenna inside the unit, move the panel to the outside wall, or make the internet path primary and cellular the backup. Wireless sensors get the same test from their mounting points, because concrete floors and steel racking cut range sharply; a repeater halfway or a short wired zone fixes most dead spots.

High roofs and open floors

Standard motion detectors are designed for ceilings of roughly eight to twelve feet. On a double-height shop floor we use detectors rated for the actual mounting height, curtain detectors along a wall of windows, or photoelectric beams across an aisle, and we aim them away from heaters, fans, steam lines and doors that let birds in. On a busy floor the alarm’s job overnight is the perimeter and the high-value rooms; we do not try to cover every square foot of an empty hall.

Doors that take a beating

Roll-up doors get wide-gap, armored overhead-door contacts mounted where a pallet or a forklift mast will not hit them. Heavy steel corridor doors get recessed contacts where the frame allows or surface contacts on a steel bracket where it does not. Fire exits keep their panic hardware; the alarm watches them and never locks them.

Ground floors near the water

On ground-floor units we put the panel, battery and communicator in the office at desk height or higher, never on the floor, and route cable high along the wall. The manual tells tenants not to store anything in common hallways and the resilience work at the Yard is about lifting equipment; the same thinking applies to a small alarm cabinet.

Fixings you can undo

Leased space goes back to the landlord one day. We fix devices to your own walls, partitions and ceiling grid inside your unit, keep a list of every fixing point and cable route, and leave the drawing with you so the space can be made good when the lease ends.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Systems in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Most Navy Yard units are businesses whose stock is not cash in a drawer but tools, materials and work that took weeks to make. BNYDC lists its tenants across food and beverage, fashion and visual design, manufacturing, transportation and clean energy, and its 2025 Industry Development Council names medtech, robotics, wearables, beauty and personal care, life science and sustainable design-build. Each of those has its own alarm pattern, though the rules of the Yard are the same for all of them.

Fabrication and maker shops

CNC machines, saws, hand tools and sheet stock. Protection goes on the doors and the tool crib; detection on the floor is set for the hours the shop is empty, with a separate code for the person who opens at 6 a.m.

Food and beverage production

Breweries, distilleries, bakeries and commissaries with walk-in coolers, bottling lines and loading doors. Doors and the office get the alarm; temperature alerts on coolers can report through the same panel.

Film, photo and media

Camera, lighting and grip equipment on carts and in cages, often moved in and out by crews who are not your staff. Cages and the gear room are their own armed area; crew codes expire when the job does.

Fashion, textile and design studios

Sample rooms, fabric stock, sewing machines and computers in studios on shared floors. A small wireless system on the studio door, a motion detector and a glass-break, reporting to the owner’s phone, covers most of them.

Hardware, robotics and medtech

Prototypes, test equipment and data. Server closets and lab rooms are separate zones with their own user list, and audit logs show who entered when.

Retail counters and the food hall

Businesses that take payment on site — in the Market @ 77 food hall on the ground floor of Building 77, for example — add hold-up buttons and a stockroom zone.

The roads inside the Yard are private, so callers describe a place by building and gate rather than by street: “Building 3, near the Flushing Avenue entrance,” “by the Sands Street gate,” “the long building by the dry docks.” Around the fence the commercial streets are Flushing Avenue along the south side, Kent Avenue to the east, and Navy and Sands Streets to the west. We also work on the other side of the fence, in the neighborhoods listed further down, where the same company may have a showroom or a second space.

Rules that apply to every Navy Yard alarm

  • Your lease and BNYDC approval. Plans for alterations generally need BNYDC approval before work starts; electrical work needs approved plans and a licensed electrician. Unapproved construction or unclosed permits are a default under Yard leases, according to the Tenant Manual.
  • Who may install an alarm. New York State’s Department of State licenses anyone who installs, services or maintains a security or fire alarm system. Ours is NYS license #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • Siren limits. NYC Administrative Code §24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes of starting. We set every cutoff inside it.
  • Common areas and roofs. The manual bars storing anything in common hallways, posting notices outside your premises without consent and using roofs except in emergencies unless the lease grants roof rights. Devices stay inside your unit.
  • Fire alarms are separate. Building fire alarm systems and FDNY contacts are a different system with different rules. An intrusion alarm does not replace or connect to the building’s fire alarm unless that work is designed and approved separately; see our Navy Yard fire alarm page.
  • Signage. BNYDC says all signage requests need its review. Alarm warning decals on your own door glass are usually the only sign involved; ask your Property Manager before putting anything on a corridor wall.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in the Brooklyn Navy Yard: Questions Answered

These are the questions Navy Yard tenants actually bring to us, grouped by subject. Where an answer depends on BNYDC’s rules, we say what its published Tenant Manual says and tell you to confirm with your Property Manager; your lease controls.

Leases, approvals and getting in

Our lease says alterations need BNYDC’s approval. Does a wireless intrusion alarm count, or can it go in without sending plans?

Assume it counts until your Property Manager tells you otherwise. BNYDC’s Tenant Manual says plans for alterations must generally be submitted and approved before work begins, that licensees may not make any alteration without approval, and that only some minor non-structural work, the example given being interior painting, may fall under the “Non-Material Alterations” language in a lease. Even a battery-powered sensor is screwed to your door frame, and a panel needs power and sometimes a circuit, so it is better to send a short package than to argue the point afterward. We give you a one-page device list, a marked-up plan of your unit with every device, the fixing method for each, any cable route, and a note that nothing goes outside your space. Most Property Managers can answer quickly when they can see exactly that. If the panel needs a new dedicated circuit, that part is electrical work under BNYDC’s separate rules and goes to a licensed electrician with its own approved plan.

How does your crew get through the gate on install day: visitor QR codes, or temporary Yard IDs?

For a normal one- or two-day job, you register our technicians as visitors and each of us gets an emailed QR code to scan at the gate; we send you names the day before. BNYDC’s manual says recurring visitors and consultants on long-term projects should not use the visitor system and should instead be given temporary ID cards by BNYDC Security, so if your job is phased over several weeks, or you want us on a service contract with regular visits, that is the route to arrange. Tell us which gate is nearest your building and whether your building’s lobby needs a swipe card at night — the manual says Building 77’s lobby does between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. — so we arrive at the right entrance at the right time.

Who responds when it goes off

BNYDC already has guards at every gate. What does an alarm inside our unit add?

It covers the part of your security the landlord says is yours. The gates control who comes onto the campus, but the manual is explicit that tenants put in their own locks, BNYDC holds no keys to leased spaces, and tenants are responsible for security within their leased space. Once someone is past a gate with an ID or a visitor code — and thousands of people are, every day, working for hundreds of other businesses, delivering, visiting or doing contract work — the gate has done all it can. Nobody at a gate sees your corridor door open at night or notices that a cage of tools has been emptied over a long weekend. An alarm tells you, at the moment it happens, which door opened and when, and the log afterward is evidence for the police and your insurer. It also tells you the boring things that cost money: the roll-up door left open, the cooler warming up, the system nobody armed on Friday.

Can BNYDC Security be one of the contacts the monitoring station calls, or does the call list have to be our own staff?

Your own people should come first, and anything involving BNYDC Security has to be agreed with BNYDC, not just written into an alarm account. The manual tells tenants to call BNYDC Security for after-hours and weekend emergencies, which is a sensible step for whoever on your list gets the alarm call. But we do not put the landlord’s desk on your monitoring account without its consent, and we do not assume a guard will open or enter your space; BNYDC has no key to it. The list we set up usually reads: the person who locked up, a second key-holder close enough to come in, police dispatch on a confirmed alarm, and an instruction to the responder to tell BNYDC Security at the gate. If your company has an arrangement with BNYDC for its officers to check your door, we add it exactly as agreed.

Our unit is in the middle of a big multi-tenant building. Will anyone hear an interior siren at three in the morning?

Possibly the cleaners, rarely anyone who can help. In a large building a siren inside one unit is heard on that floor and maybe the stairwell, and New York City’s Administrative Code §24-238 makes a building alarm stop on its own within fifteen minutes anyway. But the siren is the warning, not the response. What actually brings someone is the signal to the monitoring station and the call list behind it, so we put most of the effort there: a reliable communicator, confirmed alarms before police dispatch, and contacts who will answer. We place one sounder inside the unit and, where the space is large, a second one on the work floor, and we never put one in the common corridor.

Signals out of thick buildings

Building 77’s lower floors were built with walls about two feet thick and no windows. Will a cellular communicator get a signal in there?

Sometimes, but we do not assume it. Wikipedia’s account of the building says its lower eleven stories were built in 1941 with 25-inch walls and no windows, and that windows were only added in the 2017 renovation. Thick concrete and a deep floor plate can leave the middle of a unit with a weak signal even when your phone shows a bar or two. Before choosing where the panel goes we test the communicator’s signal at the spot, and on more than one carrier if the hardware allows it. If the reading is poor we move the panel toward a window or an outside wall, add a small remote antenna inside your unit on a short cable, or switch the primary path to your wired internet with cellular as the backup. We do not run antennas through exterior walls or onto the roof; both are outside your lease unless it says otherwise.

Should the alarm report over the building’s internet or over cellular, given the Yard’s telecom closets are controlled by the landlord?

Use both, with cellular as the main path where the signal allows. The manual lists four telecom providers on campus and says access to a building’s telecom closets goes through your Property Manager. Your internet line therefore depends on your router, your provider and a closet you do not control; a provider outage or a router reboot at the wrong moment and the alarm cannot report. A cellular communicator in your own unit has none of those dependencies. If cellular is weak in your space, we make the internet path primary, put the router and the alarm panel on a small battery backup together, and set the panel to report a communication failure so you hear about it the same day. Either way the monitoring station gets a regular test signal, and a missed one raises a trouble call.

Big floors, heat and doors

Our shop floor has very high ceilings and racks of material. Where do motion detectors go so they see a person and not a forklift, a fan or a pigeon?

Usually not on the open floor at all. Standard detectors are designed for ordinary ceiling heights; on a double-height floor we use detectors rated for the mounting height or switch to photoelectric beams across the aisles that lead to your doors and high-value rooms. They are aimed along walls rather than into open space, away from unit heaters, ceiling fans, steam lines and the top of any roll-up door where birds roost. Racks block detectors, so we protect what the racks surround rather than trying to see through them. On many shop floors the best overnight plan is contacts on every door, glass-break on the windows, beams across the two or three routes to the tool crib and office, and full motion only inside those rooms. A forklift parked in place does not trip anything; a person walking to the crib does.

Steam heat in our space runs on building zones we can’t adjust. Can heating cycles set off motion detectors overnight?

They can, if a detector looks at a radiator or a pipe. BNYDC’s manual says most multi-tenant spaces have no individual thermostats and are heated by zone, so the heat may come on in the middle of the night without anyone in your company touching anything. A passive infrared detector reacts to a warm moving shape, and a radiator warming quickly or air moving over a steam pipe can look like one to a cheap or badly aimed sensor. We map the radiators and pipes during the walk-through, aim detectors so they do not look at them, choose dual-technology detectors in rooms where that is not possible, and run a walk test with the heat on. If you already get false alarms on cold mornings, send us the times.

Our roll-up door opens onto a dock that other tenants share. Can the alarm protect our door without reacting to traffic in the common area?

Yes. The alarm watches your door, not the dock. A heavy-duty overhead-door contact tells the panel when the door lifts more than a few inches; it does not care how many trucks pass outside. Inside, a motion detector or beam is aimed back into your unit, away from the door glass and the dock. We do not mount anything on the dock itself or on the building’s walls outside your space, and we do not use outdoor motion detectors there. BNYDC’s manual says loading docks must be kept clear except while loading and that tenants should cooperate over shared freight, so a door left half-open for another tenant’s delivery is a real situation; we can program a short delay or a separate code for the person who opens the door for a delivery.

Can the alarm tell us when the overhead door is left open after the last shift, even when nobody arms it?

Yes. The door contact reports its state all the time, armed or not. We set a rule on the panel or in the app: if the overhead door is still open at a given time, or stays open for longer than a set number of minutes after it should be closed, send a notification to the shift lead and the owner. The same trick works for an unarmed system — if nobody has armed the alarm by a set hour, you get a message. In a busy shop that rule is often worth more than the siren.

People, keys and materials

A crew sometimes works late on the floor. Can the tool crib and the office stay armed while they’re there?

Yes, by splitting the system into areas. The tool crib, the office and any cage or server closet become their own armed area with their own users, and the floor and its doors become another. The late crew disarms only the floor and works normally; the crib and office stay armed, and only a manager’s code opens them. When the last person leaves, they arm the floor too, or the system reminds them at a set time. The panel records which area was armed by which person and when, so the next morning you can see exactly what happened.

When someone leaves the company, BNYDC deactivates their Yard ID. How do we make their alarm code stop working the same day?

Give every person their own code or fob, never a shared one, and delete it as part of the same checklist that revokes their Yard ID. BNYDC’s manual asks tenants to revoke a departing employee’s ID immediately and notes that the change takes effect at once through its ID Portal. Most current alarm platforms let a named manager add and delete users from an app, so you do not need to call us. We set that up at handover, give the manager’s login to whoever runs your offboarding, and leave a printed list of users and their access areas. A shared code, by contrast, cannot be revoked without changing it for everyone, so in practice it never is.

Copper and materials have been stolen from outdoor areas at the Yard. Can an alarm watch material we keep outside our building?

Only partly, and only where your lease gives you that outdoor area. Williamsburg365 reported in January 2025 that copper wiring was taken from a crane at the shipyard on the Yard’s waterfront and that a guard caught another man cutting copper wiring there in the morning. An indoor alarm does not see a yard. For outdoor storage that is yours, we use outdoor beam detectors along a fence line or the front of a cage, or outdoor motion detectors with pet and vegetation filtering, and we pair them with cameras so an alert comes with a picture. If the area is shared or is BNYDC’s, we do not put devices there; the best answer is usually to move high-value material inside your unit and protect it there.

Is an alarm worth it for a single small studio on a shared floor?

Often yes, and it can be small. A studio with one door, a window and a few thousand dollars of equipment can be covered by a wireless kit: a contact on the door, a motion detector looking at the door from inside, a glass-break detector if there is a window, a keypad or fob, and alerts to your phone. Nothing is wired through shared walls and nothing goes in the corridor. If you already bought a kit, we can install it for the labor-only price and check that it reports reliably from inside your building. The main question is who you want called if it goes off when you are not there; for many studios the answer is two people and no paid monitoring.

Can an antenna, siren or sensor go on our building’s roof?

Not unless your lease gives you the roof. BNYDC’s manual says roof use is prohibited except for emergencies, the only exception being a lease or license for the roof that grants access rights. So we do not plan rooftop antennas, sirens or sensors at the Yard. If the cellular signal is weak, the fixes are inside your unit — a remote antenna by a window, a different panel location, or a wired internet path — and if you are on a top floor with roof hatches inside your space, we can put contacts on the hatch from below, inside your demised area, with your Property Manager’s approval.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Our build environment could not open Google’s AI Overview, so nothing below is a quotation of it. These are claims that general-purpose answers, chatbots and national cost guides commonly make about business alarms, set against what the Yard’s own rules and buildings say.

“A building with round-the-clock security doesn’t need its own alarm.”

It is an easy assumption at a gated campus with an ID system and a security department. It mixes up two different jobs. Campus security controls who comes onto the Yard and responds to emergencies on its roads and common areas; it does not watch the inside of hundreds of leased units.

BNYDC says so itself. Its 2026 Tenant Manual tells tenants to install their own locks, says it does not maintain or duplicate keys to leased spaces, and states that tenants are responsible for security within their leased space. A guard has no view of your corridor door and no key to it.

An alarm fills exactly that gap. It does not replace the Yard’s security and does not ask BNYDC to do anything new; it makes sure that your side of the line is watched, and that the call goes to someone who can open your door.

“Wireless alarms work anywhere your phone gets a signal.”

A phone held up by a window and an alarm communicator screwed to the back wall of an inner office are in different places. In ordinary buildings the difference is small. In some Yard buildings it is not.

Building 77’s lower floors were built with walls about 25 inches thick and no windows, and Building 3 is a reinforced-concrete storehouse. Concrete, steel racking and deep floor plates weaken both cellular signals and the radio links between wireless sensors and the panel.

The fix is not exotic, but it has to be done before installation: measure signal strength at the panel spot and at each sensor position, then move the panel, add a remote antenna inside the unit, add a repeater, or make the wired internet path primary. A system that tests fine on day one in the right spot keeps working.

“National cost guides tell you what a business alarm costs.”

Guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give national ranges built from many kinds of jobs. They are a reasonable starting point for a house. They do not price the parts of a Yard job that are specific to the Yard.

Those include preparing an approval package for your Property Manager, scheduling around freight elevators that shut down at 8 p.m. on weekdays and stay off all weekend, a possible after-hours freight booking at BNYDC’s rates, signal testing in concrete buildings and detectors chosen for high ceilings.

Our Navy Yard prices are the Brooklyn list, with no area surcharge, and they are in the pricing table below. Where an after-hours freight booking or an electrician’s circuit is needed, we tell you before you approve the quote so nothing appears on the invoice that you did not see first.

“The louder the siren, the better.”

Outdoors, in a quiet street, a loud siren draws attention. Inside a multi-tenant industrial building at night it mostly reaches empty floors, and New York City’s Administrative Code §24-238 requires a building alarm to stop by itself within fifteen minutes.

At the Yard an outdoor siren is rarely an option anyway: the Tenant Manual bars roof use except in emergencies unless your lease grants roof rights, and it bars using areas outside your premises without consent. Sounders stay inside your unit.

So the siren’s job is to make an intruder leave sooner. What brings a response is the monitoring signal and the call list behind it. We spend our design time there, and set the siren cutoff inside the legal limit.

“A plug-and-play kit from a big brand is all a business needs.”

For a one-room studio it may be. Ring, SimpliSafe and similar kits are cheap, quick and send alerts to a phone, and we install them for customers who have already bought one.

A production space usually needs more: separate areas for a crib or a lab, a code for each person and a log of who used it, door-left-open rules, communicators that work through concrete, and a drawing your Property Manager can approve. National dealers such as ADT can provide professional systems, but customers on Reddit describe multi-year monitoring agreements as a common condition; read the cancellation terms.

Whatever you choose, ask who holds the installer code. If you leave a dealer or move units, the code decides whether you can keep and reprogram the equipment you paid for.

“If something is stolen inside the building, the landlord’s insurance deals with it.”

Leases at the Yard require each tenant to carry its own insurance; the Tenant Manual points tenants to the insurance articles of their lease and says BNYDC tracks every tenant’s certificates of insurance through a compliance company.

That puts the burden of proof on you. An insurer asking what happened will want to know when the door opened, whether the system was armed, and who had access. Without an alarm log and a code for each person, the answer is a guess.

We cannot tell you what your policy requires or whether an alarm changes your premium; ask your broker. We can make sure that the event log, user list and zone names are clear enough that the record helps you.

“Motion detectors cover a whole warehouse.”

A typical motion detector is designed for a room with an ordinary ceiling. Mounted high on a double-height wall, or aimed across a floor full of racks, it either misses people or reacts to fans, heaters and birds.

Building 128, now the Green Manufacturing Center and Newlab, is a single-story shop under a high gable-monitor roof; plenty of Yard units have similar heights and open floors. Coverage there is planned with detectors rated for the height, beams across routes and full motion only in the rooms that matter.

The result is fewer devices, fewer false alarms and better protection of the things a thief would actually carry out, which is the point of an alarm in the first place.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Draw the Line on the Floor Plan First

This describes how we survey a unit at the Yard in general, not any particular customer. Before we talk about sensors, we take a copy of your floor plan — the one attached to your lease, if you have it — and draw one line on it: the boundary of the space you lease. Everything inside that line is ours to plan; everything outside it, including corridors, docks, lobbies, roofs and building walls, is not. Then we mark three things: every way into your space, every room where something expensive lives, and every place where heat or air moves (heaters, steam pipes, fans, cooler condensers, roll-up doors).

Only then do we choose hardware, and the choices follow almost by themselves. Doors on the line get contacts. Rooms with expensive things get their own area. Heat and air sources tell us where not to aim a detector. The signal test tells us where the panel can live. The same drawing, cleaned up, becomes the approval package for your Property Manager and, years later, the list of fixing points to make good when you hand the space back.

Can you install outside our production hours, when the freight elevator is shut down?

What happens to the holes and mounting points when our lease ends and we hand the space back?
+

That is set by your lease, not by us, so check the surrender terms. What we do is make it easy. Every device we fix is listed on the drawing we leave with you, with its location and fixing method, and we fix to your own walls, partitions and ceiling grid inside your unit rather than to building structure where we can avoid it. When you leave, we can remove the system, take out any cable we ran, and fill and touch up the small screw holes, or leave it in place if the landlord or the next tenant wants it. Can the alarm and the cameras we already have be watched in one app?
+

Often, depending on the brands. Many current alarm platforms can show video from compatible cameras alongside alarm events, so an alert for the crib door arrives with a clip from the camera that looks at it. If your cameras are on a separate recorder that the alarm platform does not support, we can still link them: the recorder can bookmark or start recording on an alarm signal, so you can find the footage quickly afterward, even if you use two apps. We check model numbers before you commit.

Can a food-hall or retail counter get a hold-up button?
+

Yes. A hold-up or panic button is a small, silent switch fixed under a counter or desk where staff can reach it without being seen. Pressing it sends a priority signal to the monitoring station, which follows a separate instruction for a hold-up — usually dispatching police without calling the premises first, so nobody is put on the spot. Buttons can also be wireless pendants for staff who move around. We place them where they will not be knocked by accident, and we train staff on when to use them, because false hold-up signals are taken seriously.

Can the alarm keep a log of who disarmed it and when, for an insurance claim after a theft?
+

Yes, if each person has their own code or fob. The panel and the monitoring platform record every arm, disarm, door opening and alarm with a time and a user, and the log can be exported. That record answers the questions an insurer or the police ask first: was the system armed, which door opened, at what time, and whose code was used. A shared code makes the log useless, because every entry looks the same. We set up individual users at handover, name zones by what they actually are, such as “roll-up door” or “crib,” and show the manager how to pull a report. Whether an alarm affects your premium or your claim is a question for your broker.

Our older brick building has huge multi-pane factory windows. Can glass-break detectors cover them?
+

Yes, and they are usually the right tool for that kind of window. Putting a contact on every sash of a large steel or wood factory window is impractical, and many of those windows do not open anyway. An acoustic glass-break detector listens for the sound pattern of breaking glass and can cover a number of panes in one room, within the range the manufacturer specifies. We mount detectors on the opposite wall or ceiling, test them with a glass-break simulator after installation, and check that loud work noise from saws or compressors does not trip them; in very noisy shops we use detectors with adjustable sensitivity or add shock sensors on the most exposed panes.

Small jobs are billed as a service visit, $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, unless they are part of a larger installation. The gate and approval steps still apply: your Property Manager may treat even a couple of added devices as an alteration.

Does an intrusion alarm change who our company lists as its fire-alarm contact with BNYDC?
+

No. BNYDC’s ID Portal asks each company to name people in roles, and the manual defines the “Fire Safety & Security” role as the local fire-alarm contact for FDNY response. That role is about the building’s fire alarm and emergency contact, and it stays whatever it is today. An intrusion alarm has its own call list held by your monitoring provider. It makes sense for the same reliable person to appear on both lists, but they are separate systems. If you want smoke or heat detection inside your own unit beyond what the building provides, that is fire alarm work with its own design and approval, which we handle through our fire alarm service.
