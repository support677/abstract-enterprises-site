What Elevator Access Control Means for a Suffolk County Building

Elevator access control in Suffolk County is a newer conversation than it is in the city, and that changes how a first call usually goes. A property manager in Patchogue has just taken over a five-story rental near the train and discovers that anyone who gets through the front door can ride to every floor, including the rooftop lounge. A medical office owner in Hauppauge wants the second-floor surgical suite closed to walk-in patients without locking out the doctors who arrive through the rear lot. A condo board on the North Fork wants to know whether the fobs it already uses at the lobby door can also run the elevator. They are asking the same question in different words: how do we make the elevator stop taking strangers upstairs, and what does that involve?

The short answer is that an elevator, on its own, has no idea who is riding it. It answers buttons. If a person is inside the car and presses five, the car goes to five. In the city, many buildings have lived with doormen, buzzers and locked vestibules for a century, so the elevator was rarely the weak point. In Suffolk, most elevator buildings are young, many sit beside a surface lot or a garage, and many have more than one way in: the lobby, a parking-deck door, a side door by the bike room, a loading door behind the retail space. Each of those doors is a chance for someone without permission to reach the car. Elevator access control closes that gap at the one place every upstairs trip has to pass through.

It works by adding a reader to the elevator and a small panel that decides whether the reader's request is allowed. The reader can be a card or fob reader, a keypad, a phone reader that accepts a mobile credential, a video intercom station, or a biometric reader that checks a face or a fingerprint. The panel sits in the machine room, the controller closet or a nearby electrical room. When an approved credential is shown, the panel closes a relay, which is simply a switch, and the elevator controller accepts the floor call. When the credential is not approved, nothing happens and the attempt is logged with the time and the credential that was tried.

That is the whole mechanism. What makes one Suffolk building's system different from another's is how strict it needs to be, how many cars and floors it covers, who besides residents or employees has to ride, and how it ties in with the door system, the intercom and the cameras the building already owns. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems installs the low-voltage side of all of this across the county, from the Huntington and Babylon line out to Riverhead and the forks: the readers, the panel, the battery backup, the relays, the cable, the credentials and the programming. The connection inside the elevator controller is made with, or by, the building's own licensed elevator contractor, because New York State licenses elevator work separately and nobody else should be inside that cabinet.

We describe the work as three levels, and most Suffolk buildings start on the first one:

  • Level 1: a reader in the car. The car will not accept a floor call until an approved credential is shown. After that, the rider can pick any floor the car serves. For a single-tenant office building, a small condo or a senior building where the concern is outsiders, this is usually enough.
  • Level 2: biometric readers. The same car-level control, but the credential is a face, a fingerprint or an iris. Suffolk buildings that choose it are usually medical or professional buildings where staff share or lose badges, or residential buildings where fob sharing has become a problem.
  • Level 3: floor-by-floor control and the extras around it. Each rider reaches only the floors their permission allows. Readers can be added at the lobby or garage call buttons. Floors can lock and unlock on a schedule. The intercom can release a single floor for a visitor. This is what a multi-tenant medical building, a rental with amenity floors or an office building with separate companies on each floor tends to need.

The rest of this page explains how each of those levels plays out in the buildings Suffolk actually has, what the county's town-by-town code offices care about, what it costs at the Suffolk rate, and how to tell whether a contractor really knows elevators or just knows doors.

Suffolk in Numbers

Suffolk is a county of single-family houses, and that is the starting point for anyone planning elevator security here. Elevator buildings are a small share of the housing stock and a modest share of the commercial stock, and they are scattered across ten towns and dozens of villages rather than packed into a few neighborhoods. Over the last fifteen years, though, the number has grown quickly, and nearly all of that growth has happened in the same kind of place: a downtown or a station area where a town or village rezoned for four- and five-story mixed-use buildings. How It Works

How Elevator Access Control Works Inside a Suffolk Elevator, From Parking Deck to Hallway

The easiest way to understand the system is to follow one rider. Picture a tenant in a five-story rental a few minutes' walk from an LIRR station. He drives home, parks on the second level of the building's garage, and walks to the garage elevator lobby. The building has a Level 3 system with a reader at the garage call button and floor control inside the car. This is what happens.

  1. He calls the car from the garage. The call button on the garage level only works after a valid credential is presented at the reader next to it. He taps his phone. The panel checks the credential, sees he is a resident, and enables the hall call for a few seconds. He presses the button and the car comes.
  2. He steps inside. The reader in the car is mounted on the front return panel at a height a wheelchair user can reach. He taps again. The panel looks up his permissions: lobby, garage levels and the fourth floor, where his apartment is, plus the fifth floor amenity lounge between 7 a.m. and 11 p.m.
  3. The panel enables his floors. For each floor he is allowed, it closes a relay that tells the elevator controller to accept that floor's button for a short window, typically eight to fifteen seconds. Floors he is not allowed stay locked.
  4. He presses four. The button lights and the car goes. If he had pressed three, where he does not live, the button would not have responded.
  5. The event is logged. The panel records the time, the credential and the floor. The property manager can see it later from a browser or an app, and so can we if the building has asked us to support the system remotely.
  6. A visitor rides differently. If his friend arrives at the lobby, the friend uses the video intercom. When the tenant answers on his phone and presses the release, the system unlocks the lobby door and enables only the fourth floor in the car for the next ride. The friend cannot stop at the amenity floor or the roof.

A Level 1 building does a simpler version of the same thing. There is one relay for the whole car instead of one per floor. A valid credential enables every button in the car for a few seconds, so the question it answers is "should this person be riding at all?" rather than "where may they go?" In many Suffolk buildings, especially single-tenant offices, small condos and senior residences where the concern is outsiders, that is the right question.

Three practical details decide how easy the job is in a given building:

  • The path from the car to the panel. The reader in the car needs a few conductors to reach the panel. They usually run through the traveling cable, the flexible bundle that hangs from the bottom of the car. Newer Suffolk buildings often have spare conductors, or even a network pair, already in that cable. If they do not, the elevator contractor either adds a cable or provides a wireless or network link, and that is a real cost. We look for it on the survey.
  • The type of controller. Most buildings put up in the county in the last two decades have microprocessor controllers with inputs designed for exactly this kind of tie-in. Some controllers can accept a network connection from the access system instead of a bank of relays, which simplifies floor control in taller buildings. Older hydraulic elevators in 1970s and 1980s office and medical buildings usually need relays.
  • The door system. The cheapest and most convenient result is when the elevator uses the same credentials and the same software as the lobby and garage doors. If the building already has a door system we can extend, we usually recommend it. If the door system is closed to other installers or past its life, it may be the moment to replace both.
Floor Control

Elevator Floor Access Control: Restricting Certain Floors in a Suffolk Building

Floor control, sometimes called elevator floor restriction, is the part people ask about most. It is the difference between "you may ride" and "you may ride to these floors only." In Suffolk the request almost always comes from one of four situations: a building with stores or offices downstairs and apartments above, a medical or professional building with several practices, a rental with amenity floors that the owner wants open only at certain hours, or a building where the roof, the mechanical floor or a storage level should be off-limits to everyone but staff.

In the car, at the landing, or both

There are two places to put the restriction. In the car, the reader enables only the rider's floors, as described above. At the landing, a reader beside the hall call button decides whether the car can be called to that floor at all. Garage levels are the classic landing case in Suffolk: the building wants anyone in the lobby to be able to ride up, but only residents to be able to call the car from the parking deck, because the deck has an open entrance. Many buildings use both, a garage landing reader plus in-car floor control.

What it does not do

Floor control decides which buttons respond. It does not stop someone from walking up the stairs, and it should not. Stair doors are governed by the fire code, and in most buildings the stair doors on the residential floors have to allow people out into the stair at all times. Where a building wants to control re-entry from the stair onto certain floors, that is a separate door-hardware conversation that has to match the code and the building's fire alarm. We cover it on the survey, but elevator floor control is not a way around it.

How many relays a Suffolk building needs

The count is roughly floors controlled, times cars, plus any landing readers. A five-story rental with one car and four controlled floors needs a handful of relay points. A five-story rental with two cars and a garage stop needs roughly twice that, plus a landing reader at the garage. A ten-story medical office with three cars and every floor controlled can need dozens. That count, more than anything else, is why Level 3 is quoted per building after a survey instead of off a price list.

When a Suffolk building should not bother with floor control

Plenty of buildings ask for floor control and end up better off without it. A three-story office with one tenant, a small condo where every owner is welcome on every floor, or a senior building where residents visit each other constantly will often be happier with a Level 1 reader and a locked roof. Floor control adds programming, adds things to maintain, and adds one more reason for a resident to be told "your fob doesn't work on that floor." We would rather install the simpler system that people use than the complicated one they resent.

Building Types

Suffolk Building Types and the Elevator Access Control Each One Usually Needs

Suffolk's elevator buildings fall into a handful of families. Each has its own habits, its own decision-makers and its own typical system. The descriptions below are drawn from what is publicly known about the county's building stock and from how these buildings are generally run. Buildings named here are examples of a type, not our customers.

Downtown and station-area rentals: Patchogue, Ronkonkoma, Lindenhurst, Huntington Station, Riverhead

These are the buildings that have changed Suffolk's skyline, such as it is, over the last fifteen years. New Village at Patchogue, the Carriage House going up on West Main Street, the Alston and Station Yards buildings at the Ronkonkoma Hub, the proposed five-story, 172-unit Lindenhurst buildings over retail and parking, Avalon Huntington Station, and Heritage on Main in downtown Riverhead all follow a similar pattern: four or five stories, apartments over ground-floor retail or amenity space, a garage or a large lot, one or two elevator cars, and a lobby that is staffed by a leasing office during business hours and by nobody at night.

The typical request is Level 3, but a narrow version of it: residents can ride to their own floor and to the amenity floor during posted hours; the garage call buttons need a credential; the retail floor is separated from the residential floors entirely. Most of these buildings already have a cloud-managed door system at the lobby and garage, and most owners want the elevator to join it so a resident has one credential and the leasing office has one place to turn access on and off. When an owner is in the middle of a lease-up, or when a phase two building is about to open next to phase one, getting the credential plan right matters more than the hardware.

Affordable and supportive housing: Wyandanch, Huntington Station and beyond

Suffolk's affordable buildings, such as the Wyandanch Village buildings and Huntington Apartments at 201 Fall Street, where a nineteenth-century building now holds 53 affordable homes including 27 with supportive services for veterans, have a different emphasis. The owner is usually a nonprofit or a mission-driven developer working with a property manager and a social-services partner. Access control has to support case managers and service staff who move around the building, residents who may be new to fobs and apps, and funders who expect clear records. Level 1 with a well-kept credential list is often enough for the residential floors, with floor control used only for offices, program rooms and the roof.

Senior housing

Senior buildings, such as 11 Park Drive in Wyandanch and the many senior apartment communities across Brookhaven and Islip, are where access control can do the most harm if it is done badly. Residents may have limited hand strength, low vision or memory problems. Home health aides, family members and medical transport crews come and go all day. And an elevator that becomes harder to use is a real hardship: when an East Patchogue senior facility's elevator went unrepaired in the spring of 2025, residents were stuck upstairs, as NBC New York reported. Our rule in these buildings is that the access system must never be a reason the elevator does not work. That means large, simple readers, long enable windows, credentials that do not depend on a smartphone, an aide credential that is easy to issue and revoke, and a battery and power-failure plan agreed in advance.

Medical and professional buildings

Suffolk has a large outpatient medical sector, from multi-specialty centers like Stony Brook Medicine's Advanced Specialty Care at 500 Commack Road and its larger Lake Grove facility to two- and three-story professional buildings along Route 25, Route 347 and Route 111. These buildings almost always need floor control, because patients should reach the floors they are visiting and no others, and some floors hold surgical suites, imaging, records or pharmacy areas that are staff-only. Hours matter: the building is open to the public during clinic hours and closed at night, but staff, cleaners and on-call clinicians still need to ride. A schedule-driven Level 3 system, with public floors open by day and everything on credentials at night, is the usual answer.

Office buildings and office parks: Melville, Hauppauge, Islandia, Ronkonkoma

Route 110 in Melville, the Hauppauge park and the office corridors near the Long Island Expressway hold most of the county's multi-story office space. Buildings like the 400,000-square-foot 3 Huntington Quadrangle, which renovated its lobby, corridors and elevators to attract tenants, compete on amenities and security. Tenants arrive with their own badge systems, and the landlord wants one elevator system that recognizes all of them, or at least issues landlord credentials that open tenant floors. Floor control by tenant, after-hours schedules, and a lobby desk or visitor kiosk that issues temporary credentials are standard. Several older Melville buildings are being discussed for conversion to residential or live/work use under the Town of Huntington's revived rezoning plans, which brings a residential access model into a building whose elevator was set up for offices.

Condos, co-ops and East End buildings

Suffolk has fewer co-ops than Nassau or the city, but it has many condominiums, from 1980s garden-style complexes with a single elevator in each building to waterfront condos and hotels on the South Shore and the East End. Boards here tend to be practical: they want the strangers out of the building, they want residents to keep using the fobs they already have, and they want a system the management company can run without calling us every week. Level 1 with a locked roof or storage level is common. Seasonal buildings on the East End add a twist: owners who are away for months, renters who need access for a week, and staff who need a different schedule in July than in January.

Mixed-use buildings with stores below

Many village downtowns, from Babylon and Bay Shore to Port Jefferson, Northport and Riverhead, have three- to five-story buildings with stores or restaurants on the ground floor and apartments or offices above, sometimes with a small elevator added during a renovation. The concern is simple: customers of the ground-floor business should not be able to ride upstairs. Level 1 in the car, sometimes paired with a landing reader at the ground floor, solves it. In Riverhead, where the Town Board approved the Zenith Group's request in March 2026 to take a downtown project to five stories with 15 apartments, buildings like this are being added right now.

Codes and Permits

Ten Towns, Many Code Offices: What Suffolk Building and Fire Officials Expect

One thing that surprises owners coming from the city is that there is no single building department for Suffolk County. Building permits and code enforcement are handled by each of the ten towns and by the incorporated villages within them, under the New York State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code. Fire prevention is handled the same way, by town fire marshals' offices or by village officials. The rules for New York City, including its Department of Buildings bulletins and Local Law 63 on tenant data privacy, do not apply in Suffolk, and a contractor who quotes them to you as Suffolk law is mixing up jurisdictions.

What stays the same everywhere is the elevator standard. Elevators in New York State are built and altered under the ASME A17.1 safety code as adopted in the state codes, and New York's Labor Law requires the companies and mechanics who install, alter and maintain elevators to be licensed by the state Department of Labor. Under that standard, anything added to an elevator car or a hall station must not interfere with the elevator's normal or emergency operation, and it must stop having any effect when firefighters' emergency operation is in use. Every reader we install is wired to drop out of the way in that situation, and the elevator contractor confirms it at the controller.

Here is how the local side usually works in practice:

  • Town fire marshals look closely at anything that touches the fire alarm. In the Town of Huntington, for example, the Fire Prevention Bureau requires that the licensed company installing fire alarm, sprinkler or suppression equipment apply for a construction permit and perform the acceptance inspection with a fire marshal witnessing it. Our access system does not modify the fire alarm, but if a building wants doors or elevator functions to release on alarm, the interface is made by the building's fire alarm company under its own permit, and we coordinate the timing.
  • Brookhaven and Islip run their own fire prevention divisions. Brookhaven's Division of Fire Prevention takes permits through its online portal, and its inspectors, who are state-certified building safety inspectors, inspect commercial buildings that do not need annual inspection at least once every three years. Islip's Fire Marshal's Office publishes its own submission requirements. A building owner should expect the local office to ask about any change to egress doors or fire alarm interfaces, even when the elevator reader itself needs no permit.
  • Building departments care about the elevator alteration. Whether a particular elevator access project needs a building permit depends on the town or village and on what the elevator contractor changes at the controller. We ask the elevator contractor how they file their part, and we tell you in writing whether we expect any filing for ours.
  • Villages may have their own requirements. A building in Patchogue, Lindenhurst, Port Jefferson or Northport answers to the village, not the town, on many of these questions. We check which office applies before we schedule.

The practical lesson for a Suffolk owner is to decide early who will speak to the local office, and to make sure the elevator contractor, the fire alarm company and we are working from the same plan. In our experience the projects that run long are almost never slowed by the access hardware; they are slowed by a missing conversation with an inspector about a door.

Safety

Fire Service, Storms and Power Failures in Suffolk Elevator Buildings

Every elevator access system has to answer three questions before it is installed: what happens in a fire, what happens when the power goes out, and what happens when somebody urgently needs to ride and their credential does not work. Suffolk adds a fourth: what happens when a storm knocks out power to half the county for days.

Fire: the readers step aside

When the building's fire alarm recalls the elevators, or a firefighter turns the key for in-car operation, the access system must have no effect on the car. That is the rule in the elevator safety code, and it is the first thing we test with the elevator contractor at the end of a job. In practice, the elevator controller ignores the access relays once it is on fire service, and in some designs the access panel also receives a signal so it can log the event.

Power failure: decide the answer in advance

If utility power fails, the elevator either stops or runs on the building's emergency power if it has a generator. Our panel has its own battery, sized to keep the readers working for a period the building chooses. The more important decision is what the elevator does if our panel loses power entirely. In most Suffolk residential and senior buildings, the right answer is that the car goes back to working for everyone, because trapping residents upstairs is a far bigger risk than a stranger riding the elevator during an outage. In a medical building with controlled substances or an office with sensitive floors, the owner may choose differently for certain floors. We write the decision down and wire to it.

Storms: protect the panel, plan the restart

Hurricanes, tropical storms and nor'easters have all cut power across Long Island for days at a time. For an elevator building that means two things. Put the access panel and its battery somewhere dry and reachable, not in a basement pit or a ground-floor closet that floods in a South Shore storm surge. And plan the restart: when power comes back, the panel, the network equipment and the cloud connection have to come up in the right order, and somebody has to check that the readers are online before residents are told everything is normal. We leave a one-page restart sheet in the panel.

The override

Every system we install has an override that the building controls: usually a key switch, a supervisor credential, or a software command that puts the car back on free access. A paramedic crew arriving at a senior building at 2 a.m. should never be stopped by a reader. We agree with the building who carries the override and how it is used, and we train the staff who need it.

Privacy

Biometrics, Resident Data and Privacy in Suffolk Elevator Buildings

Buildings in New York City have Local Law 63, which sets out how residential buildings may collect and keep data from smart access systems. Suffolk buildings are not covered by that city law. That does not mean anything goes. An elevator access system creates a detailed record of who rode where and when, and a Suffolk owner should treat that record carefully for three reasons: residents and tenants expect it, insurers and lenders increasingly ask about it, and New York State law already limits some uses, such as the restriction in the state's Labor Law on employers requiring fingerprints as a condition of employment.

Our recommendations for Suffolk buildings are practical rather than legal, and an owner with specific concerns should speak to their own attorney:

  • Collect only what the system needs. A fob number, a name and a unit or suite is enough for most buildings. There is no need to store dates of birth or other personal details in the access system.
  • Write a short policy. One page that tells residents or tenants what is recorded, who can see it, how long it is kept and how to ask about it. We can give you a plain-language starting point.
  • Set a retention period. Most platforms let you delete logs older than a set number of days. Pick a period that suits your building and turn it on.
  • Make biometrics optional where you can. If a Level 2 face or fingerprint reader is used by residents, give anyone who does not want to enroll a fob or card that works just as well. For employees, check with counsel before making a fingerprint reader mandatory.
  • Remove people promptly. When a tenant moves out or an employee leaves, their credential should be deleted the same day, not when someone gets around to it.
Credentials

Fobs, Cards, Phones, Codes and Faces: Choosing an Elevator Credential for Suffolk Riders

The credential is what a rider carries, types or is. It is also the part residents and staff notice every day. In Suffolk, where many residents drive and keep their keys on one ring, and many offices already issue badges, the right choice usually builds on what people already have.

  • Key fobs. The most common credential in Suffolk apartment and condo buildings. Cheap to issue, easy to use with a car key in hand, and simple to cancel when lost. Choose a secure fob format rather than the oldest proximity type, which can be copied with inexpensive tools.
  • Cards and office badges. The default in office and medical buildings. If tenants already carry badges from their own systems, we check whether the elevator readers can read the same technology. Often they can, which means employees use one badge for their suite door and the elevator.
  • Phone credentials. Increasingly popular in the newer station-area rentals, where the leasing office wants to issue and revoke access without handing out hardware. Riders tap their phone or hold it near the reader. Good for residents, less good for seniors or anyone without a current smartphone, so we never make it the only option.
  • Keypad codes. Useful as a backup and for staff who should not carry a fob home. A code alone is weak security because codes are shared, so we usually pair codes with another credential or limit them to service hours.
  • Video intercom stations. A station with a camera, a call button and a reader serves both residents and visitors. It is often the best choice for a single-car building where the elevator lobby is also the entrance.
  • Faces and fingerprints. Level 2. Strong against shared credentials and good for staff-only areas, with the privacy steps described above.
  • Wristbands and pendants. Some senior residences prefer a wristband or pendant to a fob because residents are less likely to lose it. Many readers accept them; we confirm the format during the survey.

Whatever you choose, plan the credential life cycle before the first one is issued: who issues them, how a lost one is shut off, what a replacement costs the resident, and how moving out works. Most of the day-to-day problems in elevator access come from that process, not from the hardware.

Visitors and Deliveries

Guests, Aides, Deliveries and Commuter Parking in Suffolk Elevator Buildings

The hard part of elevator access control is never the residents. It is everybody else. Suffolk buildings have their own mix of people who need to ride but do not live or work there:

  • Guests. In a building with a video intercom, the resident answers on a phone or an in-unit monitor and releases the visitor's floor. Where there is no intercom, the resident can send a time-limited phone pass, or the leasing office can issue a temporary credential.
  • Home health aides and caregivers. Common in senior buildings and in any building with older residents. A separate aide credential, tied to the resident it serves and limited to the hours the aide works, is better than a resident lending out a fob.
  • Package and food deliveries. Most Suffolk rentals now have a package room off the lobby, which means delivery drivers never need the elevator. Where they do, a delivery credential that opens only the lobby and package floor during set hours keeps them moving without opening the building.
  • Contractors and movers. Moves happen on weekends and during lease-ups, often several in a day. We set up a move mode that puts one car on a restricted schedule with a credential for the moving crew, so the other car keeps serving residents.
  • Patients and their drivers. In medical buildings, patients ride to the floor they are visiting during clinic hours. Ambulette and medical transport crews need a way up after hours, usually a call to the front desk or a code issued per trip.
  • Commuter and shopper parking. Several station-area buildings share or sit next to commuter parking, and many mixed-use buildings share a lot with their retail tenants. A landing reader on the parking level keeps commuters and shoppers from using the residential elevator as a shortcut.
  • Emergency responders. Police, fire and EMS get in through the building's emergency access arrangements, and the elevator goes to fire service when the firefighters take it. Nothing about our system should ever slow them down.

Upgrades and Repair

Upgrading, Replacing and Repairing Elevator Card Reader Systems in Suffolk

Suffolk's first generation of elevator access systems went into office buildings and medical buildings in the 1990s and 2000s, and into the early station-area rentals in the 2010s. Many of them are now due for attention. The symptoms we hear about most:

  • Readers that work for some fobs and not others, usually because the building has issued two generations of credentials over the years, or because a reader is failing.
  • A system nobody can log into, because the company that installed it has closed, been sold, or stopped answering, and the admin password went with them.
  • A software platform that has reached end of life, or a panel that the manufacturer no longer supports with updates.
  • Floor schedules that no longer match the building, such as a medical floor that changed tenants but still opens at 7 a.m. for a practice that left years ago.
  • Intermittent faults in the car, often a loose or worn conductor in the traveling cable or a corroded termination, which shows up as a reader that drops offline when the car is at certain floors.

For a repair, we start with what you have: test the readers, check the panel, check the conductors to the car with the elevator contractor, and fix what is broken. For an upgrade, we look at whether the existing relays and wiring can be reused with a new panel and new readers, which often saves a lot of money, and whether existing credentials can be read by the new readers so residents do not all have to be re-issued fobs on the same day. When an old system is being replaced, we plan a cutover that keeps at least one car in service and moves people from the old credentials to the new ones over a set period.

If another company installed your system, we can usually take it over. We will tell you honestly after the survey whether it is worth supporting or whether replacing it will cost less over the next five years.

What AI Gets Wrong About Elevator Access Control in Suffolk

People increasingly ask a chatbot before they call a contractor. The answers are often reasonable in general and wrong about Suffolk in particular. These are the mistakes we see most:
