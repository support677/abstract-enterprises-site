The Neighborhood

Why Clason Point Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Most of Clason Point ends at water, and almost every camera decision on the peninsula comes back to that. Wikipedia draws the neighborhood as a point of land of about 0.47 square miles, bounded by Lafayette Avenue to the north, White Plains Road and Pugsley Creek Park to the east, the East River to the south and the Bronx River to the west, in ZIP code 10473 and Bronx Community Board 9. Soundview Avenue, once Clason's Point Road, runs down the middle to the ferry landing at the tip. The people who live here have a short list of reasons to want a camera: a delivery left on a step, a car on a street that fills with ferry riders, a yard that backs onto a creek or a park, a quiet shop after closing. The same short distance to the water that makes the place pleasant is what makes cameras harder to place well. Glare off the river at the start and end of the day, wind across open ground, salt in the air and streets that flood after heavy rain all change which camera goes where and what it is mounted with.

The largest single block of homes is Shorehaven. Wikipedia records that the old amusement grounds became the Shorehaven Beach Club in 1949 and, in 1999, the Shorehaven Condominiums, a gated community of townhome-style units built mostly in phases through the 2000s; other sources count the homes differently, and a 2020 trade report described 899 units on 40 acres run by seven homeowners associations under one umbrella association. The association's written guidelines say its contracted guards control the entrance, announce visitors and watch the vehicles that come in. They also say plainly that security does not accept packages for residents. So the gate covers who drives in, and nothing covers the doorstep except whatever the owner puts there. Shorehaven's 2020 rules add the part that matters for mounting: repairs inside a home belong to the unit owner, while the residential buildings themselves, roofs and exterior walls, belong to the condominium Board of Managers, and the front of a condo unit is common property shared by the lower and upper units. A camera on that front is a board question before it is an equipment question.

Harding Park, in the southwest corner of the point, is built at a completely different scale. The Department of City Planning's 2016 Resilient Neighborhoods report describes about 400 residents in 226 single-family homes on land owned cooperatively and administered by the Harding Park Homeowners Association, with homes fronting narrow paved lanes without sidewalks, private streets of nine to thirteen feet south of Gildersleeve Avenue. Forgotten New York adds that some of those lanes are interrupted by properties and some appear on no official map. For a camera, a lane that narrow is the whole problem in one picture: a lens on the front of a bungalow cannot help seeing the lane, and very often the front door of the bungalow across it. Planning a Harding Park camera is mostly an exercise in deciding what to leave out of the frame.

Outside the gate and the lanes, Clason Point is a neighborhood of houses on the city grid. City lot data for the peninsula, as compiled in our earlier research, counts roughly 2,293 residential lots, of which about 1,018 hold two-family houses and 492 hold walk-up buildings of three or more apartments, with some 840 lots built before 1931 and another wave of two- and three-unit rowhouses since the 1990s. Real estate listings describe Craftsman cottages, ranch homes, townhouses and multifamily rowhouses, and a Harding Park area listing on Redfin describes a fully detached two-family with a finished basement and a separate entrance. Nextdoor lists the neighborhood as 62 percent homeowners. That mix means many owners who can make their own camera decisions, many houses with two or three entrances to cover, and a good number of older walls that still carry cable from some earlier system nobody remembers fitting.

Public housing is the other anchor, and it has its own camera history. Clason Point Gardens, which the Bronx Times describes as the first public housing in the Bronx and NYCHA's first low-rise, garden-style development, opened in 1941 and marked 85 years in September 2026. Residents have told the Bronx Times about exterior light problems and a badly wired light, among other conditions, and in July 2026 News 12 Bronx reported tenants saying a parking lot at the complex had been turned into a temporary garbage area overrun with trash and rodents. On ApartmentRatings, someone asked simply whether the Gardens has surveillance cameras on site. Nearby, CityLand reported in November 2023 that NYCHA's Monroe Houses were among developments that received new CCTV cameras, lighting, access control and intercoms. City Limits recalls that in 1976 NYCHA ran a $90,000 closed-circuit television experiment in three Bronxdale Houses buildings in Soundview. In all of these places the cameras are NYCHA's, and residents cannot alter the buildings, which leaves in-unit or no-drill devices as the realistic private option.

Then there is the water itself, which belongs in a camera plan because it decides where the expensive parts live. DCP's report puts about a third of Harding Park's buildings, roughly 80, inside the one-percent annual chance floodplain, including velocity and coastal A zones, and notes that the association's land is largely unsewered, leading to regular inundation of streets, yards and homes in rainstorms. The Bronx Times reported that FEMA's post-Sandy advisory maps added or expanded flood zones along the Clason Point and Harding Park shoreline. During Sandy, Bronx Ink found a Harding Park home about 150 feet from the water with roughly a foot of water inside, a resident still without power the next day, and some 49,000 Bronx Con Edison customers out. A recorder on a basement shelf or a battery backup on a garage floor is the first thing lost in that kind of night. The city's own advice to Harding Park was to elevate critical systems, and we treat a camera recorder as one of them.

The streets give owners their own reasons. The peninsula is covered by the NYPD's 43rd Precinct at 900 Fteley Avenue, and public housing by Police Service Area 8. News 12 Bronx reported a shooting near 1722 Randall Avenue in June 2026, and Fox 5 reported three people shot near 552 Commonwealth Avenue on August 20, 2026. Homes.com's summary of the CAP Index puts overall crime on par with the national score while rating some violent categories high. At the tip, the Bronx Times reported in 2018 that Soundview Avenue is the lone road to the ferry slip and has limited street parking, which is why owners on the southern blocks often ask about watching a car parked on the street. A camera will not stop any of this, but it can give an owner a clear, timed record of what happened at their own door, yard or curb.

We also want to be clear about what we could not find. Our research turned up no Reddit threads, Yelp reviews or local news stories about home security cameras in Clason Point specifically, no HPD or 311 records pulled for camera complaints here, and no Landmarks Preservation Commission district on the peninsula, although we have not checked LPC's maps line by line. A short Reddit comment describes the area as having nice new builds and the ferry but a long trip to stores and the train, which matches the quiet, residential character of most blocks. Where this page describes how a Shorehaven front or a Harding Park bungalow is built, it relies on the sources above and on what we find in buildings of that type. The only way to know your walls and sight lines is to stand in front of them.

Six facts about the point that change a camera plan

  • A gate that does not hold packages: Shorehaven's contracted guards announce visitors and watch vehicles at the entrance, and the guidelines state they do not accept packages for residents.
  • Exterior walls that belong to a board: under Shorehaven's 2020 rules, roofs and exterior walls are the condominium Board of Managers' responsibility, and the front of a unit is shared by its lower and upper homes.
  • Lanes nine to thirteen feet wide: Harding Park's 226 bungalows face private paved lanes without sidewalks, so a front camera sees across the lane almost by default.
  • Ground that floods: about 80 Harding Park buildings sit in the one-percent floodplain, the shoreline flood zones grew after Sandy, and Wikipedia notes that many streets flood after heavy rain.
  • Houses with several doors: roughly a thousand two-family lots and nearly five hundred walk-ups, many with side or basement entrances, plus newer two- and three-unit rowhouses since the 1990s.
  • Public housing with its own cameras: NYCHA's Clason Point Gardens, opened in 1941, and nearby Monroe Houses, where NYCHA added new CCTV in 2023, are NYCHA's to equip.

What owners here often add next. Once a Clason Point owner can see the front step on a phone, the next request is usually to answer the door from that phone too, or to see who is at the side entrance of a two-family before releasing it. A doorbell camera, an intercom and an exterior camera can share one doorway and sometimes one cable path. Tell us what is already on your door when you book, and we will place the camera so it complements the bell instead of crowding it.

Water, Lanes, Courts and Curbs: Reading the View Before Choosing the Camera

On most blocks in the city, the first camera question is the wall. On Clason Point the first question is the view, because the same peninsula hands you four very different things to look at: open water, a lane narrower than a living room is long, a court of matching townhome fronts, or a curb lined with parked cars. Each one fails in its own way if the camera is chosen first and pointed second. We decide what each camera is for, who has to agree to it, and what it must not see, and only then pick the camera and the mount.

When the view is water

A camera that looks toward the East River, the Bronx River or Pugsley Creek is looking toward the brightest thing in the picture for part of every clear day. Low sun bouncing off water can wash out the foreground so badly that a person standing on your step becomes a silhouette. The fix is rarely a better camera. It is usually a better angle: mount the camera so the water is behind it or to the side, tilt it down onto the step or the yard, and let the sky and river fall out of the top of the frame. Where the water side has to be covered, we pick a camera with strong backlight handling and set it up looking along the shoreline rather than straight out. We also think about weather. Wind off the water shakes a tall mount, and spray, fog and drizzle sit on a lens and blur the night picture, so water-facing cameras go under an eave where possible, on a short, rigid bracket, with stainless hardware.

When the view is a narrow lane

In Harding Park the lane is the street, the sidewalk and the front yard all at once, and it is only nine to thirteen feet wide south of Gildersleeve. A camera on the front of a bungalow sees the lane in front, the doors either side and, unless it is carefully aimed, straight into the windows opposite. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera that intrudes on their property, and on a lane this tight that risk is real even for an owner with the best intentions. So we frame lane cameras tight on your own door and frontage, mount them lower and angled along the lane rather than across it, and mask any window or door across the way. A camera aimed along the lane also shows people coming and going, which is usually what an owner actually wants to know.

When the view is a court of matching fronts

Shorehaven's townhomes were built in phases with the same facades repeated down a court. That has two consequences. First, your front is not entirely yours: the rules treat it as common property shared with the unit above or below, so a camera there should cover both doors fairly or neither, and the other owner should know about it. Second, the board cares about appearance and the building envelope, because it is responsible for exterior walls. A small dome or turret in a neutral color, a short cable route sealed behind the mount, and a clean drawing showing exactly where each camera goes make that conversation short. We prepare that drawing as part of the quote.

When the view is a parked car

On the blocks near the ferry, and anywhere a driveway is shared, the car an owner worries about is often at the curb. A camera can record the curb in front of your own house well, provided it is mounted close enough and lit well enough. What it cannot do from a high corner of the house is identify a person forty feet down the block at night. We set curb cameras to cover the length of your frontage, at a height that sees into the space between parked cars, and we are honest about where the useful picture ends.

Bungalows: light framing, low eaves and a lane in front

A Harding Park bungalow is usually wood frame, and many have low eaves that put a camera well within reach of anyone on the lane. We look for the highest solid backing on the front, often a fascia board or the framing above the door, and use a mounting block where the camera has to sit on siding so the screws hold in wood and the base stays sealed. Cable through a frame wall is short and easy, and in a bungalow it usually goes up rather than down: into an attic space or a high closet where the recorder can live well above any water that reaches the floor. Many bungalows have been added onto over the years, so walls that look continuous from outside may hide an old exterior wall inside. We check before drilling and route around surprises instead of through them.

Townhomes: neat, approved and inside your own unit

The townhomes behind the gate are newer construction, so the work is cleaner but the rules are stricter. Because exterior walls belong to the board, we keep penetrations to the minimum, place the cable entry directly behind the camera base so nothing runs along the facade, seal it with an outdoor-rated sealant, and choose housings that suit a repeated facade. Inside, we follow the owner's own walls and ceilings to a closet or utility space, never into the other unit. Where the owner wants the water side covered, we use the back of the unit's own wall or balcony structure, angled so the river does not wash out the picture.

Brick and frame houses on the grid

Older grid houses mix brick fronts with frame sides and rears. On brick we anchor into the face of sound brick rather than the mortar, drill once for the cable at a slight downward slope toward the outside, sleeve the hole and seal it, and put the connection in a weatherproof junction box so it is protected from rain and road spray. On frame walls we use siding blocks and find the studs. The one habit we change here, compared with drier parts of the borough, is where the cable ends up: we run it up to an upstairs closet or the owner's floor instead of down to a cellar, because these blocks have flooded before and the recorder is the part that holds every bit of footage.

Rowhouses with garages and stacked doors

The two- and three-unit rowhouses built since the 1990s often have a garage or driveway at grade and the front doors up a short flight. That garage is the obvious place for a recorder and the wrong one on a low block. We use the garage ceiling as a cable path when it helps, but put the recorder, switch and battery backup on an upper floor or at least high on a wall. At the front, one camera usually covers the stacked doors and the steps, framed so each household's door is visible without the camera looking into anyone's window.

Association Clubhouse and Pool

Shorehaven's rules mention a clubhouse, pool, playgrounds and an after-hours clubhouse alarm. Cameras there are the association's decision and are contracted by the association, not by individual owners.

Landlord-Owned Walk-Ups

Owners of private walk-ups and small apartment buildings on the grid, who are running a business as much as a home and need cameras that hold up when an incident has to be reviewed.

A shop on a quiet street needs a different plan

A business on Soundview Avenue is not on a crowded strip. After closing there may be few people on the block, so a camera cannot rely on passers-by or neighboring shop lights. We start with the front door from inside, set at a height that catches a face as it comes through the door, then the counter and register seen from above so hands and cash are clear, then the back or side door and the stock area. Outside, a camera under the sign or awning covers the sidewalk directly in front, with its own light if the street lighting is weak. The recorder goes in a locked back room or cabinet, on battery backup, and the owner gets the app so a motion alert at two in the morning can be checked from home without calling anyone.

Lots, loading and the Bruckner side

Premises facing the Bruckner or set around a parking lot have long distances and steady traffic. Wide cameras on the building show the lot as a whole, but they rarely identify anyone. We pair them with narrower cameras at the points every person or vehicle has to pass, such as an entrance gap or a loading door, mounted high enough to be out of reach and aimed so headlights and streetlights sit outside the frame. Cable outside within reach goes into metal conduit, and long inside runs ride on hooks or tray rather than on ducts and light fittings.

Rules and practical limits for business and building owners

  • No general camera requirement. Security guidance on NYC building rules says there is no general city law requiring cameras in apartment buildings; the same is true of most shops. The owner chooses the scope.
  • Common areas belong to the landlord. Brick Underground's guidance is that tenants need the landlord's permission to install anything in common areas, including the hallway outside their own door, and that landlords may put cameras in common areas but may not aim them into apartments.
  • Write down the reason. For rent-stabilized buildings, Seneca Security advises owners to document their security reasons in writing and tell tenants before cameras go in. We include a short written purpose for each common-area camera with the quote.
  • Light before lens. A camera aimed at a dark corridor or an unlit side yard records almost nothing useful. We note every position that needs light and say so before the camera is chosen.
  • Sealed openings. Where camera cable passes through a fire-rated wall or floor, the opening is sealed with rated firestopping. On outside walls, every hole is sealed against water, which on this peninsula matters as much as fire.
  • New circuits are an electrician's job. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable powered from the recorder or a switch. If a shop wants a new outlet or circuit, a licensed electrician does it, and our quote says so.
  • Private places stay private. No cameras in restrooms, changing areas or other places people expect privacy, and we tell you plainly if a requested view crosses that line.
  • City and NYCHA property is not ours to use. We do not mount cameras on park fences, street poles or NYCHA buildings for private customers.

Field Notes: Walking the Lane and the Water Side First

Security Camera Installation in Clason Point: Questions Answered

Behind the Shorehaven gate

The guards at the entrance announce visitors and watch cars, but they won't hold packages. Does anything at the gate actually cover our front step?

No. The association's guidelines describe the guards' job as controlling the entrance, announcing visitors and monitoring the vehicles that come in, and they state that security does not accept packages for residents. A 2020 trade report described a planned gatehouse renovation with perimeter cameras, but we have no confirmation it was carried out, and either way gate and perimeter cameras watch the edges of the community, not your door. A delivery left on your step is covered only by a camera you put there, framed on the step from the side so it catches the courier's face and the parcel.

The front of our two-level building is shared with the unit upstairs. Can one of us put a camera there?

Shorehaven's rules treat the front of a condo unit as common property shared by the lower and upper units, and they put exterior walls under the condominium Board of Managers. So a camera on that front needs the board's approval, and in practice the other owner's agreement. The arrangement that works best is a single small camera that covers both doors evenly, with both households able to view it in the app. We prepare a drawing showing the position, the view and the cable entry so the board and your neighbor can see exactly what is proposed.

Our townhome row faces the river. Will sunlight off the water blind the camera?

For part of every clear day, a camera pointed toward open water is pointed at a mirror, and the foreground can turn into a silhouette. We avoid that with placement before equipment: the camera goes where the water is behind it or to the side, tilted down onto the step or walkway so the bright band falls out of the top of the frame. Where the water side itself has to be watched, we use a camera with strong backlight handling aimed along the shore. We check the view on a phone at the time of day the glare is worst.

We bought one of the newer Riverwalk townhomes. How do we find out whether it was wired for cameras when it was built?

The sources we read do not say what low-voltage wiring the builder included, so we would not assume either way. Signs to look for are blank cover plates high on outside walls or under the eaves, a cluster of unused cables in a closet or utility area, and network cables that run to rooms with no jacks. On a survey we trace anything we find to see where it ends and whether it is sound enough to carry a camera. Reusing a good existing run means one less hole in a wall the board looks after.

Harding Park bungalows

Our bungalow sits right on a private lane with no sidewalk. What should a front camera actually be watching?

Your door, your step and your own frontage, and people coming and going along the lane, rather than the houses opposite. DCP describes homes here fronting narrow paved lanes without sidewalks, nine to thirteen feet wide south of Gildersleeve, so a wide camera aimed straight out shows little except the neighbor's front window. We mount the camera to look along the lane at a slight angle, frame it tight on your entrance, and mask anything across the way. That framing also identifies visitors better, because they walk toward the camera instead of across it.

Do we need the Harding Park Homeowners Association's sign-off before putting cameras on our bungalow?

The homes sit on land the association owns cooperatively and administers, so check its rules before any exterior work. We did not find published camera rules for Harding Park, and we will not guess what they say. Ask the association office, and if they want details, send them our drawing showing each camera's position, size and view. A camera on your own wall aimed at your own door and frontage is usually the easiest request to approve, especially when the drawing shows masks over the homes across the lane.

Our bungalow has low eaves. Where can a camera go so nobody on the lane can just reach up and grab it?

Wherever the highest solid backing is, which on many bungalows is the fascia, a gable end or the framing above the door. If the highest point is still within reach, we use a dome housing that is hard to knock out of aim, a vandal-resistant mount, and a cable that enters the wall directly behind the base so there is nothing to pull. A camera that is a little lower but tucked into a corner, looking along the lane, often stays safer than one stuck out on a long arm.

We are replacing our old bungalow with a new two-unit house. When should the camera cable go in?

Before the walls are closed. A DOB filing reported by Marketproof in February 2026 shows this kind of rebuild happening on Harding Park, a three-story, two-unit building replacing a 1945 house. During framing, we can run cable from each planned camera position to a dry, upper-floor spot for the recorder in a few hours, with no exterior drilling later. Bring us the plans, mark the doors and the lane side, and we will mark camera positions and cable routes that your contractor can work around.

Houses on the city streets

Our basement apartment's door is down a few steps at the side of the house. Can a camera get a usable face there?

Yes, if it is mounted at the bottom of the steps or close above the door, not on the main wall high overhead. A camera looking down a stairwell from the top sees heads and shoulders; one set near the door, angled up the steps, sees faces as people come down. Below-grade entrances are often dark, so we check whether the existing light reaches the door and add a camera with its own light if it does not. Since a tenant uses that door, we plan it so the camera covers the entry only and gives the tenant their own app view if the owner agrees.

We live a few blocks from the water. Will salt air corrode the camera and its mount?

Over the years, ordinary steel screws and cheap brackets near the water rust, stain the wall and loosen. We use stainless steel fixings, sealed junction boxes for every outdoor connection and housings rated for outdoor use, and we keep connectors inside the box rather than hanging in the open air. A weatherproof rating tells you the camera resists rain and dust; it says nothing about how the screws holding it up will age. Once a year, check the mounts and wipe the housings while you are at it.

Mist off the river leaves the night picture milky and full of glowing dots. What helps?

The dots are usually infrared light bouncing off droplets, dust or spider silk right in front of the lens, and the milkiness is moisture on the dome. Mounting under an eave or soffit keeps most of the wet off. Turning off the camera's built-in infrared and lighting the scene with a separate light set a little away from the camera stops the bounce. On the most exposed positions we choose a camera with its own visible light and color night mode, and we show you how to clean the lens without scratching it.

Public housing

Apartments at Clason Point Gardens open onto outdoor walks, not hallways. Does that change what a resident can put up?

Not really. The walks, doors and exterior walls of the garden buildings are NYCHA property, and residents cannot make building alterations, so a camera screwed to the outside wall or the frame is not an option, however short the walk to the street. The realistic private choices are an in-unit camera aimed through your own window or door, or a no-drill device that follows NYCHA's own guidance for resident doors. Check NYCHA's current rules or ask your management office first, and we will help you set up a device that fits them.

Tenants say a Gardens parking lot became a temporary garbage area. Can we put up cameras to catch whoever is dumping?

Not on NYCHA's lot. News 12 Bronx reported in July 2026 that tenants said a parking lot at the complex had been turned into a temporary garbage area overrun with trash and rodents, and the lot, its lighting and any cameras on it are NYCHA's to manage. The routes for residents are the tenant association, NYCHA's management office and repair requests, and elected officials. We would rather tell you that plainly than install equipment on property neither you nor we can authorize.

Shops and small businesses

Ours is one of only a few shops on our stretch of Soundview Avenue, and the block empties out at night. What should the cameras cover first?

The door, from inside and outside. On a quiet block a break-in usually means the front door, a window next to it or the back door, and there may be no one on the street to see. Start with an inside camera framed on the door at face height, an outside camera covering the sidewalk in front with its own light, and one on the rear or side entrance. Add the counter and register next. Keep the recorder locked away from the front of the shop and on battery backup, so the cameras keep recording if someone cuts the power on the way in.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Clason Point

Below are claims that turn up in quick online answers about home cameras in New York and on the waterfront, set against the sources on this page and what we see on site. Cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr shape many of those answers; they are a fair starting point for a budget, but none of them knows whether your camera faces a river, a lane or a court.

"A gated, guarded community doesn't need private cameras."

Shorehaven's gate is a real layer of security, and its guards do exactly what the association says: control the entrance, announce visitors and watch the vehicles that come in. That is a check on who arrives, not a record of what happens at each door.

The same guidelines say security does not accept packages for residents. Couriers who are let through still leave parcels on steps, and the gate camera, if there is one pointed at the entrance, will not show your step.

So owners inside the gate still ask for a door camera, mostly for deliveries and visitors. The difference is scale: one or two small cameras, approved by the board, rather than a whole perimeter.

"Any outdoor camera with a weatherproof rating will be fine near the water."

An outdoor rating describes how well a housing keeps out rain and dust. It does not describe how the screws, brackets and connectors around it will cope with years of salt air and wind off the East River.

The failures we look for near water are rust running down a wall from a mild steel bracket, a connector corroded where it hung outside a junction box, and a tall arm that shakes in the wind so every motion alert is the camera itself moving.

The answer is unglamorous: stainless fixings, sealed boxes for every connection, short rigid mounts, and a look at the hardware once a year. None of that shows up on a product box.

"Put the recorder in the basement where it's out of the way."

That is common advice for houses anywhere, and on much of the peninsula it is the wrong place. DCP puts about 80 Harding Park buildings in the one-percent floodplain, the shoreline flood zones grew after Sandy, and Wikipedia notes that many streets flood after heavy rain.

The recorder holds every hour of footage, and the battery backup and switch beside it keep the system alive. A foot of water on a basement floor, which Bronx Ink found in one Harding Park home during Sandy, ends all three.

The city's advice to Harding Park was to elevate critical systems. For a camera system, that means an upper-floor closet, an attic space in a bungalow, or at least a high wall shelf, with cable run up instead of down.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer for older houses."

Wireless cameras are easy to put up, and in some places, such as a NYCHA apartment or a rented floor, they may be the only thing allowed. But "wireless" usually refers to the picture, not the power, and a camera still needs a battery that runs down or an outlet nearby.

On a bungalow, signal has to get through framing, siding and sometimes an old exterior wall buried inside an addition. On a townhome, a router on the ground floor may struggle to reach a camera at the top of the stairs.

For owners who can run cable, a short wired run through a frame wall is often simpler in the long run than chasing batteries and signal drops. We say which suits each position, and we set up wireless devices properly where they are the right call.

"New York City law requires a notice sign wherever there are building cameras."

Vendor and legal-information sites disagree here. One vendor blog says Local Law 50 requires residential buildings with common-area cameras to notify residents; another site cites "Local Law 3 of 2019." We could not confirm either, and they do not agree with each other.

What is well supported is narrower: landlords may put cameras in common areas but may not aim them into apartments, and owners of rent-stabilized buildings are advised to document their security reasons and tell tenants in advance.

Telling residents is good practice even if no law demands it, and a visible camera with a plain sign discourages more than a hidden one. For a firm legal answer, ask an attorney, not a blog, including ours.

"Night vision means you'll see everything after dark."

Infrared lets a camera see in the dark close up, in black and white, as far as its own small lamps reach. It does not light a park edge, a long backyard running down to Pugsley Creek or a lane between bungalows.

Near the water, infrared has a second problem: it bounces off mist, drizzle and spider silk in front of the lens, filling the picture with glowing specks. Residents at Clason Point Gardens have also described exterior light problems, a reminder that dark ground is common here.

So we pair every night view with real light on the target, use light-equipped cameras on exposed positions, and tell you which views will identify a person at night and which will only show that someone was there.

"Clason Point is a high-crime area, so cover everything."

The record is more mixed than a one-line summary. Homes.com's reading of the CAP Index puts overall crime on par with the national score while rating some violent categories high, and local news has reported shootings near Randall Avenue and Commonwealth Avenue in 2026.

For a homeowner, that argues for a few cameras placed where they matter, the doors, the step, the side entrance, the car, rather than a ring of cameras that mostly record the neighbors and the sky.

More cameras also mean more footage nobody watches and more chances to intrude on someone else's yard. A tight plan with good light usually serves a Clason Point house better than a big one.

Clason Point Security Camera Installation FAQ

Our association talked about adding perimeter cameras at the gate. Should we wait before putting cameras on our own townhome?
+

There is no need to wait. A 2020 trade report described a planned gatehouse renovation with perimeter cameras, but we found nothing confirming it was completed, and perimeter cameras watch the edges and entrance of a community, not individual doors. Your doorstep, your delivery spot and the shared front of your building are separate questions, and only a camera you put there, with the board's approval, will cover them.

What should we give the condo board when we ask to put cameras on the outside of our unit?
+

A simple drawing showing each camera's position on the facade, its size and color, where the cable enters the wall and how the opening is sealed, plus a picture of what each camera will see. If the front is shared with another unit, include that owner's agreement. We prepare the drawing and the sample views with the quote, which usually answers the board's questions before they are asked.

Can the whole camera job be done from outside, or does the technician need to come into the house?
+

A battery or plug-in device can sometimes be set up from outside, but a wired system needs inside access, because the cable comes through the wall and the recorder lives indoors. On the peninsula we also want to see where the recorder will sit, so it is above any water that might reach the lower floor. We keep the inside work to the closet or utility space the cable passes through and tell you in advance which rooms we need.

Our yard holds water after heavy rain. Can camera cable be buried across it to reach a fence or detached garage?
+

We avoid burying camera cable in ground that ponds, because water sits around the run and finds any weak joint. Where a camera must reach a fence or outbuilding, we prefer an overhead or wall-mounted route, cable rated for outdoor and wet locations, sealed connections inside a box mounted well above the ground, and a drip loop at every entry. Sometimes a wireless link for that one camera is the better answer.

A Wi-Fi camera on our top-floor balcony keeps dropping off. Is the router just too far away?
+

Often, yes. A router on the ground floor of a townhome has to push signal up through floors and out through an exterior wall, and a camera on the far side of the building may get only a weak connection. Moving the router, adding a wired access point upstairs, or running one cable to that camera usually fixes it. We test the signal at the camera position before recommending which.

A coastal storm is coming. Is there anything we should do with the camera system beforehand?
+

Save any footage you want to keep to your phone or a USB drive, make sure the battery backup is plugged in and charged, and check that nothing in the system is sitting on a floor that could take water. If the recorder or switch is in a basement or garage, move anything you safely can to a higher shelf. Do not unplug outdoor cameras. After the storm, check each camera tile in the app and call us if any stay dark.

The house came with a recorder in the basement that once sat in floodwater. Can we keep using it?
+

We would not rely on it. Electronics that have been underwater can work for a while and then fail without warning, and the hard drive that holds the footage is the part most likely to go. We test what is there, recover any footage you need if possible, and usually recommend replacing the recorder and moving the new one to a higher, dry spot. Cable that was submerged gets tested too.

Can our homeowners association hire you for cameras at the clubhouse, pool or a shared path?
+

Yes, when the association itself contracts the work. Shared facilities belong to the association, so the request, the written approval and the payment should come from it, not from one owner. We walk the space with the association's representative, plan each camera's purpose and view, and quote in writing. We will not install cameras on common property at an individual owner's request.

Can an association use its outdoor cameras to check whether residents are following the rules?
+

That question gets asked on legal Q&A sites, and the answer depends on the association's governing documents and the law, so it is one for an attorney. What we can say is how we install: common-area cameras are placed for the safety and security of shared spaces, aimed away from the inside of homes, and the association should decide in writing who may view footage and why. Clear rules prevent most disputes.

Will a camera on a pole in a windy yard near the water stay steady enough to be useful?
+

Only if the pole and mount are rigid and set properly. A tall, thin pole sways in a strong wind off the river, which blurs the picture and triggers motion alerts all night. We prefer mounting on the house or a solid structure.
