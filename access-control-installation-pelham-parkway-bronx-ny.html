Pelham Parkway, Bronx

Arch First · Every Lobby on Its Own Reader · Ornament Left Intact · Free Exit Always

Pelham Parkway was laid out as a park drive before it was a neighborhood. The rules for the parkway kept buildings well back from its center and kept bars and hotels off it, and from 1927 builders filled the blocks to the south with six-story elevator apartment houses for families moving up from older parts of the city. Some, like the Alhambra Gardens of 1928, were “garden apartment houses”: residents walked under an arch from the parkway into a planted courtyard and only then reached one of several separate lobbies. Many of those buildings are co-ops now, landmark protection is rare for buildings of their type in the Bronx, and their entrances still have the cast stone, terrazzo and decorative doors they were built with. We plan entry for that shape: the arch as the first door, each lobby on its own reader, one call that finds the right apartment, and hardware fixed so that it can come off one day without taking the ornament with it.

The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Parkway Properties Need Access Control

The neighborhood takes its name from the road, and the road came first. According to NYC Parks, the Bronx and Pelham Parkway grew out of John Mullaly’s New York Park Association of 1881; the land was taken by condemnation in 1884 and named by an act of the Legislature on June 14 of that year, to tie Bronx Park to Pelham Bay Park. Parks describes it today as 2.5 miles long and about as wide as a city block, with three traffic lanes each way, two large center plots, two marginal streets, a bridle path and its American elms. The White Plains Road Business Improvement District’s history gives 2.3 miles and 400 feet, and dates the first single lane to 1911. The sources do not agree on the numbers, and we give both.

What the sources do agree on is the rule that shaped the buildings. The BID’s history and Wikipedia both record a strict building code for the parkway: nobody could build within 150 feet of its center, bars and hotels were barred from its edge, and no railroad could cross it, which is why the old commuter line that became the 5 train’s Dyre Avenue branch runs through a tunnel under the green. So the apartment houses stand back behind the service roads, facing a park, and their front doors open onto a quiet marginal street rather than a commercial avenue. That is a pleasant front door to come home to. It is also a front door with lawns and trees across the street instead of shop windows, and no storefront light falling on it at night.

The buildings came in a rush. David Bady’s essay for Lehman College explains that a developer bought a tract of the Astor estate of 1,000 building lots in 1927, and that by the following spring thirteen buildings were finished or under way, with plans filed for nine more. The buyers he describes were families who had come up from the Lower East Side to the older Bronx and now wanted better homes near the new subway lines. Wikipedia’s summary of a Columbia University preservation study calls the result a compact stretch of the speculative six-story elevator apartment house, a building type so common across the city that it rarely gets noticed. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as dominated by six- and seven-story elevator apartment and co-op buildings, with detached houses and larger Art Deco and Tudor apartment buildings among them.

The Alhambra Gardens is the example Bady chose, and it is the reason this page is built around arches and courtyards. Designed by Springsteen and Goldhammer and renting from October 1928, it is a 132-unit, six-story U-shaped building that fills an end-block on Pelham Parkway South between Holland and Wallace Avenues. Its six linked sections wrap a deep planted court. Residents, Bady writes, passed beneath small arches from the parkway into the courtyard, where paths led them to separate lobbies whose elevators served only three or four apartments on each floor. Other Pelham Parkway apartment houses of the same years stood shoulder to shoulder on the street and used light courts instead. Either way, the entrance sequence was designed, and it is still there.

Many of these buildings changed hands, and some changed form. Wikipedia notes that a number of them were converted to cooperative ownership as the neighborhood recovered after late-century decline, and that, although most households rent, there is a large community of co-op owners. The Bronx Art Deco Apartment House Archive adds a warning that applies directly to entry work: only three individual Art Deco apartment houses in the Bronx, plus 27 inside the Grand Concourse Historic District, have landmark designation, the Landmarks Preservation Commission rarely includes lobbies in its designations, and the archive keeps a “Lost Deco” list of buildings damaged by insensitive repair and bad renovation. In Pelham Parkway, the board and the installer are usually the only protection a 1930s entrance has.

The neighborhood’s own news shows why the entry matters. In August 2011 the Bronx Times reported that tenants of the Alhambra Gardens called a meeting after a push-in robbery in one of its apartments. Their camera system had been switched off after a change of ownership, because the previous owner had left a bill unpaid. A housing specialist working with the tenants said people were dealing drugs in the courtyard because word had spread that the cameras did not work, and that cameras were needed in the lobby, on the roof and in the courtyard. The new management said it had installed new locks on all five of the building’s front doors, because the old ones did not comply with city housing rules. Lehman counts six entrances around the court; the Bronx Times counts five front doors. We give both and do not try to settle it.

Around the apartment houses the neighborhood has the ordinary edges of a city district. Wikipedia sets its boundaries as Pelham Parkway South to the north, the 5 train’s Dyre Avenue tracks to the east, Bronxdale Avenue to the south and Bronx Park East to the west, with White Plains Road as the main shopping street; it sits in Community District 11 and is patrolled by the NYPD’s 49th Precinct. NYCHA’s Pelham Parkway Houses, 23 six-story buildings completed in June 1950 according to Wikipedia, stand north of the parkway and are named on this page only as a landmark; AESS does not work for NYCHA. Our work here is on privately owned apartment houses and co-ops, small walk-ups and newer two- and three-unit row houses, and the stores along Lydig Avenue and White Plains Road.

Arch Gate Stuck or a Lobby Reader Dark in Pelham Parkway?

A courtyard building fails in its own particular ways. The arch gate’s closer weakens and the gate rests an inch short of the latch, so the whole court is open while every lobby still looks locked. One lobby’s reader goes dark and its residents start propping the door with a mat. A ninety-year-old door has settled on its hinges and the strike buzzes without releasing. Ring (646) 490-0629, say which door and what it is doing; most single faults are cleared within an hour or two of the technician arriving, and anything that could keep someone from getting out is made safe first.

We work on systems other companies put in, including panels whose maker has disappeared and readers whose administrator password left with a former managing agent. Tell us which lobby or gate is affected and who in the building holds the administrator account. Shareholders and tenants should go through the board or the management office; we work with their authorization.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Pelham Parkway’s Architecture

Six kinds of building share these streets. We describe them from the sources cited on this page and from what anyone can see from the public sidewalk; no building named here is an AESS customer, and none of the photographs on this page was taken in Pelham Parkway.

Courtyard “garden apartment houses”

U-shaped or closed plans around a planted court, entered through one or more arches, with several lobbies each serving its own stair and elevator. The arch is the real front door, the court is a shared outdoor hall, and fire escapes often come down into it. Each lobby may have its own street number, its own mailboxes and its own history of intercom panels.

Shoulder-to-shoulder six-story apartment houses

The more common Pelham Parkway type: one lobby on the street and light courts cut into the rear or sides, as Lehman describes them; in our experience of the type, laundry and refuse rooms sit in the basement and a bulkhead tops the stair. One street door, one inner door, and a vestibule between them that was designed for a buzzer panel and a row of mailboxes.

Art Deco apartment houses of 1929–1941

BADAHA’s period for the Bronx style: polychrome brick, cast-stone entrance portals, terrazzo lobby floors in geometric patterns, decorative plaster and lobbies that climb a few steps to a landing. Entrances like these are the hardest to modernize well and the easiest to spoil, because the ornament is exactly where hardware wants to go.

Co-ops converted from rentals

Buildings where a board decides, a managing agent runs the day-to-day, and shareholders, subtenants and, often, rent-regulated tenants who stayed through the conversion all live behind the same door. Entry work here starts with a written policy and an approval path, not with a hardware catalog.

Detached houses and newer row houses

Wikipedia notes detached houses on the residential streets and, in the last decade, new two- and three-unit row houses and small apartment buildings. These have their own front doors and often a separate entrance for a rental unit; they are planned door by door, with the multiple-dwelling rules checked when a third unit appears.

Public housing north of the parkway

NYCHA’s Pelham Parkway Houses, 23 six-story buildings completed in 1950. They are named here only as a landmark: their doors, intercoms and repairs are NYCHA’s responsibility and AESS has no role there. Residents with a door problem should contact NYCHA.

How the 1920s plan changes the method

  • Count routes, not doors. In a courtyard building every resident, guest, courier and porter starts at the same arch and ends at different doors. We draw those routes before choosing a single reader, because the arch decides how much each lobby has to do.
  • One controller for the whole court. Lobbies that were wired at different times with different panels are brought onto one controller and one credential list, so a fob lost in lobby A can be cancelled at every door in one step.
  • Survey the ornament first. Before quoting, we photograph each entrance and mark what must not be drilled: carved or cast stone, original bronze or brass, decorative ironwork, terrazzo and mosaic. Hardware goes on metal frames, plain vestibule walls, mortar joints and new posts.
  • Use the paths the building already has. Old bell and intercom wiring often runs in conduit from the vestibule to the cellar. Where it tests sound we pull new cable through the same route rather than cutting a new one through plaster or stone.
  • The board’s policy, written down. Who may open which lobby, how long entry records are kept and who may read them are board decisions. We put them in writing at installation, so the next board inherits a policy and not a mystery.

What to gather before the first board discussion

  • The lobby count and names. How many lobbies open off the court, what staff and residents call each one, and which street number each uses.
  • What is on the doors now. A photo of each panel, reader and strike, and the name of whoever installed or services them, if anyone still knows.
  • Who holds the keys and accounts. The super, the managing agent, past board members: who can add or delete a fob today, and in whose name the accounts are registered.
  • The exits. Where the fire escapes come down, which doors are exits, and whether the building has a fire alarm system that doors could be tied to.
  • The protected surfaces. Any portal, mosaic, terrazzo or original metalwork the board wants left untouched, photographed and listed.
  • The budget shape. Whether the board wants everything at once or in stages, so that the first quote can size the controller for the whole plan.

The Courtyard

Through the Arch: The Court Is the First Door

In an ordinary apartment house the sequence is short: sidewalk, street door, vestibule, inner door, lobby. A garden apartment house adds a stretch of open air in the middle. A visitor passes the arch, crosses a court that may be eighty or a hundred feet deep, and only then picks one of several doors. Everything about planning entry here follows from that extra stretch, because the court is shared by every lobby and belongs to none of them.

When the arch stands open, the court behaves like a public plaza that happens to be surrounded by bedrooms. That is what the 2011 Bronx Times story described: once word spread that the cameras were off, people who did not live there used the courtyard as a place to do business. When the arch is locked, the court becomes a private hall, quiet and safe for children, but also a dead end for anyone coming down a fire escape, and an extra door for every guest, courier, porter and paramedic to get through. Neither state is right by default. The decision is the board’s, and the hardware has to make whichever decision it takes work every night.

The method we use is simple to describe. First, decide whether the arch is a barrier or only a doorway. If it is a barrier, it gets a lock, a closer, a call panel with a directory, and a free-exit device on the court side that anyone can open without a key. Second, decide what each lobby still has to do once the arch is locked. In most buildings the answer is “almost everything”: lobby doors stay on readers, because residents of one lobby should not have keys to the others, and because the arch will sometimes be open for a move, a delivery or a repair. Third, decide who may cross the court to which door: residents to their own lobby and the shared rooms, couriers to the parcel lockers, porters everywhere, guests only to the lobby that let them in.

The last decision is how a visitor finds the way. A five-lobby building with one directory panel at the arch has to tell the visitor more than “press 4C”. The panel should show which lobby 4C belongs to, and the lobby doors should be lettered or numbered clearly enough to be read from the arch at night. Where a building has its own street number for each section, the directory can use those numbers instead. The goal is a guest who walks straight to the right door, rather than one who tries each lobby in turn while residents watch from the windows.

Weather is the arch’s other test. An arch through a six-story building can act as a funnel, pushing a gate open or holding it short of the latch on a windy night, and snow blown into the passage can stop a gate from closing at all. We set closers for the worst wind the passage sees rather than the calmest, fit a contact that reports a gate that has not latched, and tell the super which way to clear snow so the gate’s swing is never blocked. These are practice notes, not local measurements; every arch is tested in its own conditions.

Sample access plan for a courtyard building, drawn by AESS. Each board sets its own groups; free exit from inside is available at every door regardless.

  • At the arch: the directory panel, the arch lock and closer, a free-exit handle or bar on the court side, and a way for the fire service to get in.
  • Across the court: lighting on the paths and clear lobby lettering; cameras only where the board decides it wants them.
  • At each lobby’s street door: a reader for that lobby’s residents and staff, released remotely for a guest the resident has let in at the arch.
  • At each inner door: the same reader group, so a resident who forgets a fob at work is not trapped in the vestibule.
  • At the shared rooms: laundry and package rooms opened by residents of every lobby, on their own hours.
  • At the cellar and refuse doors: staff and booked trades only, with a contact so the super knows when one is left open.

Original Entrances

Keeping a 1930s Entrance Off the “Lost Deco” List

BADAHA’s description of the Bronx Art Deco apartment house reads like a list of the places an installer is tempted to drill: cast-stone entrance portals, terrazzo lobby floors laid in geometric patterns, decorative plaster pilasters and ceilings, and lobbies that rise by a few steps from the foyer to a landing. The archive’s point is that these features are mostly unprotected, and that its “Lost Deco” entries record what happens to them through insensitive repair. Security work is one of the most common ways it happens: a surface conduit stapled across a portal, a panel screwed into carved stone, a slot cut through a terrazzo floor for a cable.

None of that is necessary. Almost every entrance has plain surfaces next to the ornamental ones. Door frames are usually metal. Vestibules usually have a plain wall. Mortar joints can take a fixing and be repointed afterwards; carved stone cannot. A call panel can stand on a slim post in front of the entrance or sit inside the vestibule where the old buzzer panel was, instead of on the portal itself. Cable can follow the conduit the original bell wiring used, run through a hollow transom bar, or drop into the cellar through a hole drilled in plain plaster behind the panel. A reader can sit on the steel door frame at a height a wheelchair user can reach.

The other half of the method is restraint about what goes on the street face at all. Readers and panels are small and dark; posts can be painted to match the stonework or the ironwork; the controller, power supply and battery go in the cellar, not in a box on the lobby wall. Nothing about a modern system requires a lobby to look modern. A board that wants the entrance to read as 1930s from across the parkway can have that, and still have fobs, video calls and logs.

Finally, every fixing is recorded. A board in twenty years may want a different system, or none. If the handover record lists every hole and what it is in, removing our work is a matter of a few hours and some filler, not a restoration project. We think that is the right standard for a building that has kept its entrance for ninety years.

After 2011

Five Front Doors, a Dark Camera System and What a Board Can Learn From It

The 2011 Bronx Times story is worth reading slowly, because almost every problem in it is a door problem dressed as something else. The cameras went dark not because they broke but because a bill tied to the previous owner went unpaid when the building changed hands. The front-door locks were replaced not because they had failed but because they did not comply with city housing rules. The courtyard became a place for dealing not because anyone forced the arch, but because people knew nobody was watching. And the robbery that started the meeting was a push-in at an apartment door, the one layer the building’s front doors cannot protect on their own.

The lessons we take from it shape how we hand over a system. Everything the doors need to keep working, the controller, the power, the credentials and the administrator accounts, belongs to the building and lives in the building, so that a sale, an unpaid invoice or a change of managing agent cannot switch it off. The front-door hardware is chosen with the housing rules in mind from the start: the Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, in buildings of eight or more apartments, an intercom or equivalent, and the Housing Maintenance Code sets out the owner’s duties for entrance-door locks. And the board gets a plain statement of what the entry system can and cannot do at the apartment door, so that residents are not told that new fobs will stop push-ins.

The other lesson is about attention. A system that nobody checks becomes a system that only looks as though it works. Our upkeep schedule puts a closer check on the arch and on every lobby door twice a year, a battery test on every supply, and a credential review with the board, because a building with five lobbies has five times the chance that one door is quietly failing.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control on Lydig Avenue and White Plains Road

Pelham Parkway’s shops sit on two streets. White Plains Road runs north and south under the elevated 2 and 5, and Lydig Avenue crosses it a short walk south of the parkway. The city’s directory lists the White Plains Road Business Improvement District, formed in October 1994, as covering 7 block faces with 105 ground-floor retail businesses, and Wikipedia describes the shops of White Plains Road and Lydig Avenue as a mix of kosher and Italian butchers and bakeries beside restaurants from many countries. These are the settings we work in; no business on these streets is named as an AESS client.

Stores in the base of apartment houses

Where a store occupies the ground floor of an apartment building, its back door often opens into the building’s rear hall or yard, which makes it a door between a business and other people’s homes. It needs a reader on one side, free exit on both, and a schedule that matches the shop’s hours.

Sidewalk cellar doors

Steel doors set in the sidewalk that open onto a stair to the store’s cellar, used for deliveries. They are opened from below and should be closed and locked whenever nobody is working the stair; a contact on them tells the owner when one is left open.

Under the El on White Plains Road

Storefronts facing the elevated structure, with apartments above reached by a narrow street door between shops. The apartment door belongs to the building, not to any store, and needs its own panel and readers even when the store below has none.

Around Pelham Parkway station

The White Plains Road Line station at the parkway, which Wikipedia records opening on March 3, 1917 and the BID describes as the only station in the system built over parkland. Stores near its stairs see more people pausing in front of them, which shapes where a keypad goes and whether it should be a keypad at all.

Pharmacies, clinics and offices

Businesses whose back rooms hold medicines, records or cash, where the question is not only who enters the store but who enters one room inside it, and whether the log will satisfy the rules that apply to what is kept there.

Houses of worship and community rooms

Congregations and community organizations whose doors open for services, classes or meals at set hours. Wikipedia notes that Bronx House, the settlement house, moved to Pelham Parkway in the 1950s. A schedule that opens a door for its hours and locks it afterwards replaces the habit of propping it.

The rules a Pelham Parkway entry system has to meet

  • Free exit, always. Every electrically locked door and gate must open from the inside with one motion and no key or code. Where they apply, the NYC Building Code’s electrified-egress sections (§§1010.1.9.8–1010.1.9.9) add release on loss of power and on fire alarm, and put any manual release button between 40 and 48 inches above the floor. An arch gate that fire escapes discharge toward is part of the way out and is treated the same way.
  • Multiple dwellings. The Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, in buildings with eight or more apartments, an intercom or equivalent so tenants can identify and admit visitors; the Housing Maintenance Code adds owners’ duties for entrance-door locks.
  • Tenant data. The Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) limits what owners of multiple dwellings may collect and keep through smart access systems and requires written policies; we set the shortest retention the board needs and record the policy at handover.
  • Landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s review applies to designated buildings and districts, and the city’s landmark map shows whether a building is one. BADAHA counts only three individually designated Art Deco apartment houses in the Bronx outside the Grand Concourse district, which suggests that most Deco entrances here are not; the board’s own alteration rules apply either way.
  • Controlled substances. For pharmacies and practices, the federal rules in 21 CFR 1301.75 govern how controlled substances are stored. A door reader adds a layer and a log; it does not replace the storage those rules require.
  • Motorized gates. If an arch gate is ever given a motor, the operator must meet UL 325 entrapment protection, as DASMA’s technical data sheet #356 explains. We do not add a motor to a gate without the required sensors.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Pelham Parkway: Questions Answered

These are the questions Pelham Parkway boards, managing agents, owners and shopkeepers bring us, grouped by where in the building they arise. Each is answered here once; the FAQ further down covers different ground. We answer from installation practice and from the public rules linked on this page, not as legal advice; on a filing, an alteration agreement or a lease, the building’s architect or lawyer has the final word.

The Arch and the Court

Once the arch gate off the parkway is locked, do the lobby doors around our courtyard still need readers of their own?

In almost every building, yes. A locked arch keeps strangers out of the court, but it does nothing to separate one lobby’s residents from another’s, and the arch will be open more often than anyone expects: for a move, a boiler delivery, a film of snow that stops the gate closing. If the lobby doors fall back to plain keys, the building’s security on those days is a set of keys that have been copied for decades.

What the locked arch does change is how hard each lobby door has to work. With the court private, lobby doors can use simpler readers, a shorter release time and no keypad at all. The money saved there usually pays for the arch’s panel and lock.

A visitor standing at our arch cannot tell which of the lobby doors across the courtyard leads to the apartment they want. How should the call panel at the arch send them to the right entrance?

Put the lobby on the screen. A directory panel can show the lobby letter or section number next to each apartment and, after the resident answers and releases the gate, display a line such as “Lobby C, left side of the court”. Paired with large, lit letters over each lobby door, that gets most guests to the right door on the first try.

Behind the screen, each directory entry is linked to its lobby, so the same release that opens the arch also unlocks that lobby’s street door for a short time. The guest is never given a code, and no other lobby opens.

Each lobby in our building has its own elevator serving a few apartments on each floor. Should a resident’s fob open every lobby off the courtyard, or only their own?

Usually only their own, plus the shared rooms. The original plan gave each lobby its own small community, a stair and an elevator for a handful of apartments per floor, and that privacy is worth keeping. A fob that opens every lobby turns five small buildings into one big one.

There are exceptions the board can write into the plan: a resident who looks after a parent in another section, a board member who needs every lobby for inspections, staff. Each is a named exception on the credential, with an end date where it makes sense, rather than a building-wide rule.

Our fire escapes come down into the courtyard. If the arch gate is locked at night, how do people leaving by the fire escapes get out to the street, and how do firefighters get in?

The court side of the arch must always open without a key, code or power. Anyone who reaches the court, down a fire escape or out of a lobby, pushes a bar or turns a lever and is on the parkway sidewalk. We fit that hardware first and test it before any lock goes live, and we treat the arch as part of the exit route in every choice that follows, including how the gate behaves when power fails.

For the way in, the arch lock can be released by the fire alarm system where the building has one, and fitted with a key override the fire service can use. Exactly what applies to a particular building is a question for its architect and the code, and we do not install the lock until that answer is in writing.

Our arch has stood open since the building went up in the late 1920s. What does adding a gate under it involve?

More than a lock. A new gate under a masonry arch has to be designed to fit the opening without being anchored into carved stone, has to swing toward the street or slide so that it never blocks people leaving, and needs a closer that latches it in wind. That is ironwork, and it is usually made by a fabricator to drawings, with the lock, the closer and the cable path planned into it.

Before any of that, the board should decide whether the gate is wanted at all, and check what its alteration rules and its architect say. We help with the access side of the drawings and install the electrical parts once the gate is hung; the gate itself is priced after the survey.

The Entrance, the Ornament and the Apartment Door

We have watched other Art Deco buildings lose their lobby character to cheap security work. What should our board write into the installation contract so that does not happen here?

Four things. A list of protected surfaces, with photographs: the portal, any carved or cast stone, original metalwork, terrazzo, mosaic and decorative plaster, none of which may be drilled, cut or fixed to. A statement of approved routes for cable, ideally existing conduit and cellar paths, and a ban on surface conduit on the street face. Approval of each device’s position before it is fixed, by the board or its architect. And a handover record that lists every fixing point.

We are happy to work to a clause like that, and we would rather have it than not, because it settles every argument before it starts. If the contractor you are talking to will not accept it, that is useful information.

If a future board wants the reader and call panel taken off our entrance, what will be left behind?

Very little, if the work was done with removal in mind. A reader on a metal door frame leaves two small screw holes that can be plugged and painted. A panel on a post leaves anchor holes in the paving, not in the facade. A strike leaves a slightly larger pocket in the frame, which takes a filler plate or the original strike. A power-transfer hinge comes out and the original hinge goes back.

What cannot be undone easily is anything cut into stone or terrazzo, which is why we never do it. The handover record lists every fixing so whoever removes the system knows where to look.

After a push-in robbery in a building on our block, neighbors want to see who is standing at their own apartment door before they open it. What helps besides the lobby panel?

A push-in happens at the apartment door, after the stranger is already inside, so the building’s doors help only if they stop that person getting in at all: video calls at the arch and lobby, releases that open only for the apartment that answered, doors that close and lock on their own. At the apartment itself, the useful additions are a clear door viewer or a small video viewer on the door, a sound door and frame, and a habit of not opening to anyone unexpected.

An in-unit answering screen can also show the lobby camera when someone calls, so the resident sees who was let in. What we do not recommend is a building camera aimed at apartment doors in the halls: it records everyone’s comings and goings, and it raises exactly the tenant-data questions the city’s privacy law is about.

Stores on Lydig Avenue and White Plains Road

Our store on Lydig Avenue has a back door into the apartment building’s rear hall. How do we let staff use it without giving them the residents’ halls?

Give the back door a reader on the store side only, for staff on the store’s schedule, and leave the hall side of it free to open for anyone leaving the store. The residents’ hall keeps its own doors on the residents’ credentials. Staff can pass from the store into the hall when the store needs that route, usually to reach the refuse area, but cannot enter the residents’ lobby or stairs.

Because the door joins a business to a residential building, the landlord or board decides how it is used, and both sides should know who holds which credential. We write that into the handover record for both the store and the building.

Our shop’s steel sidewalk cellar doors open onto the avenue for deliveries. Can they be part of the access system?

Partly. Sidewalk doors are opened and closed from below by whoever is working the stair, so an electric lock that pops them open from the street is not the right idea. What works is a lock on the inside that only staff can release, a contact that tells the owner whenever the doors are open, and an alert if they stay open past the delivery window.

The more important door is usually the one at the bottom of the stair, between the cellar and the store or the building. That is the one that gets a reader and a schedule, so a delivery crew working the sidewalk doors can reach the stockroom and nothing else.

Power, batteries and what each door does in an outage

Every door in a Pelham Parkway building should have a written answer to one question: what happens when the power goes out? AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Pelham Parkway

We could not load Google’s AI Overview or its related-question boxes while researching this page, so the headings below are not quotations from them. They are ideas Pelham Parkway boards and owners repeat to us after reading quick answers online and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Most of that material imagines a single-family house or a modern office building, and very little of it imagines a 1928 courtyard building with six entrances.

“One reader on the front door secures an apartment building.”

That is true of a building with one front door. A garden apartment house has an arch and then several front doors, and the people who use them are not interchangeable.

A single reader at the arch with plain keys on every lobby leaves the lobbies on keys that have been copied for decades. Readers on every lobby with the arch left open leave the court to anyone. The plan has to cover both, and decide which does what.

The quick answer is not wrong so much as written for a different building.

“Older buildings need to be rewired for access control.”

Many 1920s and 1930s apartment houses have conduit from the vestibule to the cellar for their original bell and buzzer wiring. Where it is sound, new cable goes through the same route.

Where it is not, there is almost always a plain-plaster or cellar path that avoids the decorative surfaces. Phone-based calling can also remove the need for new cable to each apartment.

Rewiring every apartment is sometimes the right answer. It should come out of a survey, not out of a cost guide.

“Cloud-managed systems are always better.”

Remote management is useful, especially for a managing agent who looks after several buildings. But the doors themselves should keep working on the credentials they already know when the internet, the cloud service or the subscription stops.

The 2011 Bronx Times story describes a building whose cameras went dark over an unpaid bill after a sale. A door system that depends on a vendor account can fail the same way.

We set systems up so the building owns its controller and its data, and the cloud is an extra, not a dependency.

“Budget a fixed price per door and multiply.”

National guides quote a per-door range, usually written with an office suite in mind, and multiply it by the number of doors. For a single door that is a fair starting point: our own installed single-door price begins at $1,500.

A courtyard building, though, is not priced by multiplying doors. The arch carries most of the cost of the call panel and directory; lobby doors behind a locked arch can be simpler; the controller is bought once for the whole court.

The useful number is the one for your routes, door by door, at the Bronx rate shown in the price table further down.

“Landmark rules mean you can’t touch a historic entrance.”

Landmarks review applies to designated buildings and districts, and BADAHA’s count suggests very few Bronx Art Deco apartment houses outside the Grand Concourse are designated. Unless a building is designated, its entrance can be changed without a Landmarks permit; check the city’s landmark map first.

That cuts both ways. No permit means no outside review, and the only thing standing between a decorative portal and a drill is the board and its contractor.

The right standard for an undesignated Deco entrance is the one a careful owner would choose anyway: plain surfaces only, removable fixings, and nothing cut into stone or terrazzo.

“Video intercoms show residents who is at their door.”

They show who is at the arch or the lobby door. In a courtyard building, that is two doors and a courtyard away from the apartment.

Once a guest has been let in, the system has done its job; what happens at the apartment door depends on the resident, the door, and a viewer in it.

We say this plainly because the push-in robbery in the 2011 story happened at an apartment door, behind front doors that were about to get new locks.

“A magnet on the arch gate is the most secure choice.”

Magnetic locks hold only while powered and must release on power loss and fire alarm, which means an arch gate on a magnetic lock opens to the street in every outage.

For an arch gate and lobby doors we prefer electric bolts and strikes that stay locked from outside without power and always open from inside with a bar or lever.

The point is to choose per door, never to fit a lock that could hold anyone in, and never to leave the court open whenever the power blinks.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Naming the Lobbies

This is a note on method, not a story about one building. In a courtyard building the first question we ask at the route walk is what each lobby is called, and we ask the super, the managing agent and a board member separately. The answers do not always match: one person uses letters, another the street numbers the sections were given, a third whatever the old buzzer panel says.

So before any directory is loaded, the building agrees on one name for each lobby, and that name goes on the panel, over the door, on the credential groups and on the handover record. It sounds like a small thing. It is the difference between a guest who walks straight to the right door and a guest who tries three, and between a super who can tell us which reader has failed and one who has to walk us round the court to point at it.

The same agreement settles who is in charge of what. Each lobby’s residents, the shared rooms, the staff, the couriers: we write the groups down with the lobby names attached, the board signs the list, and the system is built to match it.

At Bronx rates, roughly what does a courtyard building with one arch gate and five lobby doors cost?

Our building is not a designated landmark. Does anyone outside the co-op or the owner have to approve a reader on the entrance?
+

Landmarks review applies only to designated buildings and districts, so an undesignated building does not need a Landmarks permit for a reader or panel; the city’s landmark map confirms which applies. Whether changes to the doors themselves, a new gate or anything affecting the exits need a Buildings Department filing is a question for the building’s architect. Inside the building, the co-op’s alteration rules or the owner’s approval still apply, and in a co-op the board usually has to approve any work on the common entrance.

Where should parcel lockers go in a building with several lobbies around one courtyard: one bank at the arch, or one in each lobby?
+

One bank, placed where couriers can reach it without entering any lobby, is usually the better choice: inside the arch on a sheltered wall of the court, or in a room off the arch passage. A courier code opens the lockers and nothing else, residents of every lobby collect from the same place, and nobody has to cross the court with a hand truck. Lockers in each lobby make sense only where the court is open to the street and each lobby is effectively its own building.

When a resident buzzes a guest in at the arch, does the guest have to be buzzed in again at the lobby door?
+

Not if the system is set up for it. When the resident answers a call from the arch and presses unlock, the controller can release the arch and that apartment’s lobby street door together, each for a short time, and relock both on its own. The guest walks across the court and pushes the lobby door open without a second call. If the guest takes too long, the lobby relocks and the resident can release it again from the screen or phone.

Our building has two street numbers for its two halves. Can one controller run both, and does anything have to be kept separately for each?
+

One controller can run both halves, with the lobbies of each grouped under its own street number, so residents of one half do not open the other’s doors unless the board wants them to. The directory can list apartments under the right number, and the panel can say which entrance to use. Where the two halves are separate buildings for city records, the board should keep the written access policy and any entry records in a way that can be produced for each building separately; we set the system up so that is easy.

There is a mosaic in our lobby a few feet from the inner door. Can drilling for the new hardware crack it?
+

Hammer drilling near a mosaic or terrazzo can, which is why we do not use it there. Holes near decorative surfaces are made with a rotary drill without hammer action, on plain surfaces only, after the mosaic’s edges and any loose tesserae have been photographed. Where a fixing would have to be close to the mosaic, we move the device instead: a reader on the door frame, a panel on the other side of the vestibule, cable through the transom. Nothing is fixed to, through or under the mosaic itself.

Our pharmacy on White Plains Road keeps medicines in a back room. What should the door to that room do?
+

Lock on its own every time it closes, open with a credential only for the staff who need it, and keep a record of each opening that you can produce if stock goes missing. A door contact that alerts when it is left open matters as much as the reader. Federal rules in 21 CFR 1301.75 set how controlled substances themselves must be stored, so the door is an extra layer around that storage, not a substitute for it. The inside of the door always opens without a credential.

If we put a reader on the arch this year and add the lobbies later, should this year’s controller be sized for all of them?
+

Yes. The controller’s door capacity, the power supply and its battery, the directory panel and the cable pathway to each future lobby are all cheaper to provide once, at the start, than to replace when the third lobby arrives. Adding a lobby to a controller sized for it is then a reader, a strike and an hour of programming. We show the full plan in the first quote, price only the doors being done now, and mark the spare capacity on the handover record.
