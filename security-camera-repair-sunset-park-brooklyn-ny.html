The Neighborhood

Why Sunset Park Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Sunset Park runs from the Green-Wood Cemetery edge and Greenwood Heights in the north down to Bay Ridge, and from the harbor east to Borough Park. Community Board 7 describes it as split by the Gowanus Expressway, with the industrial area mainly west of Third Avenue and the shopping strips on Fifth and Seventh Avenues; Eighth Avenue is the spine of Brooklyn's largest Chinatown. Most of the houses are two stories over a basement with a bay window on the front, built mainly between 1892 and 1910, and most were built as two-family homes. That is the context. What matters for a camera repair is who uses each system, where its recorder and power actually are, and whose rules apply to the cable.

Start with the houses. When the Hamilton Brothers finished their row on 50th Street around 1903, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report notes, the south side held "25 two-family houses," each "Containing a separate second-floor apartment that could be rented out." That plan, an owner downstairs and a rent-paying household upstairs, is still the basic shape of the neighborhood. What has changed is how many people now live inside it. The Fifth Avenue Committee's 2020 Sunset Park Housing Report calls Sunset Park the "4th most overcrowded neighborhood in NYC," puts homeownership at 28.1 percent, says more than half of the district's 45,903 homes were built before 1939, and reports an increase in illegal basement apartments. Community Board 7 calls the district's housing the "second oldest housing stock in NYC."

Put a camera system into that house and it stops being one family's gadget. The recorder was bought by the owner and put on the cellar shelf when the cellar was storage. The cameras were added one at a time, as each floor asked to see the front door or the yard. Some are powered from the owner's outlets and some from whatever socket was closest on the day, which may now be inside a tenant's apartment. The app login was shared with a son, a tenant, a cousin who set it up and has since moved. Most of the repair calls we get from these blocks are some version of that history catching up: a recorder in a room the owner can no longer walk into freely, cameras that go dark when someone else's breaker trips, a drive that filled and stopped saving months ago because nobody whose phone gets the alerts still lives there.

Then there is the cable itself. In a 2011 Brownstoner forum thread, an owner of a private house in Sunset Park asked how to get cameras in without the wiring showing, and was told that coax for a wired system is "a bit too thick to be hidden around moldings," so it has to go through ceilings or walls. Plenty of installers never took that advice. On these houses we find coax and power cable stapled along baseboards and door casings, pushed through the window frames of the parlor bay, and run down the outside of the front through Formstone or vinyl siding that has since let water into every hole. That kind of cable fails slowly, at the staples and the bends, and usually first at the connector where it meets the camera.

West of Third Avenue the picture is completely different. NYCEDC describes the Sunset Park District as 200 acres of waterfront with four big industrial campuses: the Brooklyn Army Terminal, the South Brooklyn Marine Terminal, MADE Bush Terminal and the Brooklyn Wholesale Meat Market. Next to them is Industry City, which the Brooklyn Eagle described in April 2025 as a 35-acre, 6-million-square-foot complex of 16 buildings with more than 650 businesses. The Army Terminal, by the Mann Report's count in January 2026, is 59 acres and 4.1 million square feet with more than 100 businesses, and leases suites from 1,500 square feet. Liberty View at 850 Third Avenue is an eight-story, 1.3-million-square-foot former Navy warehouse from 1916. A camera repair in one of these buildings is a tenant's job in a landlord's building: concrete you may not cut, a network you do not control, a loading dock you share and an elevator you have to book.

Running through the middle of it all is the power. On June 24, 2025, Con Edison reduced voltage by 8 percent for 105,800 customers in southwest Brooklyn, naming Sunset Park, "to protect equipment and maintain service as crews make repairs." News 12 reported Sunset Park among the hardest-hit neighborhoods the next day and summed it up this way: "In some cases, the power isn't fully out—but it's running lower than usual." On July 3, 2026, Con Edison did it again for an area of 106,000 customers that again listed Sunset Park. A recorder, a network switch and a strip of plug-in camera adapters do not like low voltage. Some ride through it, some reboot over and over, and some come back with a drive the recorder no longer recognizes. Those are repair calls, and they often surface weeks later.

Row houses where the original two homes are now three, four or more households. One recorder, many users, power drawn from several apartments, and a cellar that is no longer simply the owner's.

Waterfront industrial buildings

Industry City, the Bush Terminal buildings, Liberty View and the Army Terminal: concrete floors, deep spans, building-run networks, shared docks and freight elevators, with each tenant's cameras inside a landlord's building.

Bayed two-family row houses: the cable is the weak point

On a typical Sunset Park row house, the camera over the stoop looks down the steps toward the sidewalk, a second camera watches the yard from the back of the parlor floor or the rear extension, and both run to a recorder on a cellar shelf. The cameras themselves tend to be fine for years. The cable is what fails. If it was run inside, it often goes along a baseboard or a door casing and down through a hole by the radiator pipe, held with staples that bite into the jacket a little more every time a door bangs. If it was run outside, it comes down the front through Formstone or siding and in through the foundation, and every hole it passes through is a place for water to follow it. When we repair one of these houses, we test the run end to end before blaming the camera, replace the connector at the camera with a sealed one rather than taping the old one, and where we can, move the cable off the outside of the front and into the hollow behind the bay or through the cellar ceiling.

Formstone and vinyl siding each need their own method. Formstone is a cement coating over the brick, and a camera screwed only into the coating can work loose as the coating cracks around the anchors. The repair is to anchor through it into the masonry behind, or move the mount to a solid band like a window head or a lintel. Vinyl siding is hollow, so a camera fixed to it alone will sag and twist in the wind; the right fix is a mounting block that sits on the siding but is screwed into the wall behind. Either way, the hole the cable uses gets sealed with a drip loop below it, so the next rainstorm runs off instead of in.

Houses divided into several apartments: one recorder, many keys

Many Sunset Park houses that were built for two households now hold more. The Fifth Avenue Committee's housing report found an increase in illegal basement apartments, and in November 2026 ABC7 reported that a house on 58th Street where six people died in a fire had complaints on file that its basement had been made into an apartment and its two upper floors divided into three apartments each. We are a camera company, not inspectors, and we do not ask who lives where. But a camera system in a house like that has to be repaired with the house as it actually is. The recorder may now be in a room someone sleeps in. Cameras may be powered from outlets in three different apartments, so one tenant turning off a power strip takes the hallway camera down. The internet the recorder uses may belong to someone who is about to move out.

So a repair in a divided house starts with a short list. Whose recorder is it, and who is paying? Which outlets feed which cameras? Whose internet is it using? Who can see which camera on their phone, and who should? Then we fix the fault and, where the owner agrees, put the system's power on one outlet the owner controls, move the recorder somewhere the owner can reach without knocking on a tenant's door, give each household access only to the shared door and its own views, and remove users who no longer live there. We do not add load to a crowded power strip or run cable through a window that has to stay clear, and we will tell an owner plainly if the cellar power we find is not something a camera system should be plugged into. Wiring work on outlets themselves is for a licensed electrician; we do not do electrical work.

Landmarked rows: back in the same joint

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated four Sunset Park historic districts on June 18, 2019: Sunset Park South, roughly 285 buildings on 54th to 59th Streets between Fourth and Fifth Avenues; the 50th Street row of 50 houses; Central Sunset Park, about 148 buildings on 47th and 48th Streets and Sixth Avenue; and Sunset Park North, about 56 buildings on the south side of 44th Street. The 50th Street report notes that "nearly all retain their massive L-shaped stone stoops." On those blocks a front camera is something the Commission cares about. Its permit guidebook treats "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment" as work that needs no permit, while new equipment is expected to be small, well spaced, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metal and never through decorative surfaces, with only about ten inches of visible conduit. In practice that means a repair puts a camera of about the same size back in the same position on the same anchors, and keeps the cable tight in a joint. If the right fix is a new spot or an extra camera, we tell you it is a different kind of job before anything is drilled.

Walk-ups over avenue shops: two systems at one door

On Fourth, Fifth and Seventh Avenues the usual building has a store at street level and two or three floors of apartments above, reached through a narrow street door next to the shop window. The store often has its own cameras, bought by the tenant and recorded under the counter or in the back. The landlord may have a separate camera on the residential door and in the stair hall, recorded on another box in the cellar. Sometimes one camera even feeds both. When one of them fails, nobody is sure whose it is, and the store owner and the landlord each assume the other will call. We start these repairs by tracing which camera goes to which recorder and who owns each, label everything at both ends, and only then fix what is broken, so the next call does not start from zero.

Third Avenue under the expressway: daylight that never arrives

The Gowanus Expressway was built over Third Avenue in 1941 on the old elevated railway's structure and widened to six lanes in the 1960s, and it carries well over 100,000 cars and trucks a day. Underneath it, the sidewalks along Third Avenue sit in shade for most of the day, with bright strips of sun only where the deck ends at the cross streets. That light is hard on cameras. A camera that decides by light level when to switch to night mode may stay in black and white well into the morning, flip back and forth whenever a truck's shadow passes, or expose for the sunlit cross street and leave the sidewalk in front of the shop nearly black. Roll-up doors make it worse: open, they let a wall of daylight into a dim garage; shut, they leave a camera looking at a blank steel face. Most Third Avenue picture complaints we see are fixed with settings, an angle, a lens or a light of the owner's own, not with a new system.

Waterfront industrial buildings: a tenant in a landlord's building

In Industry City, the Bush Terminal buildings, Liberty View and the Army Terminal, a business's camera system lives inside a building it does not control. The floors and ceilings are concrete, and as one Sunset Park electrician put it in a Yelp review, "It was not allowed to cut the concrete to recess the wiring in this building and we had to install electrical wiremold." Camera cable on those floors runs in surface raceway or in a ceiling tray, which means a damaged run is usually reachable but a new route needs the landlord's approval. Many suites get their network from the building: Industry City's carrier room is run by DataVerge at 882 Third Avenue, which describes a campus fiber network to tenant suites. Floors can be very deep, so a camera at the dock end may be a long way from the recorder at the office end. And every visit involves the building: a loading-dock or freight-elevator slot, sign-in at security, sometimes a certificate of insurance. We plan for all of that when you book, so the visit is spent on the cameras.

Fault by Fault

Brownouts, Borrowed Outlets, Silent Drives, Building Networks and Pre-Dawn Doors: How Sunset Park Camera Systems Go Wrong

Brownouts: when the power is low rather than off

An outage is obvious. A voltage reduction is not. When Con Edison lowered voltage by 8 percent across parts of southwest Brooklyn including Sunset Park in June 2025, and again in July 2026, most households noticed only dim lights and slower air conditioners. Camera equipment notices more. A recorder's power supply that is already old may drop out at the bottom of a dip and restart, and a recorder that restarts many times in an afternoon can come back with a drive it reports as abnormal. Small plug-in adapters for coax cameras run hot and fail. A network switch powering several cameras can shut ports off when its input sags, so the cameras farthest down the list go dark first. A technical guide from Security Wholesalers lists the usual aftermath plainly: devices "started in the wrong order," a drive that "reports an abnormal state," "Time/NTP was lost," and it warns that "Repeated power cycling can worsen storage and power faults." After a brownout we check every one of those, not only the camera that is dark, and we size any battery backup for what it actually has to carry and set it to shut the recorder down cleanly rather than let it bounce.

Borrowed outlets: when someone else's switch is your camera's power

In a house where several households share one system, the cameras are often powered from whichever outlet was closest when each was added. One plugs into an adapter behind the owner's couch, one into a socket in the tenant's hallway, one into the cellar outlet that shares a circuit with the washer. Each of those is a way for someone who has no idea the camera exists to switch it off: a tenant unplugs a power strip to vacuum, a breaker in the other apartment trips, an outlet is controlled by a wall switch somebody turns off at night. The camera looks broken; it is simply unpowered. The repair is to bring every camera's power back to one place the owner controls, ideally a single supply or a PoE switch beside the recorder, so the system lives or dies on one outlet, and that outlet sits on a backup.

Silent drives: the warning nobody received

A recorder writing four or eight cameras around the clock wears its drive out, and when the drive starts to fail the recorder may still show live video while saving nothing, or saving in fragments. Many recorders can warn about it with a beep, an email or a push alert. In Sunset Park's shared houses, those warnings tend to go to the person who set the system up, and that person is often no longer there: a relative who moved, a tenant who left, an installer's email address. The recorder on the cellar shelf beeps into an empty room. A drive swap is one of the cheapest repairs on our list. The important part is to copy what can be copied first, then send the recorder's health alerts to phones that belong to people who live in the house now, and test them in front of you so you know what one looks like.

Building networks: the change you were never told about

In a waterfront building, a tenant's cameras and recorder often sit on a network the landlord or a building provider runs. That network changes: a switch in the riser is replaced, addresses are handed out differently, a security setting is tightened, the internet in the suite moves to another provider. None of those is aimed at your cameras, but any of them can break them. The usual symptoms are cameras that show on the recorder in the office but not on phones outside it, a recorder that cannot find cameras on the far side of the floor, or a whole system that vanished on a Monday morning. The repair is mostly detective work, done with a laptop on the recorder and a tester on the cable. When the fault is on the building's side, we write down exactly what we need, an open port, a fixed address, a cable patched in the closet, and pass it to building IT in their language, so the fix does not bounce back and forth for a week.

Pre-dawn doors: the camera that sees the break-in and not the burglar

Business break-ins in the local reports cluster before dawn and at the back: the 2017 pair whose burglaries all fell between 2:30 and 5 a.m., the supermarket gate cut around 4 a.m. in 2019. That is the hardest light a camera faces. A back door or a gate in an alley or a rear yard has no street lamp, so the camera depends on its own infrared, which lights the first few feet and the nearest wall and leaves a face at the door as a grey smear. A camera aimed down a long alley at the door shows that someone was there, never who. The repairs that change that are not expensive: a camera lower and closer to the door, aimed across it at face height; a small light of your own on a motion sensor; infrared turned down and a separate illuminator added so the frame does not wash out; and recording set to full quality at night on the cameras that matter. We test those at night, or by covering the lens and lighting the scene the way the back of the building looks at 4 a.m.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Sunset Park: Questions Answered

Row houses and the people in them

Whoever wired our 1900s row house ran the camera coax along the parlor moldings, and our painter wants it gone before he starts. Can the cable be moved out of sight without losing the cameras?

Usually, yes, and it is a good moment to do it. Coax is thick and stiff, which is why it ends up stapled along trim, but it can almost always be rerouted through the cellar ceiling and up inside a wall cavity or the hollow behind the bay, coming out only where the camera is. On many houses it is simpler to replace the old coax with one thinner network cable and switch those cameras to network models, which also improves the picture. Either way we test the cameras before the painter covers anything, and the old staples and holes are left ready for him to fill.

Our house was built as an owner's duplex with a rented top floor, and the top-floor tenant is moving out next month. What should change on the camera system before the new tenant arrives?

Three things. Remove the departing tenant's app access and change any shared password they knew, including the recorder's admin login if they ever had it. Check whether any camera, adapter or the recorder's internet runs through their apartment, because when their electric or internet account closes, that part of the system goes dark. And decide what the new tenant should see: normally the shared street door and stoop, not your yard or your hallway. We can do all three on one visit and leave a short written list of who has access to what.

Our front is vinyl siding, and the stoop camera's mount has pulled loose from it and droops after every windy night. Can a camera be fixed solidly to siding?

Not to the siding alone; vinyl is hollow and flexes. The repair is a mounting block made for siding, which fits the profile of the panels and is screwed through into the sheathing or masonry behind, with the camera fixed to the block. The cable goes through the block, so the hole is covered and sealed instead of open to rain. On a house where the siding hides old brick, we anchor into the brick. If the camera has been swinging for a while, we also check its connector and the cable at the hole, because constant movement is what breaks them.

Our house has three apartments with three separate internet accounts. Which one should the camera recorder be connected to?

Ideally the owner's, or a small account the owner keeps just for the building, because the recorder needs an internet connection that will not disappear when a tenant moves or stops paying a bill. The recorder records without internet, but phone viewing and alerts stop the moment that connection goes. If the only option is a tenant's router, we connect it with a cable rather than Wi-Fi, give it a fixed address on that router, and write down what to do when the tenant's provider swaps equipment. Many owners decide during the repair that the few dollars a month for their own line is worth it.

We use a small indoor camera to keep an eye on an elderly parent during the day, and it shows up on the same app as the house cameras that the tenants can see. Can her room be kept private?

Yes. On most recorders and apps you can create separate users and give each one only the cameras they should see, so tenants get the street door and the stoop and family members get her room. If the camera is a separate consumer model on its own app, it may simply need to be removed from the shared account and kept on a family account. We set the permissions on the visit and check each login from a phone, so nobody is surprised later by what a tenant can see.

The avenues

Someone pried our store's back door at about 4 a.m. The camera recorded the whole thing, but the face is too dark to make out. Can the back door camera be fixed for next time?

Usually, and without a new system. A back-door camera mounted high and far from the door lights only the first few feet with its infrared, so at 4 a.m. a person at the lock is a grey shape. Moving it lower and closer, aimed across the door at face height, adding a motion-sensor light of your own, and turning down the camera's infrared if it bounces off a nearby wall usually solves it. We also set that camera to record at full quality overnight and check the clip at night before we sign off. Keep the clip you have; we can export it properly for the precinct.

We want a phone alert when the back door camera sees someone between 2 and 5 in the morning, but not when the bread delivery arrives at 6. Can alerts follow a schedule?

Yes. Most recorders and apps let you set when a camera sends alerts, separate from when it records, and many can be set to alert only on a person rather than any movement, which cuts out cats, headlights and blowing trash. We set the back-door alert for the hours the shop is closed and empty, keep recording around the clock, and test it by walking past the door so you see what the alert looks like on your phone. If the delivery time changes, the schedule is a two-minute change you can make yourself.

Our church on Fifth Avenue has cameras in the sanctuary, the hall and the office, and the volunteer who set them up has moved away. Who should hold the logins?

The organization, not a person. We recover access to the recorder, create an admin login held by the church itself, written down and kept with its records, and separate everyday logins for the people who actually check the cameras, each with only the views they need. The volunteer's old account comes off. For a nonprofit or a congregation that changes volunteers often, we also suggest that two people know the admin password, so the system never again depends on one person's phone.

West of Third Avenue

Our shop is on Third Avenue under the Gowanus Expressway, and at noon on a sunny day the sidewalk camera is still in black-and-white night mode. Why won't it switch back to color?

Because under the deck it is not bright enough for the camera to believe it is day. Most cameras switch modes based on how much light their sensor sees, and the sidewalk under the expressway stays shaded even at midday. The fix is to set the camera to switch on a schedule instead of on light level, adjust the point at which it changes modes, or add a light so the scene is bright enough for color. If the camera faces the bright cross street at the end of the deck, re-aiming it at your own frontage helps as well. This is almost always a setting, not a fault.

Our studio's cameras plug into the network jacks in our Industry City suite. Since the building changed something on its network, we can see them on the office Wi-Fi but not from home. Who fixes that?

Often both sides, in a small way each. If the cameras work inside the office, they and the recorder are fine; what broke is the path from the outside world to your recorder, which runs through the building's network. We check the recorder's settings and the app's connection method first, because some systems only need their cloud connection re-registered after a network change. If the building's side now blocks that connection, we write down exactly what is needed and give it to building IT, rather than guessing at settings on equipment that is not yours.

Building rules in our Third Avenue industrial building don't allow cutting the concrete, so our camera cables run in surface raceway. A hand truck smashed the raceway near the dock and two cameras died. Does all of it have to come out?

No. We open the raceway where it was hit, test the cables inside it and replace only the damaged length, splicing or re-terminating where it is sound on either side, then close it with a new section of raceway. If the dock area keeps getting hit, the lasting fix is to raise that stretch higher on the wall or run it in steel, and we will ask building management before changing the route. The two cameras are tested at the recorder before we leave, along with every other camera on that run.

Our business is on a low ground floor near the water, and the recorder sits on a shelf a foot off the floor. Since Sandy did $50 million in damage to buildings on 36th Street, where should we put it?

As high as the space allows, ideally above the height water reached in your building during Sandy and away from outside walls, with its backup and switch beside it. If the suite has an upper floor or a mezzanine, that is better still. We also suggest copying important clips off the recorder regularly, or adding a second copy off-site, so a flooded back room does not take the footage with it. Moving the recorder during a repair is usually a short cable extension; the cameras do not need to change.

Our manufacturing floor runs all day, and the cameras sit near the ceiling above machines. How do you reach them without stopping production?

We plan the visit with you around the floor: ladder or lift positions marked out of the machines' paths, work on one camera at a time, and the cameras over running equipment done during a break, a changeover or before the shift starts. Most camera work is quiet and quick once access is planned, and a camera replaced in the same mount needs no more than a few minutes at height. If a camera cannot be reached safely while a machine runs, we say so and schedule that one for a quiet time rather than take a chance.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair equipment from any manufacturer and any installer. These are the kinds we meet most in Sunset Park's houses, avenue stores and waterfront suites, and what usually goes wrong with each.

Coax and HD-over-coax cameras

The older systems on many row houses, wired to a DVR in the cellar, with power from a multi-camera supply or a handful of plug-in adapters. Connectors and adapters fail first; the cable can often be reused for higher resolution.

Network (IP) cameras

Standard in newer store systems and in waterfront suites, powered and connected by one cable to a PoE switch or an NVR. Faults are a switch port, a connector, a password or a network change.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that keeps the video. Drives wear out, settings vanish in brownouts, updates break phone viewing. A hybrid recorder lets a house keep its coax cameras while adding network ones.

Varifocal cameras

Cameras whose lens can be zoomed after mounting: the answer for deep loft floors, long store aisles and back doors where a face has to fill more of the frame.

Pan-tilt cameras

Motorized cameras on yards, lots and large floors. Motors, saved positions and cameras left parked facing the wrong way are what we fix most.

Wi-Fi and battery cameras

Consumer cameras on stoops, in apartments and in yards. Signal through brick, winter batteries and accounts owned by someone who moved are the usual problems.

Video door stations

Intercom stations with a camera at row-house and walk-up street doors, sometimes recorded by the main system, sometimes not. We check which before promising footage from one.

PoE switches and supplies

The parts that power everything and suffer most when the voltage sags. Checked on every brownout call and replaced with properly sized parts.

Battery backups

Small units that keep a recorder alive through a flicker and shut it down cleanly in a long outage, if they are sized and set up to. Many we find are dead or never connected.

National brands versus a local licensed repair

Owners with Ring, Arlo, Nest, Wyze or SimpliSafe cameras often ask whether to call the brand or us. For a sealed consumer camera that has died, the brand's warranty or a replacement is usually cheaper; there is nothing inside to repair. What the brand cannot do is come to the house and see that the Wi-Fi does not reach the yard through two brick walls, that the camera is plugged into a tenant's outlet, or that it is mounted on siding that flexes. For wired systems with a recorder, a licensed local repair is the normal route; those brands generally sell through installers and do not send technicians. ADT and similar monitored services repair their own equipment under contract, so check that contract first if your cameras came with one.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Sunset Park

Search summaries and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give general advice about camera repair. Some of it holds everywhere. Some of it misses how systems fail in Sunset Park's divided houses and waterfront buildings. These are the claims we hear most, and what we find on the job.

"A security camera system belongs to the homeowner"

On paper, usually. In practice, a Sunset Park camera system often has several users, power from several apartments and an internet connection that belongs to a tenant. Advice that assumes one household at one router misses most of what goes wrong here.

When a tenant moves out, an account closes, a breaker trips in another apartment or a password leaves with a relative, the system fails in ways that look like broken hardware and are not.

So our first questions are about people, not parts: who uses it, whose outlets and internet it depends on, and who should get its alerts.

"If the power didn't go out, the cameras are fine"

Con Edison's voltage reductions in June 2025 and July 2026 named Sunset Park, and News 12 described power "running lower than usual" rather than out. Low voltage restarts aging recorder supplies and switches without anyone noticing.

A recorder that restarts repeatedly can lose its clock, its settings or its grip on the drive. The live view looks fine; the recording has gaps.

After any period of dim lights, play back a random hour from that day. If it is missing, call before the next brownout makes it worse.

"Wireless cameras avoid all the wiring trouble"

They avoid running a data cable, but they still need power and a signal. In a brick row house, the yard is often several walls from the router, and the router may be a tenant's.

In the waterfront buildings, concrete floors and dozens of neighboring networks make Wi-Fi less reliable still, which is why most tenants there end up on cable.

Wireless is a fine answer for a camera inside an apartment. For a back door or a dock, a cable is usually the cheaper repair in the long run.

"If you can see the camera live, it is recording"

The live view comes straight from the camera; the recording depends on the drive. A worn drive can stop saving while live video carries on, and the warning may go to someone who no longer lives in the house.

Recorders can also be set to save a lower-quality stream, or only on motion, so the clip you need is blurry or starts too late.

The only real test is playback. On every repair we check a recorded clip, not just the live picture, and set alerts to current phones.

"Any handyman or electrician can fix a camera system"

In New York, companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for others are licensed by the Department of State. An electrician fixes the outlet; the cameras, cable, recorder and network are low-voltage work.

The tools differ too. Most faults are found with a cable tester, a PoE tester, the recorder's logs and a laptop on the network, not a meter on house wiring.

If someone has already worked on it, tell us what was changed. A well-meant fix often moves the problem rather than solving it.

"Landmark rules make any camera work a permit job"

Not for a repair. The LPC's guidebook treats "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment" as work that needs no permit, and that covers replacing a dead camera in the same place on the four 2019 districts' blocks.

What the guidebook governs is new or moved equipment: well spaced, at mortar joints or plain masonry, never on decorative surfaces, with very little visible conduit.

So a repair on 44th Street or between 54th and 59th Streets is usually simple. A new position is a different job, and we say so first.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

That describes one consumer camera somewhere simple. HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 across job sizes.

On our Brooklyn list, a setting or connector fix runs from within the $150 service call to $350, a single camera replacement $250 to $600, and a recorder replacement $700 to $1,500. The fault sets the price, and you see it in writing first.

The expensive repair is the one done twice: a camera replaced when the drive was failing, or a drive replaced when a brownout-damaged supply was killing it. Testing in order prevents that.

Sunset Park Security Camera Repair FAQ

The NYPD has offered stores a free live video link from their own cameras. If our shop wanted to sign up, would the system need repairs first?
+

Possibly. The NYPD's retail-theft program is free and opt-in, and the department has said none of it is mandatory. We cannot tell you whether it is offered through the 72nd Precinct, so ask the precinct directly. What we can do is make sure the system is worth linking: every camera recording, the recorder's clock correct, a stable internet connection, and the door and register cameras clear enough to show a face. A link to a camera that records shadows helps nobody, so fix the picture first.

When Con Edison asks the neighborhood to cut back during a voltage reduction, should we switch the camera system off to protect it?
+

Usually not. Con Edison cut voltage by 8 percent across southwest Brooklyn, naming Sunset Park, in June 2025 and again in July 2026, and asked customers to avoid washers, dryers and microwaves, not small electronics. A recorder and cameras draw little power, and switching them off and on repeatedly can make storage and power faults worse. The better protection is a battery backup that steadies the voltage and keeps the system running, so it never has to restart in the wrong order. If something already failed during one of those weeks, that is a normal repair visit.

We heard Dahua and Hikvision were banned. Is it still legal to keep our Dahua recorder and have it repaired?
+

Yes, as far as owning and fixing an existing system goes. In November 2022 the FCC barred new equipment authorizations for Hikvision and Dahua, which affects new models coming to market, and Dahua said the order does not affect products already authorized. We repair both brands every week. What can change is the supply of matching parts for an older model, so if an exact replacement camera or board is not available, we find a compatible one and tell you before anything is ordered. This is practical guidance, not legal advice.

The cameras in our Fifth Avenue store were put in by the landlord, but we run the store. Who decides who can watch and copy the video?
+

Whoever owns the system, which here is the landlord, unless your lease says otherwise. Landlords are generally not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process, so if you need to see your own sales floor, get access in writing, either a viewing login on the landlord's recorder or permission for a separate system inside the store. We work for whoever owns and approves the system, and we will not give a tenant access to a landlord's recorder without the landlord's approval, even when the tenant is paying for the visit.

Power tools were taken from a Sunset Park building through an unlocked basement window. Our cameras watch the stoop and the yard but not our cellar windows. Can a repair visit change that?
+

Often, yes. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that case in December 2019, about $2,730 in tools taken through a basement window at a building near 41st Street and Seventh Avenue. Cellar windows on Sunset Park row houses sit low, under the stoop or along the areaway, and most cameras are aimed too high to see them. On a repair visit we can often re-aim an existing camera to take in the areaway, or price one added camera if that is the only way to see the window. Any change is priced in writing first.

If the house internet goes down, does our camera system stop recording?
+

It depends on the kind of system. A wired system with a recorder in the house keeps recording to its own drive when the internet drops; what stops is viewing from your phone and the alerts, which return when the connection does. Wi-Fi cameras that save only to a cloud account may record nothing during the outage unless they have a memory card inside. On a repair visit we tell you which kind you have, and if your cameras depend on the cloud alone, we can show you how to keep a local copy.

Over the years different people added cameras of three different brands to our house. Can they all be repaired and run on one recorder?
+

In most cases. Network cameras from different makers can usually be paired to one recorder through a shared standard called ONVIF, though some features, such as the maker's own person detection, may not carry over. Older coax cameras come in several high-definition formats, and the recorder has to support each one. We test every camera on the recorder you have, re-pair the ones that dropped off, and tell you which cameras are worth keeping and which would cost more to keep alive than to replace.

Our camera faces Third Avenue truck traffic under the expressway, and a grey film builds up on the dome within weeks. Is that a repair, and how often does it need cleaning?
+

The Gowanus Expressway carries well over 100,000 cars and trucks a day, and cameras near it gather road grime faster than cameras on a side street. A clean and check is $80 on our Brooklyn list, and on those blocks every three to six months is a sensible starting point. Angling the camera down slightly, fitting a hood, or moving it a little further from the roadway can stretch that out. If the dome has turned cloudy rather than dirty, cleaning will not help, and a replacement cover is the fix.

Our warehouse floor in a waterfront building runs the length of a block, and the cameras at the far end are the ones that keep dropping. Could the distance be the cause?
+

Quite possibly. Standard network cable is rated for about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, from the switch to the camera, and on floors as large as those at the Brooklyn Army Terminal, where floorplates reach 200,000 square feet, a single run from one closet can go past that. Beyond the limit, cameras drop out, restart or lose power over the cable. The usual repair is a small switch or extender part way along, or fiber for the long stretch, and we measure the run before recommending either.

Our Wi-Fi camera on the back of the house keeps dropping off because the router is at the front, two brick walls away. What is the fix?
+

Sunset Park row houses are long and narrow, and the brick and plaster walls between the front parlor and the back of the house absorb a lot of Wi-Fi signal. The most reliable fix is a cable to the camera, either straight to it or to a small wired access point near the back. Where a cable run is hard, a wired camera replacing the Wi-Fi one on the same cable path is often similar in cost. We test the signal on the visit and price both options so you can choose.
