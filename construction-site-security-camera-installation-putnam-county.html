Element 4 · Why Putnam County Jobsites Get Hit

Big Lots, Long Driveways, Nobody Driving Past

Putnam does not build the way the counties on either side of it do. There is no million-square-foot distribution pipeline here, because most of the county drains into New York City's reservoir system and the rules that protect that water shape what can be built and how. What Putnam builds instead is subdivisions on three-quarter-acre and larger lots, custom homes at the end of long private drives, village-scale mixed-use in Brewster and Cold Spring, light industrial around the Route 6 and Route 22 corridors, and steady municipal and school work.

The security problem that comes with that mix is isolation rather than scale. A framed house on a wooded lot in Kent with the finish package stacked in the garage is worth as much to somebody with a pickup as a downtown staging area, and nobody is driving past it at two in the morning. Power tools remain the most commonly reported theft from American jobsites, recovery on any single tool is poor, and copper theft nationally runs into federal estimates around a billion dollars a year — with the damage to reach the wire usually costing more than the wire.

Access

Two highways, one exit each way

I-84 crosses the county and the Taconic runs north-south, with Route 6, Route 22 and Route 9 feeding them. A site a few minutes from an interchange is a different risk profile than one at the end of a dead-end road off Peekskill Hollow.

Watershed

Rules that shape the mounting

Much of Putnam sits in the New York City drinking-water watershed, where stormwater, buffer and ground-disturbance requirements govern what can be dug and where. That changes how a camera gets mounted, not whether you can have one.

Jurisdiction

Sheriff, town PDs and DEP police

The Putnam County Sheriff's Office, town and village departments including Carmel, Kent, Brewster and Cold Spring, State Police out of Troop K, and New York City DEP Police patrolling watershed land. Knowing whose report you are filing matters at six in the morning.

Element 5 · Systems & Configurations

Four Ways to Cover a Putnam County Site

The configuration follows the site — what it has, what phase it is in, and how long coverage has to last.

Configuration 1

Battery + cellular unit

Self-contained camera with its own battery and LTE radio, mounted to fencing, a container corner or a temporary pole. This is the default on a Putnam lot, because it needs no service, no trenching and no ground disturbance to go up.

Configuration 2

Solar-assisted unit

Same head with a panel sized to the site. Cleared subdivision lots and open parcels along the Route 22 corridor carry solar well. Wooded lots in Philipstown, Putnam Valley and Kent — which is most of them — get larger battery banks instead.

Configuration 3

Hardwired PoE

Cameras on Cat6 back to a recorder in a locked field office once temporary power is set and the building is closed. This is the answer on village mixed-use, light industrial and institutional projects, engineered to become the permanent system.

Configuration 4

Mobile surveillance trailer

For cleared acreage and phased sites with nothing to mount on and no anchoring allowed. Mast-mounted cameras with on-board recording, moved as the work moves. See solar surveillance trailers for Putnam County.

Element 5.5 · Terminology

What the Quote Actually Says

PoE

Power over Ethernet — one Cat6 run carries video and power, so a hardwired jobsite system needs a cable path rather than an outlet at every camera.

LTE / cellular

The unit has its own SIM and data plan, independent of any site network. Data budget is why clip length and alert sensitivity get tuned during commissioning.

AI detection

On-camera classification separating people and vehicles from branches, weather and wildlife — which on a wooded Putnam lot is the difference between four alerts a night and forty.

Talk-down

A speaker that lets an operator or the account owner address someone on site live rather than reviewing a clip the next morning.

Retention

How long video is stored before overwrite, set per project. A two-year subdivision and a six-week renovation need different windows.

Non-penetrating mount

Fence, container, ballasted-pole or trailer mounting that requires no excavation — the standard approach on watershed-regulated parcels.

Element 5.7 · System Selection

Four Questions That Settle the Spec

1. Can you disturb ground?

On watershed parcels the answer is often no, or not without review. That pushes the specification toward fence, container, ballasted-pole and trailer mounts from the start.

2. Is there power?

No temporary service means battery or solar. Once a service is energized and lockable, hardwired becomes available and gets cheaper per camera across a long build.

3. What does the canopy look like?

A cleared subdivision lot carries solar. A wooded Philipstown lot under mature trees does not, and gets a larger battery on a swap schedule.

4. How many lots are live?

A single custom home is one or two heads. A phased subdivision needs an entrance head plus a unit that follows the active work, which is a relocation pattern rather than a bigger camera count.

Element 5.8 · Placement Planning

Where the Cameras Actually Go

Placement comes off the drawings and the walk. On a Putnam site the priority order usually runs like this.

  1. The one way in. Most Putnam sites have a single access point — a subdivision road, a private drive, a village curb cut. A head there at plate height and angle does more evidentiary work than three wide scene cameras.
  2. The container row and gang boxes. Tools leave from where tools are stored. A dedicated head on conex doors with a tight after-hours zone is the highest-value camera on most sites.
  3. Material staging. Lumber packs, copper, appliances, stone and millwork during the weeks they sit before installation — usually in a garage or a driveway on residential work.
  4. The structure. Openings at grade and the elevation that faces away from the road, which is where entry usually happens on an isolated lot.
  5. Road frontage. On village lots in Brewster, Cold Spring and Nelsonville this doubles as documentation of the sidewalk and neighboring property before your equipment arrived.
Element 6 · Equipment & NDAA

Equipment Specified for Putnam Conditions

Jobsite units are chosen for cold-weather battery behavior, cellular reliability in terrain that blocks signal within a few hundred feet, and mounting hardware that holds through winter wind coming across a reservoir or over a ridge. Fixed systems use commercial IP cameras and recorders sized to the retention the project actually needs.

Any project touching public money in this county — a school district capital program, a town or village facility, county work at the government campus in Carmel — gets NDAA-compliant equipment with compliance documentation supplied in the submittal. Private work is not bound by that requirement, but compliant lines are specified by default so a project that later picks up public financing does not become a replacement job.

Element 22 · Build-Phase Security Map

What to Watch, Phase by Phase

Phase 1

Clearing & site work

Equipment and fuel are the exposure, and on watershed parcels the erosion controls and staging areas are fixed by the approved plan. One unit at the access point covers machine parking and documents conditions before anything gets blamed on your excavator.

Phase 2

Foundation & framing

Lumber packs and the first tool concentration arrive. Add the storage-area head and tighten motion zones so alerts stay meaningful on a lot surrounded by moving branches and wildlife.

Phase 3

Rough-in

Peak copper exposure — wire, plumbing, HVAC. This is the phase where a loss costs weeks rather than dollars, and where interior coverage starts on larger buildings.

Phase 4

Finish & closeout

Appliances, fixtures, stone and millwork with a dozen subs coming and going. Access-driven losses overtake fence-line losses here, and the camera record becomes how disputes get settled.

Element 9 · Questions Putnam Builders Actually Ask

Fourteen Real Questions, Answered Straight

We can't dig a post hole on this parcel. Can you still mount a camera?

Yes, and on watershed sites that is the normal approach. Fence mounts, container mounts, ballasted poles and trailers all avoid ground disturbance entirely, which keeps the camera out of the permitting conversation.

Our lot has no power and won't for months.

That is the normal starting condition here. Battery and cellular covers the site now; power changes what is possible later, not whether you can be watched today.

We're building 30 lots in phases. Do we need 30 cameras?

No. One head on the road into the development covers every vehicle that enters, and a second unit relocates as the active build moves. Relocations are included, which is what makes phased work practical to cover.

The lot is heavily wooded. Will solar work?

Usually not well, and that is most of this county. Wooded Philipstown, Kent and Putnam Valley lots get larger battery banks on a swap schedule rather than a panel that spends the day in shade.

Do we have to sign a year?

Rentals are month-to-month. Phases end early and schedules slip, and a twelve-month commitment on a seven-month job means paying for a camera watching a finished house.

Will deer set off alerts all night out here?

On a Putnam lot, wildlife is the single most common cause of nuisance alerts. AI classification and zone drawing handle it, and the tuning happens at commissioning rather than as a later service call.

Who owns the video?

The account owner. Access is granted per user, retention is configurable, and clip export is available to the owner on request.

We're renovating on Main Street in Cold Spring. Will the village object?

Design review cares about permanent attachments to protected structures and what the streetscape sees. Temporary units on fencing, containers and poles generally sidestep that. The permanent system is a separate conversation at turnover with the architect involved.

Does this do anything for our insurance?

Recorded footage may assist with incident and insurance documentation. Coverage requirements and any possible policy impacts must be confirmed directly with your insurer — we make no claims about your policy.

Cell service is bad at the back of the property.

Common here, and it changes over a few hundred feet rather than a few miles. Signal gets checked at the actual mounting height during the walk, with carrier selection and on-board recording covering the gaps.

What if somebody takes the camera?

Units alert on tamper and removal, and footage lives in the cloud as well as on board, so pulling a unit does not erase what it already captured.

Our district project needs NDAA documentation.

Compliant equipment specified with documentation supplied in the submittal package. That is standard on district, town, village and county work in this county.

Response times out here are slow. Does a camera actually help?

It changes what you can do on site rather than what an agency does. Visible equipment, lighting and optional talk-down give you an immediate on-site response, and the recording gives you documentation afterward. Response and outcomes remain controlled by the responding agency.

What does removal cost at the end?

Nothing additional on a rental. Closeout removal is included, which matters when the last two weeks of a job are already a scramble.

Element 23 · Delivery-Week Protocol

The Weeks That Carry the Most Risk

On most Putnam builds the highest-value window is when appliances, fixtures, stone, millwork or copper land and sit before installation — usually in a garage or on a driveway where anyone who saw the truck knows exactly where it went. The protocol is mostly scheduling rather than hardware.

  1. Tell us the delivery date so coverage is re-aimed at the staging area before the truck arrives rather than after.
  2. Motion zones tighten around the material for the duration, so alerts mean something during the window that matters.
  3. Overnight monitoring can be added for the delivery week alone on rental accounts and dropped afterward.
  4. Signage goes up at the drive and frontage — visible equipment is part of the deterrent value.
  5. When the material is installed, zones return to normal so the site is not generating noise for the rest of the job.

Element 11 · DIY vs Professional

It stops being reasonable when any of the following is true — and on a Putnam lot at least one usually is:

Where DIY breaks

Where professional pays

Licensed installation on record · equipment matched to canopy, signal and power · non-penetrating mounts for watershed parcels · cloud retention independent of the device · optional live operators · documented compliance for public work · a three-year warranty on AESS-supplied products.

Element 26 · Weather & Winter Operations

Canopy, Cold and December Sun

Three weather realities drive equipment choices here. Cold reduces usable battery capacity, so a unit sized in October can run short in a January freeze unless the bank was specified for winter. Short days cut solar harvest exactly when the site is dark for sixteen hours, and on a wooded lot the panel may be in shade for most of what daylight there is — which is why panel angle and battery sizing both get set for December rather than June. And wind coming across a reservoir or over a ridge loads a mast-mounted camera hard enough that mounting hardware is specified for the exposure.

Winter storms add the other problem — power and network outages across whole towns, and unplowed private drives. A cellular unit with on-board recording keeps working through an outage that takes a wired system down, and uploads what it captured once the link returns.
