The County

Orange County Rentals, Seen From the Management Office

Orange County is often described as suburbs and farmland, and most of it is. The rental housing that keeps a management office busy is concentrated in a handful of places, and each one is a different kind of job. The City of Newburgh is a river city of brick rowhouses and walk-ups, most of them a century old or more. The City of Middletown has older two- to four-family houses on its side streets and apartment buildings near downtown. Port Jervis, at the western end of the county and the last stop on the Port Jervis Line, has walk-ups and converted houses on narrow lots. Kiryas Joel, coterminous with the Town of Palm Tree, is almost entirely apartment buildings. And between those places, along the highways and around the village centers, sit garden complexes, townhouse rentals and small mixed-use buildings over storefronts.

A landlord or a management company with buildings in more than one of those places is running several different kinds of property at once. An entry system that works in a forty-unit Kiryas Joel building would be wrong for a three-family house in Port Jervis, and a fob plan for a garden complex says nothing useful about a Newburgh rowhouse with one front door and a buzzer from another era. What ties the list together is the office: one person who needs to know what is installed at each address, who holds the logins, what failed last and what it cost. That is the job we take on. Every building gets a file, every visit starts from the file, and every invoice describes the work at one address.

The Census Bureau's numbers show how uneven the county's rental market is. American Community Survey figures for 2019 through 2023 count 43,352 renter-occupied homes in Orange County out of 137,311 occupied homes, about 32 percent. Inside the City of Newburgh the share flips: 6,689 of 10,335 occupied homes are rented, nearly 65 percent, and 5,747 of the city's 11,624 housing units were built in 1939 or earlier. Middletown rents 4,841 of its 10,182 occupied homes, and Port Jervis 1,702 of 3,533, close to half in both cities, with more than half of Port Jervis's housing units dating from 1939 or earlier. In Kiryas Joel, 4,462 of 6,869 occupied homes are rented, and 6,347 of its 7,383 housing units sit in buildings of five or more apartments, while only 215 are detached single-family houses.

Those figures explain most of what we find on a first visit. Old buildings mean old wiring and doors that have shifted in their frames. High rental shares mean constant turnover, which is where keys, codes and fobs go missing. Dense apartment blocks mean doors that open thousands of times a week. And a county where a third of households rent, spread across a wide area, means a manager's buildings are often an hour apart, so anything that can be checked or changed remotely saves a day of driving.

Three city laws that put an inspector in your buildings

Newburgh's rental license. Under Chapter 240 of the City of Newburgh Code, adopted as Local Law No. 1 of 2024, it is unlawful to offer or rent a dwelling unit in a building that is not owner-occupied without a rental license. No license is issued or renewed until the city's municipal officer has found that the property complies with the code, based on an inspection, and the license must be renewed every year, though owners the city designates as compliant landlords can receive a two-year term. Licenses do not transfer to another person or another property, and the application must state whether the building is equipped with a fire alarm system. For a landlord, that means an inspector walks the building on a regular schedule, and a front door that doesn't close and latch, or an entry system that has been half-disconnected, is the kind of thing that gets noticed.

Middletown's rental permit. The City of Middletown's law makes it unlawful to lease a single-family or owner-occupied two-family dwelling unit without a rental permit issued by the Department of Public Works, and it bars listing, advertising or showing such a unit, or accepting rent or a security deposit, unless a permit is in effect or a complete application has been filed. Applications and renewals are granted only after an inspection, and violations carry fines of $250 to $1,000, up to fifteen days in jail, or both, with each day a separate offense. Permits run in two-year terms. The practical result is that turnovers in Middletown houses come with an inspection, which is a good moment to make sure locks, doorbells and any cameras are in order before the next tenant moves in.

Port Jervis's vacant-building rules. The City of Port Jervis requires owners and mortgagees of vacant or abandoned buildings, including those in foreclosure, to register them and secure them within 30 days, and to provide proof of inspection at least every 30 days after the vacancy begins. The building official, the fire inspector and the police may inspect, violations can bring fines of up to $500 and up to 30 days in jail, and unpaid charges go on the city tax bill. An owner holding an empty building in Port Jervis therefore has a standing reason to know what is happening inside it between visits, which is where door contacts, a motion sensor and a camera earn their keep.

We are a security contractor, not a law firm, and nothing on this page is legal advice. We read these local laws because they decide when an inspector arrives and what a manager has to show, and we cite where each rule comes from so the owner's attorney can check the current text.

The state lead registry in Newburgh and Middletown

On top of the city rules, Newburgh and Middletown are two of the 25 communities where the state's Lead Rental Registry took effect on November 3, 2025. The Department of Health says the registry covers residential rental properties built before 1980 with two or more units, that a lead hazard inspection must be performed at least every three years by a qualified inspector, and that tenants should be given the dates and times ahead of scheduled inspections or repairs. At an April 2026 meeting, county staff told the Orange County Legislature that more than 1,000 properties had registered, about a quarter of those required, as the county accepted $728,600 in state funding for the program. We don't inspect for lead. What the registry does for a manager is create a recurring day when every apartment in an older building is open, and intercom handsets, apartment stations and in-unit wiring can be serviced on the same day instead of after a second round of tenant notices.

Newburgh's short-lived rent stabilization

Managers sometimes ask whether Newburgh buildings are rent-stabilized. The city council voted unanimously on December 18, 2023 to adopt the Emergency Tenant Protection Act for buildings constructed before 1974 with six or more units, after a vacancy study reported a 3.93 percent vacancy rate. Building owners challenged the study, and on April 19, 2024 the state Supreme Court in Orange County ruled it flawed and declared the city's adoption of the ETPA null and void, according to New York State Homes and Community Renewal. For security work, the point is narrower than the politics: a building's regulatory status can change, and an owner considering a move from in-apartment intercom stations to a phone-only entry system should have counsel check whether any change in service needs tenant consent or notice before work is scheduled.

Who we answer to in an Orange County building

The owner or the managing agent authorizes work, so quotes, appointments and invoices run through them. Many Orange County rentals are owned by small landlords who manage a handful of buildings themselves, and for them the owner and the office are the same person. Tenants who reach us directly are sent back to the office, and we don't schedule anything with a tenant on our own.

The maintenance lead or super is the person who meets us at the door. In Orange County that person often covers buildings in several towns, so we label the equipment, leave a one-page sheet in each equipment room, and tell them exactly what to look for before calling.

Inspectors from the city building department, the fire inspector or a lead inspector are not our clients, but their visits drive a lot of our scheduling. When an office tells us an inspection date, we plan around it.

Tenants judge the whole system by two things: whether the door opens for them with their hands full, and whether a visitor can reach them. Their complaints are the office's early warning, and when they reach us they go back to the office.

Entry panels, buzzers and apartment intercoms

In a Newburgh rowhouse or a Port Jervis walk-up, the entry system is often a row of buttons at the street door, a transformer in the basement and a handset or speaker in each apartment, wired decades ago and patched since. When "the buzzer doesn't work," the cause is usually at one end: a button corroded by winter salt, a transformer that finally burned out, a handset a tenant pulled off the wall, or a strike that no longer releases. The cable between them is frequently fine. We test each line before recommending anything, repair what failed, and only price a new system when the wiring itself has given up or the owner wants features the old system can't give.

Our door buzzer repair page for Orange County publishes the parts ranges: $250–$525 to replace an apartment station, $250–$475 for a door release relay or button, $325–$650 for a transformer, $450–$950 for a strike with alignment, $650–$1,800 for lobby panel repair or a new faceplate, and $850–$2,400 for riser pair repair or re-termination. New systems are on our Orange County intercom installation page, which lists video intercoms for four- to eight-unit buildings at $1,200–$3,500, multi-unit building systems at $1,200–$5,000 and app-connected systems for larger buildings at $2,500–$8,000 and up. Repairs to existing intercoms of any brand are covered on our Orange County intercom repair page.

In the larger apartment buildings of Kiryas Joel and in garden complexes, the entry is usually a directory panel in a vestibule. There the failures shift toward the panel and the network: a screen that went dark, a panel that lost its connection when the internet account changed, or a directory nobody has updated since three turnovers ago. A panel that calls tenants' phones suits a building of commuters; a building with older tenants or large families sharing one phone number may do better with in-apartment stations, and the owner should hear that before choosing.

Strikes, maglocks and doors that won't latch

A large share of the "intercom" calls we take from Orange County landlords are really door calls. Old wood frames swell and shrink with the seasons, steel frames in walk-ups get knocked out of line by moving day, and doors in busy buildings simply wear out their closers. When the latch stops meeting the strike, the release upstairs buzzes and the door stays shut, or the door closes but never locks. We reset the strike to where the latch actually lands, adjust or replace the closer, add a door-position switch where the office wants to know when a door is left open, and test the release from every apartment we can reach. Where a door needs new hinges, a new frame or new mechanical locks, we tell the office so the carpenter or locksmith can do that part.

Exits get the same attention as entries. A maglock needs a way out that works when its power is cut, a request-to-exit sensor or button, and a release tied to the fire alarm where one exists. A strike must leave the door openable from inside with one motion. We install those parts as part of the job, and when existing hardware doesn't meet that standard, we say so in writing.

Fobs, readers and access control

Turnover is where keys get expensive. A fob or code system lets the office add a tenant once and cancel them once, for every door they should open: the front door, a rear door to the lot, the laundry room and the storage area. For a landlord with several small buildings, a cloud platform puts them all in one dashboard; for a single building with unreliable internet, a local controller with no monthly fee may be the better choice. We set up each staff member with their own login and only the permissions their job needs, and we show the office how to compare active fobs against the current rent roll, which is the simplest way to find a credential that should have been cancelled months ago.

Our access control installation page for Orange County publishes $1,700–$5,000 for a single door or gate with a keypad, fob reader or gate controller, $4,500–$15,000 for a multi-door property such as a lobby, service entrance and basement or a vehicle gate with a pedestrian gate, and $15,000–$50,000 and up for a multi-building campus or full apartment complex. Those are installed figures; any monthly platform fee is shown on its own line so the owner can compare the long-run cost of each option.

Gates, lots and the paths between buildings

Garden complexes and townhouse rentals in Orange County usually have lots instead of garages, and sometimes a gate at the entrance road. Each controlled point should take the same credential as the building doors, so the office manages one list. We add readers or keypads at gates, connect them to the access system, and coordinate with the gate company that maintains the operator, because the motor and the safety devices are theirs. Cameras for lots, gates and walkways are covered on our Orange County parking lot camera page.

Elevator readers in the larger buildings

Most Orange County rentals are walk-ups, but the larger apartment and senior buildings have elevators, and some owners want floors restricted to residents. A reader in the cab tied to the floor buttons does that through the elevator controller, so the building's elevator maintenance company is part of the job and we schedule with them. Our elevator access control page for Orange County publishes $2,100 per car for a Level 1 card, fob, code or intercom reader and $3,500 per car for a Level 2 face, fingerprint or iris reader, with the elevator contractor's own visit billed by them.

Cameras for entrances, halls and lots

Cameras have their own pages, so here is the management version. In Orange County rentals the incidents that reach an office are packages taken from porches and vestibules, car break-ins and dumping in the lot, damage to doors and entry panels, arguments in shared halls, and falls on stairs and walks. We plan coverage around those events, keep cameras away from apartment doors and windows, and record locally to a recorder in a locked room with remote viewing for the office. Our apartment building security camera installation page for Orange County covers rental buildings in depth and prices building systems at roughly $500–$1,500 per camera installed. Smaller properties start with the four-camera systems from $1,199 on our Orange County security camera installation page, with every model listed on our Orange County camera pricing page. Condominium buildings have their own condo security camera installation page for Orange County.

Empty units and vacant buildings

Vacancy is a bigger part of the job in Orange County than in the city. Units sit empty between tenants and during renovations, and Port Jervis requires vacant buildings to be registered and inspected every 30 days. An empty unit is exactly where pipes get stripped, squatters move in and damage goes unnoticed until the next showing. A small intrusion alarm, door contacts on each entrance, a motion sensor inside and a camera on the approach tell the office when someone enters and give whoever does the 30-day check a record of what happened in between. Central-station monitoring is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, as published on our Orange County alarm installation page. When the unit is rented, the alarm can stay for the owner's storage areas or come out.

Fire alarm systems

Fire alarm work is in our scope in Orange County. Outside New York City, approval runs through the city, town or village code enforcement office under the state Uniform Code, and the sequence is drawings from an engineer or architect, approval, installation and an acceptance test. Our Orange County fire alarm installation page explains the steps. We don't publish fire alarm prices; each system is priced from approved drawings. Where a building has electric locks, the fire alarm must release them, so we plan the two together.

Cabling, networks and equipment rooms

Every system above depends on cable and a network. We pull Cat6 to each device, set PoE switches in a locked cabinet, add battery backup and keep security equipment on its own network so cameras and readers never compete with a tenant's Wi-Fi or a laundry vendor's card readers. In old Newburgh and Port Jervis buildings the hard part is the route through plaster walls and stone basements; in garden complexes it is getting a network from one building to the next. Our Orange County structured cabling page lists Cat6 data drops at $175 and up per drop. We don't do electrical work; a new circuit for a gate operator or an equipment cabinet goes to the building's licensed electrician, and we coordinate with them.

Power and outages on three utilities

Orange County buildings sit on three different electric systems. The state Department of Public Service lists NYSEG, Central Hudson and Orange & Rockland as electric utilities in the county, and the utilities' own 2026 updates show how often storms reach this area: Orange & Rockland reported more than 47,000 customers affected by heat, storms and heavy rain between July 1 and July 6, 2026, and Central Hudson reported more than 19,000 customers out after a nor'easter in late September 2026. When power drops and returns, the equipment that fails is the unprotected equipment: a PoE switch, a panel power supply or a recorder that reboots before its cameras. We put battery backup on the access controller and the network switch, surge protection on outdoor runs, and a note in the equipment room listing what to restart in what order. Doors on fail-secure strikes stay locked from outside in an outage and still open from inside; maglocks release, so buildings that rely on them need backup sized for hours, not minutes.

Video doorbells and smart locks for houses

Many Orange County rental lists are mostly houses: single-family rentals, two-families and three-families where each apartment has its own door. A shared entry panel rarely makes sense there. A wired video doorbell at each entrance, set up in the tenant's account and reset at each turnover, does the job, with the owner's own cameras on the driveway and yard in the owner's account. Our Orange County doorbell camera installation page covers that work, and code locks for rentals with frequent turnover or furnished rooms are on our smart lock installation page for Orange County.

Who holds the logins

A surprising number of failed systems in Orange County rentals are not broken at all. The panel, the cameras or the fob system work, but the administrator account belongs to a previous owner, a former super or an installer who moved away, so nothing can be changed. When buildings change hands often, and Newburgh's rental license does not transfer with a sale, that problem repeats. We put administrator rights in the owner's name, give the managing agent a separate administrator login and give staff limited accounts, so a sale or a change of manager means removing one set of accounts and adding another.

Newburgh rowhouses and walk-ups: start with the street door

Nearly half of Newburgh's housing units were built in 1939 or earlier, and the rowhouses and walk-ups show it. The street door is usually wood in a wood frame, painted many times and swollen or shrunk depending on the season. The entry system is a row of buttons, a transformer in the basement and a handset upstairs, often with one or two apartments disconnected over the years. The most valuable single fix is almost always the door: a strike set true to the latch, a closer that pulls the door fully shut, and a release that works from every apartment. After that comes the panel. If the wiring tests sound, a new button panel or a two-wire video system can reuse it; if not, new cable per line is priced so the owner can decide floor by floor. Newburgh's annual rental license inspection is a fair reason to get this done before the inspector's visit rather than after.

Basements in these buildings matter as much as lobbies. The transformer, any recorder and the network equipment usually live there, beside the boiler or the water heater, on a damp floor. We lift equipment onto shelves or walls, give it a locked cabinet, keep it clear of the boiler's heat and label everything, because the person who next opens that basement door may not be the person who was there last time.

Converted multifamily houses: three entrances, one system

Big old houses in Middletown, Port Jervis and Newburgh were often split into apartments decades ago, and the entrances followed the floor plan rather than any security idea: one apartment off the front porch, another up a side stair, a third through the back. The best layout usually gives each entrance its own small panel or doorbell for the apartments behind it, all running from one power supply and one controller in the basement, so the owner maintains a single system. Cameras go on the approaches to each door and on the driveway, with the tenants' own doorbells kept in their own accounts. Middletown's rental permit inspections on two-year terms, and at changes of occupancy, are natural points to check that every entrance still locks and every doorbell still rings.

Kiryas Joel apartment buildings: doors that never rest

Kiryas Joel's housing is unlike anywhere else in the county. Census data shows 86 percent of its housing units in buildings of five or more apartments and an average household of about 5.1 people, so a forty-unit building can be home to two hundred people. Lobby doors cycle all day, strollers and carts hit them constantly, and the fob or code list changes often. We use heavy-duty closers and strikes rated for frequent cycling, set the hold-open timing so a parent with a stroller gets through, add kick plates and door-position switches, and keep spare credentials with the office so a new family is enrolled the day they arrive. Vestibule cameras aimed at the door and the mailboxes cover the incidents an office actually hears about.

Garden complexes: reach across the grounds

A garden complex has no single front door. It has a dozen or more building entrances spread across lawns and lots, mailbox clusters near the road, a laundry room or two, a maintenance shop and sometimes a pool. The work here is mostly about reach. Every entrance should take the same credential so the office keeps one list, every building needs a network path back to the controller and the recorder, and the outdoor areas, lots, paths, dumpster corrals and mailboxes, matter as much as the doors because that is where incidents happen. We survey the grounds first, use existing conduit where we can and wireless links between buildings where trenching isn't practical, and phase the work building by building so no tenant is ever without a working entrance.

Townhouse and newer rental communities

Newer rental communities usually arrive with a leasing office, a gate or an amenity building on a platform the developer picked, and private front doors on each unit. The management firm that takes over often finds the administrator account in a developer's name and a subscription billed to someone else's card. Our first visit is an inventory: what is installed, who holds each login, what is billed monthly, and what the developer left unfinished. Private unit doors are usually best served by a doorbell camera or a code lock per unit, reset at every turnover, rather than a shared system.

Mixed-use buildings on Orange County main streets

Apartments above a deli, a salon or a pizzeria share a building with a business that keeps different hours and has its own staff, deliveries and, often, its own cameras. The residential stair needs a hard boundary: shop staff shouldn't hold a credential for the apartment door, a shared cellar or rear yard should be controlled from the residential side, and the camera on the apartment entrance should belong to the building, not to the store. We draw that boundary into the access plan and keep the store's own cameras and monitor on its own account unless the owner wants them combined.

Student rentals and commuter rentals

Mount Saint Mary College sits in Newburgh, and SUNY Orange has campuses in Middletown and Newburgh, so both cities have houses and small buildings rented by the room. Turnover there follows the academic year, and keys walk away every May. Code or fob locks on the front door, and on bedroom doors where the lease is by the bedroom, let the office cancel one credential instead of rekeying, and an app-based intercom can ring every roommate's phone. Commuter rentals cluster near the Port Jervis Line stations at Harriman, Salisbury Mills–Cornwall, Campbell Hall, Middletown–Town of Wallkill, Otisville and Port Jervis. Tenants there leave early and come home late, so cars sit in lots all day, packages sit on porches, and the cameras that matter most are the ones on the lot and the delivery spot.

Single-family rentals and vacant buildings

Many Orange County landlords own scattered single-family houses rather than apartment buildings. Each needs only a doorbell per entrance, an owner camera on the driveway and perhaps a code lock, but the list can be long and spread over several towns. The vacant end of the list needs something different: an intrusion alarm, door contacts and a camera that let the owner know when someone goes in, and in Port Jervis, a record that supports the city's 30-day inspection requirement for registered vacant buildings.

Your Questions Answered

Property Management Security in Orange County: Questions Answered

Licenses, registries and inspections

Our Port Jervis building is on the city's vacant-property register. Can sensors and a camera back up the inspection the city asks for every 30 days?

They can support it, though they don't replace it. The Port Jervis vacant-building rules ask the owner or mortgagee to provide proof of inspection at least every 30 days after a building becomes vacant, and the city's building official, fire inspector and police can inspect too. A door contact on each entrance, a motion sensor inside, and one or two cameras with dated recordings give the person doing those checks a record of what happened between visits, and an alert if a door is forced. Ask the city what form of proof it accepts; we install the equipment, and the inspection itself stays with the owner.

The Newburgh rental license application asks whether the building has a fire alarm system. What should we have on file before we answer it?

Know exactly what is in the building before anyone fills in the form. City of Newburgh Chapter 240 asks the application to state whether the structure is equipped with a fire alarm system, and a smoke detector in each apartment is not the same thing as a panel with pull stations and horns. We can identify what is installed, whether a panel is working and who monitors it, and leave a short written description for the file. If a panel needs work, that is fire alarm work in our scope, done from approved drawings with local code enforcement.

Older houses and walk-ups

Our Newburgh rowhouse still has its original wood front door and frame. Can an electric strike go in without replacing the door?

Usually, yes. A strike is mortised into the frame, not the door, so the question is whether the frame has enough solid wood behind the latch side to hold it. On old rowhouse doors we often find layers of paint, a frame that has shifted and a latch that barely engages. We check the frame, square the strike to where the latch actually lands, and run the wire in a slim raceway if the frame can't be fished. If the frame is split or rotted, we say so before ordering anything, because a strike in soft wood fails fast.

A converted Middletown house has apartments on the front entrance and a side stair. Does it need one call panel or two?

Usually two small ones. Visitors go to the door that leads to the apartment they want, so each entrance gets a panel or a set of buttons for the apartments behind it. Both can run from one power supply and one controller in the basement, so the owner still has a single system to maintain. One panel at the front only works if the side-stair tenants are happy walking guests around the house, which in practice they rarely are.

Large families, students and commuters

Families in our Kiryas Joel buildings average more than five people per apartment. How does that change the lobby door hardware?

It means the door cycles far more often than the hardware catalog assumes, and strollers and carts hit it every day. Census figures put the village at about 5.1 people per household. We size the closer for heavy use, set the strike and the hold-open timing so a parent with a stroller can get through without the door closing on them, add a kick plate and a door-position switch, and check the hinges, because a sagging door is what usually defeats the latch. A reader that takes a fob at a distance helps when hands are full.

Our house near Mount Saint Mary College rents by the bedroom. Should each bedroom door get its own lock or code?

If the lease is per bedroom, a code or fob lock on each bedroom door makes turnover easier: when a student leaves, the office cancels one code instead of rekeying a lock. The front door then takes a code or fob for every tenant in the house. Bedroom locks must still open from inside with a simple lever, and mechanical lock changes on bedroom doors can also go to the owner's locksmith; we handle the electronic side and the front entrance.

Running buildings from a distance

Our office is in Goshen and our buildings are an hour apart. What can be fixed remotely before anyone drives out?

More than most offices expect. On networked systems we can see whether a panel or a reader is online, restart a recorder, check that every camera is still recording, review the door log for a door held open, and change codes and schedules. What can't be fixed from a laptop is physical: a strike out of line, a dead power supply, a cut cable or a broken handset. Setting buildings up for remote access is a one-time job, and it is the difference between a phone call and a wasted trip down Route 17.

Rents in Newburgh are lower than the county median. How do we keep security spending in proportion to the rent roll?

Spend first on the things that protect the building and fail the most: the street door that must latch, the entry system every tenant uses, and cameras at the two or three spots where incidents happen. Census figures put Newburgh's median gross rent at $1,363 against $1,602 for the county, so we write scopes that fix the door and the entry before anything else, reuse sound wiring, and leave optional extras on separate lines. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Our buildings are spread across three electric utilities. Should outage planning differ from building to building?

A little. The state's utility map lists NYSEG, Central Hudson and Orange & Rockland all serving parts of Orange County, and each restores power on its own schedule. The basics are the same everywhere: battery backup on the access controller and the network switch, surge protection on outdoor runs, and a note in the closet on what to restart in what order. What changes is how long a building tends to wait, so we size backup batteries for the buildings that sit at the end of long rural lines rather than the ones in town.

Daily operation

The recorder in our walk-up's boiler room keeps failing. Where else can it go?

Somewhere cooler, drier and locked. Boiler rooms run hot all winter, and the hard drive and power supply are what give out first. A closet on the first floor, a locked cabinet in a stairwell landing, or a ventilated cabinet on the basement wall farthest from the boiler all work, as long as the cameras' cables can reach it. We move the recorder, the switch and the battery backup together, raise them off the floor, and leave a note in the old spot saying where they went.

Melting snow refreezes on the threshold of our garden-building doors and the doors won't latch. Is that a hardware problem?

Partly. Ice on the sill holds the door off its latch, and once a strike is pushed out of line by a door that has been forced shut against ice, it stays out of line in spring. We set the strike and the closer so the door pulls fully shut, check that the threshold and sweep clear, and add a door-position switch so the office gets an alert when a door stays open. Clearing the ice itself is a grounds job, but a building where the door reports itself open stops losing heat and tenants' patience.

We may sell one of our Port Jervis buildings within a year. Is it worth putting in cameras and a new entry panel now?

Usually only the parts that protect the building while you hold it. Fix the street door and the entry system tenants depend on, and add cameras where the incidents are, on a recorder that stays with the building. Keep administrator accounts in the owner entity's name rather than a staff member's, so they can be handed to a buyer at closing. Skip platform subscriptions with long contract terms, which a buyer may not want, and keep the equipment list and the passwords in one file the buyer's manager can take over.

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Property Management Security in Orange County

Landlords increasingly arrive with an answer from a chatbot or a search summary. Most of those answers were written for New York City or for a single house somewhere else. These are the ones we correct most often in Orange County.

1. "Newburgh buildings are rent-stabilized"

The city adopted the ETPA in December 2023, but the state Supreme Court declared that adoption null and void in April 2024, according to New York State Homes and Community Renewal. Owners should still have counsel confirm a building's current status before changing how tenants are reached from the door.

2. "HPD rules decide when you can enter an apartment"

HPD is a New York City agency. In Orange County, access is governed by the lease, state law and local code, and the office sets the notice. We follow what the office gives tenants.

3. "Lead rules only matter in the city"

The state Lead Rental Registry now covers pre-1980 rentals with two or more units in Newburgh and Middletown, with inspections at least every three years. It isn't security work, but it decides when every apartment in an older building will be open.

4. "Kiryas Joel is a village of owner-occupied homes"

Census figures show about 65 percent of its occupied homes are rented, and 86 percent of its housing units are in buildings of five or more apartments. It is one of the most apartment-heavy places in the county, and its entry systems work harder than most.

5. "A rental inspection checks your cameras"

Newburgh's license and Middletown's permit inspections are about compliance with the code, not about whether a building has cameras. What does come up is whether doors close and lock, whether exits work and, on Newburgh's application, whether there is a fire alarm system.

6. "An apartment intercom costs about two thousand dollars"

National cost guides quote single-house prices. Our Orange County intercom page lists $1,200–$3,500 for a video intercom in a four- to eight-unit building and $2,500–$8,000 and up for app-connected systems in larger ones, while a single failed part in an old system is often a few hundred dollars. That is why we test before recommending replacement.

7. "Security wiring is the electrician's job"

Electricians handle line-voltage power. Entry, camera and access systems are low-voltage work, and alarm installers in New York are licensed by the state. The two trades work side by side, and we coordinate with the building's electrician whenever a new circuit is needed.

FAQ

Orange County Property Management Security FAQ

Will the office get an alert if someone pries open a lobby entry panel?
+

On most current panels, yes. A tamper switch inside the housing reports when the faceplate is removed, and the platform can send that alert to the office or the super. On older panels without one, a door contact or a camera aimed at the panel does the same job.

Do card and fob readers keep working at exposed garden-building doors in winter?
+

Outdoor-rated readers do. We use readers rated for the temperature range they'll face, mount them under an overhang or in a weather hood where possible, and seal the cable entry so water can't freeze inside the housing. Indoor readers on outside walls are a common cause of winter failures.

Can a lobby panel be mounted where a wheelchair user can reach it?
+

Yes. We set the panel's buttons and screen within reach from a seated position and leave clear floor space in front of it, and we can add a second, lower panel where an entrance has steps on one side and a ramp on the other.

Can part of a camera's view be blacked out so it never records a neighbor's windows?
+

Yes. Most recorders and cameras support privacy masks, which black out a chosen area of the picture before it is recorded. We set them at installation and show the office how they look on playback.

In a shared student apartment, can the intercom ring every roommate's phone?
+

On app-based systems, yes. One apartment entry can ring several phones at once, and each roommate gets their own login, so when one moves out the office removes that person without touching the others.

Can keypads at dim side entrances have lit buttons?
+

Yes. Many outdoor keypads have backlit buttons, and we choose those for side and rear doors where there is little light. A camera with its own lamp over the same door helps both the keypad user and the recording.

When a recorder's hard drive fills up, does it stop recording?
+

Not if it is set up correctly. The recorder overwrites the oldest footage first, so the drive always holds the most recent days. We set that at installation and size the drive for the number of days the owner wants kept.

Will an old telephone-entry panel stop working if the building's landline is cancelled?
+

If it dials out over a phone line, yes. Before a line is cancelled, the panel can be moved to a cellular module or replaced with a network panel, so tenants can still be called from the door.

Can our office export a month of door events for an owner's report?
+

On networked access platforms, yes. Staff with the right permission can filter the event log by building, door and dates and export it as a file. We show the office how to do it at handover.

Can a tenant's fob be set to stop working on the last day of the lease?
+

Yes, on most platforms. Each credential can carry an end date, so it stops opening doors on its own. If the tenant renews, the office extends the date instead of issuing a new fob.
