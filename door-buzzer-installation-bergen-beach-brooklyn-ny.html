The Neighborhood

Why Bergen Beach Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Bergen Beach is one of the few corners of Brooklyn where an entry system means a doorbell on a house rather than a panel in a lobby. Brooklyn Community Board 18 describes Bergen Beach and Georgetowne as predominantly residential and zoned mainly for one- and two-family homes, and the interior streets, Avenue T, Bergen Avenue, Royce Street and the numbered East 60s and 70s, are lined with low-rise brick and stucco houses built after the Second World War, most with a driveway and a patch of lawn. A house like that has no shared lobby, no superintendent and no riser of intercom cable. It has a front door, usually a side door off the driveway, sometimes a separate door for a basement or a rental apartment, and a family spread over two or three floors who all need to know when someone is there. That is the job a door buzzer installation does here.

The two-family house is where the work gets more interesting. Bergen Beach listings show the pattern over and over: a brick two-family on East 68th Street with an owner's triplex and a separate one-bedroom rental that has its own private entrance; a detached stucco two-family with a rental studio; a corner two-family on Avenue N with the owner's four-bedroom over a one-bedroom tenant apartment, over a finished basement with its own entrance. In each of those houses there are two households and at least two doors, and a single doorbell bought for a one-family house sends every visitor, every courier and every food delivery to the wrong people half the time. The fix is not complicated, but it has to be planned: one button per household, each ringing only its own floor, each household able to see and talk to its own visitor, and a release only on the doors that need one.

Most of these houses are also much newer than the brownstones and walk-ups people picture when they think of Brooklyn buzzers. Marshy ground held back building, and apart from a small cluster of houses along Avenue U the land waited for fuller landfilling in the 1960s, and large-scale building followed from then into the 1980s and 1990s, with some lots on the Georgetowne side empty until the 2000s. A two-family on Bergen Court that sold through Brownstoner, for example, was custom built in 2003 on an R3-1 lot. There are older houses, including a detached house from 1930 and a detached Cape from 1955 in recent sales, and 6710 Avenue T is listed as dating from 1899, but the typical Bergen Beach doorbell was installed by a builder in the 1960s to 1990s as a plain button, a transformer and a chime in the hall. That kind of wiring is much easier to reuse than the cloth-covered bell wire in a pre-war rowhouse, though it still has to be tested, because it was sized for a chime, not for a camera.

Water is the other thing that sets Bergen Beach apart. The neighborhood is surrounded on three sides by Paerdegat Basin, Mill Basin and East Mill Basin, with Jamaica Bay beyond, and the Homes.com neighborhood guide says most residences are at extreme risk of flooding and that more than 200 properties were affected during Hurricane Sandy. Flooding is not only a hurricane story here, either: News 12 Brooklyn reported rain flooding in Bergen Beach in December 2018, and in April 2021 ran a story headlined "Bergen Beach neighbors fed up with ongoing flooding woes." For a doorbell or buzzer, that matters in two places. The old transformer and chime are often in the basement, near the electrical panel, which is the worst place to keep electronics in a house that has taken water before. And the door station itself lives outside, facing salt wind off the bay, freeze-thaw cycles and driving rain, which ButterflyMX notes is why outdoor components usually show problems first.

Getting around shapes the job too. Bergen Beach has no subway; residents rely on buses, cars and the Belt Parkway, and the nearest Q and B station at Kings Highway is about three miles away. That is why so many houses here have driveways and garages and why the door visitors really use is so often the side door beside the driveway rather than the front door on the street. It is also why deliveries matter: when most of the household drives to work, the doorbell is the one thing at home that can answer a courier, and an owner at work wants to see and talk to that courier from a phone. The same driveways are where cars sit overnight, and a Nextdoor post from the neighborhood reported a car stolen in September 2025, so many owners ask whether the doorbell can also watch the driveway. Usually it cannot do both well, and we explain why further down.

Six Bergen Beach facts that decide a doorbell or buzzer plan before any equipment is chosen

  • Houses, not lobbies. Community Board 18 describes Bergen Beach and Georgetowne as zoned predominantly for one- and two-family homes. Most jobs here have one or two households and one or two doors, which keeps the equipment simple and puts the thinking into who answers, from where, and which door.
  • Two households, two doors. Bergen Beach listings regularly describe a rental unit with its own private entrance, a basement with a separate entrance, or an owner's unit over a tenant's. Each household needs its own button that rings only its own space.
  • The side door is the front door. Post-war houses on driveways mean visitors often walk up the drive to a side entrance. The doorbell belongs where people actually knock, not where the architect put the front steps.
  • Newer wiring, but sized for a chime. Much of the housing went up from the 1960s through the 1990s, and one listed two-family dates from 2003. Builder doorbell wiring is often reusable after testing, but its transformer may be too weak for a video doorbell.

Under New York's Multiple Dwelling Law, a building occupied by no more than two families is a private dwelling, so the intercom requirements written for apartment buildings do not reach most Bergen Beach houses. A buzzer here is the owner's choice, made for convenience and safety, not to satisfy an inspector.

Need Repair in Bergen Beach: Silent Bells, Dead Doorbell Cameras and Doors That Will Not Release

A lot of calls from Bergen Beach start as "we need a new doorbell" and turn out to be something smaller. Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Bergen Beach's Architecture

Six kinds of property make up nearly every doorbell and buzzer job we see in Bergen Beach. What separates them is not the equipment, which overlaps a great deal, but three practical things: how many households have to be told apart at the door, which doors visitors really use, and what the outside wall is made of where the station has to be fixed.

The detached one-family house

One household, often two or three floors and a finished basement, a front door and a side door off the driveway. The job is usually a wired video doorbell at the door people use, chimes on more than one floor and phones for anyone out.

The semi-detached house

A shared wall on one side, a driveway or path on the other, and a front door close to the neighbor's. The camera's angle and what it records past the property line need more thought than on a detached lot.

The two-family with one shared door

An owner's unit and a rental behind a single entrance or a shared vestibule. Two buttons at the door, each ringing only its own household, and a release that suits both floors.

The two-family with its own rental entrance

A rental apartment or basement unit with a private door, common in Bergen Beach listings. Two separate doorbells, kept apart in wiring, chimes and app accounts, so neither household hears or sees the other's visitors.

The condo or townhouse in a complex

Gated communities in the Georgetowne section, such as Mill Harbor, with association rules and a shared entry already in place. The work is usually a doorbell or station at an owner's own door, with the association's approval.

The shop, office or church

Businesses on Ralph Avenue and Avenues N and T, and institutions such as the Church of St. Bernard, where a door may need to stay locked and buzz people in during the day.

The detached one-family house: the doorbell has to reach the people, not just the hall

In a detached Bergen Beach house, the old chime usually hangs in the front hall or the kitchen, put there by the builder when the whole family spent the day on the main floor. Today the same house often has a finished basement used as a den or an office, bedrooms upstairs, a deck out back and people in and out by car. A chime in the hall does not reach a basement with the television on, and it does not reach anyone at work. The plan for a one-family house therefore starts with a short walk through it: where people are during the day, where they are at night, and where the doorbell is not heard now. That tells us whether a second chime, a plug-in chime on another floor, an indoor monitor or simply phones is the answer, and usually it is a combination.

The semi-detached house: a shared wall and a neighbor's door a few feet away

Semi-detached houses are common here; one Bergen Beach listing describes a semi-detached brick two-family on a 26-by-100-foot lot. On a house like that, the two front doors can be close together, sometimes sharing a stoop or a front walk, and the doorbell camera's field of view easily takes in the neighbor's door, their path and part of their driveway. That is a reason to choose the mounting angle carefully, to use a wedge or corner mount that turns the camera toward your own walk, and to set up privacy zones in the app so the camera does not record the other half of the building. Habitat magazine reported one New York building manager saying that as video doorbells spread, "we entered it into the house rules," and while a semi-detached house has no house rules, it does have a neighbor who will notice a camera pointed at their door.

The two-family with one shared door: two buttons, two households, one entrance

Some Bergen Beach two-families share a single street door that opens into a small hall, with the owner's apartment on one floor and the tenant's on another. These houses need what a small apartment building needs, scaled down: a door station with two buttons, an answering point in each apartment, and a release on the shared door so each household can let in its own guests. The difference from a walk-up is that the owner usually lives in the house, knows the tenant, and can decide everything in one visit. We can do it with audio handsets, video monitors, phone answering or a mix, and the price stays in the house ranges on our list because there are only two apartments to wire.

The common mistake in these houses is a single consumer video doorbell bought for the whole house. It rings everyone's phone, the owner sees the tenant's visitors and the tenant sees the owner's, and whoever is closest ends up answering the door for both. That is fine between relatives and awkward between landlord and tenant. A two-button station, or two doorbells side by side on the same door, solves it at the source. Where the households really are family, such as grown children upstairs and parents below, one shared doorbell with each person's phone added may be exactly right, and it costs less. We ask who lives in the house before recommending either.

The two-family with its own rental entrance: keep the two systems apart

The layout Bergen Beach listings describe most often is a two-family where the rental has its own way in: a one-bedroom on East 68th Street with its own private entrance, a basement apartment with a separate door, a rental studio in a detached stucco house. In those houses there is no shared door to release, and the right answer is usually two independent doorbells, one at each entrance, each with its own chime and its own app account. Keeping them separate protects both households' privacy, means the tenant does not need the owner's login and the owner does not need the tenant's, and lets either one be replaced or upgraded later without touching the other.

Separate does not have to mean twice the work. Both doorbells can often take power from the same low-voltage supply in the owner's part of the house, with separate cables to each door and separate chimes. If the tenant's entrance is at the side or back and the old doorbell wiring only ever reached the front, a new cable runs along the outside of the foundation or through the basement ceiling to reach it. And if the owner wants to keep a view of the whole property, that is a camera job, separate from the tenant's doorbell, so the tenant's visitors are not recorded on the owner's account. We price the camera on the same visit if it is wanted.

The condo or townhouse in a complex: the association comes first

The Georgetowne section has several gated condominium communities. Mill Harbor, at 1561 Royce Street, was built in 2005 with 208 units over four stories, is listed as a gated community, and has an intercom connected to a front desk. Other Bergen Beach condo listings sit on East 72nd and East 73rd Streets and on Cove Lane. In buildings and complexes like these, the main entry, the gate and the lobby intercom belong to the association, which maintains and replaces them through its own contractors. An individual owner's door is a different matter, and owners regularly want a doorbell camera or a better bell at their own unit door or townhouse entrance.

Before we install anything at a unit in a complex, the owner checks with the association or managing agent. Many associations have rules about what may be fixed to doors and exterior walls, about drilling through common elements and about cameras that look into common hallways or other owners' doors. We are happy to send the association a short description of the equipment, the mounting and the camera's view for approval, and to provide a certificate of insurance if the managing agent asks for one. If the association itself wants to replace or upgrade the complex's entry system, that is a larger job priced from the multi-unit part of our list after a walkthrough.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Look at on a Bergen Beach House Before Quoting a Doorbell

These notes describe our standard routine on post-war houses like those in Bergen Beach. They are how we work, not a story about one particular address.

Find the door people actually use. The technician asks, and then looks: which door has the worn mat, the package shelf, the path from the driveway. The answer is the side door more often than owners expect, and it decides where the best station goes.

Open the storm door. Many detached houses have a glass or aluminum storm door in front of the main door. A doorbell mounted behind it cannot be reached by a visitor and its camera looks through glass, so the station almost always goes on the trim outside the storm door, and we check there is room for it beside the storm door's hinge or latch.

Read the transformer. The old doorbell transformer, usually in the basement near the electrical panel or on a junction box, has its voltage and VA rating printed on it. A wired video doorbell needs a supply that meets the maker's requirement, and many builder-era transformers fall short. We note the rating and test the voltage at the button before deciding anything.

Test the old wire. Builder doorbell wire in a 1960s to 1990s house is usually intact, but it may be thin, spliced at the chime or nicked where it passes the door frame. If it is going to carry a video doorbell, it is tested end to end, not assumed.

Check the Wi-Fi at the door. A detached house can be long, and the router often sits at the back or in the basement. The technician checks the signal at each door with a phone before recommending a Wi-Fi doorbell, because a weak signal at the door is the most common reason a video doorbell keeps dropping offline.

Look at the wall and the water line. Brick, stucco, vinyl siding and wood trim each take a different fixing and a different seal. And in a house that has flooded, we ask how high the water came, so the power supply, chime and any recorder go above it.

Power and Wiring

Old Doorbell Wiring, New Video Doorbells: What a Bergen Beach House Can Carry

The single most common technical question on a Bergen Beach doorbell job is whether the house's existing doorbell circuit can run a modern video doorbell. A traditional doorbell circuit is simple: a small transformer steps the house's 120 volts down to a low voltage, a pair of thin wires runs to the button outside and to the chime inside, and pressing the button completes the circuit so the chime sounds. That circuit was designed to deliver a brief pulse to a chime. A video doorbell needs steady power around the clock for its camera, its radio and its speaker, and it needs more of it.

What the makers ask for. The doorbell makers publish their requirements, and they are specific. Ring lists its Video Doorbell Wired as needing a hardwired transformer of 10 to 24 volts AC, 50/60 Hz, rated 8VA to 40VA, while its Wired Doorbell Plus, Pro and 2K Plus need 16 to 24 volts AC, rated 10VA to 40VA. Lorex says its wired video doorbells "require a constant power supply with a voltage between 16V-24V AC," that a 10-volt transformer is too weak, and lists 16V 30VA and 24V 40VA transformers as compatible. Ring also warns that "Using incompatible power sources can damage your device and cause electric shock." Many transformers installed with builder doorbells are rated well below those numbers, which is why a video doorbell on old power often reboots, drops its connection or fails in the cold.

Who changes the transformer. The low-voltage side of a doorbell, the button, the chime and the thin wires between them, is a Class 2 signaling circuit, and that is our work. The transformer itself is fed from the house's 120-volt wiring, and building codes treat that connection as standard electrical work. Where a new hardwired transformer is needed, a licensed electrician makes the 120-volt connection; we tell you that before the job is priced, and we can coordinate it so it happens on the same day. In many Bergen Beach houses we avoid the question altogether by powering the doorbell from a dedicated low-voltage supply that plugs into an existing outlet, placed high on a basement wall or in a utility closet, with new low-voltage cable to the door.

What happens to the old chime. Some video doorbells work with the existing mechanical chime and some do not. Ring states, for example, that its Wired Doorbell 4K Pro and 2K Plus are not compatible with existing chimes unless the 4K Pro is powered by PoE, and points owners to a plug-in chime instead. Other models need a small adapter fitted inside the chime box. We check the chime in the house against the doorbell before ordering, and where the old chime cannot be kept we fit a plug-in chime on each floor that needs one. One Brownstoner forum member, whose old doorbell wiring was too far gone, did exactly that with plug-in chimes that sync with the camera and said the setup "Works great."

When a network cable is better. Some doorbells and most door stations built for two households can run on a single network cable that carries both power and data, known as PoE. On a long Bergen Beach house where Wi-Fi is weak at the front, or on a two-family where two stations share a power supply, a network cable to the door is often more reliable than a radio link and a transformer, and it is the same cable we use for cameras. It does mean pulling a new cable rather than reusing the bell wire, which is a little more labor, but it removes the two most common failure points at once: weak power and weak Wi-Fi.

Releasing the door. If the household wants to let visitors in remotely, the door needs an electric strike in the frame, powered separately from the doorbell. For a house street door, we fit a fail-secure strike, which stays locked if power is lost. Allegion, a major lock maker, explains that fail-secure products are locked when power is removed and that with an electric strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whichever kind it is, so nobody is ever trapped inside. Magnetic locks, by contrast, are only available fail-safe and release when power is lost, and building codes attach strict exit conditions to them, so we do not recommend them for a house door in a neighborhood where storms take out power.

Water, Wind and Walls

Stucco, Siding and the Basins: Mounting a Doorbell That Survives Bergen Beach Weather

A doorbell or door station is the one piece of an entry system that lives outdoors all year, and in Bergen Beach it lives in a harder spot than most of Brooklyn. Houses near Paerdegat Basin, Mill Basin and East Mill Basin face open water and wind on more than one side, and the area's flooding record means some doors see standing water at the bottom step during heavy rain. ButterflyMX's guide to cold-weather intercom failures puts it plainly: "Outdoor components usually show problems first because they face wind, snow, rain, ice, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles," and "Moisture can enter exterior panels, cable paths, and lock hardware, then condense or freeze during temperature swings." Our job is to keep that water out of the station, the cable and the wall.

Stucco. Many Bergen Beach houses are finished in stucco, and stucco cracks if it is drilled carelessly. We use the right masonry bit at low speed, drill only through the stucco into the substrate behind it, and set fixings that grip the substrate, not the stucco skin. Every hole is sealed with an exterior-grade sealant, and the cable entry is sealed around the cable so rain running down the wall cannot follow it inside. A BiggerPockets member weighing doorbells for his three-unit house said he was worried about damaging his siding if a product did not work out; on stucco, careful drilling and sealing are what prevent that.

Brick. On brick houses we fix into the brick face rather than the mortar where the station needs to be firm, and into the mortar where we want the fixing to be easy to repair later. A doorbell is small enough that either works, but a door station with a keypad gets pressed hard and needs to be solid. Cable enters through a drilled hole sloped slightly downward toward the outside, so water drains out rather than in.

Vinyl siding and wood trim. On sided houses, the station goes on a mounting block or the door trim rather than straight onto the siding, so the siding can still expand and contract and the station sits flat. Above the water. Inside, the power supply, any chime that needs mains power and any recorder go on a shelf or wall bracket high in the basement or on the first floor. Where a house has taken water before, we ask the owner how high it reached and set everything comfortably above that line. Cable runs in the basement ceiling joists, not along the floor.

For a business, the question is rarely whether to have a doorbell. It is how to keep a door locked without making customers, patients or delivery drivers feel shut out. In practice that means one of three set-ups. The first is a staff or delivery door at the back of a store or restaurant that stays locked, with a video station so the person inside can see who is there before opening, and a release so they do not have to walk to the door with their hands full. The second is an upstairs office, a small clinic or a professional suite above a shop, where the street door is locked and visitors buzz up. The third is an institution, such as a church office, a parish hall or a community room, that opens on a schedule and otherwise answers visitors from a desk or a phone.

Business doors are used much harder than house doors. A rear door behind a busy restaurant or supermarket can be buzzed open dozens of times a day, slammed by carts and hand trucks, and left unattended for long stretches. For those doors we choose a heavier-duty strike, a door closer adjusted to pull the door shut and latch every single time, and a vandal-resistant station that can take being knocked. Where staff in more than one room need to answer, an indoor monitor at each answering point is usually better than phones, because nobody should have to use a personal phone to open a work door. A camera on the same door is a common addition, installed on the same visit through the same cable path, and putting the two together is where most customers save $200 to $400 in labor.

Compliance points that apply to Bergen Beach entry installations

  • Multiple Dwelling Law §4. A private dwelling is "any building or structure designed and occupied exclusively for residence purposes by not more than two families"; a multiple dwelling is occupied by three or more families living independently. Most Bergen Beach houses are private dwellings, so apartment-building entry mandates do not apply to them.
  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. For multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968, the main door must be self-locking with an intercom allowing voice contact and remote release; older ones must add it when tenants of a majority of apartments ask in writing. This applies to the larger condo and apartment buildings, not to houses.
  • Egress and electrified locks. Electrically locked exit doors in commercial and public spaces need a way out that does not depend on the lock: sensor release on the inside, a push-to-exit button, release on loss of power and, where there is a fire alarm or sprinklers, release when it activates. We fit fail-secure strikes that leave the inside handle working, and we never bypass an existing exit release.
  • Accessible reach. For businesses and community spaces, call buttons and keypads go within the 2010 ADA Standards reach range, 48 inches maximum for a high forward reach and 15 inches minimum for a low reach.
  • Electrical work. Our work is low-voltage entry equipment. A new 120-volt circuit, outlet or hardwired transformer is done by a licensed electrician, and we flag it before pricing.
  • Association rules. In condominiums and gated complexes, the association's rules on exterior fixtures, drilling through common elements and cameras facing common areas apply, and we install only with its approval.
  • Landmarks. We found no designated historic district in Bergen Beach, so Landmarks Preservation Commission approval is not normally part of a doorbell job here, unlike brownstone neighborhoods such as Bedford-Stuyvesant.

Old doorbells, old wiring and new cameras

Can a battery video doorbell be fixed over our old intercom panel, or does the panel have to come out first?

Usually the old panel should come out or be made safe first. A Brownstoner forum member with two apartments sharing an old buzzer asked exactly this, because she mostly wanted "to see who is at the door, not 24-7 video surveillance." Sticking a battery doorbell over a dead panel leaves two sets of buttons for visitors to puzzle over, hides any water getting into the old box, and leaves you charging a battery every few weeks in winter. If the old intercom still works for the household and you only want to see the visitor, a small wired camera or video doorbell beside it can do that. If the old system is dead, the cleaner job is to remove it, cap or reuse its wiring, and put one station in its place that rings, shows and, if wanted, releases. We look at the panel and its wiring before recommending either, and on our Brooklyn list a single video doorbell or station sits in the $700 to $1,800 video range with the exact figure set at the walkthrough.

Our house still has its original doorbell transformer and chime. Can a wired video doorbell run on them?

Sometimes, and the transformer's label decides it. Wired video doorbells need steady power at a set voltage and rating: Ring lists 10 to 24 volts AC at 8 to 40VA for its basic wired model and 16 to 24 volts AC at 10 to 40VA for its Plus, Pro and 2K Plus models, and Lorex says a 10-volt transformer is too weak for its wired doorbells. Many builder doorbell transformers from the 1960s to the 1990s are rated at the bottom of those ranges or below them. If yours meets the doorbell's requirement and the wire tests sound, we reuse both and keep the job simple. If it falls short, there are two clean answers: a licensed electrician replaces the transformer on the 120-volt side, or we power the doorbell from a dedicated low-voltage supply that plugs into an existing outlet. The chime is checked the same way, because some video doorbells drive an existing chime and some need a plug-in chime instead.

Is it worth running a network cable to the front door instead of reusing the old bell wire?

On many Bergen Beach houses, yes. A network cable can carry both power and data to a PoE doorbell or door station, which removes the two things that make video doorbells unreliable at the front of a long detached house: a transformer that cannot supply enough power, and a Wi-Fi signal that barely reaches the door. Ring's Wired Doorbell 4K Pro, for instance, can be powered by PoE. The trade-off is labor, because a new cable has to be pulled from the basement or a utility closet to the door rather than reusing what is in the wall. We usually suggest it when the Wi-Fi test at the door is weak, when two stations share one power supply in a two-family, or when the owner plans to add cameras later, because the same cable path serves both.

Which door, and what the camera sees

Our front door sits behind a glass storm door. Where should the doorbell camera go?

Almost always on the trim outside the storm door, not on the main door frame behind it. Behind the storm door, a visitor has to open the storm door to reach the button, and the camera looks through glass that reflects light at night and fogs in cold weather. Outside it, the doorbell is where the visitor stands and sees the whole step. The catch on many detached houses is space: the strip of trim between the storm door's frame and the edge of the house can be narrow, which is why slim doorbells exist and why we sometimes fit a small mounting block. If the storm door's hinge side leaves no room at all, the latch side or the wall beside the door often does. We measure it on the walkthrough so the doorbell you buy actually fits.

Most visitors come up the driveway to our side door, not the front. Which door should get the doorbell?

The one visitors use. On a house with a driveway along the side, couriers, neighbors and the family's friends naturally walk to the side door, and a video doorbell on a front door nobody uses records an empty step. If both doors get visitors, both get a button, and we set the chimes or the app to say which door is ringing, so nobody walks to the wrong end of the house. A common and economical split is the video doorbell at the side door, where most visitors arrive, and a simple wired button at the front for holiday guests and anyone who does not know the house. That keeps the job toward the lower end of the range while putting the camera where it does the most good.

Can one doorbell camera keep an eye on the driveway as well as the door?

Not well, and it is worth knowing before you buy one hoping it will. A doorbell camera is aimed and focused for a face a few feet away at the door; a driveway needs a wider view, a longer reach and good night vision across the whole length of the car. Pointed at the drive, a doorbell sees the edge of a bumper and misses the visitor; pointed at the visitor, it sees little of the drive. Car theft does come up in the neighborhood, with a car reported stolen on Nextdoor in September 2025, and if the driveway matters, the right answer is a dedicated camera on the house wall covering it, installed on the same visit through the same cable path as the doorbell. Doing both together is where most customers save $200 to $400 in labor.

Two households in one house

In a two-family, is it better to give each household its own video doorbell or share one two-button station?

It depends on whether the two households share a door. Where the rental has its own entrance, which many Bergen Beach two-families do, two separate doorbells, each with its own chime and app account, are simplest and keep both households' visitors private. Where both households come in through one door, a single station with two buttons looks tidier than two doorbells side by side, gives one camera view to both and can release the shared door for either household. Two consumer doorbells on one door can work, but visitors often press the wrong one and the two cameras record each other's guests. A BiggerPockets member with a three-unit house found consumer doorbells "not commercial or durable enough" for that job, and a two-button station built for multiple households is the usual answer. Both options stay within our house price ranges.

Is a Bergen Beach two-family house required by law to have an intercom?

No. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law defines a private dwelling as a building occupied exclusively for residence by not more than two families, and reserves the term multiple dwelling for buildings with three or more families living independently. The intercom requirement in §50-a is written for multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968, and for older multiple dwellings whose tenants ask in writing. A one- or two-family house in Bergen Beach falls outside both. That does not make a doorbell unimportant, and the owner is still responsible for what is installed and for the house's doors and locks working safely. It does mean the choice of system is entirely yours, made for convenience and safety rather than to satisfy an inspector, and a three-family such as the one listed at 1166 Bergen Avenue is the point where the Multiple Dwelling Law starts to apply.

Our tenant moved out. How do we take them off the doorbell app and the door release?

Remove their user from the doorbell's account, change any door code they had, and check who else has access while you are there. On most app-based doorbells and stations, the account owner can see a list of shared users and remove them in a minute; the tenant's phone stops ringing and stops showing video at once. If the tenant had a keypad code for the door, delete it and set a new one for the next tenant. If they had their own account on a separate doorbell at their entrance, ask them to hand it over or reset it, so the next tenant starts fresh. This is the main reason we set up the accounts in the owner's name at installation and leave a note of how to add and remove users, so a change of tenant never needs a service call.

Should the owner and the tenant share one doorbell account?

Generally not, unless the two households are family. A shared account means both households see every visitor, every delivery and every recording from the door, and either one can change settings for both. Where the tenant has a separate entrance, give each door its own doorbell and its own account; where both households share a door with a two-button station, most systems let each button call only its own household's phones while the owner holds the administrator login. The owner keeps control of the system and its settings, the tenant gets privacy about who visits, and neither has to ask the other to answer the door. If the owner wants a view of the whole property for security, that is better done with a separate camera than by sharing the tenant's doorbell.

The outside of the house and the people next door

Will drilling for a doorbell or door station crack our stucco or damage the siding?

Not if it is drilled and sealed properly. Stucco cracks when it is hammer-drilled hard or when a fixing grips only the outer skin, so we drill at low speed with the right bit, fix into the substrate behind it and seal every hole and the cable entry with exterior-grade sealant. On vinyl siding the station goes on a mounting block or the door trim so the siding can still move, and fixings go into the framing. On brick, the cable hole slopes slightly outward so water drains out. The holes are small, and if a doorbell is ever removed they can be filled and touched up. The BiggerPockets owner who worried about damaging his siding with a doorbell that might not work out had the right instinct: choose a station that suits the door before drilling, so you only drill once.

Can the doorbell camera be kept from recording the neighbor's yard or the street?

Yes, mostly. The angle is the first tool: a wedge or corner mount turns the camera toward your own step and walk and away from a semi-detached neighbor's door. The second is the app: most video doorbells let you draw privacy zones that black out part of the picture, and motion zones so movement on the street or next door does not trigger recordings or alerts. Neither makes a camera blind to everything beyond your property, because a doorbell has to see the person standing at your door, and that view will usually include some of the sidewalk. Habitat magazine has reported New York buildings writing video doorbells into their house rules as they spread. In a semi-detached house, a word with the neighbor and a camera clearly aimed at your own door goes a long way.

We live in a gated condo with a front-desk intercom. Do we need the association's approval to add a doorbell at our own door?

In most complexes, yes. In a community like Mill Harbor, built in 2005 with 208 units and an intercom to the front desk, the entry gate, lobby and front-desk system belong to the association, and the exterior of your door may be a common element even if the door itself is yours. Association rules often cover what can be fixed to doors and walls, whether wiring can pass through common walls, and whether cameras may look into shared hallways or other owners' doors. Ask the managing agent before you buy anything. We can send them a short description of the doorbell, how it is mounted and what the camera sees, and provide a certificate of insurance if the association requires one. Once approved, a unit doorbell is a small job priced from the house ranges on our list.

Can the doorbell connect to our house alarm?

Often it can, though what "connect" means varies. Some alarm systems can show a doorbell's video in the same app, sound a chime through the alarm keypad when the doorbell is pressed, or record a doorbell event in the alarm's history. Others simply sit side by side in separate apps. Many Bergen Beach houses already have an alarm; a two-family on Bergen Court listed one among its features. Tell us the alarm's make and model when you send photos, and we will tell you whether the doorbell you want works with it and what it would add. If you are replacing both at once, we can plan them together so the doorbell, the alarm and any cameras share one app and one set of cable paths.

A doorbell only rings. An intercom adds two-way talk, and often video, between the door and the people inside. A buzzer is the release that unlocks the door when someone inside presses a button. Many Bergen Beach houses want all three in one system, but plenty only need a good video doorbell.

Can the doorbell button be moved to the other side of the door?
+

Yes. Moving it is common when a storm door hinge blocks the old spot or when a new door changes the trim. The old wire is extended or a new cable is run to the new position, and the old hole is filled and sealed.

Can a video doorbell ring a chime in the basement as well as upstairs?
+

Yes. Most video doorbells can ring more than one chime, either wired chimes on the old circuit or plug-in chimes that pair with the doorbell, so a finished basement and a bedroom floor both hear the door. We place them where the household actually spends its time.

Our router is at the back of a detached house. Will a Wi-Fi doorbell at the front stay connected?
+

Not always. We test the signal at the door before recommending a Wi-Fi doorbell. If it is weak, the fix is a mesh point or access point near the front of the house, or a PoE doorbell on a network cable that does not depend on Wi-Fi at all.

Can we see the visitor on a screen in the house instead of only on our phones?
+

Yes. An indoor monitor on a kitchen or hall wall shows the visitor and lets you talk and release the door without picking up a phone, which suits older relatives and children. Many systems ring the monitor and the household's phones at the same time.

Can I talk to a delivery driver through the doorbell while I am at work?
+

Yes, with a video doorbell or station that calls phones. You see the driver, talk to them through the doorbell's speaker and can tell them where to leave a package. That is one of the main reasons Bergen Beach households, who often commute by car, choose video over a plain bell.

Do you install buzzers for shops on Avenue N or Ralph Avenue?
+

Yes. Staff and delivery doors, upstairs offices and church or community doors are priced on the same Brooklyn list. Business doors usually get a heavier-duty strike, a closer adjusted to latch every time and a station built to be pressed all day.

Our door faces the afternoon sun. Will the doorbell picture be washed out?
+

It can be, if the camera looks straight into bright sky or a glass storm door. Mounting it lower or angled away from the sky, choosing a model with good backlight handling and keeping it outside the storm door glass usually fix it. We check the light at the door on the walkthrough.

Can a dog walker or cleaner get in with a code instead of a key?
+

Yes, with a keypad or a station that accepts codes, paired with an electric strike on the door. Each person gets their own code, which can be limited to certain hours and deleted when they stop coming, so no keys go missing.

Does a new doorbell need a hole through the door itself?
+

No. Doorbells and stations mount on the frame, trim or wall beside the door, and the cable passes through that wall. Only a release that lives in the door, rather than the frame, needs work on the door, and most house releases go in the frame instead.

Why do some video doorbells come with a wedge or corner mount?
+

To turn the camera. A wedge tilts it up or down for steps, and a corner mount turns it toward the path or driveway the visitor walks up. On semi-detached houses it also turns the view away from the neighbor's door, which helps everyone's privacy.
