Olinville is the corner of the Bronx the city wrote a zoning district for. Between those blocks and the elevated 2 and 5 on White Plains Road stand the small apartment buildings that went up in the rush before the rezoning, now about twenty years old. We plan the sidewalk gate, the side path and every door along it as one route, give each household its own credentials, and keep every door free to open from inside.

The Neighborhood

Why Olinville Properties Need Access Control

Olinville is a name people still use more than maps do. Wikipedia calls it a “disused neighborhood name for the area around Olinville Avenue” inside Williamsbridge, and notes that it survived for decades in the OLinville telephone exchanges. NYC Parks traces it to a tiny village that grew up in the 1840s beside the New York and Harlem Railroad and was named for Stephen Olin, the Methodist bishop and college president, and records that it began as two settlements, Olinville No. 1 and No. 2, separated by Olin Avenue. The Neighborhoods, an April 2026 essay places the settlement on the banks of the Bronx River on an 1868 atlas, says the dividing road is today’s Gun Hill Road, and describes the modern neighborhood as a narrow dogleg between White Plains Road and the river, from East 222nd Street down to the Parkside Houses.

Local institutions keep the name in use, and they do not agree on where it stops. Bronx Community Board 12 lists Olinville among its neighborhoods, and the NYPD’s 47th Precinct names it among the areas it serves from 4111 Laconia Avenue. Yet a 2009 Bronx Times article placed the Parkside Houses, which sit in Community Board 11, “in Olinville,” and Norwood News still files incidents near East 211th Street and White Plains Road under “the Olinville section”. The rezoning described below drew its own lines again, partly east of White Plains Road. We describe streets instead: Olinville Avenue, Bronx Boulevard along the river, Bronx Park East, the cross streets from roughly East 210th to East 222nd, and the White Plains Road corridor under the elevated tracks.

What makes Olinville distinct is a zoning decision. On December 8, 2005, according to CityLand, the City Council rezoned 39 blocks and 962 lots here and, in a separate action, created the R5A district. The Department of City Planning initiated the change because Community Board 12 and the borough president were alarmed at single-family houses being replaced by “significantly out-of-character, high-density apartments.” Planning staff told the Council that R5A was crafted for Olinville “because no existing zoning district corresponded to Olinville’s type of housing.” The same report notes that early Olinville was known for tapestry mills before it became an early residential suburb of modest homes. The Council’s own resolution and Wikipedia describe a 36-block area; both counts appear in the record, and we give both.

The Department of City Planning’s district guide says R5A allows single- and two-family detached houses, as well as ancillary dwelling units, and lists Olinville among the four places it is mapped. Its table sets a minimum lot of 2,375 square feet, a minimum lot width of 25 feet, a front yard of at least 10 feet, a rear yard of 20 feet and two side yards. The city’s 2011 Williamsbridge/Baychester presentation repeats the rule for Olinville Avenue south of 219th Street: one- and two-family detached houses, a maximum floor area ratio of 1.10, a 25-foot base and 35-foot building height, and a 10-foot front yard. In practical terms, every house on those blocks stands alone on a narrow lot with open space on both sides, which means every house has a side path, and on a two-family that path is usually how the second household gets home.

Not everything on those blocks is a house. City Limits reported in January 2006 that developers raced to build before the new limits took effect: the head of the Olinville Taxpayers and Civic Association described construction on a long-vacant lot on Barker Avenue and a flurry of complaints about late-night and weekend demolition, and Community Board 12’s district manager said that the minute the rezoning application was submitted, there was more development than she had ever seen. Some of the apartment buildings finished in that window are now about twenty years old, and so is the first entry system that went into each of them. The NYU Furman Center shows the turn in its Community District 12 numbers: units authorized by new residential building permits went from 285 in 2000 to zero in 2006.

The people living in those houses have changed less than the zoning did. In the Furman Center’s May 2026 profile of Williamsbridge/Baychester, the community district that contains Olinville, 38.6 percent of households owned their home in 2024, against 32.7 percent citywide, and the share of residents aged 65 and over had risen from 11.2 percent in 2000 to 19.2 percent. In 2024 the district recorded 243 sales of two- to four-family buildings and 148 of one-family houses. Those are the buildings this page is mostly about: houses where an owner often lives on one floor and rents another, where the family has held the keys for a generation, and where an added unit is now legal.

Two neighbors frame the strip. To the east, the elevated White Plains Road Line opened through here on March 3, 1917, according to Wikipedia, and The Neighborhoods’ April 2026 audio of Olinville is mostly the 2 and 5 trains passing overhead. To the south stand the Parkside Houses, which a residents’ history site describes as fourteen buildings of six to fifteen stories with 879 apartments, completed in 1951 between Adee and Arnow Avenues, White Plains Road and Bronx Park East. Parkside belongs to NYCHA, and we do not work for NYCHA; it appears on this page as a landmark. Our work in Olinville is on privately owned houses, small apartment buildings and the shops along White Plains Road.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Olinville’s Architecture

Six kinds of building share Olinville’s streets, and the right lock for one is often wrong for the next. We describe them from the sources cited on this page and from what is visible from the public sidewalk; no address mentioned anywhere on this page is an AESS customer.

Detached one- and two-family houses on R5A lots

The core of the rezoned blocks: a house standing alone on a lot that may be as narrow as 25 feet, with a short front yard, a side path on each side and a rear yard. Wood or steel entry doors in wood frames, a separate side or rear door for the second household, and a basement door that often opens off the side path. There is rarely conduit anywhere, so cable runs are planned along the foundation or through the basement ceiling.

Converted houses with a second entrance

Where a house has been converted from one family to two over the decades, the upper unit may be reached by a later stair or a door added at the side or rear. The second household’s route can climb outside the building, which brings weather, lighting and snow into the plan. We confirm whether each entrance is an original door or a later addition before choosing hardware for it.

Small apartment buildings finished around 2005

Low walk-ups put up under the zoning that was in force before December 2005, the buildings City Limits described being raced to completion. Usually concrete block or masonry with hollow metal or aluminum doors, an audio intercom panel at the street door and a cellar door to a refuse area. Twenty years in, the hinges, closers and intercom electronics are often at the end of their life at the same time.

Larger apartment buildings among the houses

Wikipedia notes that the area’s detached, semi-detached and attached houses are joined by a smaller number of mid- and high-rise apartment buildings, few of them taller than 70 feet. They have lobbies, vestibules and service entrances. These buildings carry the Multiple Dwelling Law’s intercom and self-locking door duties, and we treat them as building systems with a controller, not as a set of individual locks.

Mixed-use buildings on White Plains Road

Buildings with shops at street level beside the elevated structure and apartments above reached by a narrow street door between storefronts. The shop door, the apartment door and the rear door each have different users and hours, and the apartment door is the one owners most often forget to budget for.

Public housing at Parkside

NYCHA’s fourteen-building development on Olinville’s southern edge, completed in 1951. It is named here as a landmark only; its doors, intercoms and repairs are NYCHA’s responsibility, and AESS has no role there. Residents with a door problem should contact NYCHA.

How the house shape changes the method

  • Route first, doors second. On a detached two-family the order in which a visitor meets the gate, the front door and the side door decides where the call station goes. We draw the route from the sidewalk to every entrance before choosing a single lock.
  • Cable outside the living space. With two households under one roof, we keep cable runs in the basement ceiling, along the foundation in protected raceway or in the shared stair, never through a tenant’s apartment unless they agree and the owner asks.
  • Gates sized to the passage. R5A side yards can be tight. We measure the clear width, the swing and the slope of the path before proposing a gate lock, and choose a sliding or bi-fold gate when a swing would hit the neighbor’s fence or block the path.
  • Power where the door is. A wired lock needs a low-voltage power supply with a battery, usually in the basement. Where a gate stands far from the house, we compare a buried low-voltage run with a battery-powered gate lock and tell the owner what each costs to maintain.
  • Every household its own administrator scope. The owner keeps the master account; a tenant can be allowed to manage the codes for their own entrance only. We write that split down at installation.

The R5A Lot

The Side Path Is the Hallway

In an apartment building, the hallway decides who meets whom: the street door, the vestibule, the corridor and finally the apartment door. On a detached Olinville two-family there is no hallway. The route runs outdoors, from the sidewalk through a short front yard, past the owner’s front door and down a narrow side path to the tenant’s entrance, the basement door and, increasingly, a unit at the back. Every one of those points is a place where a visitor can be stopped, recognized or let through, and the plan has to decide which.

Most of the mistakes we are asked to fix come from treating each door separately. A video doorbell on the front door that rings the owner for the tenant’s visitors. A keypad on the side door that strangers reach only after walking past two bedroom windows. A gate locked with a padlock that the tenant’s aide does not have. A basement door that the oil company has had a key to for fifteen years. None of these is a hardware problem; each is a routing problem.

So we draw the lot first. The diagram below is a generic R5A-sized lot, not any particular house, with the decisions we make at each point. The rule of thumb is that the first barrier a stranger meets should be the one that can identify them, and that nobody should be invited further down the path than the door they came to reach.

Plan of a generic detached two-family on an R5A-sized lot, drawn by AESS to show decisions, not any real house. Lot dimensions follow the Department of City Planning’s R5A minimums; device positions are typical AESS practice.

  • At the sidewalk gate: a call station with one button per household, a delivery instruction, and either a lock or no lock depending on how deep the front yard is.
  • At the owner’s front door: the owner’s own keypad or reader, separate codes for the family, and a key override.
  • At the start of the side path: where there is room, a gate that keeps strangers out of the side yard altogether, with free exit from the yard side.
  • At the tenant’s side door: the tenant’s credential, the tenant’s doorbell, and nothing that tells the owner when the tenant comes and goes unless the tenant agrees.
  • At the basement door: codes for service trades that work only during a set window, and a door contact so the owner knows when it is left open.
  • At a backyard unit: its occupant’s credential for the gate and their own door, and no credential for anything else on the lot.

Older Systems

The 2005 Line Through Olinville’s Buildings

The rezoning drew a line in time as well as on the map. On one side are houses that were already standing and the detached houses built under R5A since. On the other are the buildings that were rushed to completion under the earlier zoning, the ones City Limits reported being built on formerly vacant lots with late-night and weekend demolition in the months before the vote. Those buildings were finished in roughly the same years, so their original door hardware and intercoms are now reaching the end of their working life in roughly the same years too.

Twenty years is long for the parts that move. Door closers lose their adjustment and let the door bounce off the latch. Hinges wear, the door drops, and the strike no longer lines up. Audio intercom handsets fail one by one, and the replacement model is no longer made. Power supplies that were never given a battery stop working at the first outage. None of it shows from the street until the day the door does not latch.

Our approach in these buildings is to test before replacing. We check the door and frame first, because no electric lock works on a door that does not close. Then we test the cable from the panel to each apartment: if the old intercom ran enough sound conductors, a modern system can often reuse them; if it ran a two-wire bus, the new system has to be chosen to match or the building needs new cable. The written quote shows both options and the difference in price.

Owners of these buildings often ask whether to do everything at once. Our usual advice is to split the work by urgency. The street door comes first, because it is the one the law and the residents depend on: door, closer, strike and the call station. The cellar and service doors follow, since they are the quiet way in. Apartment-by-apartment upgrades, such as video screens, can come last and can be offered to residents who want them rather than imposed on everyone. Splitting the work this way keeps the building secure at every stage and spreads the cost over a season rather than a single invoice.

Added Units

Planning a Backyard or Basement Unit Around the Entrances

Since December 2024, the Department of Buildings’ ancillary dwelling unit rules have let the owner of a one- or two-family house who lives there add one more unit of up to 800 square feet, and the Department of City Planning’s description of R5A now lists ancillary dwelling units alongside detached one- and two-family houses. On an Olinville lot that means a possible third household, either in a small building in the rear yard or in the basement, cellar or attic of the house.

Either way the new unit needs its own entrance, and on a narrow detached lot that entrance is reached from the same side path the tenant already uses. The access plan therefore changes for everyone, not just for the new occupant. The sidewalk call station needs a third button. The side-path gate needs a third set of credentials. The basement door, if the new unit is below ground, stops being a service door and becomes somebody’s front door, with everything that implies about free exit, lighting and a doorbell.

The rules have consequences beyond the door. The Department of Buildings notes that an attic, cellar, basement or attached unit added to a two-family can turn the house into a three-family, which the Multiple Dwelling Law treats differently, unless the separation the code describes is provided. Whether that applies to a particular house is a question for the architect who files the plans, and we do not answer it for them. What we do is design the entry hardware after the plans are settled, so the doors match what was approved.

The cheapest moment to prepare is during construction. While the yard is dug for the new unit’s utilities, an empty conduit from the basement to the unit and to the gate post costs very little. While the basement is open for a cellar unit, a cable from the controller location to the new entrance takes an hour. We are happy to walk the plans with the owner and the contractor before any of it is closed up.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control Along White Plains Road

Olinville’s commercial life runs along White Plains Road, under and beside the elevated tracks of the 2 and 5. These are the settings we work in. Each is described from the sources above or by its type; none of the businesses on these blocks is named as an AESS client.

Storefronts beside the elevated structure

Shops whose front doors face the steel of the El and whose deliveries, staff and customers all use the same narrow sidewalk. Back doors open onto yards or alleys. A reader on the back door and a scheduled lock on the front are the usual first step.

Apartment doors between shops

The narrow street door that leads upstairs to the apartments above a row of stores. It belongs to the building, not to any shop, and it is the door most often left unbudgeted. The Multiple Dwelling Law’s self-closing and self-locking duties apply to it in buildings that count as multiple dwellings.

Around Gun Hill Road station

The express station at Gun Hill Road, which Wikipedia records opened in 1917 and was renovated in 2007, brings the heaviest foot traffic. Doors near station stairs see more strangers pausing in front of them, which shapes where we put keypads and how we shield them.

Burke Avenue and 219th Street

The local stations on either side. Burke Avenue’s station, renovated in 2004 and 2005 and decorated with Béatrice Coron’s “Bronx Literature,” anchors a smaller cluster of shops; the 219th Street station is the stop NYC Parks gives for the Bronx River launch at Bronx Park and 219th Street.

Long-running family businesses

Workshops and stores that have been on the corridor for decades, like the upholstery shop whose owner told The Neighborhoods his father opened it more than forty years ago. Their doors often carry several generations of locks, and their keys have been copied for staff who left long ago.

Houses of worship and community rooms

Storefront congregations and community spaces whose doors open for services and meetings at set hours. A schedule that unlocks for a service and locks afterward, with a code for whoever holds the keys, replaces the habit of leaving the door propped.

The rules an Olinville entry system has to meet

  • Free exit, always. Every electrically locked door and gate must open from the inside or the yard side with one motion and no key or code. On egress doors the NYC Building Code’s electrified-egress sections require release on loss of power and on fire alarm activation where they apply, with any manual release button mounted between 40 and 48 inches.
  • Multiple dwellings. The Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, in buildings with eight or more apartments, an intercom or equivalent that lets tenants identify and admit visitors. The Housing Maintenance Code adds owners’ duties for entrance-door locks.
  • Tenant data. The Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) limits what owners of multiple dwellings may collect and keep through smart access systems and requires written policies. We set data retention to the minimum the owner needs.
  • Added units. The Department of Buildings’ ancillary dwelling unit rules (Local Laws 126 and 127 of 2024) allow one ADU on a one- or two-family lot where the owner lives, require it to have a separate entrance, and note that an attic, cellar, basement or attached ADU added to a two-family can make the house a three-family under the Multiple Dwelling Law unless the code’s separation is provided.
  • Motorized gates. Any gate operator must meet UL 325 entrapment protection, as DASMA’s technical data sheet #356 explains. We never add a motor to an existing gate without the required sensors.

The Side Path and the Gate

Choosing a gate for a narrow side passage, drawn by AESS from typical field measurements. Measure the actual clear width and slope before buying anything.

Our tenant’s door is halfway down the side yard. Should the visitor call station go at the sidewalk or right beside that side door?

Usually at the sidewalk, with one button for each household. A call station beside the side door means every visitor walks the length of the path, past the owner’s windows, before anyone knows who they are. A station at the gate or the start of the path stops them where the whole street can see them, lets the tenant answer from a phone or an in-unit screen, and keeps the side yard private.

The exception is a front yard so shallow that the gate is effectively the front step. There we put the station on the house wall beside the front door, still with separate buttons, and fit the side door with only a credential reader for the tenant. Either way, the tenant’s call goes to the tenant, and the owner’s to the owner.

The passage between our house and the next one is narrow, and a gate there would swing into the neighbor’s fence. What gate and lock suit a passage that tight?

Measure before buying anything: the clear width between the house wall and the fence, the height of any step or slope, and where gutters and meters stick out. If a swinging gate cannot open fully toward the yard without striking the fence, a narrow bi-fold gate or a short sliding gate on a top track often fits where a swing does not.

For the lock, a surface-mounted electromagnetic gate lock or an electric bolt designed for outdoor gates works on most metal frames; on a wooden gate we fit a strike-and-keeper set made for timber. The yard side gets a mechanical handle or push bar that always opens it, because a side passage is an escape route from the back of the house. A gate closer makes sure it latches every time, not only when someone remembers.

With a front yard only about ten feet deep, is a locked gate at the sidewalk worth it, or should control start at the front door?

It depends on what the yard holds. If the front yard is just a strip of planting and the mailbox is on the house, a locked sidewalk gate adds little: the front door is a few steps away and is the real barrier. If the front yard leads to the side path, holds the trash bins or gives access to a basement stair, a locked gate keeps strangers off all of it at once, and it is worth doing.

Ten feet is the R5A minimum front yard, so many Olinville houses are close to it. Where the gate is locked, the mail carrier and delivery services need a way through, which usually means moving the mailbox to the fence or giving the gate a delivery code limited to daytime hours.

The oil delivery, the meter reader and the boiler technician all use the basement door off the side path. How do we let them in without leaving a key out?

Give each trade a code that opens only the gate and the basement door, and only during a window you choose: weekday mornings for a scheduled delivery, or a single day for a boiler service. The code stops working outside that window, and the log shows when it was used.

If the oil fill pipe or a meter is reachable from the path without entering the basement, the trade may only need the gate. We ask the owner which visits actually require the door before we set anything up, and we remove any long-forgotten keys from the plan by changing the basement cylinder at the same visit.

Two Households and an Added Unit

Call routing on a two-family with a backyard unit, drawn by AESS as a generic example.

Couriers ring our bell for the tenant upstairs and leave everything in our front hall. Can one small station send each visitor and each delivery to the right household?

Yes. A two-button video station, or a keypad station with a directory, puts the choice in front of the visitor: owner or tenant. Each button calls a different phone or in-unit screen, and each household can open only the door or gate that leads to its own entrance.

For parcels, the station can carry a printed instruction such as “Upstairs deliveries: side door” and, if you want, a delivery code that opens only a parcel box at the front of the lot. That ends the habit of packages piling up in the owner’s hall, without letting couriers down the side path.

The hall door between our apartment and our tenant’s locks at night and cuts us off from the basement laundry. How should a shared interior door be set up?

Start by deciding what the door is for. If it separates two apartments, each side should be able to open it to reach the shared basement, and neither should be able to enter the other’s home through it. That usually means the door to each apartment stays locked as it is, while the door to the basement stair gets a keypad or reader that both households’ credentials open.

Any door on an exit path must open from the inside without a key, so an interior door that locks against the stair has to be checked against the house’s egress before we electrify it. Where the laundry is the only reason for the shared door, a schedule that leaves the basement door unlocked during agreed hours is often the simplest answer.

If we build an ancillary unit in the back yard, how does its occupant get through our gate and down the side path without any access to our house?

Give the occupant two things: a credential for the sidewalk or side-path gate, and the credential for their own door. Nothing else. The owner’s front and side doors stay on the owner’s codes, the basement stays on its own, and the controller or app shows the backyard unit as a separate household.

The Department of Buildings requires an ADU to have a separate entrance, and the route to it runs through your gate. Ask your architect to confirm that the gate and path meet whatever the approved plans show for that entrance before we electrify the gate, and we will fit hardware that keeps the path free to leave from the back of the lot at all times.

Buildings From the 2005 Rush

Our building went up just before the 2005 rezoning and its original intercom is failing. Is a twenty-year-old system worth repairing?

Sometimes. If the panel still works and only a few apartment handsets have failed, and replacement handsets are still available, a repair at our service rate keeps the building running for less than a new system. If the panel itself is failing, the maker is gone, or several parts are failing at once, repair money is better spent on replacement.

We test before we advise: the panel, the power supply, the strike and the cable to each apartment. The written report gives you both numbers, a repair and a replacement, and tells you which parts of the old system, including the cable, the new one can keep.

Can a new video intercom reuse the wires the original audio intercom ran to each apartment?

Often, if the cable tests clean and the new system is chosen to suit it. Many older audio systems ran several conductors to each apartment; others used a two-wire bus. There are video systems built to run over two wires, and others that need network cable. We test continuity and insulation on every run before committing to either.

If most runs test well and a few do not, we replace only the bad ones. If the building has no usable cable at all, residents’ phones can take the place of in-unit screens, so only the street door and the controller need new cable.

Our street door has sagged and now needs a shove before it latches. Is that the electric strike or the door?

Almost always the door. A strike that buzzes but will not release, or a latch that catches on the strike lip, is the symptom; the cause is usually worn hinges, a loose frame or a closer that has lost its latch speed. Replacing the strike without fixing the door only buys a few weeks.

We tighten or replace the hinges, re-set the closer so the door latches on its own, and only then align or replace the strike. In a multiple dwelling the street door must close and lock by itself, so this repair is not cosmetic.

Under the El on White Plains Road

A column of the elevated structure stands right in front of our shop. Could the call station or reader be mounted on the column instead of our wall?

No. The columns and everything attached to the elevated line belong to the transit system, not to the building owner, and we do not fix anything to them or run cable across to them. A device there would also face away from the door it serves and invite people to gather on the sidewalk.

The station belongs on the building itself, ideally inside the door recess where it is out of the weather and close to the person who will open the door. If the recess is too shallow, a slim surface station on the wall beside the frame, at a height a wheelchair user can reach, does the same job.

We rent the apartment above our shop to one of our own employees. Should their entry be separate from the shop’s?

Yes, and on separate credentials. The employee is two different people as far as the doors are concerned: a worker who needs the shop during business hours, and a tenant who needs the apartment door at all hours. Giving one fob both roles means that when the job ends, the home access is tangled with the work access.

Set the shop credential on the shop’s schedule and the apartment credential on the residential door with no time limit. If the employee leaves the job but keeps the apartment, the owner deletes one credential and leaves the other alone, and the tenancy is handled the way any other tenancy would be.

Our building’s street door is also the second way out of the shop’s back room. Can we lock it against the sidewalk without blocking the shop’s exit?

Yes, as long as the door always opens from the inside in one motion. An electric strike or electrified lock controls entry from the sidewalk only; the lever or push bar on the inside never locks. That keeps the residents’ door secure and the shop’s exit open at the same time.

What must not happen is a deadbolt, a chain or a padlocked bar added later by someone worried about break-ins, because it would trap whoever is in the back room in a fire. We note the shared exit on the handover record and fit a door contact so the owner knows if the door is propped or forced from either side.

Power, batteries and what each door does in an outage

Every electric lock on an Olinville property should have an answer to one question: what happens when the power goes out? Wired locks run from a low-voltage power supply with a backup battery, usually in the basement, which keeps them working for hours. Battery locks carry their own cells and keep working until those run down, which is why they need a schedule. Gate locks at the end of a long side path can be either.

The answer differs by door. A house door with a keypad deadbolt stays locked and opens with its key or code. A street door on an electric strike usually stays locked from outside and always opens from inside. A magnetic lock, where one is used on a gate, releases when power fails, so a gate that must stay closed needs its battery sized for a long outage. We write the answer for each door on the handover record, so nobody finds out during a storm.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Olinville

We could not load Google’s AI Overview or its related-question boxes while researching this page, so the headings below are not quotations from them. They are beliefs Olinville owners repeat to us after reading quick answers online and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, most of it written with a suburban single-family house or a large apartment building in mind, and little of it with a detached two-family on a 25-foot Bronx lot.

“A video doorbell is all a two-family needs.”

A doorbell camera is a good start for a one-family house. On a two-family it has one button and one owner, so every visitor for the tenant rings the owner’s phone, and the tenant either shares the owner’s app or has no say over their own door.

The usual Olinville arrangement, with the second household’s entrance down the side path, needs at least two call destinations and two sets of credentials. That is a small call station with two buttons and two locks managed separately, not a single doorbell.

Doorbells still have a place: on the owner’s own front door, as the owner’s camera. They are just not a substitute for a way to route visitors to the right household.

“Put the keypad on the front door and you are done.”

On a detached house with a side path, the front door is often not the weakest point. The side door, the basement door and the rear of the lot are out of sight of the street, and that is where an unwelcome visitor would rather be.

Access control on these lots works as a route: what stops someone at the sidewalk, what stops them at the start of the path, and what each door along it needs. Locking only the front door leaves the rest of the route open.

That does not mean a gate on every lot. It means deciding, for each point along the route, who should get past it.

“Smart locks need Wi-Fi at the door.”

Many keypad locks work with no network at all, storing codes in the lock itself. Others connect over Bluetooth to a nearby hub or phone. Wi-Fi is only one option, and at a gate fifty feet down a side path, often a poor one.

Where the owner wants remote control or a log on a phone, we use a small hub inside the house, or run a cable to a wired controller in the basement, rather than relying on a home router reaching through masonry walls.

The question to ask is what the lock does when the network is down. The right answer is that it keeps working on the codes it already knows.

“Access control costs $1,500 to $5,000 a door.”

Figures like that appear in national cost guides and installer blogs, and they describe commercial doors with readers, controllers and network cabling. They are not wrong for those doors, and our own single-door installed price starts at $1,500.

On an Olinville house, though, the doors vary much more than that range suggests. A battery keypad deadbolt on a sound door costs far less than a wired gate lock at the end of a long side path that needs a cable trench, and a two-family plan mixes both.

The useful number is the one for your route, door by door. That is what our written quote gives, at the Bronx rate shown in the price table further down.

“The tenant data rules are for big landlords.”

The Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems. A detached two-family is not a multiple dwelling, but a two-family that gains a third unit can become one, and many small buildings on and near White Plains Road already are.

Even where the law does not apply, the same habits are sensible: collect only what you need to run the door, keep logs only as long as you need them, and do not use the entry system to watch a tenant’s comings and goings.

We set up systems so the owner sees what an owner needs, such as forced doors and failed batteries, and not a diary of the tenant’s life.

“A magnetic lock is the strongest lock you can buy, so use it everywhere.”

Magnetic locks are strong and simple, but they hold a door only while powered, so they must release on power loss and on fire alarm and need a sensor and a manual button on the inside. On a house or small building, that makes them the wrong default.

For house doors and building street doors we prefer electric strikes or electrified locks that keep the door latched mechanically and always let people out. Outdoor gates are different: an electromagnetic gate lock with a mechanical exit handle can be the right choice there.

The point is to choose per door, not per catalog, and to never fit a lock that could hold someone inside.

“Older buildings cannot take modern access control without rewiring everything.”

The small buildings finished around 2005 are not that old, and the larger apartment houses nearby often have usable intercom cable. Modern systems include models that run over existing two-wire intercom cable and others that call residents’ phones instead of in-unit screens.

We test the existing cable before proposing anything. When most runs test well, the building keeps them; when they do not, only the failed runs or only the street door need new cable.

Rewiring a whole building is sometimes the right answer. It should be a finding, not an assumption.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Counting the Keys

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular house. On a two-family that has been in one family for a long time, the first thing we ask at the route walk is how many keys exist to each door, and the honest answer is almost always that nobody knows. Keys went to tenants who moved out years ago, to a cousin who looked after the house one summer, to the oil company, to a contractor, to a neighbor for emergencies.

So we count by door instead. For the front door, the side door, the basement door and the gate, we list everyone who should be able to open it today, and nobody else. That list becomes the credential plan. Then, at the same visit as the new hardware, we change or rekey the mechanical cylinders that remain, because a new keypad on a door that dozens of old keys still open is only half a change.

Owners are often surprised that the list is short: two households, a relative or two, an aide, a few service trades with time windows. Writing it down is the part of the job that makes the new system work.

FAQ

Olinville Access Control FAQ

What does it cost at Bronx rates to give each household of a detached two-family its own controlled entrance?
+

For two doors with keypads or fob readers and electric strikes, expect two single-door prices from the table on this page, $1,500 to $2,500 each, at the Bronx rate of 1.00. Adding a two-button video call station, a side-path gate lock and contacts moves the job into the multi-door range, which starts at $4,000. Battery keypad deadbolts on sound doors come in below these figures. The written quote prices each door and gate separately, so you can do the doors now and the gate later.

Does the R5A zoning on our block say anything about a new front gate or a keypad post in the front yard?
+

Zoning controls the size and placement of buildings, not locks, so a keypad lock on a door is not a zoning question. A new fence, gate or post in the front yard can be, along with the Building Code, if it changes the yard or adds a structure. For a like-for-like gate with a lock added, owners rarely need anything; for a new fence line or a masonry pier, ask your architect or expediter before we install. We mount call stations and keypads on existing gates, fences or house walls wherever possible.

Is a mechanical push-button combination lock good enough for a side gate, or does it need to be electronic?
+

For a gate used by one household and a few trusted visitors, a good mechanical combination lock can be enough: no batteries, no wiring and nothing to fail in the cold. Its weaknesses are that one code is shared by everyone, changing it means taking the lock apart, and there is no record of use. Once a gate serves two households, a backyard unit or service trades on a schedule, an electronic lock with separate codes and a log is worth the extra cost.

The backyard unit’s occupant gets deliveries too. Can parcels be left at the front without opening the side path to couriers?
+

Yes. A parcel box or small locker at the front fence or front wall, opened by a delivery code or by the courier scanning a label, takes packages for every household without anyone going down the path. Each household can be given its own compartment or notified when a box is used. The side-path gate then stays closed to couriers altogether.

We own a two-family on Olinville Avenue but live elsewhere now. How much should the system tell us without becoming a stream of alerts?
+

Ask for exceptions, not activity. Useful alerts for an owner who lives elsewhere are a door or gate forced or left open for a long time, a lock or keypad battery running low, and a lock going offline. Each entry and exit by the tenants is not the owner’s business and quickly becomes noise. We set notifications to that short list and leave the full log on the controller, where it is available if something happens.

We are replacing the concrete on our side path next month. What should go under it in case we want a gate reader later?
+

An empty conduit with a pull string, run from the basement wall to where the gate post will stand, sized for a low-voltage cable and capped at both ends. If the gate will ever have a motor, ask for a second conduit for power, installed by an electrician. Note where the conduit ends on a sketch or photograph before the concrete is poured. It costs little now and saves cutting the new path later.

If the new intercom can call residents’ phones, do the apartments still need wired screens on the wall?
+

Not necessarily. Phone calling suits residents who keep their phones with them and works well in buildings where the old apartment wiring is unusable. Wired screens suit older residents, families with children answering the door, and anyone who does not want the building system on their phone. Many systems allow both, apartment by apartment, so the owner does not have to choose one for everyone.

Can our tenant change the code on their own entrance without calling us?
+

Yes, if the system supports limited administrator roles, and most current ones do. The tenant can be given control of the codes for their own door only, while the owner keeps control of the gate, the basement and the owner’s own door. We set those roles at installation and note them on the handover record, so a change of tenant means removing one account rather than resetting everything.

Can the side gate open by itself for a resident who uses a wheelchair, and close safely behind them?
+

It can, with a gate operator and the safety devices UL 325 requires, triggered by a fob, a phone or a button. On a narrow side path the operator has to fit the passage, and the swing or slide has to leave a clear, level route; sometimes the path itself needs work first. We measure the passage, check the slope and the surface, and only then propose an operator, priced after the survey.

People waiting for the bus stand right beside our gate on White Plains Road. Can someone watch us type the code?
+

They can, which is why a keypad on a busy sidewalk is not the best choice. A fob or phone credential reveals nothing to someone standing nearby. Where a keypad is needed, a scramble keypad that shifts the digits each time, a hood over the keys or a position on the inside face of the gate post all make watching harder. Changing shared codes on a schedule limits the harm if one leaks.
