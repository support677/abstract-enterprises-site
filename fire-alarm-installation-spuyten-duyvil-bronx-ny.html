The large postwar tower: two systems sharing one building

A tower like River Point Towers carries two fire protection systems that residents often think of as one. The building system belongs to the co-op: a panel near the lobby or in a service room, detectors and notification devices in corridors, stairs and shared rooms, and whatever connections the panel has to elevators, monitoring and the attendant's desk. The apartment alarms belong, in law and in practice, to a different conversation: HPD's rules on smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in multiple dwellings, the proprietary lease, and the co-op's house rules on who buys and maintains them. When we survey a building like this, we write those two halves up separately from the start. For the building system, we walk every corridor, stair and shared room, photograph the panel and its event history, and check whether the building's height raises the question of voice communication, which a SPARC summary of the Department of Buildings bulletin on existing buildings ties to buildings over 125 feet measured from the lowest fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. A 25-story tower is the kind of building where that question belongs with the design professional. For the apartments, we offer the board a single program: every unit done to one standard, in a planned order of lines and floors, with a list of every alarm, its model and its replacement date that the board can keep and hand to the next managing agent.

The mid-size postwar co-op: fewer rooms, fewer staff, the same corridors

The 1930s co-op: brick, plaster and a cellar full of services

The prewar buildings on West 235th Street and Henry Hudson Parkway West were built when a fire alarm meant a bell and a pull station, if anything. Their walls are typically masonry with plaster, their apartments have been renovated many times, and their cellars hold the boiler, the meters, laundry and storage, and at 511 West 235th Street an indoor garage. That cellar is where we spend the most time on a survey, because it holds the equipment most likely to start a fire and the room least likely to have anyone in it at night. Heat detection usually suits a boiler room; the garage calls for detection chosen for exhaust and cold air, along with carbon monoxide considerations that the design professional decides on a filed system. Upstairs, plaster walls make new cable expensive and messy, so apartment alarms in these buildings are often sealed ten-year units with wireless interconnect, linked within each apartment so a fire in the kitchen sounds in the bedroom. Where a building wants hardwired apartment alarms, we test the circuits first and bring in an electrician for any new 120-volt work. If the building has an old hall system with bells, we tell the board honestly whether it can be repaired like for like or whether anything they want to change turns it into a filing.

The villa co-op: stucco, stairways and separate front doors

The Villa Charlotte Brontë, built in 1925 and 1926 for the Northern Realty Company to Robert Gardner's design, is nothing like a tower. Its apartments sit in two groups of connected sections below the level of Palisade Avenue, reached by flying staircases and balconies, and several have wood-burning fireplaces, terraces and French doors. For fire alarms that means three things. First, each apartment is closer to a house than to a tower unit: it may have more than one level, its own entrance from outside and rooms that open onto balconies, so its alarms have to be placed and linked as a household on their own. Second, the buildings are stucco, and the board will rightly want nothing new on the outside. Wireless interconnect inside each apartment, and concealed routes for anything the board decides should be shared, usually solve that. Third, the fireplaces bring carbon monoxide into the plan, and HPD's guidance puts carbon monoxide alarms within 15 feet of the main entrance to each sleeping room. The villa is not a designated city landmark, according to the survey we found, but its interiors deserve care regardless, and we show the board and each shareholder exactly where every device will go before anything is mounted.

The care residence: a different occupancy, a different chain of approval

An adult care facility is not an apartment house with older tenants. It is classed differently, it is run by an operator with staff, and its fire protection was designed and approved as part of that use. The residence at 3247 Johnson Avenue is listed under that building class, built in 1999 with seven stories and 59 units. For a building like that, our role is usually supporting the operator rather than redesigning anything: servicing devices like for like, replacing in-room alarms that suit the residents, keeping dated records, and testing on a schedule that staff and residents know about in advance. Any change to the design, such as adding devices, moving a panel or changing notification, goes back to the design professional and the FDNY, and may also involve the agency that licenses the facility. We do not know that agency's procedures for this building and do not describe them here. What we can do is make sure the operator knows, before we quote, which parts of the request are routine service and which have to be designed and approved first.

The house on the slope: one family, several levels, one decision-maker

The houses east of the parkway are mostly detached, in styles from Tudor and Italian Revival to Cape Cod, cottage and colonial, and many sit on lots that drop steeply from front to back. On a slope, a house can have a full floor below the front door that opens onto the garden, a garage tucked under the living room, and bedrooms two floors up. The owner decides how far above the minimum to go, and our usual recommendation is a linked system: a smoke alarm on every level, outside each sleeping area and in each bedroom, carbon monoxide alarms near bedrooms and near the boiler, water heater and any fireplace, and all of it interconnected so a fire on the garden level sounds beside the beds upstairs. Where walls are plaster on lath, sealed ten-year alarms with wireless interconnect avoid opening them. Where a family wants hardwired alarms, we check the circuit before connecting anything. For houses that sit empty for long stretches, partner central station monitoring at $19.99 a month means a fire is reported even when nobody is home.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Surveying a Spuyten Duyvil Tower One Corridor at a Time

Ride up before looking around the lobby. On a tower survey the technician goes first to a typical upper floor, usually near the top, and walks the corridor from the elevator lobby to the last apartment door and back. The Glenbriar report of a long, narrow hallway full of smoke is the picture we keep in mind: the question is how early a device in that corridor would notice smoke coming from under a door at the far end, and whether the stair doors would keep it out of the stairs.

Count, measure and look up. We count the corridor devices, note their type and labels, pace off the gaps between them and check the ceiling for soffits, beams and dropped sections that trap or divert smoke. Painted-over devices, missing heads and empty bases are photographed.

Check every door on the way. Apartment entry doors and stair doors are tried: do they close and latch on their own, are closers broken, are any propped? None of these are fire alarm devices, but detection only buys time if doors protect it, and the board hears about every one.

Then the base of the building. Pool, fitness room, laundry, package room, playroom, community room, bike room, storage, garage, boiler and compactor rooms each get a line in the notes: what is in the room, what detection is there, and when it is occupied.

Finish at the panel and the mailboxes. The panel screen, event history and battery dates are photographed, the monitoring arrangement identified, the attendant asked how often the system has sounded this year, and the HPD notices by the mailboxes read. Only then do we start a drawing or a price.

Compliance notes for Spuyten Duyvil buildings and businesses

  • Notices by the mailboxes. HPD's required-signage page says owners of Class A multiple dwellings must post notices about carbon monoxide and smoke detectors, and a signage supplier's summary of the rule places the smoke detector notice at or near the mailboxes.
  • Gas detectors join the notices in 2027. The same HPD page says that starting January 1, 2027, the notices must also cover natural gas detectors in buildings with gas piping, or a combined notice can cover all three.
  • CO alarm placement and type. HPD filing guidance places carbon monoxide alarms within 15 feet of the primary entrance to each sleeping room and requires an end-of-life alarm on the device.
  • What occupants pay. The same guidance describes occupants paying $25 per carbon monoxide alarm, or up to $50 per device where a combined smoke and carbon monoxide device is installed.
  • Sealed or hardwired. A secondary summary we found says new and replacement smoke alarms must be hardwired or use a ten-year non-removable battery. We follow that standard on every install and recommend owners confirm the current rule with HPD or the FDNY.
  • Building systems start with approved plans. The FDNY's fire code chapter on fire protection systems calls for fire alarm design and installation documents to be submitted for review and approval before installation. We install, label and pre-test to those plans; we are not the design professional.
  • Repair or redesign. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules treats replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no design change as a repair, and moving or replacing a control panel as work that needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Tall towers and voice. SPARC's summary of the Department of Buildings bulletin on existing buildings says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. Several Spuyten Duyvil towers are tall enough for the question to arise.
  • Small houses and the code. UpCodes' copy of the 2022 Building Code says a manual fire alarm system is not required in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit. That exemption concerns building systems; household smoke and CO alarms are a separate matter.
  • Landmarks. Edgehill Church is a designated New York City landmark and on the National Register. The Villa Charlotte Brontë is listed as never landmarked or heard, and we found no confirmation that any historic district covers Spuyten Duyvil.
One Street, Three Towers

Kappock Street's Fires and What a Co-op Board Can Take From Them

Kappock Street runs past three of the neighborhood's best-known co-ops: River Point Towers at number 555, the Presidential at 601 and the Glenbriar at 750. Two of them appear in the local fire record, and between them they illustrate nearly every lesson a board in a large Spuyten Duyvil building needs.

A fire in one apartment becomes the building's fire. The Riverdale Press reported that the call to 601 Kappock Street came just after 10 a.m. on January 21, 2020, and that nearly 140 emergency personnel responded. News 12 reported that the fire started in one apartment and spread to others. That is the central fact of co-op living: a shareholder's apartment is private property, but a fire inside it threatens every neighbor on the floor and above. For the board, it means the condition of alarms inside apartments is not purely the shareholder's business. Where house rules allow, a building-wide alarm program, with every apartment done to the same standard and recorded, protects the corporation as much as the household.

What is inside an apartment changes the fire. Officials told News 12 that one of the apartments at 601 Kappock held heavy clutter, which made the fire harder to put out. No alarm removes the fuel in a crowded room, and we do not offer opinions about any resident. What detection can do is warn earlier, which matters more when a fire will grow quickly once it starts. An apartment full of stored belongings is also an apartment where an alarm can be hidden behind stacked items, knocked from the ceiling, or simply never tested. When a board is aware of an apartment like that, the practical steps are the ones within its control: confirm the apartment has working, sealed or hardwired alarms in the right places, confirm the corridor devices outside that door are working, and make sure the entry door closes on its own.

The corridor is everyone's escape route. The Riverdale Press account of the Glenbriar fire at 750 Kappock describes a long, narrow tenth-floor hallway full of black smoke. In a postwar tower, the corridor is the only way from most apartments to the stairs, and smoke in it is the hazard that reaches people who are not near the fire at all. Corridor detection exists to sound before that hallway becomes impassable. That means devices spaced along its whole length, not just near the elevators, kept clean and working, and connected to notification that people on the floor will actually hear behind closed doors. It also means stair doors that close and latch, so the stairs stay clear while the corridor does not.

Leaving is hard for some residents, especially in winter. PIX11 reported that some senior residents of 601 Kappock were forced out of their apartments and onto a bus to keep warm. A fire alarm plan cannot change the weather, but it can change how much time residents have and how much they know. Residents who are slow to move need early warning and alarms they can hear. Staff need a current list of residents who may need help, shared with responders. And a building that knows its residents are older should test with notice, explain what the different sounds mean, and make sure every apartment's alarms are working before the coldest months, when doors stay shut, windows stay closed and people sleep more deeply under heavy blankets.

The tallest building on the street has the most to manage. River Point Towers, 25 stories and 412 apartments, lists a seasonal outdoor pool, a fitness center, a community room, a children's playroom, a laundry room, a bike room and a package room. Each of those rooms has a different fire risk, a different pattern of use and a different detector that suits it, and each one is a place where a fire could grow while the residents upstairs sleep. The board's fire protection plan in a building like that is really a set of small plans, one per room and one per corridor, held together by the panel and the people who watch it.

Older Residents and the Small Hours

Arlington Avenue at 2 a.m.: Planning Alarms for People Who Live Alone

The Riverdale Press reported that first responders were called just after 2 a.m. on January 13, 2021 to an apartment fire in unit 2A at 3215 Arlington Avenue, and that an 84-year-old man died. We have no information about the alarms in that apartment and make no claim about them. We mention the fire because its circumstances, an older person, at night, in an apartment, are common across Spuyten Duyvil, where long-time residents have aged in place in buildings they bought into decades ago.

Sleep is when alarms matter most. A fire that begins in the small hours has no one awake to smell it. The alarm in the bedroom, or right outside it, is what turns a fire into an evacuation instead of a tragedy, and it only works if it is there, powered and loud enough to wake the person in the bed. For an older resident, we look at three things. The alarm has to be the sealed ten-year or hardwired kind, so no one has borrowed its battery. It has to be placed where the rules and the layout call for it, which in HPD's guidance for carbon monoxide means within 15 feet of the main entrance to each sleeping room. And it has to be audible from the bed with the bedroom door closed, which we test rather than assume.

Linked alarms buy minutes. In an apartment with a kitchen at one end and the bedroom at the other, a fire on the stove may not reach the bedroom alarm until the hall is already full of smoke. Alarms linked within the apartment, by wire or by wireless interconnect from the same manufacturer, sound everywhere at once. For someone who needs longer to get up, find a robe and reach the door, those minutes are the difference.

Hearing, medication and routine. Many older residents take out hearing aids at night or take medication that deepens sleep. Alarms that combine a sound with a strobe or a bed shaker are available, and the right choice depends on the person. We do not make medical judgments; we offer the options, show the family how each works, and install the one they choose.

Someone else needs to know. In a household where one person lives alone, an apartment alarm that sounds only inside the apartment depends on a neighbor hearing it through the door. Families sometimes ask for a way to be told directly. Some alarms and monitored systems can report outside the apartment, and partner central station monitoring is available at $19.99 a month for households that want a fire reported even if nobody inside can act. In a co-op, anything that leaves the apartment, such as a communicator or a cable to the building system, is a question for the board as well as the family, and we raise it with both.

Electrical habits change in summer. The Riverdale Press account of a co-op fire at 2465 Palisade Avenue, which sent an elderly couple to the hospital, records an insurance investigator's view that it was electrical and connected to the wiring of a portable air conditioning unit. No detector tells you a cord is overheating before it smokes. What an alarm can do is catch that smoke early, which is one more reason to put a working smoke alarm in every bedroom, where portable units usually sit, and to ask an electrician about any room where cords run hot.

Corridors and the shared rooms in a tower's base

The Glenbriar fire filled a long, narrow tenth-floor hallway with black smoke. Our tower has corridors just like it. How should detection be spread along a hallway that long, and what does it do for neighbors behind closed doors?

A long corridor needs devices along its whole length, not a cluster near the elevators, because smoke from an apartment at the far end has to travel past everyone else's door before it reaches a detector placed by the lifts. The spacing on a filed system is set by the design professional under the code, and our job on a survey is to check that what is on the ceiling matches what should be there: count each device, measure the gaps, note where beams, soffits or dropped ceilings break the corridor into pockets that hold smoke, and find heads that were painted over or removed. A corridor detector does not protect the apartment where the fire is; that household relies on its own alarms. What the corridor device does is tell the floor, and through the panel the building, that the escape route is filling, early enough that neighbors can still use it. That only works if residents behind closed doors can hear the notification, so we test the sound level from inside apartments with doors shut. The other half of a corridor's protection is the doors themselves. Entry doors that close and latch keep smoke inside the apartment of origin, and stair doors that do the same keep the stairs clear. Broken closers and propped doors go on our report every time, even though they are not fire alarm devices.

Our co-op has an indoor pool on the lower level. Will the warm, chlorinated air damage the detectors there or set them off, and what kind of detection suits that room?

An indoor pool is one of the harshest rooms in a building for detection. The air is warm and humid all year, chlorine and its compounds corrode metal contacts and circuit boards over time, and condensation forms wherever warm air meets a cold surface, including the ceiling. Ordinary smoke detectors in that room tend to give false alarms when humidity spikes and to fail early as corrosion sets in. The usual answer is a device chosen for the environment rather than a standard one: heat detection rated for damp locations, or devices and enclosures made for corrosive atmospheres, and wiring and boxes protected accordingly. On a filed system, the choice and placement belong to the design professional, and we raise the pool room specifically so it is not designed like a corridor. The rooms next to the pool matter as much as the pool itself. The pump and filter room, chemical storage, the changing rooms and the corridor outside each have different conditions, and chemical storage in particular deserves the board's attention as a fire and safety space in its own right. We note every one of those rooms on the survey. For the Blue Building and any other co-op with an indoor pool, we also suggest the board check its pool-room devices on a shorter cycle than the rest of the building, since that is where they wear out first.

Deliveries pile up in our building's package room, especially in December, and the room stays closed most of the night. Does a package room need detection of its own?

Yes, and it is one of the rooms boards most often forget, because it was usually created out of a storage space or a corner of the lobby long after the building's fire alarm was designed. A package room is full of cardboard, plastic wrap and, increasingly, shipments that contain lithium-ion batteries: phones, laptops, tools, e-bike parts, toys. Firehouse.com reported the FDNY saying that battery-caused fires citywide were up about 70 percent this year. Most packages are harmless, but a damaged battery in a stack of cardboard in a closed room is exactly the kind of fire that can grow before anyone opens the door. If your building has a fire alarm system, adding detection to the package room means adding devices to that system, and depending on the change, the FDNY may need to see plans. Smoke detection usually suits a clean, dry package room; heat detection may suit one that shares space with mechanical equipment. Just as important are the things a board can do without any devices: keep the room from overflowing into the corridor, keep the door closing on its own, set a limit on how long packages can sit, and do not let it double as storage for residents' bikes or batteries. We can survey the room, tell you which category of work it falls into and price the detection.

Co-op paperwork and responsibilities

HPD says that from January 1, 2027 our mailbox notices have to cover natural gas detectors as well as smoke and carbon monoxide. What should our board do between now and then?

Start by reading what is actually posted now, then decide which notice format you want, then make sure the building matches whatever the notice says. HPD's required-signage page describes the change: from January 1, 2027, buildings with gas piping must post notices covering natural gas detectors as well as smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, or a single combined notice covering all three. So the first step is simple: walk to the mailboxes, photograph what is there, and check that the smoke and carbon monoxide notices exist, are legible and name the right contacts. Second, ask your managing agent and counsel to confirm the building's obligations on gas detectors themselves, because a sign about a device implies something about the device. We do not interpret that rule for you. Third, use the change as a reason to confirm the smoke and carbon monoxide side is in order. That means an apartment-by-apartment record: every bedroom, every alarm, its type, its power and its replacement date. Our survey produces that list and prices whatever is missing. Boards that work on a monthly meeting cycle should put the notice, the record and any apartment work on the next agenda, because November and December meetings fill quickly and the date will not move. We can visit, read the notices, sample apartments and give the board a written summary in time for that meeting.

Our co-op plans to install carbon monoxide alarms in every apartment and recover the cost from shareholders. What does HPD's guidance say occupants can be charged, and what records should we keep?

HPD filing guidance describes occupants paying $25 per carbon monoxide alarm, or up to $50 per device where a combined smoke and carbon monoxide alarm is installed. That figure is HPD's, not our price, and how it applies in a co-op, where the occupant is often also a shareholder bound by a proprietary lease, is a question for the board's attorney. What we can tell you is what makes any charge easy to defend. First, a device record tied to each apartment: which alarms went in, where, of what type, on what date. Second, consistency: every apartment done to the same standard, so no shareholder can say they were charged for something their neighbor did not get. Third, placement that follows the rule. HPD's guidance puts carbon monoxide alarms within 15 feet of the main entrance to each sleeping room and requires an end-of-life signal on the device, so an alarm in the wrong room or of the wrong type is not just a protection gap but a billing problem. Our per-unit apartment work runs $400 to $800 per unit on our list, depending on the number of bedrooms and whether the alarms are sealed or hardwired, and every invoice we issue for a building program lists devices by apartment so the managing agent can bill from it directly.

Several of our shareholders spend the winter away and leave their apartments locked for months. What should the co-op have in place for the day an alarm starts sounding behind one of those doors?

The policy has to exist before the alarm sounds, because the minutes after it starts are the wrong time to decide who may enter someone's home. Most proprietary leases and house rules give the corporation some right of entry in an emergency, and many co-ops ask shareholders to leave a key or a contact. Ask your managing agent what yours say, then make the practical arrangements match. The staff need to know where keys are kept, who may use them, and who must be called before and after entry. For a sounding smoke alarm, the building's instinct should be to treat it as a fire until someone has seen otherwise, and to call the fire department rather than send a porter in alone. For a chirp, which usually means a low battery or an alarm at end of life, the response can wait for the super and the shareholder's contact, but not for months; an alarm chirping in an empty apartment is an alarm that will soon be silent. Two things we install make this easier. Sealed ten-year alarms remove most chirp calls in the first place. And for an apartment that sits empty for long periods, partner monitoring at $19.99 a month can report a fire alarm to a central station and the contacts the shareholder chooses. In a co-op, anything that reports outside the apartment should be cleared with the board, and we raise that with both sides.

Villas and care buildings

Our villa co-op on Palisade Avenue is a set of connected stucco sections with outdoor stairways and separate front doors. Should the alarms in each apartment be linked to the neighbors, and can that be done without anything on the facade?

The first step is deciding what each part of the system is for, because a villa building sits somewhere between a house and an apartment block. Inside each apartment, alarms should be linked to each other, so a fire in a lower-level kitchen sounds in an upper-level bedroom; that is a household system, and it is the part we can usually do without touching the outside at all. Sealed ten-year alarms with wireless interconnect from one manufacturer link the rooms of one apartment through stucco and masonry walls, and we test the signal room by room, because thick walls do weaken it. Whether alarms should also link between apartments is a separate question for the board and, if it becomes a building system, for a design professional. Linking every household's alarms together means one family's burnt toast sounds in sixteen homes, which quickly teaches everyone to ignore it. A better arrangement for a building like this is usually household alarms within each apartment, plus whatever shared detection the board decides belongs in common spaces, such as stairways, a shared cellar or a boiler room, reporting to a panel that the board controls. Any cable for shared devices can travel inside through existing chases and cellars rather than across the stucco. We draw that arrangement for the board, show every device location to each shareholder, and do nothing on the outside of the building.

We operate a building classed as an adult care facility rather than an apartment house. Does that change who designs, approves and services our fire alarm?

It changes the starting point. A building classed for adult care, like the residence at 3247 Johnson Avenue, was designed and approved for that use, with a fire alarm system laid out by a design professional and accepted for that occupancy. That means its system is not something to be altered casually, and it also means the operator usually has more than one authority to satisfy. The FDNY reviews and approves fire alarm design and installation documents before installation, and a facility that is licensed or supervised by a state agency may have its own expectations about notification, staff response and records. We did not research that agency's rules and do not describe them here. In practice, we sort a care operator's requests into three groups. Routine service, such as cleaning, testing and replacing failed devices with identical ones, we can schedule and perform. In-room alarms and accessories that suit residents, such as strobes or low-frequency sounders for people who are hard of hearing, we can supply and install where they do not change the building system. Anything that adds devices, moves the panel or changes how the building notifies residents goes back to the design professional and the FDNY first. Testing in a care setting is always scheduled with staff, announced to residents in advance, and done in short sections so no one is frightened or confused about whether to leave.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Spuyten Duyvil

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this search in our build environment, so nothing here quotes it. These are claims that turn up repeatedly in generic online answers and national guides, paraphrased, each followed by what Spuyten Duyvil's buildings and the published rules actually show.

"In a co-op, the board takes care of all the smoke alarms"

Generic answers often describe a co-op as one owner responsible for everything inside the building, the way a landlord might be.

In practice a co-op splits the work. The board controls the building system in corridors, stairs, the lobby and shared rooms, while the alarms inside each apartment depend on HPD's rules for multiple dwellings, the proprietary lease and the house rules, which may place maintenance on the shareholder.

In a neighborhood where most homes are postwar co-ops, assuming the board covers everything leaves apartments unchecked. The first step is a written split of who maintains what, then a record that each half is done.

"Any smoke or CO alarm from the hardware store will meet the rules"

Shopping guides tend to treat every alarm on the shelf as interchangeable, and suggest picking whatever is cheapest.

HPD filing guidance requires carbon monoxide alarms to have an end-of-life signal, and the summaries we found say new and replacement smoke alarms should be hardwired or use a sealed ten-year battery. A replaceable-battery alarm may work on the day it goes up and still be the wrong choice.

In Spuyten Duyvil co-ops and rentals, the right purchase is a sealed or hardwired smoke alarm and a carbon monoxide alarm with an end-of-life signal, recorded by apartment and date.

"A carbon monoxide alarm can go anywhere in the apartment"

General advice often says one carbon monoxide alarm per home is enough, wherever there is an outlet or a convenient shelf.

HPD's guidance places carbon monoxide alarms within 15 feet of the main entrance to each sleeping room. In a long postwar apartment with bedrooms at both ends, or a two-level villa unit, one alarm in the kitchen does not meet that.

Placement follows the bedrooms. In villas and houses with fireplaces or a boiler, an additional alarm near the fuel-burning equipment is worth adding on top.

"Detector notices are a one-time posting"

Answers about building signage often treat notices as something put up once when a building opens and never revisited.

HPD's required-signage page says that from January 1, 2027, notices in buildings with gas piping must also cover natural gas detectors, or a combined notice can cover all three. Many Spuyten Duyvil co-ops still have gas ranges.

A notice that describes alarms the building does not have is worse than no notice at all. The change is a good moment to check that the apartments match what the sign promises.

"Fire alarm installation costs about what national averages say"

National cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish averages built from many places and building types, and short answers repeat them as if they fit any address.

An average cannot see a 25-story corridor, an indoor pool, a villa unit with no cable allowed on the stucco, or a co-op that wants 400 apartments done in one program with records for the board.

Our standard Bronx list starts at $600 for a basic residential system, runs $400 to $800 per unit for building programs, and $2,000 to $6,000 for a custom hardwired system. Filed building systems are priced after the design.

"Prewar buildings are exempt from current alarm rules"

Some answers suggest that old buildings are grandfathered and can keep whatever alarms they were built with.

The building system in a 1930s co-op may well date from an older code, and the repair-versus-redesign line decides what must change when work is done. But the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms inside its apartments fall under today's HPD rules, whatever year the bricks went up.

For the Linden House or 511 West 235th Street, the honest answer is both: the building system is judged by what work is proposed, and the apartment alarms are judged by the current rule.

"A fire in one apartment is mostly that household's emergency"

Consumer answers usually frame fire alarms as protecting the family that owns them, as if each apartment were a separate house.

A building listing for our co-op says "Ladder 52." What does that mean for our fire alarm?
+

Real estate listings such as Compass show the FDNY ladder company assigned to a building, and River Point Towers and the Linden House are both listed under Ladder 52. It tells you which company is likely to respond, not anything about your system. It does not change what detection the building needs, and responders still depend on the alarm or a caller to know there is a fire.

The ceilings in our postwar co-op apartment are bare concrete slab. How do the alarms get fastened?
+

We drill small holes with a masonry bit and set anchors made for concrete, then mount the alarm's base plate to them. It takes a few minutes per alarm and leaves only small holes when an alarm is later replaced. We avoid adhesive mounts on alarms that must stay put for ten years, and we never drill near where a ceiling might carry conduit without checking first.

When we sell our co-op apartment, can we take the alarms we paid for with us?
+

No, and you should not want to. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are part of the protection the apartment is required to have, and the buyer and the board will expect them in place at closing. Leave them up, along with the device list and dates we gave you, so the next owner knows when each one is due for replacement.

What is the end-of-life signal on a carbon monoxide alarm, and what should we do when it starts?
+

HPD guidance requires carbon monoxide alarms to have an end-of-life alarm, a distinct chirp or display message that means the sensor has reached the end of its rated life. It is not a fire or gas warning and it will not stop with a new battery. The alarm has to be replaced, and in a co-op it is worth checking whether alarms installed at the same time are close to the same point.

Where in the lobby is the smoke detector notice supposed to hang?
+

A signage supplier's summary of HPD's rule places the smoke detector notice at or near the mailboxes, and HPD's required-signage page lists the carbon monoxide notice alongside it. On a survey we read what is posted, note anything missing or out of date, and include it in the summary for the board.

How can our board find out whether the building's fire alarm is monitored at all?
+

Start with paperwork: a monitoring contract, a central station invoice or a communicator noted on the panel. If none can be found, we check the panel on a survey and tell the board in writing what it shows.
