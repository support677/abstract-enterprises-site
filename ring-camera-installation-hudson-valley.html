The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need Ring Camera Installation

Ring cameras are sold as a do-it-yourself product, and on a new house with modern wiring and a router in the next room, that is often true. Most Hudson Valley houses are not that house. The valley was settled early and built slowly. The Hudson River Valley National Heritage Area notes that the Dutch were the first colonists after Henry Hudson's 1609 voyage up the river, that Dutch houses with steep roofs and gable ends facing the street remained the region's most prominent house form for about two hundred years, and that by 1750 most of the region had been settled in a network of small local communities that still survives today. Kingston, which began as the Dutch settlement of Wiltwyck, still has colonial-era stone houses standing on all four corners of Crown and John Streets. Around those old cores came river estates, mill and factory towns, railroad suburbs, and then the large postwar subdivisions of the Lower Valley. A Ring installer here works on every one of those eras, often in the same week.

The age of the housing is not a guess. Stacker's September 2025 analysis of U.S. Census Bureau data puts the median home in Westchester County at 1958, with 28.0 percent of the county's homes, about 109,608 of them, built in 1939 or earlier. Putnam's median year is 1968 with 17.9 percent built before 1940, Rockland's is 1970 with 11.5 percent, and Dutchess's is 1971 with 18.0 percent. For a Ring doorbell that matters in a very specific way. A wired Ring doorbell takes its power from the same small transformer and chime circuit that rang the old button, and in a house from the 1950s, 1960s or earlier that transformer may be original, undersized for a video doorbell, or wired to a mechanical chime that no longer behaves. One owner, writing in a product listing, described having to upgrade a 35-year-old doorbell transformer to have enough power to run a Ring Pro. That story is ordinary in an old housing stock, and it is the reason we meter the circuit before we mount anything.

The weather and the grid are the second reason. A Ring camera is a Wi-Fi device, and when the router loses power the camera loses its connection even if the camera itself has a battery. The valley's storms make that a regular event rather than a rare one. Times Hudson Valley reported that at noon on the Sunday after the July 4, 2026 storms, Central Hudson had 669 active outages affecting 41,323 customers across the region, and Central Hudson's own update said about 17,200 customers were still without power days later, with Dutchess and Ulster counties hit hardest. The Daily Freeman's headline from July 5 put the number at nearly 50,000 Central Hudson customers. In late September, Mid-Hudson News reported Central Hudson restoring power to more than 24,000 customers after a nor'easter. Further south, Daily Voice reported during Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020 that NYSEG had 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers without power at one point that morning. A Ring system set up with no thought for outages simply goes dark for the length of the outage, and some devices do not reconnect cleanly afterwards. Setting it up so it recovers on its own is part of the job.

Distance is the third. The valley's houses sit on bigger lots than city houses, with detached garages, barns, sheds, pool houses and long drives. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 620 farms in Dutchess County and 486 in Ulster. Even outside farm country, a camera on a garage across the yard can be 80 or 100 feet of walls, trees and siding away from the router. Ring cameras need a steady Wi-Fi signal at the mount, not just in the kitchen, and a weak link shows up as late alerts, missed motion and grey live view. Some households also start with weak internet. Ulster County announced in September 2025 that it had won a state ConnectALL award to bring fiber to nearly 1,300 unserved households, businesses and community facilities in remote parts of the county, which tells you how many places are still waiting for a solid connection. A Ring camera is only as good as the upload speed behind it.

Then there are the rules on the outside of the house. New York City's Landmarks Preservation Commission has no say up here, but the valley has its own commissions. The City of Kingston has five local historic districts, and in a January 2025 approval its Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission recorded that the owner should be required to paint all externally mounted lines or line hides to match the facade's existing color. The City of Newburgh's East End Historic District Design Guidelines speak to security features directly: integrate them with the architecture, do not obscure architectural features, and do not damage historic building fabric when attaching them. A Ring doorbell on a Stockade stone house or a Floodlight Cam on a Newburgh cornice is a small thing, but it is exactly the kind of small thing those rules are written for.

Finally, there is why people buy Ring at all. The local news record is full of porch and driveway footage. Hudson Valley Post published a reader's account of a thief who stole from a house on Fitchett Avenue in Poughkeepsie and then a porch on De Laval Place an hour later; both houses had Ring doorbells that caught him. WPDH reported a Ring doorbell at a Carter Avenue home in Newburgh recording a package theft. News 12 showed Ring footage from Stony Point of a man walking a bike off a porch near Jerben and Major Andre Drive at about 12:20 a.m., and Ring footage from neighbors on and near Tower Hill Drive in Port Chester of car break-ins around 4 a.m. In January 2025, Hudson Valley Post reported the State Police investigating residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam counties, with a tip list that included installing or activating security systems and cameras. That footage only helps if the camera was online, aimed at the right place, and recording when it mattered.

We want to be honest about the gaps in our research too. We looked for Reddit threads from r/hudsonvalley and similar forums about Ring installation in the region and found none we could cite. Where this page describes what we find inside old walls or doorbell circuits, it draws on the age and type of the housing in the public record and on our general field practice, not on a survey of Hudson Valley homes.

Hudson Valley facts that shape a Ring installation

  • Old houses, old doorbell circuits: Westchester's median home dates to 1958, with 28.0 percent built in 1939 or earlier; Putnam's median is 1968, Rockland's 1970 and Dutchess's 1971 (Stacker, from U.S. Census data).
  • Long outages: after the July 4, 2026 storms Central Hudson reported 41,323 customers out at one point, with Dutchess and Ulster the most heavily affected.
  • Remote addresses: Ulster County is funding fiber for nearly 1,300 unserved households, businesses and community facilities in remote areas.
  • Working land: 620 farms in Dutchess and 486 in Ulster in the 2022 Census of Agriculture, many with barns and outbuildings far from the house.
  • Historic exteriors: Newburgh's East End Historic District has 2,239 resources, the most of any federally recognized district in the state, and Kingston has five local historic districts.
  • Four utilities: Con Edison and NYSEG in Westchester, Orange & Rockland in Rockland and Orange, NYSEG in parts of Putnam, and Central Hudson from the northern suburbs up toward the Capital District.

What people often add once the doorbell works. After a Ring doorbell is up and talking to the chime, the next request in the valley is usually a camera on the driveway or the garage, and on larger lots a Ring Alarm base with door sensors for a weekend house. If you think you will want those, tell us when you book. It is easier to plan the Wi-Fi for three devices once than to come back and find the garage camera cannot reach the router.

One Ring Doorbell, Six Different Jobs: How the Install Changes From Westchester to Ulster

This page is our hub for the whole valley. The Hudson River Valley Heritage Area treats Westchester and Rockland as the Lower Hudson Valley and Putnam, Orange, Dutchess and Ulster as the Middle Hudson Valley, and that split is a fair rough guide to how a Ring job changes as you drive north. South, the lots are smaller, the houses are more often postwar, neighbors are closer and the questions are about aim, privacy and porch theft. North, the questions shift to distance, internet, outages, outbuildings and old stone. Each county also licenses electrical work a little differently, which decides who connects anything on the 120-volt side. Westchester County

Westchester has the oldest housing of the six by median year, and a lot of it sits on suburban lots where the front door faces a sidewalk and a neighbor's house is a few yards away. That makes two things matter: a doorbell circuit that may be seventy years old, and careful aiming so a camera watches your porch and drive, not the house across the street. The local news gives the reasons people call. News 12 reported in June 2026 that police data across the county showed reported package thefts going down, while still airing a Yonkers resident's Ring footage of someone taking a package from a porch on Yonkers Avenue. In Port Chester, neighbors showed News 12 Ring footage of car break-ins around 4 a.m. near Tower Hill Drive, and Rye police reported 99 vehicle thefts since 2020, all involving cars left unlocked with the key fob inside. A Ring camera on the driveway records those events; it does not lock the car. In Scarsdale, police confirmed that burglars had hidden camouflaged cameras in bushes to watch homes, which is a good reason to walk your own yard with us and know what is pointed at what. Westchester also requires electricians to hold a county trade license, so a new transformer circuit or a hardwired Floodlight Cam connection is handled by a county-licensed electrician. Prices: Ring camera installation prices for Westchester County.

Rockland County

Rockland's housing is younger, with a median year of 1970, and much of it is the split-level, raised ranch and colonial stock of the postwar decades, with an attached garage and a front door set back from the road. Those houses usually have a doorbell circuit that is easier to work with than a prewar one, but the transformer may still be the original and the chime may be buried in a hallway ceiling. Rockland's county code defines an electrical contractor as someone doing wiring for light, heat, power or signaling at 50 volts or more, so a Ring doorbell on its low-voltage circuit is our work, and anything on the house wiring is for a licensed electrician. Rockland's Ring stories in the news include a bike walked off a porch in Stony Point and, from Patch, a Ring camera in Rockland that helped find a missing New Jersey hiker. Another News 12 story from Stony Point described a crash caught on a homeowner's Ring doorbell on what neighbors called a very curvy stretch of road, a reminder that a doorbell facing a busy road sees a lot more than your porch. Prices: Rockland County Ring installation prices.

Orange County

Orange County runs from the city of Newburgh's dense historic streets to farm roads and new subdivisions. Newburgh's East End Historic District is the largest federally recognized district in the state by number of resources, with townhouses carrying ornate cornices and decorative brackets and, on Parmenter Street, gabled two-story Federal-style houses. The city's design guidelines say security features should be integrated with the architecture and not damage historic fabric. In practice that means a Ring doorbell placed on the door frame or the side of the entry rather than across carved trim, and any visible wire kept short and painted. A Carter Avenue home in Newburgh made the news when its Ring doorbell recorded a package theft. Orange County requires electricians to hold a county license under Local Law 8 of 2007, revised by Local Law 1 of 2024, and the City of Middletown has its own Electrical Licensing Board. Newburgh also lets an owner-occupant of a one- or two-family house pull an electrical permit, with inspection by an approved agency. Internet is changing in parts of the county: Greenlight Networks says it serves Goshen, Middletown, Wallkill and Port Jervis with fiber, which helps Ring cameras that upload a lot of video. Prices: Orange County Ring camera installation prices.

Putnam County

Putnam is the county where Ring installs most often meet trees, water and long outages. Lake houses around Lake Mahopac, wooded lots off winding roads and homes in Cold Spring and Nelsonville all bring the same pair of questions: will the Wi-Fi reach the dock, the garage or the end of the drive, and what happens when NYSEG or Central Hudson loses power for a day. The Isaias figures from 2020, with NYSEG at one point reporting 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers out, are the extreme case, but they show why we put a battery backup on the router in many Putnam houses. Putnam's Chapter 145 licenses low-voltage and limited data communications technicians for cabling that includes video surveillance, at 90 volts or less, fed from an outlet installed by a licensed electrician. That is the category a wired Ring camera or a network cable to a garage access point falls into. The state DEC also notes that black bear range in the Taconic Highlands runs south into Putnam, which means some Ring motion alerts at night will be bears at the trash, not people. Prices: Putnam County Ring installation prices.

Dutchess County

Dutchess holds both city porches and some of the largest estates in the state. In the City of Poughkeepsie, the porch thefts on Fitchett Avenue and De Laval Place were caught on Ring doorbells, and in December 2024 Mid-Hudson News reported detectives searching 94 South Hamilton Street and recovering numerous stolen items in a porch-theft case. North of the city, the Hudson River National Historic Landmark District, designated in 1990 and described as the largest such district on the mainland of the contiguous United States, includes river estates, villages and hamlets where only 2 percent of the acreage is not considered historic. Rhinebeck's village has its own National Register district. Out in the farm towns, the 620 farms counted in 2022 mean barns and sheds far from the house. We found no county-level electrical contractor license in Dutchess, but towns set their own rules: the Town of Fishkill's permit package says all electrical work must be inspected by an approved independent electrical inspection agency. Prices: Dutchess County Ring camera installation prices.

Ulster County

Ulster has the oldest buildings and some of the hardest internet. Kingston's Stockade District holds the early Dutch stone houses; the city's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work visible from the street, and its 2025 minutes show it asking for externally mounted lines to be painted to match the facade. A Kingston police warning about porch pirates described a package stolen from a McEntee Street porch and caught on the homeowner's camera. News 12 reported the Ulster County Sheriff's Office warning about burglaries and car break-ins on Union Center Road and nearby streets in Esopus. The Southern Ulster Times reported that Town of Lloyd police joined Ring's Neighbors app and can see what residents post publicly there. Ulster's Chapter 185 Electrical Licensing Law treats signaling, communication, alarm and data transmission as electrical work, so we check the town's expectations before we run any cable. In the remote parts of the county, the ConnectALL fiber project is still under way, and some Ring owners are on slow or satellite connections. Prices: Ulster County Ring installation prices.

Porch, Chime and Router: What to Photograph at a Hudson Valley House Before We Come

Most of the time lost on a Ring install is lost before anything is mounted: a transformer that cannot power the doorbell, a chime that turns out to be a digital unit Ring does not support, a router hidden in a basement corner that cannot reach the porch, or a historic commission nobody called. Five minutes with a phone camera prevents nearly all of it. Please do not open the transformer, the chime cover or the electrical panel if you are not comfortable doing so; photos of what you can see are enough.

Look at your phone's Wi-Fi where the cameras will go

The easiest test you can do yourself is to stand at each place you want a camera, on the porch, by the garage door, at the barn, and look at your phone's Wi-Fi bars and try a short video call. If the call stutters or the bars drop to one, a Ring camera at that spot will struggle too. Tell us what you saw at each place. It tells us whether a camera can go straight on your existing Wi-Fi, needs a Ring Chime Pro or another extender nearer to it, or needs a cable run to a proper access point. In stone houses and old brick, the walls themselves can cut a strong signal to almost nothing a few feet outside the door.

Think about who comes to the door, and from where

A doorbell camera should see a face and a package, not the sky. In the valley, front doors come in every arrangement: a stone stoop straight off a Kingston sidewalk, a deep porch on a Victorian in Poughkeepsie, a side door that everyone actually uses on a farmhouse, a door at the top of steep steps on a hillside house. Tell us which door delivery drivers use, where packages are usually left, and whether the door faces a road. That decides the height, the angle, whether a wedge or corner kit is needed, and whether a second camera on the drive is worth it.

Building Specialists

Ring Camera Installation for the Hudson Valley's Architecture

The valley's building types each change three things about a Ring job: what the mount is fixed into, where power comes from, and how the Wi-Fi gets through the walls. These are the types the public record and local guides describe, and how we handle each.

Dutch Stone Houses

Colonial-era stone houses like those on the corners of Crown and John Streets in Kingston's Stockade. Thick fieldstone and mortar walls, wood door frames, very little room for hidden wire, and a commission watching the facade.

River-City Row Houses

Italianate and Federal-style townhouses in Newburgh's East End and similar blocks in Poughkeepsie and Kingston. Brick, ornate cornices and brackets, doors on stoops close to the sidewalk, often split into apartments.

River Estates and Carriage Houses

Large houses inside the Hudson River National Historic Landmark District and around Rhinebeck and Hyde Park, with gatehouses, carriage houses and long drives far from the main house.

Postwar Colonials, Split-Levels and Ranches

The bulk of Westchester, Rockland and Putnam housing from the 1950s to the 1970s: siding over wood frame, attached garages, doorbell circuits that may be original.

Lake Houses and Wooded Lots

Houses around Lake Mahopac and on wooded hillsides, with docks, boathouses and drives through trees, where Wi-Fi reach and outages matter most.

Farmhouses and Barns

Working and former farms across Dutchess, Ulster and Orange, with the side door everyone uses, barns well away from the house, and equipment and animals that set off motion.

Stone walls: drilling less, not more

A Ring doorbell on a fieldstone wall is the job people imagine is hardest, and the trick is usually to avoid drilling the stone at all. Most stone houses have a wood door frame or casing, and a slim Ring doorbell can often mount on the casing itself, where screws go into wood and leave no mark on the stone. If the old bell wire comes through a hole in the frame, we use that hole. If there is no wire, a battery doorbell avoids a new hole altogether. When a mount really must go into the stone, we drill into the face of a sound stone, not the mortar joint, with a bit sized for the anchor, and we keep the hole count to the minimum the mounting plate allows. On a house in a Kingston district, we also photograph the planned position before drilling so you can show the commission exactly what will be visible, and any exposed wire gets a line hide painted to match the wall, the way the 2025 HLPC minutes describe.

Row houses: working around the cornice and the neighbors

The row houses of Newburgh's East End and the river cities are mostly brick, built tight to the sidewalk, and many are now apartments. The front door often still has a bell circuit, but in a building split into units the circuit may be shared, rewired over the years, or part of a buzzer system. We trace it before we use it. Mounting goes on the door frame or the brick beside it, never across the decorative brackets or trim that the Newburgh guidelines ask owners to protect. Floodlight Cams on these facades are rarely a good idea, both for the commission and for the neighbors whose windows are a few feet away; a small camera tucked under the entry or at the rear yard does the job with far less visual impact. Wi-Fi in row houses is crowded, with many neighbors' networks on the same channels, so we check the signal at the door rather than trusting the router's bars upstairs.

Postwar houses: the original transformer question

The colonials, split-levels and ranches built across Westchester, Rockland and Putnam after the war are the easiest houses to put Ring on physically, with wood trim at the door and siding that takes a mounting block well. The catch is almost always the doorbell transformer. Many of these houses still have the transformer they were built with, sometimes in the basement next to the panel, sometimes on a junction box in the garage, sometimes behind the furnace. A transformer that ran a mechanical bell for decades may not supply enough power for a video doorbell's camera, radio and night vision, and the symptoms are a doorbell that keeps dropping offline, a battery that never charges, or a chime that buzzes. We meter it first. If it is too weak, replacing it is a line-voltage job for an electrician licensed in your county, and we say so in the quote rather than leaving you with a doorbell that limps.

Estates, farms and lake houses: distance and outbuildings

On the river estates, farms and lake properties, the house itself is often the easy part. The hard part is the gatehouse at the end of a long drive, the barn across a field, or the dock below a slope. Ring cameras on battery or solar can work at those buildings if the Wi-Fi reaches them, which in practice means planning the network first. Sometimes a Ring Chime Pro or another extender in a window facing the outbuilding is enough. Sometimes the right answer is a network cable buried or run to the barn with a weatherproof access point, which then gives the cameras there a strong local signal. Metal roofs and steel siding on barns block Wi-Fi badly, so we mount the access point or extender where it can see out. On solar cameras, we check winter sun before we promise anything; a panel under deciduous trees can look perfect in October and be in shade by noon in January.

Where Ring suits a small business, and where it does not

Ring works well for a business when the job is simple: a doorbell camera at a back door where deliveries arrive, a camera over a register or a stock room, a Floodlight Cam over a small parking area, and alerts on the owner's phone after hours. It works less well when a business needs many cameras recording all the time, footage stored on site for a set period, or several staff watching the same feeds with different permissions. Ring stores recorded video in the cloud under a Ring subscription, and each camera depends on the Wi-Fi. A tasting room on a farm road with a weak internet connection may find that four Ring cameras uploading at once crowd out its card reader. We look at the internet service and the number of cameras before recommending Ring for a business, and if a wired recorder system fits better we explain why.

Rules that apply to cameras at a business

  • Licensed installer. New York requires a state license to install security systems, which include cameras; the MBFAA licensing course page warns that unlicensed installers can be fined up to ten thousand dollars. We work under NYS license #12000287431. A business owner installing on their own premises is a separate case.
  • No cameras where people expect privacy. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install or use video surveillance where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and Penal Law 250.45 makes it a crime to record a person in a bathroom, changing room or similar space without consent. No Ring camera goes in or aimed into a restroom or staff changing area.
  • Post a notice. Penal Law 250.65 protects systems where a written notice that video surveillance is installed is conspicuously posted, or where the cameras are clearly obvious. A small sign at the door is cheap insurance.
  • Think hard about audio. Ring cameras record sound by default. In a shop, staff and customers talk all day, and recording conversations you are not part of raises legal questions under New York's eavesdropping law. We usually turn audio recording off for businesses unless the owner has had legal advice otherwise.
  • Historic facades. In Kingston's districts and Newburgh's East End, visible cameras, floodlights and wiring on a storefront can fall under commission review. We plan placement to keep the visual impact small and give you photos for the application.
  • Line-voltage work. Floodlight Cams and new outlets for cameras are connected by an electrician licensed for that county, such as Westchester's county trade license or Orange County's electrician license. Our quote names which part is theirs.
Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check Before the Ring Doorbell Comes Out of the Box

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address in the valley. It is the routine our technicians follow at a front door of the kind the Hudson Valley is full of: an older house, an old push button, a mechanical chime somewhere in the hall, and a router in a back room.

We leave the Ring box closed. First we press the old button and listen for the chime, because a chime that already rings weakly is telling us about the transformer. We find the transformer and put a meter on it, with the bell pressed and released, and write the reading down. We take the chime cover off and look at its terminals and whether it is mechanical or electronic. Then we walk outside with a phone and check the Wi-Fi strength at the door, not at the router. We stand where a delivery driver stands and where a package lands, and we look at the door frame to see what it is made of. Only when those four answers are in, power, chime, signal and frame, do we choose the doorbell model and its mount. In a postwar colonial that might take five minutes; in a stone house it can take longer. Either way, it is what stops the call two weeks later saying the doorbell has gone offline.

Our place is a long way up a dirt road in the back of Ulster or Dutchess. Will you come out that far for a Ring install?

Yes. We cover all six counties, including rural towns well off the main roads, and the county rate already accounts for the drive. What we ask for on remote properties is more information up front, so the visit is not wasted: photos of each door and building, the internet provider and speed if you know it, and the distance from the house to any outbuilding. Remote houses are where the internet question matters most, since a Ring camera uploads video and a slow or satellite connection can struggle, and we would rather tell you that before the drive than after.

Power, wiring and wireless

The doorbell transformer in our 1950s house is probably original. Will a wired Ring doorbell run on it, or does it have to be replaced?

Sometimes it runs, often it struggles. Ring lists the power each wired doorbell needs, and an old transformer that rang a mechanical bell for decades may fall short, which shows up as a doorbell that drops offline, a chime that hums, or night video that cuts out. We meter it before installing. If it is enough, the doorbell goes straight on. If not, the transformer is replaced, which is a line-voltage job done by an electrician licensed for your county, and we coordinate it so both parts happen on the same day where possible.

Wired, battery, solar or plug-in: which kind of Ring device makes sense where on a Hudson Valley property?

We usually mix them. A wired doorbell is best where a working bell circuit already exists, because it never needs charging. Battery doorbells and cameras suit doors and corners with no wiring, at the cost of taking them down to charge; one Yelp reviewer elsewhere in New York complained of changing a Ring doorbell battery every month, which is the main reason people later switch to wired. Solar panels help battery cameras where winter sun reaches them, which on a wooded valley lot is not everywhere. Plug-in cameras fit garages and barns with an outlet. We pick per location after checking power and sun.

In our county, who is allowed to connect the 120-volt side of a Ring Floodlight Cam or a new doorbell transformer?

An electrician licensed for that county or town. Westchester requires a county trade license for electricians, Rockland licenses electrical contractors for wiring at 50 volts or more, Orange County requires a county electrician license and Middletown has its own board, Putnam has an electrical board and a separate low-voltage technician license, and Ulster has its Electrical Licensing Law. We found no county license in Dutchess, but towns such as Fishkill require third-party electrical inspection. The low-voltage doorbell, camera mounting, Wi-Fi and app setup are ours under our state license.

Can I put Ring up myself, or does New York require a licensed installer?

You can install Ring on your own home yourself; the licensing guidance we found says no state license is required for homeowner self-installation. The license matters when you hire someone: New York requires people who install security systems, including cameras, for others to be licensed, and unlicensed installers face fines. Ring's own pro-install option uses a network of third-party contractors, so it is worth asking whoever comes to show their license. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and we show it on request.

Wi-Fi, internet and outages

The garage is about a hundred feet from the house. Will a Ring camera on it stay connected to our Wi-Fi?

Maybe, and we test before promising. Distance is only part of it; walls, siding, trees and a metal garage door all cut the signal. If your phone has weak Wi-Fi standing at the garage, a Ring camera there will too. The fixes, from simplest to strongest, are a Ring Chime Pro or another extender in a window facing the garage, a mesh node in the garage itself, or a network cable to an access point in the garage. We measure signal at the mount during the visit and choose the cheapest option that gives a steady connection.

When Central Hudson loses power for a day, what happens to our Ring cameras, and can we keep them watching?

Without planning, they go blind even if they have batteries, because the router and modem lose power and the cameras have no internet to send to; some also drain their batteries searching for the lost network, as one forum user described with Wi-Fi cameras after outages. The usual fix is a battery backup on the modem and router, so they keep running for a while after the power drops. How long depends on the backup and your equipment, and it only helps if your internet provider's own lines stay up. After an outage, we also make sure every device reconnects on its own.

Our internet in the hills is slow. Is Ring still worth installing on it?

It depends on the upload speed, not the download speed most plans advertise. Ring sends video up to the cloud, and several cameras recording at once can overwhelm a slow upload, giving late alerts and choppy playback. We check your connection's real upload speed during the visit. On slow lines we limit the number of cameras, lower video quality where it does not matter, and use motion zones so cameras record less. If fiber is on its way, as Ulster's ConnectALL project and Greenlight's Orange County service suggest it is for some households, Ring works much better afterwards.

Historic houses

We own a stone house in Kingston's Stockade District. What should we check before a Ring doorbell goes on the front?

Call the City of Kingston's planning office first and ask whether a doorbell camera, a small exterior camera or any visible wire needs review by the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission, because exterior work visible from the street is reviewed there and a contractor's guide says a Certificate of Appropriateness can be needed before a building permit. The 2025 HLPC minutes show the commission asking for exterior lines to be painted to match the facade. We help by choosing the least visible position, often on the wood door casing, and giving you photos and a written description to submit.

Will a Ring camera on a Newburgh East End row house meet the city's design guidelines?

It can, if it is placed with the guidelines in mind. Newburgh's East End guidelines say security features should be integrated with the architecture, should not obscure architectural features, and should not damage historic building fabric when attached. That points toward a slim doorbell on the door frame, small cameras under the entry or at the rear, and no floodlights across a cornice. The city's Architectural Review Commission makes the decision, so check with it if your work needs review; we plan placement that respects the facade either way.

Privacy and the law

Is it legal in New York for my Ring camera to record sound on my porch?

Video of your own porch is generally fine; sound is where care is needed. New York's eavesdropping law requires at least one party to a conversation to consent, and a lawyer answering a New York homeowner on Justia advised disabling audio unless you know a consenting participant, including you, will be part of any recorded conversation. A doorbell where you talk to visitors is different from a yard camera picking up neighbors chatting on the sidewalk. We set audio per device during setup and explain the choice; for legal certainty, ask a lawyer.

If we turn off the Neighbors app, can the police still get our Ring footage?

Turning off Neighbors posts is not the whole story. Consumer Reports describes a Ring program called Community Requests that lets police ask users for video, and it can be switched off in the Neighbors settings. Police can also seek footage through legal process that has nothing to do with app settings. Locally, the Southern Ulster Times reported that Town of Lloyd police joined Neighbors and can see what residents post publicly. Consumer Reports also notes that once you share video with police you lose control over what happens to it. We show you each setting so the choice is yours.

How do we make sure Ring's facial recognition stays off?

Ring's facial recognition feature, Familiar Faces, launched in the United States in December 2025 and, according to a 2026 privacy guide, is off by default. Biometric Update reported that it is not offered in Illinois, Texas or Portland, Oregon because of local laws; New York is not on that list, so the option exists here. TechCrunch reported a class action filed in June 2026 claiming the feature stores images of passersby without consent. During setup we open the setting with you, confirm it is off unless you choose otherwise, and show you where to check it after app updates.

What AI Gets Wrong About Ring Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers and national cost guides about Ring and video doorbells, set against what the local record and our field practice say. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost and how-to guides that feed many of those answers; they are useful for a rough idea, but they do not know your transformer, your walls or your county.

"A Ring doorbell installs in fifteen minutes with the screws in the box."

On a newer house with a healthy doorbell circuit and a wood door frame, that can be close to true. The quick answers assume that house. In the Hudson Valley, a large share of homes predate it: Westchester's median home is from 1958, and more than a quarter of the county's homes were built in 1939 or earlier.

Older circuits are the problem. A transformer that rang a bell for decades may not power a video doorbell well, and a chime may need checking or an adapter. A HomeAdvisor review from outside the region captures the result: an owner hired an electrician to hardwire a Ring camera, and it never worked consistently.

On stone or brick, the screws in the box are the wrong fixings anyway. We meter the circuit, check the chime and choose fixings for the wall, which is the difference between fifteen minutes and a doorbell that keeps working.

"Battery Ring cameras are set-and-forget."

Battery cameras avoid wiring, but they are not maintenance-free. They need charging, and how often depends on how busy the spot is, how cold it gets and how strong the Wi-Fi is, because a weak signal makes the radio work harder. A reviewer on Yelp in New York described changing a Ring doorbell battery every month as a real nuisance.

Valley conditions make that worse in some places. Busy driveways, deer and bears tripping motion all night, cold winters, and weak signal at a barn all drain batteries faster. Solar panels help only where winter sun actually reaches them.

We use battery devices where they make sense and say where they do not. Where a bell circuit, an outlet or a network cable is available, a wired device usually saves you a ladder trip every few weeks.

"Ring's professional install is the same as hiring a licensed local installer."

Ring's own install page offers professional installation priced per device and billed separately. One local company's page describes Ring's pro install as a third-party contractor network that varies in licensing and quality. That is not a criticism of any one technician; it means you should ask who is coming.

In New York, installing security systems, which include cameras, for someone else requires a state license, and the MBFAA licensing course page warns of fines of up to ten thousand dollars for unlicensed work. On top of that, counties license electrical work differently, from Westchester's trade license to Putnam's low-voltage technician license.

Whoever you hire, ask for the state license number and who will handle any line-voltage work. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and our quote names the electrician's part where one is needed.

"If the router is in the house, the Wi-Fi will reach the cameras."

The quick answers treat Wi-Fi as a given. On a valley property with a detached garage, a barn, a dock or a gatehouse, it rarely is. Stone walls, brick, foil-backed insulation, metal barn siding and trees all cut the signal, and distance does the rest.

The internet behind the Wi-Fi matters too. Ulster County is still bringing fiber to nearly 1,300 unserved households, businesses and community facilities in remote areas, and a Ring camera on a slow upload sends late alerts and choppy video.

We test the signal at each mounting point and the upload speed of the line, then choose between an extender, a mesh node or a cable to an access point. A camera that is installed but cannot reach the network is worse than no camera, because you think it is watching.

"Your Ring video is private unless you choose to share it."

Several Ring features affect who sees or asks for your video, and some are on by default. A 2026 privacy guide reports that Search Party, which uses outdoor cameras to look for lost dogs, is on by default, while Familiar Faces facial recognition is off by default.

Police requests have changed over the years. Consumer Reports describes Community Requests, which lets police ask users for video and can be switched off in the Neighbors settings. In the valley, the Town of Lloyd's police joined Neighbors and can see what residents post publicly. Ring ended a planned partnership with Flock Safety in February 2026 after a backlash.

None of this means Ring is a bad choice. It means the settings deserve ten minutes at setup. We go through each one with you and leave them as you decide, not as the app shipped.

"There are no rules on where you can put a doorbell camera on your own house."

Outside New York City there is no Landmarks Preservation Commission, so quick answers assume there are no rules at all. The valley has its own. Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews visible exterior work in the city's five districts and has asked owners to paint external lines to match the facade. Newburgh's East End guidelines speak directly about attaching security features without damaging historic fabric.

State law adds limits that apply everywhere. Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to install video surveillance where someone has a reasonable expectation of privacy, and one New York installer advises using privacy masking so exterior cameras do not capture a neighbor's backyard.

Some villages also regulate outdoor lighting, which matters for Floodlight Cams: New Paltz's standards say floodlighting is discouraged and, if used, must be shielded. We check these before we mount.

"Recording sound on your own property is always fine."

Ring cameras and doorbells record audio unless you turn it off, and quick answers often treat that as harmless. New York's eavesdropping law is about conversations, and a camera that records strangers talking on the sidewalk or a neighbor's chat over the fence is not the same as your own doorbell conversation.

A lawyer answering a New York homeowner on Justia advised disabling audio on outdoor and garage cameras unless a consenting participant, including the owner, will be part of any recorded conversation.

We set audio per device: usually on at the doorbell, where you talk to visitors, and often off on yard and driveway cameras. That is a practical default, not legal advice; if you need certainty, ask a lawyer.

Our Installation Process

Every Hudson Valley Ring job follows the same eight steps, whether it is one doorbell on a Rockland split-level or a doorbell, four cameras and an alarm across a Dutchess farm. The order matters most in older houses and on bigger properties, where a skipped check is the usual cause of a device that drops offline a week later.

1. Send the county, the house and the devices

The town and county, the house type, which Ring devices you own or want, and photos of the door, the old button and chime, and the walls where cameras should go.

2. Check power, wiring and Wi-Fi from the photos

We look at the transformer and chime, the age of the house, the router's location and outbuilding distances, and match wired, battery, solar or plug-in models to each spot.

3. Confirm the rules first

Landlord or board approval, homeowners association rules, and for houses in Kingston's districts, Newburgh's East End or a lighting-code village, what the local commission or code expects.

4. Walk the property and mark positions

We walk the visitor's and driver's route, measure Wi-Fi at each mount, and agree heights and angles with you before any hole goes in.

5. Run power and cable, then mount

Mounts go into wood, brick, stone or siding with the right fixings; low-voltage wire or network cable is run where needed; a county-licensed electrician handles any line-voltage connection.

6. Connect to Wi-Fi and the Ring app

Each device is added to your account, named by location, updated, and checked for a steady connection from where it is mounted.

7. Set zones and privacy settings

Motion zones drawn to your paths, privacy zones over neighbors' yards and windows, audio set per device, and Neighbors, Community Requests, Search Party and Familiar Faces set by your choice.

8. Test from the street and hand over

We ring the doorbell, walk the yard, check alerts on every phone in the household, show live view and playback, and leave a written note of what is where.

Step 8 includes one more check on valley houses: we ask you to unplug the router for a minute while we watch, so we both see the devices drop and then reconnect on their own. If something does not come back cleanly, we fix it then, not after the next storm.

What moves the price on a Hudson Valley Ring job

  • The number and type of devices. One battery doorbell is the smallest job; a wired doorbell, several cameras and an alarm base is a larger one, priced device by device.
  • The wall. Wood trim and siding are quickest; brick and fieldstone take masonry fixings and more care, especially on historic facades.
  • The doorbell circuit. A healthy transformer means the doorbell goes straight on. A weak one means an electrician's visit, quoted separately by them or coordinated through us.
  • Distance and Wi-Fi. Cameras on garages, barns, docks or gatehouses may need an extender, a mesh node or a cable run before they connect reliably.
  • Line-voltage work. Floodlight Cams and new outlets need an electrician licensed for that county; that part is named in the quote.
  • Ring's own subscription. Recorded video history and alarm monitoring are Ring plans you pay Ring for directly. They are not part of our installation price.

Hudson Valley Ring Camera Installation FAQ

Can we watch the Ring cameras at our weekend house from our phones back in the city?
+

Yes. Ring cameras and doorbells are watched through the Ring app, so live view and motion alerts work from anywhere your phone has a connection, as long as the house's internet is up. During setup we add every household member to the account with their own login, name each device by location so alerts make sense, and show you how to check live view and the device health screen. For a house that sits empty midweek, we also recommend battery backup on the router so a short outage does not leave you blind.

Do you put Ring cameras inside the house too, or only outdoors?
+

Both. Indoor Ring cameras are useful at a back entry, in a garage, or looking at the main door from inside a weekend house. We avoid bedrooms and bathrooms entirely, both because New York law prohibits recording people in places where they expect privacy and because most families do not want it. Indoor cameras can be set to pause when you are home, and we show you how. Outdoors, we focus on doors, drives and outbuildings.

Do Ring cameras need a monthly subscription to be useful?
+

Without a Ring subscription you can still get motion alerts and watch live view, but recorded video history, which is what lets you go back and see what happened while you were not looking, comes with a Ring plan. Professional monitoring for Ring Alarm is also a Ring plan. Those plans are between you and Ring and are not part of our installation price. We explain what each option gives you so you can decide whether you need it.

How high should a Ring doorbell go beside our front door?
+

Around chest height for an adult is the usual target, so the camera sees faces and the package on the step. Reolink, for comparison, recommends about 48 inches for its doorbells. We adjust for the door: a stone stoop straight off a sidewalk, a deep porch, or a door at the top of steep steps may need a little higher or lower, or a wedge kit to angle the view toward where people actually stand. We check the picture on your phone before we fix it.

I rent an apartment in Poughkeepsie. Can I put a Ring on my door without asking the landlord?
+

If it involves drilling, you need the landlord's written permission first. Without it, the Ring Peephole Cam is often the answer, because Ring says it installs in the existing door viewer without permanent changes to the door, and it has privacy zones to limit what it sees in a shared hallway. A battery doorbell on an adhesive or no-drill mount is another option on some doors. Either way, aim only at your own door and its immediate area, not at neighbors' doors.

We own a two-family in Newburgh. Can we put Ring cameras on the shared entry and the yard?
+

Generally yes, on common areas such as the front entry, the hallway door and the yard, as long as nothing is aimed into places where tenants expect privacy, such as inside their apartments or through their windows. Posting a clear notice that video surveillance is in use is wise, since New York law treats posted notice as a safeguard. Tell your tenants before the cameras go up. In the East End district, keep the exterior placement modest and check whether the city's review applies.

Our neighbor's yard is right beside our drive. How do we keep the camera out of it?
+

We aim the camera at your drive first, then use Ring's privacy zones to black out the neighbor's yard and windows in the picture, and motion zones so their movement does not trigger alerts. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a makes it unlawful to record where someone has a reasonable expectation of privacy, so on tight Westchester and Rockland lots this matters. We check the masked view with you on your phone before we leave.

What is Ring's Search Party, and should we leave it on?
+

Search Party is a Ring feature that uses outdoor cameras to help look for lost dogs reported nearby, and a 2026 privacy guide says it is on by default. Some owners like helping neighbors find pets; others prefer their cameras not take part in anything beyond their own property. It is your choice. During setup we show you where the setting is and leave it on or off as you decide, alongside the other sharing settings.

Bears and deer keep setting off our alerts at night. Can that be cut down?
+

Mostly, yes. The state DEC says black bear populations are increasing, especially in southern New York, and Teatown reported Westchester bear sightings rising from 2 in 2019 to 16 in 2021. Ring cameras can be set to alert on people rather than all motion on supported models, motion zones can exclude the woods line and the trash area, and sensitivity can be lowered. We tune these during setup and suggest checking again after a week of real alerts.

The house came with Ring devices from the previous owner. Can you switch them over to us?
+

Usually. The previous owner needs to remove the devices from their Ring account, or Ring's support team has to release them, before they can be added to yours. Once that is done, we reset each device, add it to your account, check that the doorbell circuit and batteries are healthy, and reset the zones and privacy settings, since the old owner's choices carry nothing useful for you. We also label any extenders or cables they left so you know what they do.

What should we ask any company before letting it install Ring at our house?
+

Ask for its New York State security installer license number, who will do any line-voltage work and whether they hold your county's electrical license, whether it will meter the doorbell transformer before mounting, and whether it tests Wi-Fi at each camera position. Ask too whether it will walk you through the privacy settings rather than leaving the defaults. A clean, neatly finished install with concealed wiring is what local reviewers praise; a vague answer to any of those questions is a warning sign.

Can Ring cameras be set up at a small shop or tasting room as well as at home?
+

Yes, for small spaces with a few doors and a modest number of cameras, such as a back door, a register and a small parking area. We check the internet upload speed first, because several cameras uploading video can crowd out a card reader on a rural line. We usually turn audio recording off where staff and customers talk, never place cameras in restrooms, and recommend a posted surveillance notice. Larger sites are better served by a recorder-based system.
