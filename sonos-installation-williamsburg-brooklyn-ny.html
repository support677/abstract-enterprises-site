The Neighborhood

Why Williamsburg Properties Need Sonos Installation

Williamsburg has been making noise in industrial buildings for a long time, and it has been arguing about it for nearly as long. Wikipedia’s history of the neighbourhood describes how, from the late 1980s through the 1990s, unlicensed performance and music venues ran in abandoned industrial buildings, and one 1993 event at the Old Dutch Mustard Factory drew more than 2,000 people. By the early 2000s the neighbourhood was a centre of indie rock and electroclash, and soul and funk labels such as Daptone grew up here. The warehouses became lofts, the lofts filled with residents, and the clubs that remained became licensed bars, restaurants and venues with neighbours above them.

That last part is the one that shapes our work. Wikipedia’s land-use summary describes North Williamsburg as light industrial and medium-density residential buildings, “as well as some residential structures with commercial space on the ground floors,” high-density residential developments with commercial space along the waterfront, Broadway as mainly stores and offices, and South Williamsburg as largely medium-to-high density residential “with some commercial space on the ground floors.” We checked that against the city’s own lot records. In the city’s PLUTO data for ZIPs 11211 and 11249, 1,675 lots are classed as mixed residential and commercial buildings, and they hold 20,098 of the area’s 51,973 homes. Across Brooklyn the same category holds about one home in five; here it is close to two in five.

The businesses are just as dense. The State Liquor Authority’s open list of active licences shows 286 restaurant and food-and-beverage licences in ZIP 11211 alone, the most of any Brooklyn ZIP, plus 107 in the riverfront ZIP 11249. The Postal Service split 11211 in 2011, citing, in Wikipedia’s account, a large increase in population “and in the number of companies doing business in our area.” Add the 60 additional-bar licences, ten hotel licences and two cabarets across the two ZIPs and you have a neighbourhood where background music is part of the business plan on most commercial blocks.

The friction is on the public record. In April 2011 the Brooklyn Paper reported Community Board 1’s chairman proposing a moratorium on new liquor licences, saying the district had “reached its breaking point” and was “oversaturated” with venues that brought, among other things, “loud music.” The board’s own liquor licence questionnaire now asks applicants whether they will keep openable fronts closed while amplified music plays, whether they will follow a certified sound engineer’s recommendations “including placing speakers on the floor,” and, for outdoor space, it states “No speakers or live music in backyard. No background music.” In 2023 Greenpointers reported residents next to a hotel near McCarren Park telling the board about bass from a rooftop lounge and a basement club; the city’s Department of Environmental Protection had issued a summons for commercial music over the allowable limits.

So this page is about both sides of the floor. For a business, a good Sonos system in Williamsburg is one designed around what the licence and the board will allow, with speakers placed and capped so the room sounds full without the flat upstairs hearing the bass, staff who can run it without being able to break it, and music the business is licensed to play. For a home above a business, it is a system that is considerate in its own right, plus a clear idea of what to ask of the room downstairs. Most of the tools are already in the Sonos app and the Sonos Pro dashboard. They just need to be set by someone who has read the stipulations.

The Licence Map

What the Liquor Licence Record Says About Williamsburg Music

We wanted to know how much of the neighbourhood’s music is played by businesses before writing a page about it, so we went to the open data. The State Liquor Authority publishes every active licence with its ZIP code and type. Grouping Brooklyn by ZIP and counting the two descriptions that cover most sit-down eating and drinking places, “Restaurant” and “Food & Beverage Business,” gives the ranking below.

Williamsburg leads by a wide margin. ZIP 11211 has 286 of these licences; the next, 11215 around Park Slope, has 200, and Brooklyn Heights and Downtown in 11201 have 197. Add 11249 and the two Williamsburg ZIPs hold 393 of the 2,728 such licences listed in Brooklyn, about one in seven, in a small corner of the borough. Each of those rooms either plays music or chooses not to, and most of them have neighbours.

The licence type tells us something about the system before we visit. A restaurant licence usually means one dining room and a bar. An additional-bar licence means a second bar inside a licensed premises, so a back room, a basement or a roof with its own schedule. A hotel licence often means a lobby, a restaurant and a rooftop under one owner. A temporary retail permit is a pop-up that will move. Each gets a different layout and a different set of permissions.

Busy and mixed at once. Licences are only half the picture; the other half is who lives above them. Putting the licence counts beside the share of homes on mixed-use lots for ten of Brooklyn’s busiest restaurant ZIPs shows why Williamsburg needs its own page.

Close up, the density is even plainer. Every licence application comes with a list of the licensed premises nearby, because the State Liquor Authority weighs how many there are. The list filed with one 2019 application on Grand Street is a fair picture of a Williamsburg commercial block.

Two cautions. 11211 reaches into East Williamsburg, so its count includes some of that area, and South Williamsburg is partly in 11206, which we left out. And a licence is not a music system; some licensed places play nothing at all. We use the figures to understand the neighbourhood, not to point at anyone, and the dataset is public if you want to repeat the query.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Williamsburg’s Architecture

Williamsburg’s buildings come from two bursts. PLUTO puts about half of today’s homes in buildings from before 1940, most of them from the decades after the Williamsburg Bridge opened in 1903 and the neighbourhood became, in Wikipedia’s words, the most densely populated in the city. A third are in buildings from 2005 or later. Very little was built in between. Each of the building types below puts a business and a home together in its own way, and that decides how sound gets from one to the other.

Pre-War Storefront Walk-Ups

The four- and five-storey buildings with a shop on the ground floor that line Grand Street, Metropolitan Avenue, Graham Avenue and much of the South Side. PLUTO counts 1,316 pre-1940 mixed-use lots here holding about 7,500 homes. The floor between shop and flat is usually timber joists under an old tin or plaster ceiling, and it carries bass well.

Railroad Flats Over the Shop

Inside those walk-ups, long apartments with rooms in a line from street to rear, sitting directly over the business below. A Sub here goes on a rug, off the outside wall and away from the front room that sits over the bar’s speakers, and the soundbar faces down the length of the flat, not into the floor.

Loft Law Buildings

Former factories and warehouses where people live under the Loft Law, the 1982 state law the city’s Loft Board administers, which the Board says covers buildings in the Greenpoint and Williamsburg business zones. Ground floors are often still commercial: studios, bars, workshops. Concrete or heavy timber floors, exposed ceilings and long, open rooms.

Post-2005 Towers With Retail Bases

The waterfront and inland towers that followed the May 2005 rezoning, from Kent Avenue to the Domino site. PLUTO shows 239 mixed-use lots built since 2005 holding 9,573 homes, up to 52 floors. Concrete slabs, metal-stud partitions, a café or gym under the lowest apartments, and building rules on alterations.

Converted Factories Now Offices

Buildings that went from industry to workplaces, the Refinery at Domino among them, which is now offices inside a landmarked brick shell. Kitchens and lounges get music; meeting rooms and phone booths do not. Ceilings are often open, so cable is planned to be seen and tidy.

Apartment Buildings South of Division Avenue

Medium-to-high density residential buildings of South Williamsburg, many with ground-floor shops on Lee Avenue and Broadway. Families are often large and apartments busy, so we favour simple controls, sleep timers and alarms, and limits that respect the floor below.

On tin and plaster ceilings in old storefronts: many ground-floor shops on the commercial strips still have their original pressed-tin or plaster ceilings on wood joists, with the floor of the apartment above resting on the same joists. A speaker screwed into that ceiling is, for practical purposes, screwed into the neighbour’s floor. It is the reason we read the board’s “speakers on the floor” wording literally in these buildings, and why a cut-in ceiling speaker in an old storefront is a decision for the sound engineer’s report rather than a default.

On new towers: the slabs in post-2005 buildings stop far more airborne sound than a timber floor, so a well-placed home system rarely troubles anyone. What travels is structure-borne energy: a Sub standing on the slab, a speaker fixed to a column. Isolation feet, a rug and placement off the concrete walls do most of the work. Any cut into a ceiling or wall needs the building’s approval first, and we work from the alteration rules the management office gives you.

Business Floor, Home Floor

Keeping a Business’s Music Out of the Flat Upstairs

The single most useful thing we can do for a business with homes above it costs nothing in hardware: deciding where the speakers are fixed. Music reaches an upstairs flat in two ways. Some travels through the air and the gaps in the floor, and the floor stops a fair amount of it. The rest is vibration: a speaker or Sub bolted to the building shakes the structure itself, and the ceiling of the shop is the floor of the flat. The bass in that vibration is what neighbours describe, which is why the complaint in Greenpointers’ 2023 report was “the bass of my head,” and why a reader replying to it wrote that no engineer could guarantee immunity from bass travelling through shared walls.

Why “speakers on the floor” is good advice

Community Board 1 asks licence applicants whether they will follow a certified sound engineer’s recommendations “including placing speakers on the floor of the establishment.” Acoustically, that makes sense. A speaker on a stand or a low wall bracket has no direct connection to the upstairs floor, and it is aimed at the people in the room, so the room can sound full at a lower level. A speaker hung from the ceiling is the opposite on both counts: fixed to the joists and aimed down from the furthest point from the listeners. For Sonos, this usually means Era 100 or Five speakers on stands, shelves or wall mounts, or Era 100 Pro on surface mounts on walls, rather than in-ceiling speakers on an Amp.

Many small speakers, not a few loud ones

In a long Williamsburg storefront, two loud speakers by the bar leave the back tables quiet, so the volume goes up until the back is happy and the front is too loud. Six smaller speakers spread down the room do the same job at a lower level each, which means less energy into the structure and more even sound. It is the same reasoning that sits behind Sonos’s zones feature, which treats several speakers as one semi-permanent group controlled like a single speaker. One slider, one cap, many speakers at a sensible level.

The Sub is optional, and it is the part that travels

A background-music room rarely needs a Sub at all. Where it earns its place, for a dinner room or a bar that wants weight, it goes on isolation feet, off the party wall and off any slab shared with a home, with Sub level set by ear at the busiest hour and the cap set after that. When a hotel owner told Greenpointers he had asked his managers to turn down the bass, he was describing the right lever. We make sure that lever is set once, by the owner, and is not a slider any member of staff can push back up.

Business Sonos Installation in Williamsburg

Williamsburg’s commercial streets each have their own kind of room. In all of them, the system we install is shaped by three documents more than by taste: the liquor licence and its method of operation, any community agreement signed with Community Board 1, and the music licence that lets the business play songs in public at all.

Bedford Avenue

The North Side’s main shopping street, around the L train’s Bedford Avenue station, where large chain stores arrived in 2016 alongside independent shops, cafés and bars. Retail floors want music customers barely notice; cafés want it to change through the day.

Grand Street

One of Williamsburg’s two major commercial strips and the line between North Side and South Side, with its own business improvement district. Restaurants and bars in pre-war walk-ups, flats above, and dozens of licensed premises within a short walk of each other.

Metropolitan Avenue

The other main commercial strip, with bars, restaurants and shops in mixed-use buildings toward the BQE and beyond. Many rooms are long and narrow with a back area, which suits zones and a lower evening cap.

Kent and Wythe Avenues

The waterfront corridor of hotels, music venues and post-2005 towers with retail bases: Brooklyn Bowl at 61 Wythe Avenue, hotel rooftops above, and restaurants at the foot of new residential buildings.

Broadway Under the El

Stores and offices under the J, M and Z trains, plus venues such as Baby’s All Right at 146 Broadway and the event space in the landmarked Williamsburgh Savings Bank at 175 Broadway. Train noise outside tempts rooms to play louder; caps stop that creeping up.

Graham, Lee and Moore

Graham Avenue, the “Avenue of Puerto Rico”; Lee Avenue, the commercial spine of South Williamsburg; and the Moore Street Market, opened in 1941. Small shops with homes above, where a quiet, simple system matters more than a powerful one.

Rules that shape a business Sonos system in Williamsburg

  • Community Board 1 stipulations. The board’s questionnaire asks whether the applicant will have music and of what type, and asks for agreement that French doors, garage doors, retractable roofs and sliding windows stay closed when amplified music plays and close by 11 p.m. Friday and Saturday and 10 p.m. on other days. Applicants agree to have the stipulations “incorporated in the method of operation of their liquor license,” signed and notarised.
  • Outdoor space. The same form’s outdoor stipulations close rear yards and rooftops by 11 p.m. Sunday to Thursday and 1 a.m. Friday and Saturday, and say: “No speakers or live music in backyard. No background music.” Sidewalk cafés under the city’s Dining Out NYC rules may not use musical instruments or amplified sound without a Street Activity Permit.
  • Method of operation. The State Liquor Authority’s meeting agendas show approvals made subject to conditions such as “recorded background music only,” and separate applications when a licensee wants to add live music or a DJ. A sound system that outgrows the method of operation is a licensing problem, not just a noise one.
  • Music licensing. ASCAP’s guidance is that businesses need permission to play recorded music, including their own records and CDs. Sonos Pro includes Sonos Backgrounds, which Sonos describes as commercially licensed, and connects to other commercial services such as Soundtrack Your Brand, Rockbot and Cloud Cover Music. Sonos notes that music used in a fitness class needs an additional public performance licence.
  • The Noise Code. Commercial music is measured inside the homes that receive it under Administrative Code §24-231, with a separate test for the low-frequency bands; after 10 p.m., 311 says bar and restaurant music may not exceed 42 decibels inside nearby homes (as recorded in our silo research). §24-244 also bars using a sound device to attract attention outside or through an opening onto the street.
  • Low-voltage work. Speaker and network cable is low-voltage work under our NYS licence; new outlets, circuits or moved receptacles are for a licensed electrician, and we say so in the quote.

We build the day around the stipulated hours rather than around what staff feel like at 11 p.m.: quieter mornings, a background lunch, a fuller dinner, and a late block with a lower cap that starts before the openable fronts have to close. Who Controls the Music

Owners, Staff and Customers on One Sonos System

A home system has one household. A business system has an owner, managers, a changing cast of staff and a room full of customers with phones, and the most common reason a well-installed bar system ends up too loud is that the wrong person could change it. Sonos already separates these people, if it is set up to.

Owner and manager. The Sonos account sits with the owner, and the owner or a named manager holds the settings: volume limits per product, EQ and Sub level, schedules, and the list of products in the system. On Sonos Pro, the dashboard adds admins you invite and lets you limit employees to the features you choose. We change any password a previous installer or staff member used at handover.

Staff. Sonos describes Guest Access as letting devices on the same Wi-Fi network control playback, volume and grouping without access to administrative features, and it is on by default. That is exactly the right level for a bar tablet: skip a track, move the music to the back room, turn down for a toast, but never above the cap and never into the EQ. The cap is the guard rail; Guest Access is the reason the guard rail stays where it was put.

Customers. Customers should have no way in at all. Guest Wi-Fi for customers runs on a separate network that the speakers are not on, and on Sonos Pro the line-in and AirPlay 2 features can be disabled so nobody can stream from a phone at the bar. A business that also has a customer TV area keeps the soundbar for that screen on the same managed system, so it follows the same caps and schedule.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Williamsburg: Questions Answered

Owners, managers and residents here ask us things we are not asked elsewhere, mostly about licences, staff and the people upstairs. The long answers are below, grouped by who is asking; quick practical points sit in the FAQ near the end of the page.

Licences, stipulations and the community board

Our liquor licence was approved for “recorded background music only.” What does that change about how the Sonos system is designed and set?

It changes almost everything except the brand. Background-only is a common stipulation in New York licence decisions; the State Liquor Authority’s own meeting agendas record approvals “subject to” it, and operators who later want a DJ or live sets have to file a change to their method of operation. So the system is built so that background is what it naturally does: many small speakers spread through the room at low level rather than two big ones near the bar, a Sub left out or kept modest, no DJ inputs or line-in jacks left where anyone can plug in, and per-room volume caps set below the point where conversation has to be raised. We write the caps down with the date we set them. If the room later earns a different method of operation, we revisit the layout rather than just raising the limits.

The community board form asks if we will follow a certified sound engineer’s recommendations. Where does a Sonos installer fit alongside that engineer?

Different jobs, done in order. The engineer measures, models and writes the recommendations: how much the building lets through, what the floor-ceiling needs, where speakers may and may not go, what levels to aim for. We build the audio system to that report, with speakers where it says, mounts and isolation it calls for, and caps it can test against. Where there is no engineer yet, we give you a written layout so the engineer has something specific to review. We do not certify that a room complies with the Noise Code; that measurement belongs to the engineer, and on a violation the city wants measurements, not an installer’s opinion.

Our stipulations bar speakers in the backyard. Does a portable Sonos speaker carried out for a private party count?

Treat it as counting. The Community Board 1 wording is plain: “No speakers or live music in backyard. No background music.” A Move 2 or Roam 2 is a speaker whether it is screwed to a fence or carried out on a tray, and stipulations are written into the method of operation of the licence, which is what an inspector or the SLA reads. If your agreement allows something different for private events, follow its exact words. Otherwise we set the system so the yard has no room in the app at all, and keep portable speakers on their charging bases indoors, which removes the temptation on a busy night.

We are applying to change our method of operation so we can add a DJ night. Should the sound system change before or after the State Liquor Authority decides?

Plan before, install after. Design work costs little and helps the application: a layout, the products, where a DJ would connect and how the rest of the room would be capped while they play. Physical changes that only make sense for the new method, such as a booth input, a bigger Sub or extra speakers, wait until the change is approved, because an SLA agenda can approve a request with new conditions or not at all. Until then the current stipulations apply every night, so the system stays set for background music even if the hardware for the next stage is ready in the stockroom.

Running a room that other people control

Our bartenders keep nudging the bass and the volume up after the manager leaves. Can those settings be locked?

Mostly, yes. In the ordinary Sonos app, volume limits, EQ and Sub level are settings that need account access, while Guest Access, on by default, lets anyone on the same Wi-Fi play, pause, change volume and group rooms without administrative features. So the bar tablet stays on Guest Access and only the owner or manager holds the account. The volume limit then caps what the staff slider can reach. Sonos Pro adds dashboard control of which features each employee can use and remote volume limits for the whole site. What no setting fixes is a bartender with the account password, so we change it at handover.

We play our own records in the shop. Does that get around needing a music licence?

No. ASCAP’s licensing guidance answers the exact question: you need permission to play records or tapes in your establishment. Buying a record gives you the right to listen to it, not to perform it publicly. The narrow exemption in that guidance covers radio and television reception in certain eating, drinking and retail places of limited size, with a limited number of speakers or TVs, no admission charge and no retransmission to another room. A turntable feeding Sonos speakers is outside it. The practical answer is a licence from the performing rights organisations, or a commercially licensed service for everyday background, with the records saved for when the licence covers them.

Our café’s licensed background stream sounds thinner than the playlists we play at home. Is something wrong with the speakers?

Usually not, though it is worth checking. Sonos says its own Sonos Backgrounds service streams at 128 kbps, about 60 MB per hour, which is fine for music under conversation but can sound less open than a high-quality home stream on a quiet afternoon. More often the cause is the room: speakers high on a bare wall, a Sub switched off, or a volume cap set so low that the music sits underneath the room’s own noise. We check placement, Trueplay where the product supports it, and EQ before anyone blames the service. If it still sounds thin, try the other commercial services Sonos Pro connects to and compare at the same level.

Our hotel has a lobby, a restaurant and a rooftop. Can each space keep its own hours and limits from one dashboard?

Yes, that is what the Sonos Pro dashboard is for: Sonos describes monitoring multiple locations and individual products from one place, scheduling music for every opening hour, and setting volume limits remotely. We set each space up as its own room or zone, with the rooftop schedule and limit matched to whatever the licence and any community agreement say about outdoor hours, and the lobby running quieter than the bar. Staff permissions are set per role. The one thing we keep separate is anything a guest can reach: guest Wi-Fi never shares a network with the speakers.

Before you sign a lease

We are about to lease a ground-floor space with apartments above. What should we check about the ceiling before buying any speakers?

Four things, ideally before the lease is signed. What the ceiling is: original tin or plaster on joists, a newer drop ceiling, or a concrete slab, because each carries vibration differently and decides how speakers can be fixed. What sits directly above: a bedroom over the bar area is a different problem from a stair hall. What the certificate of occupancy allows, since CB1’s questionnaire asks for it and asks whether your use fits it. And whether the landlord will let you add isolation or a new ceiling layer if an engineer recommends it. We can look at the space on a setup-only visit and tell you what the audio side would need.

Living over the business

We live above a bar. Can our own Sonos drown out its music at night?

Not the part that bothers most people. What comes up through a floor is mainly bass, and playing your own sound over it does not cancel it; at bedtime you would be adding volume to cover volume. Steady, broadband sound such as fan noise or rain can make the upper part of the bar’s music less noticeable, and a sleep timer will turn it off once you are asleep, but it will not hide a kick drum. Noise-cancelling headphones, Sonos Ace among them, do better on low steady sound. The lasting fix is downstairs: placement, isolation and levels in the bar itself.

Our tenants’ association is meeting the bar downstairs. What technical changes can we reasonably ask for?

Ask for things that can be checked. Speakers off the ceiling that is your floor, or at least on isolating mounts. The Sub moved off the party wall and off the floor slab onto isolation, with its level reduced. More, smaller speakers at lower level instead of a few loud ones. Volume caps on every speaker that staff cannot override. A schedule that lowers the cap at the hour your bedrooms need it, and openable fronts closed on time. One Williamsburg resident on Reddit put the first step simply: ask the owner and the landlord to come up and hear it. If talks stall, the city’s MEND NYC programme offers free mediation between residents and businesses when both sides agree.

A Short Glossary for Williamsburg Rooms

These are the words that come up on almost every Williamsburg business job. Most of them are licensing terms rather than audio terms, which is rather the point of this page.

Method of operation

The description of how a licensed premises runs — hours, music, entertainment, occupancy — that the State Liquor Authority approves and that board stipulations are written into.

Stipulation

A condition the applicant agrees with the community board, such as closing hours for openable fronts or no music outdoors, signed and incorporated in the method of operation.

Additional bar

An SLA licence for a second bar inside an already licensed premises: often a back room, a basement or a roof, and usually its own Sonos room.

Background music

Recorded music at a level below conversation. A common licence condition; on a Sonos system it becomes a cap, a schedule and a speaker layout.

Guest Access

A Sonos setting, on by default, that lets devices on the same Wi-Fi play, pause, change volume and group rooms without administrative features.

Volume limit

A per-product maximum set in the Sonos app. Staff sliders stop there; only someone with account access can move it.

Zone

A semi-permanent Sonos grouping of several speakers that is controlled as one, useful for a dining room with many small speakers.

Sonos Pro

Sonos’s US subscription for businesses: a web dashboard for schedules, volume limits, staff permissions and multiple locations, with commercially licensed music.

Sonos Backgrounds

The commercially licensed music service inside Sonos Pro, which Sonos says streams at 128 kbps and filters explicit content.

PoE+

Power over Ethernet at the higher IEEE 802.3at level; Era 100 Pro is optimised for it and takes power and data on one network cable.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Williamsburg

We could not open Google’s AI Overview from the build system, so none of the claims below is quoted from it. They are seven things said about music in Williamsburg businesses and the flats above them in forums, product marketing, cost guides and local reporting, the sort of line an answer engine tends to repeat, and each is checked against a source in the list at the end.

Claim one: “Any music app works for a business”

Chatbots and forum answers often suggest a café simply play a playlist from a personal streaming account through its speakers. It is the easiest thing to do, and it sounds the same to the customer.

It is not the same in law. ASCAP’s licensing guidance says a business needs permission to play recorded music, including CDs, records and digital files, and Sonos itself says playing music in a business generally requires commercially licensed music. Personal streaming terms are written for personal listening.

In Williamsburg, with hundreds of licensed rooms in two ZIPs, this is one of the first questions we ask. The answer is usually a commercial service, through Sonos Pro or one of the services it connects to, set up in the owner’s name.

Claim two: “In-ceiling speakers are the professional choice for a restaurant”

Cost guides and installer marketing often present ceiling speakers as the clean, professional option for any commercial room, and in an office tower or a standalone building they often are.

In a storefront whose ceiling is the floor of someone’s bedroom, they are the speakers most firmly connected to that bedroom. Community Board 1’s own questionnaire asks applicants whether they will follow a sound engineer’s advice “including placing speakers on the floor of the establishment.”

So the professional choice depends on what is upstairs. Under homes, we start with stand and wall mounts, Era 100 Pro on surface mounts, and isolation, and leave cut-in ceiling speakers to the rooms where an engineer says the ceiling can take them.

Claim three: “Background music can’t cause a noise problem”

“Background music” sounds harmless, and many applicants describe their plans that way to a community board in good faith.

But background is a description of intent, not a setting. The State Liquor Authority’s agendas record approvals conditioned on recorded background music only, which an operator then has to keep to every night, and the city’s commercial music rule tests the low-frequency bands separately, so a quiet-sounding room with a busy Sub can still fail inside the flat above.

We turn the phrase into numbers: a per-room cap, a Sub level, an evening setting and a written record of when they were set. That is what makes “background” something a manager can actually keep to.

Claim four: “Soundproofing the roof fixes a rooftop bar”

When a rooftop or club draws complaints, the usual answer in the news is soundproofing. The hotel owner in Greenpointers’ 2023 report said the business had built soundproof walls and “encapsulated the roof completely.”

Residents still described bass, and a reader comment under the article made the point that no engineer can guarantee immunity from bass travelling through shared walls. Enclosures help most with voices and the upper range; low frequencies get through structure.

The audio side has to do its part too: Sub level, speaker count and placement, caps and schedules. In that same report, the owner said he had asked managers to turn down the bass. That is a setting we lock so it stays down.

Claim five: “Staff can be trusted with the app”

Most staff can, and the Sonos app is easy to learn, which is why so many guides suggest just installing it on the bar tablet and every manager’s phone.

Easy is the problem. With full account access, anyone can change the volume limit, the EQ or the schedule, and on a busy night someone will. Sonos separates this already: Guest Access gives playback, volume and grouping without administrative features, and Sonos Pro lets an owner restrict employees to chosen features.

We set the tablet to the guest level, keep the account with the owner, and leave a one-page sheet so staff know what they can do instead of discovering what they can break.

Claim six: “A hotel can use one speaker group for the whole building”

Simple setups group everything so one tap plays music everywhere, and for a small café that is fine.

A Williamsburg hotel is several businesses in one licence: a lobby, a restaurant, often a bar on the roof with outdoor hours that differ from the indoor ones. Hotel rooftop bars here sit as high as the 22nd and 23rd floors, above a neighbourhood of homes.

We build each space as its own room or zone with its own schedule and cap in the Sonos Pro dashboard, so the rooftop can end at the time its licence or agreement says while the lobby carries on quietly.

Claim seven: “Playing your own music upstairs will cover the bar below”

Residents above a bar are sometimes told, half seriously, to fight fire with fire and play their own music louder.

It does not work on the part that disturbs sleep. What comes up through the floor is mostly bass, and adding your own sound over it raises the total level in your bedroom rather than cancelling anything. Steady broadband sound can mask some of the upper range, and noise-cancelling headphones help with low steady sound.

The lasting fixes are downstairs, and they are specific: speakers off the ceiling, an isolated and lower Sub, caps and schedules. If conversation has stalled, MEND NYC offers free mediation between residents and businesses.

Sonos sells Era 100 Pro in pairs through select partners specialising in professional installation, and AESS is not presented here as a Sonos dealer, so the speakers come from that partner. What the speaker needs from the building is a network cable run and a PoE+ switch port, since it takes power and data on one cable. Our licensed crew runs and tests that cabling, mounts the surface mounts if you want us to, and sets the system up in the app.

We are opening a pop-up for a few months. Can the system be set up so it moves with us?
+

Yes, and it changes the product choice. We favour freestanding speakers, such as Era 100 or Five, on shelves or stands, a soundbar placed on a console at $79 instead of wall-mounted, and no cut-ins at all, so the space is returned as found. The system is registered to your business account, not the address. When you move, a setup-only visit at the next location reconnects it to the new network and resets the limits for the new room.

Will you show our staff how to run the music before you leave?
+

Yes. At the end of the visit we walk the manager and whoever is on shift through what they can and cannot change: how to start and pause, move between rooms, use the agreed evening setting, and what to do if a speaker drops off. The manager gets a one-page sheet with the room names, the caps, the schedule and the account owner, so a new hire does not have to guess.
