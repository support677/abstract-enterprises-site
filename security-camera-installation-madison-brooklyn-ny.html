Why Madison Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Madison is a neighborhood of deep lots and long sight lines, and that is the single biggest thing that shapes a camera plan here. CityNeighborhoods.nyc places it between Midwood, Marine Park and Sheepshead Bay, bounded by Kings Highway to the north, Avenue U to the south and Ocean Avenue to the west, with Nostrand and Gerritsen Avenues on the east. Untapped New York calls it a subsection of Sheepshead Bay and one of the last parts of Brooklyn to be developed, starting in 1877, with tree-lined streets and historic homes. Most of the houses went up between about 1910 and 1930: two-family brick houses, limestone rowhouses and modest single-family homes, with detached brick and stucco houses and their driveways on the interior blocks, and larger apartment buildings along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue. Quentin Road, Avenue R and Avenue S cross the middle of it, and the numbered East streets run north to south between them.

Look at a typical Madison house listing and the camera problem is right there in the numbers. A detached brick house at 1702 East 28th Street, built in 1925, sits on a lot 32 feet wide and 100 feet deep, with a building 19 feet wide and 58 feet long, a private driveway, a two-car garage, a fully finished basement with its own separate entrance and an alarm system. Put those dimensions together and you have a front door roughly 20 feet from the curb, a side door partway down a driveway that runs most of the lot's length, a back yard and a garage that can be 70 or 80 feet from the front wall of the house, and a basement door that a different person may use every day. A house at 2401 Avenue R is described as a fully detached Dutch Colonial on an oversized corner lot, which adds a second street frontage to the same problem. None of these spots can be covered well from one place, and the farther a camera is from what it watches, the smaller a face becomes in the picture.

The apartment buildings bring a different set of distances. The co-ops along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue are mostly six stories and date from the 1920s to the 1950s: 2301 Kings Highway, a 1936 co-op with 96 apartments, carries addresses on Kings Highway, East 23rd Street and East 24th Street, which means one building with entrances or frontage on three streets. 2331 Ocean Avenue, a 1955 co-op, lists indoor parking with a waiting list, a laundry area and security cameras among its features. Lot by Lot

Planning Madison Cameras by Distance: the Curb, the Driveway, the Rear Garage and the Back Fence

Ask most people where a camera should go and they name a wall. We ask a different question first: how far is it from the camera to the thing it has to show, and what does it have to show there? A camera can be asked to detect that someone is in the driveway, to recognize a neighbor or a delivery driver, or to identify a stranger well enough that police can use the picture. Those are different jobs, and on a Madison lot they usually sit at different distances from the house.

The arithmetic behind a usable face

The international video surveillance standard IEC 62676-4 describes these levels in terms of how many pixels of the picture fall across each meter of the scene: roughly 25 pixels per meter to detect a person, 62 to observe what they are doing, 125 to recognize someone you know and 250 to identify a stranger. A common 4-megapixel camera has about 2,560 pixels across its picture. With a wide lens covering around 100 degrees, that picture spreads across about 2.4 times the distance to the subject, so the camera reaches identification level only out to roughly 14 feet, and recognition to roughly 28 feet. With a narrower lens of about 45 degrees, the same camera holds identification level out to roughly 40 feet, but covers a much narrower slice of the yard. Those numbers are approximate, and light, motion and compression all cut into them, but they explain why a single wide camera on the front of a 58-foot house cannot identify someone at a garage 80 feet away. On the walk-through we measure each distance with a laser meter, and the quote names the lens chosen for each one.

The curb and the parked car

Many Madison families park on the street even when they have a driveway, because the driveway is shared, blocked by a second car or used by the tenant. In the January 2026 case near Avenue U and East 26th Street, the theft began with the driver's side window of a parked car. A camera that sees the car has a different job from the camera at the front door. It sits higher, often under the eave or at the corner of the porch roof, aimed along the curb at a shallow angle rather than straight across the street, with a lens chosen for the 20 to 35 feet between a typical Madison front wall and the far side of the parked car. It is set to show a person standing at the driver's door large enough to describe, not to read every plate on the block. Reading plates on moving cars is a separate, narrow job that needs its own camera and is rarely worth it on a residential street.

The driveway and the side door

A private driveway along the side of a detached house is a corridor: anyone heading for the back of the property walks down it, and on many Madison houses the side door, the basement entrance and the meter all open onto it. The best position is usually at the far end looking back toward the street, so a person walking in is facing the camera and getting closer to it with every step. A second, wider camera near the front corner records that someone turned into the driveway at all. On a driveway shared between two houses, which is common on the interior blocks, the cameras are aimed down your side of the shared strip, and we use privacy masking to black out the neighbor's windows before anything is recorded.

The garage at the back of the lot

A detached two-car garage at the back of a 100-foot lot is the hardest spot on a Madison property to cover from the house, and often the most valuable, because it holds the car, the bikes, the tools or a workshop. A camera mounted on the back wall of the house can show the garage door, but at 30 or 40 feet it needs a narrower lens to show a face. The better answer, where the owner agrees, is a camera on the garage itself looking back up the driveway and across the yard. That camera needs power and a connection, which means a cable across the yard. We run it in conduit along a fence line or under a path, never loose on the ground, and where a trench is not possible, a pair of point-to-point wireless bridges between the house and the garage can carry the signal while the camera takes power locally.

The back yard and the rear windows

The 2017 Midwood pattern that CBS New York reported, screens removed and entry through rear windows, is a reminder that the back of a house is where a burglar has the most time and the least chance of being seen from the street. On a Madison house the rear windows often sit above a back porch, a one-story extension or a basement stair. We aim a back camera along the rear wall from one corner, so it records anyone who reaches a window, plus one camera looking down the yard toward the fence or the garage. Where the yards behind a row of houses run together with only low fences between them, we keep each camera's view on your own yard and windows, because a camera that looks into three neighbors' gardens creates its own problems.

Detached brick houses: long runs from one basement

On a detached brick house, the recorder usually lives in the basement next to the internet modem, and every camera cable starts there. The front and driveway cameras are easy: up through the basement ceiling joists to an exterior wall, out through a sealed hole in the brick at the point closest to the camera. The rear and garage cameras are long: the cable runs the full length of the basement, then out the back wall, then across the yard. Brick takes a masonry anchor, not a screw, and every hole through the wall is drilled at a slight downward angle to the outside and sealed so water runs away from the house. Where the basement is finished, as so many in Madison are, we look for the route behind the boiler, along the laundry wall or above a dropped ceiling before we suggest opening any drywall.

Colonials, stucco and frame houses: trim, soffits and attics

A Dutch Colonial or a stucco house takes cameras differently from brick. Stucco cracks if it is drilled carelessly, so we score the surface first, use the right bit and fill the hole with a matching patch around the cable. Wood soffits and porch ceilings are often the best mounting spots on these houses, because the attic or the porch roof behind them makes the cable route simple and the camera sits out of the rain. On a corner house, the second street side usually gets its own camera at the far corner looking back along that street, because a camera at the front corner cannot see the length of a long side wall.

Two-family brick houses: two households, one recorder or two

On a two-family, the first decision is not technical. Will the owner run one recorder for the whole house, with a separate account for the tenant that shows only the tenant's door and the shared areas, or will each household have its own system? One recorder is cheaper and keeps the driveway, the back yard and the curb in one place. Two systems give each household full privacy. We set out both options on the quote. Whichever you choose, the recorder goes in a part of the house the owner controls, on a circuit the owner pays for, so a tenant's power or internet problem does not take the whole system down.

Six-story co-ops: three streets, a cellar and a board

The co-ops along Ocean Avenue and Kings Highway are where Madison camera work looks most like apartment-building work anywhere in Brooklyn: a lobby door, mailboxes, a package area, a laundry room, a back or service door, a cellar and a roof. What is particular here is how many of these buildings face more than one street. 2301 Kings Highway has addresses on three, which can mean a main entrance on the avenue and a service or garage entrance on a side street, each needing its own camera and its own cable route back to one recorder. In a building with indoor parking, like 2331 Ocean Avenue, the garage is usually the longest run and the one most worth putting in conduit. Every camera position in a co-op needs the board's approval, and we prepare the list the board needs to decide.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Madison

License. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance. The New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers for systems that detect intrusion, break-in, movement, sound or fire; ask any camera installer for their license and insurance before work starts. Private areas. No camera views restrooms, changing areas, exam rooms or any spot where people undress, and we will not install one that does. Sound. New York is a one-party consent state for audio, and recording a conversation you are not part of can be eavesdropping under Penal Law 250.05, so we leave microphones off unless the owner has taken advice. Landmarks. We found no historic district covering Madison, but we still check a building's status before any facade work. City property. Street trees, light poles and the sidewalk are not places for a private camera; everything we mount goes on the owner's building, fence or post. Sharing with police. In May 2024 the Mayor's Office announced an NYPD pilot with Fusus by Axon in which businesses can register their cameras, which tells police only where they are, or integrate them for live access on terms the owner sets, at no cost to the business. It began in June 2023 in the 109th Precinct in Flushing with 35 businesses and was set to expand to nine more precincts. "We cannot allow shoplifters and organized crime rings to prey on businesses any longer," Mayor Adams said at the time. Whether or not a store joins, we set up exports so the owner can hand over a clip quickly.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Madison Lot With a Tape and a Second Person

This describes how we approach properties here, not one particular job. On a Madison house we bring a laser distance meter and, where possible, a second person. We start at the curb, where the family car usually sits, and measure back to the front wall. Then we walk the driveway to the side door and the basement entrance, then to the back corner of the house, then across the yard to the garage door and the back fence, measuring each one from every wall a camera could go on. At each spot the second person stands where a stranger would stand while we hold a test camera at the proposed mount and look at the picture on a phone. If the face at the garage is the size of a thumbnail, the plan changes before the quote is written, not after the camera is up.

Then we come back to two questions that are not about distance at all. Who lives where: the owner upstairs, a tenant downstairs, a parent in the basement? And who should see which camera? On the co-op row the same walk happens with the super, from the avenue entrance to the side-street entrance to the garage, the package area and the cellar. Only then do we write the quote, one line per camera, each with its job and its distance next to it: curb, driver's door, 28 feet; side door, face, 12 feet; garage, looking back, 40 feet.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Madison: Questions Answered

Our detached house on an East street sits on a 100-foot lot with the garage at the very back. Can one camera on the back wall of the house identify someone standing at the garage door?

Usually not with a standard wide camera, and that is the most common surprise on Madison jobs. If the back wall of the house is 30 to 40 feet from the garage door, a wide-angle 4-megapixel camera spreads its picture across so much of the yard that a face at the garage covers only a few dozen pixels, enough to see that someone is there but not who. There are three ways to fix it. The first is a narrower lens on the house camera, aimed only at the garage door, which identifies a person there but no longer covers the whole yard, so you add a second wide camera for the yard. The second is a varifocal camera that we zoom on site to the measured distance. The third, and usually the best, is a camera mounted on the garage itself looking back toward the house and the driveway, which puts the face close to the lens at exactly the spot where someone stops to work on the garage lock. On the walk-through we stand a person at the garage door, hold a test camera at each option and show you the picture before you decide.

The January 2026 car break-in near Avenue U and East 26th Street started with a smashed driver's side window. We park at the curb in front of our house. What should a curb camera actually record?

It should record the person at the car, not the whole street. In that case News 12 reported that thieves broke the driver's side window, took a suitcase and left east on Avenue U in a gray minivan. The pieces of that story a home camera could realistically capture are a person standing at the driver's door, what they were wearing, the vehicle they came in and the direction they went. So we mount the curb camera high, under the eave or porch roof, and aim it along the curb at a shallow angle so the driver's side of a car parked in front of your house is in the middle of the frame. We choose the lens for the measured distance to the far side of the car, which on most Madison houses is between 20 and 35 feet, and we set the camera to handle headlights at night without turning the scene black. It will not read the plate of a minivan driving past at speed; that needs a separate, narrow camera with a fast shutter, and on a residential street it rarely pays off. If you park in a different spot each night, aiming along the curb covers a few car lengths either side of your house rather than one exact space.

Our house already has an alarm with a keypad by the front door. Can cameras be added without replacing the alarm, and can a door opening tell the cameras to start recording?

Yes on both counts in most cases. Many Madison houses, like the listing on East 28th Street, already have an alarm, and cameras do not need to replace it. The simplest setup keeps the two systems side by side: the alarm keeps its own panel and monitoring, the cameras record continuously to their own recorder, and both sit on the same battery-backed shelf in the basement with the internet equipment. Where the owner wants more, many recorders accept a signal from an alarm output or a door contact, so that an alarm event marks the recording, sends a camera snapshot to your phone or switches a camera to a higher frame rate. Whether that works on your system depends on the alarm panel's model and outputs, which we check on the walk-through. We do not recommend recording only when a door opens: continuous recording on a properly sized drive means the minutes before an event are on file too, which is often where the useful picture is.

Former tenants at 2306 Ocean Avenue wrote that the building's cameras were "just a decoration." I am a shareholder in a co-op on the avenue. How can the board find out whether our cameras actually record?

Ask for three simple tests, which any working system can pass in a few minutes. First, pick a time from yesterday, for example 6:15 p.m., and ask the super or managing agent to play back every camera at that moment; a camera that shows a live picture but has nothing on the drive for yesterday is not recording. Second, check the date and time stamped on the footage against a phone; a clock that is hours or years off makes clips hard to use. Third, ask how many days back the oldest footage goes, which tells you how long a tenant has to report a lost package before the evidence is overwritten. If the board wants an outside check, we do this as a takeover visit: we find the recorder, confirm who holds the main password, test every camera, trace any cable that goes nowhere and write down what works, what does not and what it would cost to fix. Packages that "get lost all the time," as the Openigloo reviewers put it, are a lobby and package-area problem, and a working camera aimed at the mailboxes and the package shelf is usually the first fix.

Our co-op's main entrance is on Kings Highway, but the garage door and the service entrance open onto the side street. Can one recorder handle entrances on two different streets?

Yes, and in buildings like 2301 Kings Highway, which carries addresses on Kings Highway, East 23rd Street and East 24th Street, it is the normal arrangement. All the cameras come back to one recorder in a locked room, usually in the cellar, which keeps footage, accounts and exports in one place. The work is in the routes: the avenue entrance is often a short run, while the garage and service door on the side street can be the length of the building away and have to travel through the cellar ceiling, along a garage wall in conduit or up and over through a corridor. Where a run is longer than a single network cable can carry, a small switch partway along or a fiber link solves it. Each entrance gets at least two cameras: one close to the door for faces and one wider for the approach, so the recording shows who came in and from which way along the street.

Our two-family shares its driveway with the house next door. Can our camera cover the driveway, and what about the neighbor's side?

You can cover your own part of it and the path to your own doors, and the camera should stay out of your neighbor's windows and yard. Shared driveways are common on Madison's interior blocks, and they are exactly the corridor someone uses to reach the back of either house, so leaving them uncovered is a real gap. We mount the camera on your house, aimed down the driveway toward the street so anyone walking in faces it, and use the camera's privacy masking to black out your neighbor's windows, side door and yard before anything is recorded. If the neighbor wants coverage too, the cleanest answer is a camera on each house, each owned and recorded by its own household, rather than one system that records both properties. New York's law on cameras aimed at a neighbor's backyard lets someone go to court over a camera that is used to annoy or harass them, and a camera that stays on your own property and your own doors avoids that conversation entirely. Showing your neighbor what the camera actually sees, before or after it goes up, settles most worries.

We bought a Dutch Colonial on a corner lot. How does a corner house change the number and placement of cameras?

A corner house has two street sides, which usually means one or two more cameras than the same house in the middle of the block, and a different way of thinking about the yard. On a mid-block house the side is a driveway or a narrow gap, and the back yard is hidden from the street. On a corner, the long side wall faces a sidewalk, the back yard often has a fence along the cross street, and a car or a person can reach the rear of the property without passing the front door at all. We usually put one camera at the front door, one at the far corner of the long side looking back along the cross street, one covering the gate or fence where the back yard meets that street, and the rear and garage cameras the same as on any house. The corner itself is a good spot for a camera that looks along both sidewalks, which shows the direction someone came from and went.

We want a camera on the detached garage, but there is no power or internet out there. What are the options?

There are three, and the right one depends on the yard. The most reliable is a single network cable from the house to the garage, which carries both power and picture to a camera that uses Power over Ethernet. That cable runs inside conduit, either buried along a fence line or under a path, or fastened along the fence itself, never loose on the grass. If the garage already has an electrical circuit, a second option is to keep power local and send the picture back to the house over a pair of small point-to-point wireless bridges, one on the garage and one on the back of the house, aimed at each other across the yard. On a typical Madison lot, with 30 to 80 feet between house and garage and a clear line of sight, that works well. The third option, a battery or Wi-Fi camera on the garage, is the cheapest to install but the least dependable, because the house Wi-Fi often barely reaches the back of the lot through a brick wall and batteries need charging. We will tell you which of the three fits your yard and write the trench, conduit or bridge as its own line on the quote.

Our small synagogue on a Madison side street is empty most weekdays and full on Shabbat, when nobody will check a phone. How should the cameras and alerts be set up?

Plan the system so it does its job without anyone touching it during services. Recording runs continuously, every day, on a drive sized for at least a couple of weeks, so nothing depends on someone arming or starting it. Phone alerts are scheduled for the hours the building should be empty, typically weekday nights, and sent to a board member or caretaker who is not prevented from reading them on the Sabbath, or simply reviewed afterward. Screens that show the cameras can be left on a fixed view so nobody has to operate anything. The camera positions follow the building's use: the main door people use during services, the side and back doors that should stay shut, any area where coats, bags or strollers are left, and the sidewalk outside during arrival and departure. Because small congregations are often run by volunteers, we leave a printed page explaining how to export a clip, and set up separate logins so the rabbi, the president and the caretaker each see what they need.

James Madison High School lets out onto our block every afternoon. Will our driveway and curb cameras send motion alerts all day?

They will if they are left on factory settings, and that is one of the first things we change. Blocks near James Madison High School and other schools see a wave of students on the sidewalk at arrival and dismissal, and a camera that alerts on any motion in its whole frame will buzz your phone dozens of times. Recording is not the problem: the cameras should record everything, all day, because that costs nothing extra on a properly sized drive. Alerts are a separate setting. We draw the alert zone around your own property line, the driveway and the doors, so people walking along the public sidewalk do not trigger it; we use person and vehicle detection rather than raw motion, so shadows and passing cars do not count; and we schedule the most sensitive alerts for the hours your house is empty or everyone is asleep. If you want to know when someone actually turns into the driveway, a line-crossing rule at the driveway mouth does exactly that and nothing else.

Accounts and Privacy

Two Households and a Basement Tenant: Who Watches the Cameras in a Madison House

Madison houses often hold more than one household, and every outside camera has neighbors close by. Settling who approves each camera and who can watch it, in writing, before the work starts, avoids most of the disputes we hear about later.

Two-families and basement apartments

The owner of a two-family decides where the cameras go, but a tenant has a reasonable expectation of privacy inside their own apartment and at their own door. We never put a camera inside a rented unit, and Deep Sentinel's summary of New York law notes that cameras in bathrooms, changing areas and the living quarters of live-in workers can be unlawful surveillance under Penal Law 250.45. Outside, a camera on the shared front path or driveway is normal. A camera aimed squarely at a basement tenant's door is the delicate one: it protects them, but it also records every visitor. We suggest telling the tenant in writing what the camera covers, giving them their own login to the cameras that affect them if they want it, and keeping the owner's view of that door for security reviews rather than daily watching.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Madison

These are claims that turn up again and again in AI-written answers, cost directories and installer listings when people ask about cameras for a Brooklyn house or building. Where a claim comes from a named source, we say so. Each one is followed by what we see on Madison properties.

"Camera installation costs about $100 for one camera and $1,000 to $2,200 for a whole system."

That range comes from Thumbtack's Brooklyn camera installer page, and HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page gives an average of $1,416 with a typical range of $680 to $2,145. Both are fair snapshots of simple jobs, and both say the final price depends on the number and type of cameras. Neither tells you what a Madison lot needs.

A detached house with a curb camera, a driveway camera, a back-yard camera and a camera on a garage 80 feet from the basement is not a simple job. The garage run alone can involve conduit along a fence, a wireless bridge or a short trench, and the varifocal lenses that make faces usable at those distances cost more than the wide cameras in a kit.

Our own Brooklyn page publishes $850 to $1,500 as the typical range for a four-camera home system and $850 to $2,500 for four to eight cameras. Where your lot lands in that range depends on the garage, the yard and the walls, and the written quote shows each of those as its own line.

"Get a 4K camera and you will be able to identify anyone on your property."

Resolution helps, but distance and lens matter more. A 4K camera with a very wide lens still spreads its pixels across a huge scene, and a face 40 feet away at the back of a Madison lot can come out too small to identify. The IEC 62676-4 standard puts identification at about 250 pixels per meter of scene, which a wide camera only reaches close to the lens.

On a deep lot, two moderate cameras, each with a lens matched to its distance, usually beat one expensive wide camera trying to cover everything. Night makes this worse: compression and low light both soften detail, so the margin you have in daylight disappears after dark.

That is why our quotes list a distance next to every camera, and why we test each one with a person standing at the measured spot, in daylight and after dark, before the job is handed over.

"Wireless cameras are the easiest choice for a house."

For one camera at the front door of a small house, a Wi-Fi camera can be fine. For a Madison house with brick walls, a basement router and a garage at the back of the lot, the Wi-Fi signal often fades long before it reaches the yard, and the camera that drops out is the one at the garage, which is the one you most need.

Battery cameras add another problem: they only record when motion wakes them, they miss the seconds before, and someone has to remember to charge them. On a two-family, a Wi-Fi camera also depends on whose router it is joined to, and a tenant changing their internet plan can silently take it offline.

Wired cameras get power and picture over one cable and keep recording when the Wi-Fi is down. Where a cable truly cannot reach, we use a point-to-point bridge for that one spot rather than putting the whole system on Wi-Fi.

"Put a camera on every door and you are covered."

Doors matter, but on Madison properties some of the most important views are not doors at all. The January 2026 case near Avenue U and East 26th Street began at a parked car's window. The 2017 burglaries just north of Kings Highway that CBS New York covered came through rear windows after the burglar walked through yards.

A plan that spends the budget on doors can leave the curb, the driveway corridor and the rear windows unrecorded. The first question on our walk-through is where someone would go on your lot to do harm without being seen from the street, and the answer is usually down the side and around the back.

The front door still gets a camera, and often a doorbell. But on many Madison jobs the driveway, the curb and the garage cameras do more of the work.

"Record only on motion to save storage."

Motion-only recording was a way to stretch small hard drives. Today it mostly means missing the useful seconds: the person approaching the car before the window breaks, the figure in the driveway before the motion sensor wakes up, the car that drops someone off before they walk in.

Drives are now large enough to record a typical four-camera Madison house continuously for two weeks or more, and our Brooklyn page includes two weeks of recording with its systems, with longer storage as a priced upgrade. Continuous recording plus smart alerts gives you the full record and a quiet phone.

We keep alerts on motion and person detection, scheduled for the hours the house is empty, and keep recording on all the time. That combination is what makes a clip available when police or an insurer ask for it.

"Anyone handy can install cameras; no license is needed."

Installing a camera on your own house is your business. But the New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers for systems that detect intrusion, break-in, movement, sound or fire, and Deep Sentinel's summary of New York camera law advises confirming with the Department before installing cameras as part of a security system.

For a co-op board, a landlord or a store, hiring a licensed and insured installer is also about who is accountable when a cable is run through a cellar, a hole is drilled in a facade or a camera falls off a garage. Ask any installer for both the license number and the insurance certificate before work starts.

Madison Security Camera Installation FAQ

Is any part of Madison a historic district that limits cameras on the front of a house?
+

We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district covering Madison. The Wyckoff-Bennett homestead at 1669 East 22nd Street is individually landmarked, and work on its exterior would go through the Commission. For any other building we still check its status before quoting facade work, and on every house we keep cameras small, neatly placed and wired with the cable hidden or in a raceway painted to match.

We travel for several weeks every winter. Can the recorder keep a month or more of footage from the whole house?
+

Yes. A typical four-camera system comes with about two weeks of continuous recording, and our Brooklyn page lists storage upgrades of $180 for one month, $270 for two months, $360 for three months and $450 for four months. More cameras or higher resolution use the drive faster, so we size the storage to your actual cameras and settings and show you the oldest date on the drive at handover.

Should the recorder sit out in the detached garage or inside the house?
+

Inside the house, almost always. The basement next to the internet modem is the usual spot, because it is heated, dry, on battery backup and not the first place a burglar reaches. A recorder in the garage is easy to steal along with whatever else is out there, and it suffers in summer heat and winter cold. The garage gets the camera, and the cable or wireless bridge brings its picture back to the house.

If the power goes out on our block overnight, do the cameras keep recording?
+

For a short outage, yes, if the system has a battery backup unit, which we include on our installs. It keeps the recorder, the switch that powers the cameras and the internet modem running for a period that depends on how many cameras the system has and the size of the unit, which the quote states. For long outages the system shuts down cleanly and restarts by itself when power returns, and we test that restart before we leave.

Can our basement tenant have a login that shows only their own entrance?
+

Yes. Modern recorders let us create separate user accounts that each see only the cameras you choose. A basement tenant can be given the camera at their stairwell and, if you like, the driveway they walk through, while the owner keeps access to everything. The tenant cannot change settings or delete footage, and you can remove their access when they move out.

We just closed on a Madison house that came with cameras and nobody handed over the password. Can you take the system over?
+

Usually, yes. We find the recorder, reset it so the main password belongs to you, update its software where the manufacturer allows, check which cameras actually record, trace any unmarked cable and sync the clock. Then we tell you plainly which parts are worth keeping and which are not. Many inherited systems have one or two dead cameras and a drive that is nearly worn out, and those can be replaced without starting over.

Can a driveway camera read the license plate of a car that pulls in?
+

Yes, at the mouth of your own driveway, with a camera dedicated to that job. It uses a narrow lens aimed at the spot where a car slows to turn in, a fast shutter so the plate is not blurred, and infrared tuned so the reflective plate does not glow white at night. That camera does nothing else well, so it is added alongside the wider driveway camera rather than replacing it. Plates on cars passing at speed on the street are a much harder job and rarely worth it at a house.

Can our co-op board review lobby footage while the super sees only the package room and the service door?
+

Yes. We set up separate accounts on the building's recorder for the managing agent, the super and any board members the board names, each with only the cameras and rights it needs. The super might see the package room and service door live but not export, while the agent can review and export everything. Each account has its own password, so access can be removed when people change.

Is it legal to record the sidewalk and the street in front of our house?
+

Generally yes. Deep Sentinel's summary of New York law notes that there is no specific criminal law against exterior cameras on a home, and the sidewalk and street are public. The limits are about privacy: do not aim a camera into a neighbor's windows or back yard, keep cameras out of places where people expect privacy, and leave audio off unless you have taken advice. We aim front cameras along your own frontage and the curb and mask out windows across the street.

Headlights from cars turning onto our block wash out the front camera at night. What fixes that?
+

Usually a combination of angle and the right camera. A camera aimed straight at the street takes headlights head-on, so we turn it to look along the curb instead. We use cameras with strong wide dynamic range, which balance bright lights and dark shadows in the same picture, and we adjust the night exposure for your porch and car rather than the street. Where a face at the door still comes out dark, a small, warm light by the door does more than a better camera.

Do you need to get inside a co-op apartment to put a camera at the apartment door?
+

Usually only briefly. A door camera or peephole-style camera for an apartment is often powered and connected from inside the unit, so we need access to the doorway and a nearby outlet or a short cable route. Before anything is installed, check the co-op's rules: because the camera sees the shared hallway, most boards want to approve it, and some want it on the building's system rather than a separate one.
