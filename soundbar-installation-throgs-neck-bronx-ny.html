The Neighborhood

Why Throgs Neck Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Most soundbar advice is written for a big living room in a suburban house or a tall apartment block. Throgs Neck is neither. The peninsula runs south-east from the Bruckner Expressway and Baisley Avenue to the point where the East River meets Long Island Sound, with Westchester Creek along its western side, and much of what is built on it is small. When the estates that once covered the neck were broken up in the 1920s, the land went largely to modest row homes and tightly packed bungalow lots. Two of those bungalow colonies, Edgewater Park and Silver Beach, started as summer camps by the water and turned into year-round neighbourhoods; both are now private cooperatives. Around them stand blocks of detached houses and brick two-families along and off East Tremont Avenue, the Throggs Neck Houses public housing development, and a scattering of waterfront condominiums. A soundbar has to suit all of them, and the smallest are the ones that most often call for help.

Start with the bungalows, because they set the tone for the whole job. The Department of City Planning's 2016 study of Edgewater Park describes a co-op of 675 single-family homes on 55 acres, most of them summer cottages put up in the 1920s and winterized during the Depression, on plots only 20 to 30 feet wide and 50 to 70 feet deep. Side yards are often two to five feet, and in places the eaves of one roof meet the next. City Planning calls these “older, light-frame” buildings. Inside, that usually means smaller rooms than the soundbar's box art imagines, walls whose framing was added to or doubled up as each owner extended the house, and ceilings that can be low, sloped or both. A 55-inch TV and a full-width bar can fill the wall of a room like that, and the sofa may sit eight or nine feet from the screen rather than twelve.

Then there is the question of who decides. In Edgewater Park and Silver Beach you own your house outright but lease the ground beneath it from the cooperative, and the co-op's board publishes architectural and construction guidelines for what gets built. A soundbar inside your living room is your own business; anything that changes the outside of the house — a cable through an exterior wall, a speaker on a deck, a TV on a porch — is the kind of thing those guidelines exist for, and the co-op office is the place to ask first. Getting there is its own small project: City Planning describes Edgewater Park's streets as small, narrow and mostly unmapped, often 12 to 15 feet wide with no sidewalks, and Silver Beach sits behind a gate. We plan the arrival with you so the visit starts on time.

The third thing is the water. Edgewater Park's front row of houses sits as close as ten feet from the Sound behind a five-foot concrete bulkhead, and City Planning counted roughly 278 of its homes — just over 40 percent — inside the 1% annual chance floodplain, with base flood elevations it describes as among the highest in the city. The study projects that share could reach three-quarters of the community by the 2050s. A soundbar is not flood equipment, but where it, the subwoofer and the power strip sit decides whether they survive a wet night. In a Throgs Neck house near the shore, the answer is up: on the bracket, on a shelf, never on the floor of a ground-level room, and never routed through a basement that takes water. That is the opposite of the advice we give in parts of the Bronx where a dry basement is the best cable route in the house.

None of this makes a Throgs Neck job difficult. It makes it particular. Small rooms want a bar sized to the screen and a sub set for the room rather than the showroom. Light walls want the right fixing, found by scanning and a pilot hole rather than assumed. Co-op lanes want a time and a place for the van agreed in advance. And homes near the water want the equipment kept high and the cables tidy enough that moving everything before a storm takes minutes, not an afternoon. We build every quote around those four points.

Console Setup, No Holes

The bar placed on the media console or a dresser, plugged into the TV's eARC or ARC socket, nudged clear of the remote sensor and tuned to the room. A sensible first step in a rented apartment, a bedroom, or a cottage whose walls you'd rather not touch yet.

Bracket on the Wall Beneath the Screen

Most of our peninsula bookings are this one: a dedicated bracket an inch or two under the screen, squared to the picture, screwed to timber when a stud falls in the right place and to toggles or masonry anchors when it doesn't.

Bar Carried on the TV's Arm

A TV-attached bracket hangs the bar from the TV mount itself, so bar and screen swing together. Handy in an open cottage room where the TV turns between the sofa and the kitchen table.

Wires Hidden Inside a Hollow Wall

Power and HDMI fished through the cavity with a code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit, fed from the outlet already on that wall. Quoted only after a scan shows a clear stud bay, and only with the homeowner's yes.

Paintable Channel on the Surface

A slim cord cover from the bar to the outlet, ready to paint. The tidy finish on block, brick, a wall with no clear cavity, or a wall in a co-op house you'd rather not open.

Cable Run to a Shelf or Closet

Up to 25 feet of HDMI and power along a skirting, through a partition or round a corner, so the cable box and console can live on a shelf at a sensible height instead of on the floor.

TV and Bar in One Visit

When the screen goes up at the same time, its height, the gap to the bar and the cable path are settled together — the best way to make a small wall look planned rather than crowded.

Sound Settings for a Small Room

eARC or ARC chosen and switched on, CEC enabled so the TV remote runs the bar, lip-sync checked, firmware updated, and the subwoofer and dialogue levels set for a room the size of yours rather than the factory default.

Sub and Rear Speakers, Placed High and Dry

Wireless subwoofer and rears paired, put where there's power, and set off the floor where water is a risk. In a narrow house, placed so their sound stays in the room instead of crossing a two-foot side yard.

Most bookings combine two or three of these. A bracket, a painted channel and a settings pass is the everyday co-op cottage job; a console setup with a universal remote is a common choice for an older relative's front room; a bracket with hidden wiring suits the drywalled extension many houses gained over the years. Walls and fixings in a Throgs Neck house

Light framing in the old cottages. Houses built as summer camps and closed in later were rarely framed like a modern home. Stud spacing can wander, an old exterior wall can end up inside the house after an extension, and a single wall may change from one build-up to another halfway along. We don't assume a sixteen-inch grid. We scan the whole width of the wall, confirm what the scanner shows with one small pilot hole where the bracket will hide it, and fix to the strongest timber we find. Where there isn't timber where the bar needs to sit, a toggle sized for the bracket spreads the load behind the board. Drywall on wood studs carries no surcharge on our wall table.

Panelling and planks. Plenty of cottages still have wood panelling or board walls from an earlier life. Panelling over furring strips is not a fixing in itself; we go through it to the framing or to whatever solid surface lies behind. Where boards are the wall, there may be no cavity at all, so hidden wiring is off the table and a channel or a TV-attached bracket is the tidy answer. We tell you which before anything is drilled.

Plaster in the older detached houses. Some of the houses along and off East Tremont Avenue kept their original plaster over wood lath. Plaster is hard and brittle, so we drill slowly without hammer action and reach the framing where possible. Plaster is a $45 line on our table, because it takes longer and more care.

Masonry. Expect solid brick or block in the brick two-families, in the public-housing towers, and in some ground floors and foundations close to the shore. Our hammer drill sets sleeve or screw anchors into the solid face of each unit rather than the joint between them. With no void to fish through, cables travel in a painted channel instead. On the wall table that's $65 for brick or stone and $75 for concrete or block.

Rebuilt and raised houses. A few homes in the flood zone have been rebuilt or raised under flood-resistant rules since Hurricane Sandy, and City Planning's study sets out how an Edgewater Park home can be lifted so its living space sits above the design flood elevation. New framing behind fresh drywall is the easiest wall on the peninsula to work with; we simply ask before drilling whether the builder ran anything in that wall we should know about.

Compliance points for Throgs Neck businesses

  • The commercial music limit applies to a TV too. The Department of Environmental Protection's Noise Code summary limits music from a business to 42 decibels measured inside a nearby home, and from 10 PM to 7 AM to 7 decibels above the street's ambient level at 15 feet or more, with a separate rule for pervasive bass. On East Tremont Avenue, with flats over most shops, that usually means a bar set for speech and a sub turned low or left out.
  • Watch what sticks out over a walkway. Under section 307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards, objects mounted between 27 and 80 inches above the floor along a circulation route may project no more than 4 inches; a TV with a bar beneath it projects more. Behind a counter is fine; beside a narrow aisle usually means mounting higher.
  • Outlets are for electricians. Our licence covers the signal side — HDMI, the bar, its low-voltage links — and needs no Buildings permit of its own. A new socket behind the screen is a licensed electrician's work, so either the TV goes where power already is or the socket comes first.
  • Insurance certificates for landlords. Commercial leases often require contractors to name the owner on a certificate of insurance. Send us the wording; the certificate is free and usually goes out the same day.
  • The right cable where nobody sees it. Runs hidden in a ceiling void or inside a partition use cable whose jacket is rated for that space, and the light patch lead from the bar's box is kept to the short exposed hop.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Throgs Neck: Questions Answered

These are the questions people on the peninsula ask before they book, grouped by what sits behind them: living inside one of the co-op colonies, the quirks of a house that began as a summer cottage, the water, and the businesses on the avenue. Prices for particular situations are in the FAQ further down. Nothing here is legal advice; where a co-op's guidelines, a lease or a code is involved, that document and whoever wrote it have the last word.

Inside the co-op colonies

We own our Edgewater Park house but lease the land from the co-op — whose say-so does an indoor soundbar job need?

For work that stays inside the four walls, usually yours. City Planning's study of Edgewater Park describes the arrangement plainly: residents own their homes outright and lease the land, divided into shares, from the cooperative, which sets architectural and construction guidelines through a board of stockholders. A bracket on an interior wall, a cord cover and a sound setup don't change the building's footprint, height or appearance, which is what those guidelines are written to control.

The picture changes the moment anything reaches the outside. A cable through an exterior wall to a TV on the deck, a speaker under the eaves, a dish, or a new opening for power is the kind of change a co-op board expects to hear about first. The same goes for Silver Beach, where the house is likewise yours and the ground the cooperative's. If your plan includes anything like that, ask the co-op office before we book, and we'll quote the indoor part separately so it isn't held up.

If you rent your house from its owner, the owner decides about holes, exactly as in any rented home. A short email from them saying the bracket is fine is all we need on the day; until then, a console setup gives you the sound with nothing fixed to the wall.

Our house fronts a footpath, not a street — how does the equipment get from the van to the door?

On foot, and we plan for it. Silver Beach has lanes that are walkways rather than roads — one writer who walked Indian Trail described it as a five-foot-wide strip of asphalt between the cottages and the drop to the beach — and City Planning describes Edgewater Park's streets as narrow, mostly unmapped and often 12 to 15 feet wide, with two surface parking lots serving the community. A work van can't always stop outside, and sometimes it can't get close at all.

So when you book, tell us where a van can legally stand and how far it is to your door. We bring what we need for your wall in one carry: the bracket, the fixings for the wall type we expect, a drill, a scanner, a level, cables and drop cloths, all packed so one technician can walk it in. If a large TV is going up at the same visit, say so when you book, and we plan a second pair of hands for the carry.

Gated colonies add one more step. Silver Beach has a guard booth at its entrance, and visitors are expected to be visiting someone. Give the gate your name and ours for the day, and answer your phone when the technician calls from the entrance; the job starts the moment we're through.

Houses that began as summer cottages

Our converted cottage has a sloped ceiling that follows the roofline — will an Atmos bar's up-firing speakers work under it?

Sometimes, and it depends on which way the slope runs. Up-firing drivers send sound toward the ceiling so it reflects down onto the seats and seems to come from above. Dolby's own placement advice assumes a flat, reflective ceiling. Under a sloped ceiling the reflection lands somewhere else: if the ceiling rises away from the TV toward the back of the room, the bounce tends to carry over and past the sofa; if it slopes down toward the seats, it can land in front of you.

In a cottage room the ceiling may also be low, which shortens the path and often helps, or clad in boards, which reflect well, or finished with acoustic tile in a later renovation, which soaks the effect up. We can't promise height effects from a spec sheet. What we can do is test: with the bar in place we play a height-test clip, sit where you sit, and tell you honestly whether it's working.

If it isn't, the bar still does everything else well, and many have a setting to turn the up-firing channels down. And if you haven't bought yet, a good bar without up-firing drivers can be the better spend under a pitched ceiling; we'll say so when you send us a photo of the room.

The last owner hung the TV almost at the ceiling of our cottage living room — can you bring it down and fit the bar beneath it?

Yes, and in a small room it usually transforms the space. A screen hung near the ceiling of a cottage-sized room forces everyone to look up from a sofa only a few feet away, and a soundbar under it ends up far above ear height, so voices seem to come from the wrong place. Dolby's advice is to keep the bar below the TV and as close to it as practical, and Yamaha's guide suggests aiming for roughly where a seated listener's ears are; in a low, small room both point to bringing the whole arrangement down.

On our Bronx menu the parts are simple. Lifting the TV off its old mount is $85 and unbolting the old bracket from the wall is $45. Re-hanging a 32- to 55-inch TV on drywall at the new height is the Bronx TV-mounting base of $209.99. The soundbar wall mount is $129, plus $84.99 if you need our Sanus bracket. So a typical lower-and-add-a-bar visit is $85 + $45 + $209.99 + $129 + $84.99 = $553.98, before any surcharge for plaster or block.

The old holes stay behind when the bracket comes off. Filling and painting them is a decorator's job rather than ours, but the new TV position often covers most of them, and we plan the height with you so the screen, the bar and any leftover marks line up as neatly as the wall allows.

The cable company's line comes in through the outside wall exactly where the TV should go — can it be moved or worked around?

Worked around, almost always; moved, sometimes. In small houses the point where the TV service enters is often the living-room wall nearest the street or the lane, and the coax or fibre may pop out of a plate right where the screen ought to hang. A bracket can sit beside or above that plate, and a short channel can carry the line to a cable box on a shelf, so nothing looks improvised.

Moving the entry point itself is your provider's job: the line from the pole or the street to the house belongs to them, and so does the equipment that terminates it. If the plate truly has to move, ask them to relocate it before we come, and we'll plan the TV wall around the new position.

What we won't do is bury a provider's live line inside a wall behind the TV, where nobody can reach it when the service needs work. The bar's own HDMI and power can go in the wall; the provider's cable stays where a technician can find it.

Near the water

Damp air comes off the Sound at our end of the street — does that change which bracket and fixings you use?

Indoors, not much. The brackets we fit are powder-coated steel made for living rooms, and a heated, lived-in room by the water is still a living room. The guides that call for corrosion-resistant hardware, sealed cable entries and drip loops near the coast are written for TVs mounted outdoors, on decks and patios, where spray and rain reach the metal directly.

Where damp does matter indoors is in rooms that aren't heated or lived in every day: an enclosed porch, a ground-floor room left closed up, a space below the living floor in a raised house. We'd rather not put a soundbar in any of those. Where the TV is going to be there anyway, we keep the bar and the power strip off outside walls that sweat in winter and leave space behind the bar so air can move.

The simplest protection is ordinary use. A room that's heated and aired stays dry enough for electronics, and a bar that's switched on most evenings keeps its own components a little warmer than the air. If you're closing a house for a long stretch, unplug the bar and the sub rather than leaving them on standby.

On the avenue

Our shop on East Tremont Avenue runs the TV from opening to closing every day — will a home soundbar stand up to that, and what should be set differently?

Most will, provided it is set up for the job rather than for a living room. A soundbar playing speech at a moderate level for twelve hours is not working hard; what shortens its life in a shop is heat and dust building up around it, a bar wedged into a cabinet with no air, or one turned up to full by whoever is nearest the remote. So we mount it in open air under the screen, away from heaters, ovens and the top of a fridge, and set a level that carries to the counter without strain.

A few settings matter more in a shop than at home. Automatic standby can switch the bar off during a quiet spell while the TV keeps playing, so we check how it behaves with your TV and adjust it where the bar allows. Automatic firmware updates can restart a bar in the middle of the afternoon; where the app lets us, we schedule them for closing time. And we lock in the input and volume behaviour so the TV remote, not the bar's own buttons, is the only thing staff need.

One thing to check yourself is the warranty. Some manufacturers word their consumer warranties around household use, so read the terms that came with your bar before buying a second one for the shop. Our own terms are set out in the service agreement, as they are for every job.

From Summer Camp to Year-Round: What a Bungalow Asks of a Soundbar

Edgewater Park and Silver Beach began as places to spend the summer by the water, and their houses still carry that history in their bones. City Planning's account of Edgewater Park traces it from the Adee family's estate, rented to summer campers who pitched tents on the beach around the turn of the twentieth century, through the cottages and bungalows put up in the 1920s and winterized during the Depression, to the cooperative its residents formed in 1988 when they bought the land. Wikipedia's history of the wider neighbourhood tells a parallel story for Silver Beach: a summer colony of bungalows, later adapted for year-round use, on streets named for the trees and flowers of the Hammond estate. A soundbar has to fit into rooms shaped by that history.

  • Small rooms change the maths. When the sofa is eight or nine feet from the screen, a compact or mid-size bar under a 43- to 55-inch TV is usually the better match. A very long bar under a modest screen looks oversized and sends much of its sound past the seats toward the side walls.
  • Bass fills a small room quickly. A subwoofer that sounds thin in a store can be overwhelming in a cottage living room. We set it to the room, place it a little out of the corner, and show you the night or dynamic-range setting.
  • Walls were built and rebuilt. A wall that was the outside of the cottage in 1925 may now sit in the middle of the house, and one wall can change construction halfway along. We scan the whole width and drill one pilot hole before choosing the fixing.
  • Ceilings vary. Low ceilings, sloped ceilings under the roof and board ceilings all change how up-firing Atmos drivers behave. We test height effects in your room rather than promising them from a spec sheet.
  • One wall often does everything. In a small front room the only long wall may also hold the window, the radiator or the door. We plan the TV height and the bar position around those first, then the cable route.
  • Close neighbours, light walls. With side yards of two to five feet, the house next door is very near. The sub goes on an interior wall, not the side wall that faces the neighbour's window.

The good news about a small room is that it is easy to make sound clear in. At the Water's Edge

Keeping the Sound System Above the Water Line

Throgs Neck is surrounded by water on three sides, and parts of it sit low. In Edgewater Park, City Planning found 20 homes in the V zone, where waves of three feet or more are possible in a major storm, 91 in the Coastal A zone and 167 in the A zone — about 278 in all — and estimated that roughly two-thirds of homes could see one to five feet of water above grade in a 1% annual chance flood, the rest six to eleven. After Sandy, residents there reported more than $1 million of damage to buildings, and City Planning noted that a considerable number of homes have basements. A soundbar is a small thing next to that, but a few habits at installation decide whether it survives the next wet night.

  • Nothing on the floor of a ground-level room. The bar goes on the wall; the subwoofer on a stand or a sturdy shelf rather than the floor; the power strip fixed up off the skirting.
  • Boxes on a shelf, not in the bottom of the console. A cable run of up to 25 feet ($99) can bring the cable box, console and streaming box up to a shelf or closet at a sensible height.
  • No routes through a basement that takes water. Elsewhere in the Bronx a dry basement is often the neatest way to move a cable. In a house that floods, we keep the HDMI and power in the walls of the living floor or in a painted channel instead.
  • Leave a way to unplug fast. Leads are dressed so the bar, sub and boxes can be disconnected in a minute or two if a storm is forecast and you want them upstairs.
  • Don't power wet equipment. If water ever reaches an outlet, a power strip or the sub, leave it unplugged and have the electrics checked by an electrician before anything goes back on.

None of this needs special equipment or a bigger bill. It is simply the order we do things in when the house is near the water: plan the height first, then the cable route, then the bar.

Field Notes: Reading a Cottage Wall Before It's Drilled

This describes our standing method for houses like these. It is not an account of any one customer's home.

In a house that started life as a summer cottage, don't trust the wall to be what it looks like. Walk outside first and look at the shape of the house: a roofline that steps, a change in siding or a foundation that changes height usually marks where an extension was added, and the wall you want for the TV may be the old outside wall, now indoors.

Inside, scan the whole width of the wall, not just where the bracket will go. Expect irregular stud spacing, doubled framing where old met new, and panelling or boards over furring. Find the outlet, the window, the radiator and the door, and decide the TV height with the people who'll sit on the sofa, because in a small room it should be lower than they expect. Drill one small pilot hole where the bracket will cover it and read what comes out before choosing the fixing.

Before packing up, ask one question that matters more here than almost anywhere else in the borough: has water ever come into this room? If the answer is yes, move the sub, the power strip and the boxes up before you leave, and show the household how to unplug everything in a minute.

What would a TV and soundbar hung together on drywall come to, with a painted cord cover and the old TV taken off the wall and hauled away?
+

That is $646.98 in total on drywall over wood studs. Plaster adds $45 and block $75; a larger screen raises the TV line.

What does a console setup with one universal remote cost for a parent who struggles with three remotes?
+

A soundbar placed and connected on the console is $79, and programming one universal remote for the TV, the bar and the cable or streaming box is $99, so $178 in all. Nothing is drilled. We also switch on the TV’s HDMI control so the bar follows the TV, and we show whoever will use it the three or four buttons that matter.

What happens if the wall behind the TV turns out to be soft or water-stained from an old leak?
+

We stop and show you before fixing anything. A bracket needs sound material behind it, and damp-damaged board, plaster or framing will not hold one safely. If the damage is only on the surface, a plywood backer board ($85) fixed into sound framing can carry the bracket. If the wall itself needs repair, that is a job for a carpenter or contractor first, and we either use another wall or come back once it is fixed.

The electrical panel is on the only free wall in our small living room — can the TV and soundbar go over or beside it?
+

Not over it, and not in front of it. The National Electrical Code, on which New York City’s electrical code is based, requires clear working space in front of a panel, generally 30 inches wide, 3 feet deep and 6½ feet high, so the panel door can open and an electrician can work safely. A TV or bar in that space would block it. We can usually place the TV to one side with that space kept clear; your electrician has the final say on the clearances.

Will the technician move the console and the sofa if we can’t do it ourselves?
+

Yes, within reason. Shifting a sofa or sliding a media console a few feet to reach the wall is part of the visit, and we put things back where you want them afterwards. We don’t empty heavy cabinets full of glass or china, so please take fragile things off the console beforehand, and anything that needs two people we move with you.

Our lane has no street sign we can rely on and the house number isn’t visible from it — how will the technician find us?
+

Tell us the way in when you book: the entrance or gate to use, where the van can stop, and a landmark on the walk such as a corner or a neighbour’s distinctive fence. A photo of the front of your house helps a lot. The technician calls you from the entrance on the day, and you can meet them at the gate or the lot if that is easier.

Can the soundbar be set up for someone who doesn’t use a smartphone?
+

Yes. Most soundbars play the TV’s sound over HDMI without any app at all, and with HDMI control switched on the ordinary TV remote turns the bar on, off, up and down. An app is only needed for extras such as streaming music, room calibration or some firmware updates. We can do those on the day and leave the bar working purely from the TV remote, with a short written note of what was set.

Can the soundbar turn itself down automatically late at night?
+

Few bars have a timed volume schedule, so we use what they do have. Night mode or dynamic-range compression keeps quiet speech audible while trimming loud explosions and bass, and on many bars it stays on until you switch it off. Some TVs and bars also offer an automatic volume leveller. We set the one that suits your household and show you the single button that turns it on in the evening.
