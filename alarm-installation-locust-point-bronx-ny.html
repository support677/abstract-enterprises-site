The Neighborhood

Why Locust Point Properties Need Alarm Systems

Wikipedia describes Locust Point as a small peninsular neighborhood in the southeastern Bronx, bounded by Harding Avenue on the north, the Throgs Neck Bridge on the east and Eastchester Bay, inside Bronx Community District 10 and ZIP code 10465. Its infobox, citing City-Data, puts the area at 0.416 square miles and the 2011 population at 1,479. The housing it names is single-family houses. A rental site’s Locust Point listing, Openigloo, groups addresses on Tierney Place, Giegerich Place, Mullan Place, Eger Place, Casler Place, Locust Point Drive, Pennyfield Avenue, Chaffee Avenue and Schuyler Terrace; some of those streets run beyond the point itself, and neighborhood lines here are informal, so we treat the list as a guide to what callers mean rather than a boundary.

The land itself is unusual. Wikipedia says the point used to be an island, first called Wright’s Island after a Captain J. T. Wright who owned it, then Locust Island, and that the waterway separating it from the mainland was filled in and built on. Part of that fill carries the bridge’s northern ramp and toll plaza. CityNeighborhoods.NYC, a less formal source, says the streets end in cul-de-sacs at seawalls or small inlets and that small bungalows replaced farms when the land was subdivided in the 1920s and 1930s. What a visitor sees today matches that picture: short residential streets, water at the end of most of them, and houses whose back yards meet a seawall or a neighbor’s yard rather than an alley.

For an alarm, three local facts matter more than anything else. The first is access. When the Locust Point Civic Association elected a new president in December 2011, he told the Bronx Times the neighborhood had low congestion and plenty of parking but only one access road, and called it “a little bit of the country in the big city.” In 2018 the association’s next president told the same paper that drivers cutting through the expressway service road sometimes block Locust Point Road, the only access point to the neighborhood. Everyone who might answer an alarm comes in that way. So does everyone who leaves.

The second is water. Superstorm Sandy hit the point hard. A year later the Bronx Times listed Locust Point among the hardest-hit waterfront neighborhoods: a Tierney Place homeowner who had lived in his house about sixty years was still fixing a roof the wind and rain had damaged and still arguing with his insurer; a neighbor had rebuilt his private dock with about $90,000 of his own money and said he would not do it again; and several properties by the water had gone up for sale. The civic house on Tierney Place took two feet of water inside, according to the Bronx Times, and needed a near-complete rebuild. The lowest floor of a Locust Point house is the one an alarm should expect to lose first.

The third is what sits outside the house. Docks, decks, sheds, boats on trailers and boats in slips are part of ordinary life here, and they draw a different kind of thief from the one who wants a television. In March 2015 the Bronx Times reported nineteen boat break-ins at the Locust Point Yacht Club in a single weekend, with most boats laid up for winter and their cabins locked; club members said they could not remember anything like it, and the NYPD offered a $1,000 reward. An alarm cannot be wired into every boat on the bay, but it can be designed so the shore side of a waterfront lot, the shed, the cradle, the gate to the dock, reports something when nobody is around to see it.

None of this makes Locust Point a dangerous place. The patrol precinct, the 45th, reported 108 burglaries across its whole territory in 2022, and crime there fell 67 percent from 1990 to 2022, according to Wikipedia’s Throggs Neck article. The point is quiet, and quiet is exactly why it needs an alarm that does its own noticing. On a dead-end street with no through traffic, the person who would see a stranger on your deck at 3 a.m. is usually you, and you may be asleep, away, or out on the Sound.

Our Approach

One Road In, Water on Three Sides: How That Changes an Alarm

A standard alarm plan for a city house starts at the front door and works back. On Locust Point that order is upside down. The front of most houses faces a short, quiet street that only neighbors drive down. The back faces a yard that ends at a seawall, a beach or the water, and that is where the big glass is: sliders onto a deck, picture windows for the view, the door from a walkout basement. A house like the two-bedroom on Tierney Place that was listed with water views, a deck and a finished walkout basement has more openings facing the bay than facing the street. We start the sensor list on the water side.

Divide the house by height as well as by room. The panel, the cellular communicator, the backup battery and the main keypad go on the main floor or higher, never in the basement, because those are the parts that cost money and keep the system talking. The lowest floor gets inexpensive wireless contacts and a water sensor, set up as their own area so that if one floods and reports a fault, the rest of the house can still be armed that night. The City Planning Department’s 2017 flood-resiliency briefing for the Throggs Neck Homeowners Association says new and substantially improved buildings in the floodplain must put mechanical systems above the design flood elevation; an alarm is not a mechanical system in that sense, but the same logic is cheap to follow and saves a panel.

Treat the yard as a zone, not as empty space. On a waterfront lot the things worth stealing are often outside: an outboard motor in the shed, a kayak rack, tools in a detached garage, a boat on a trailer in the driveway over winter. Wireless contacts on shed and garage doors, a vibration sensor where it makes sense, and one outdoor camera that sees the gate to the water can all report to the same panel in the house, on their own area, so you can arm the yard while you sleep and still walk to the kitchen.

Plan the response around the one road. If an alarm trips, the call list matters more than the siren. On a dead-end peninsula a neighbor three doors down can look out a window in thirty seconds; a patrol car has to come from the precinct house on Barkley Avenue and then down the same road everyone uses. We set the monitoring call order with you: who lives on the point and can see your house, who has a key, and when the station should go straight to police. Video verification, a camera clip attached to the alarm signal, lets the station or you confirm what tripped before anyone is sent down a road that may be blocked by bridge traffic.

Expect wind, salt and weather on the water side. Doors on the bay side take wind that street-side doors never see, and outdoor devices take salt. We use contacts that tolerate a small gap, mount them on the frame side that stays put, keep sirens indoors or under cover, and use stainless fixings outside. None of it is exotic; it is the difference between a system that works for ten years and one that starts sending wind alarms after the first nor’easter.

Storm Readiness

Before, During and After a Storm: What a Locust Point Alarm Should Do

Sandy was not the only storm the point has seen, and it will not be the last. The Bronx Times reported in 2013 that a 13-foot surge battered the borough’s shorelines and that the civic association had asked for seawalls in the city’s resiliency plan. No alarm stops water. What a well-planned one does is keep watching the house through the storm, tell you what it can while the power and roads are out, and come back quickly afterwards. Here is how we set that up, as a matter of AESS practice.

Know your evacuation zone. The same 2013 Bronx Times article noted that areas once labeled Zone A were relabeled Zone 1 on the city’s new hurricane evacuation maps, and that Community Board 10 had surveyed city-owned seawalls to see which needed upgrading. Look up your own address on the city’s current map before the season starts. If your house is in a zone that may be told to leave, the alarm plan should assume an empty house during the worst of the storm: armed, with a call list of people who are not on the point that night, and with water and power alerts going to phones that will still be charged. Owners who plan to stay should still arm the lowest floor and the yard and keep the rest on night mode, so a door blown open in the dark is reported rather than discovered in the morning.

Before. When a storm is forecast, the useful checks take five minutes: the keypad shows no battery trouble; the app connects; the lowest-floor area is the only thing you would bypass if water comes in; and anything stored on the lowest floor is moved away from sensors and the water sensor so it cannot be knocked over. Owners who leave the point for a storm should arm the system as usual and make sure the first person on the call list is not also leaving. The station can be told you are away.

During. If the power goes, the panel switches to its backup battery and the cellular communicator keeps reporting, so you get a power-loss alert and, later, a power-restored alert. Water on the lowest floor produces a water alert to your phone. If a lower-floor door sensor fails in the water, the panel reports a fault on that one area; the rest of the house stays armed. A siren that is set inside the fifteen-minute limit will not run all night if wind rattles a door you forgot to latch.

After. The app tells you which devices are missing or in trouble before you get back to the house. When roads open, we replace salt-soaked devices rather than dry them, pair new ones to the existing zones and test every one. If the panel was above the water line, as it should be, that is a short visit at the service rate, not a new system. Every system we install comes with a zone sheet that lists each device and where it is, which is also the list you will want for an insurance claim.

Example call order after a trip, and the alerts that only ever go to phones. Your list and order are set with you when the system is programmed.

Building Specialists

Alarm Systems for Locust Point’s Architecture

Wikipedia calls Locust Point a neighborhood of single-family houses, and CityNeighborhoods describes bungalows near the shore that replaced farms in the 1920s and 1930s. Listings on Tierney and Hatting Places describe renovated and enlarged houses, one with a finished walkout basement. The types below are the ones we plan for; your house may be a mix of two.

Early bungalows, enlarged

Small frame houses from the interwar subdivision, often raised, extended at the back or given a second story since. The original part and the addition have different walls, windows and sometimes different entrances, so the sensor plan follows the joins.

Waterfront houses with walkout lower floors

Houses that step down toward the bay, with a lower level that opens straight onto the yard. That door is the most private entrance in the house and the first to take water; it gets a replaceable contact on its own area.

Houses on the seawall

Yards that end at a bulkhead or seawall, sometimes with a dock or stairs to a beach. The edge of the property is open water, so the boundary of the alarm is the house and its outbuildings, never the shoreline.

Street-end houses

The last house on a short street before the water, with neighbors on one side only. These need full side and rear coverage because nobody else overlooks them.

Houses with detached garages and sheds

Garages and sheds that hold tools, motors and boat gear, sometimes sixty feet from the panel. Range is tested before a device is promised.

Small community buildings

The civic house and clubhouse type: one-story buildings with a hall, a kitchen and storage, used for meetings, parties and elections, empty most nights.

Materials and methods on the water side

Frame walls and radio. Most houses on the point are frame construction, which is kind to wireless sensors; signal passes through wood studs and drywall easily. The exceptions are foil-faced insulation in renovated walls, metal siding on sheds and the masonry of a raised foundation, all of which can block a signal that looked fine on a spec sheet. We test signal at every proposed spot with the panel in its final place before quoting a device.

Settled frames. On filled ground and in houses that have been raised or extended, door frames move with the seasons. A contact that sits a quarter-inch off in August can sit half an inch off in February. We choose wide-gap contacts for doors that move and mount the magnet on the part that stays fixed, so the door still reads closed after a cold snap.

Sliders, screens and storm panels. A deck slider often has a screen panel inside or out and, in winter, a storm panel. The sensor goes on the glass door itself, at the top track where a foot will not kick it, and the screen is left alone. If you add a security bar in the track, we place the contact so the bar does not hold the magnet away and read as open.

Outdoors. Outdoor devices use sealed housings and stainless fixings; anything that can live indoors does. Sirens go inside or under a deep eave. Cable, where we use it, is run inside the house and above the lowest floor wherever possible, and any run that must go low is in a sealed box with a drip loop so water does not follow the wire.

A house on the point that adds an alarm often adds a video doorbell or smart lock on the side door at the same time. See our Locust Point access control page if keys and codes for family, cleaners and contractors are part of the plan.

Commercial Alarm Systems

Business Alarm Systems in Locust Point

Locust Point has almost no storefronts. Its non-residential buildings are the kind a waterfront neighborhood builds for itself, and the businesses that serve it sit just off the point. Each has its own alarm problem.

The civic house on Tierney Place. The Locust Point Civic Association leases it from the city; the Bronx Times reported in January 2014 that the Department of Citywide Administrative Services owns the building, that engineers used it while building the Throgs Neck Bridge, and that it serves as a meeting place and polling site with a $250,000 playground beside it. A 2009 article in the same paper gives the clubhouse address on Tierney Avenue and says the Board of Elections and the Census Bureau have used it. Sandy put two feet of water inside, and a NY Rising committee later proposed fortifying the civic center, according to a 2015 Bronx Times report. An alarm here has to allow many keyholders, a polling day, and the possibility of another flood, and it must respect that the city owns the walls.

Yacht clubs and marinas. The Locust Point Yacht Club occupies the outermost block before the bay, per Wikipedia; the 2015 Bronx Times report gives its address as 21 Longstreet Avenue and says Hammond Cove Marina on Reynolds Avenue also reported boat break-ins and vandalism, to a lesser extent, and the owner of a nearby family-owned restaurant said it had seen the same kind of trouble. Club buildings are busy in season and nearly empty when the boats are hauled, exactly when the 2015 break-ins happened. The building alarm protects the clubhouse, office and storage rooms; cameras and lighting do most of the work on the docks.

Home-based businesses. Contractors, tradespeople and small offices run from houses on the point keep tools, files and stock in garages, basements and sheds. A separate area for the work space lets employees or clients come and go without the family’s code, and lets the business side report to a different phone.

East Tremont Avenue and the Throggs Neck strip. The shops residents use, and the Throggs Neck post office at 3630 East Tremont Avenue, sit on the avenue north of the point. Storefront alarms there are a different job from anything on the point itself; the Throggs Neck page covers them.

Rules a Locust Point business or club alarm has to respect

  • Fifteen-minute siren limit. NYC Administrative Code §24-238 says a building’s audible burglar alarm must be capable of terminating, and must terminate, within fifteen minutes of activation. On a quiet dead-end street a siren that runs longer is the first thing neighbors complain about; we set the cutoff inside the limit.
  • State-licensed installer. Alarm installers in New York need a Security or Fire Alarm Installer license from the Department of State under General Business Law Article 6-D. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Fire alarm systems are a separate process. A clubhouse or hall with a building fire alarm system answers to the FDNY; we do not tie burglary devices into it. Monitored smoke and carbon monoxide detectors on a burglary panel supplement required detectors; they do not replace them.
  • City-owned buildings. Where the city owns the building, as with the civic house, any hardware on the structure should be cleared with the lessee’s agreement and the owning agency first. We quote in a way that keeps everything removable.
  • Detectors in rented houses. HPD requires carbon monoxide detectors in one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied and have fuel-burning appliances, and smoke detectors in multiple dwellings; if part of your house is rented, the required detectors come first.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Locust Point: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from Locust Point owners, boaters and the people who run the point’s clubs and civic house, grouped by what they are really about. Each is answered here in full, and none is repeated in the FAQ further down.

The one road on and off the point

Locust Point has a single road in and out. If our alarm goes off at night, how long before anyone can actually reach the house, and what should happen in those minutes?

Nobody can promise a police arrival time, and we will not quote one. What we can say is what the geography does. Everyone who responds, a patrol car from the 45th Precinct, a relative who lives in Throggs Neck, or us, has to come down the same road, and the civic association’s president told the Bronx Times in 2018 that traffic cutting through from the expressway service road sometimes blocks it. The minutes between the trip and the first person arriving are the minutes the system has to do its own work.

So we design those minutes. The monitoring station receives the signal and, if you choose video verification, a short clip from the camera that covers the zone that tripped. It calls the first person on your list, ideally someone on the point who can see your house from a window, then you, then the police if nobody cancels with your code word. Inside, a siren tells an intruder the house is awake; outside, a light or a camera with its own deterrent light tells them they have been seen. Most burglars do not wait to find out how long the road is.

Most of the point knows each other through the civic association. Is it wise to put a neighbor two doors down on our alarm call list, and what can they actually be asked to do?

It is usually the most useful entry on the list, as long as everyone is clear about the job. A neighbor on the call list is asked to look, not to go in. A good neighbor contact can tell the station in under a minute whether there is a car in your driveway that should not be there, whether the deck lights are on, or whether a shed door is swinging in the wind. That one sentence often decides whether police are sent at all.

We set it up with a few rules. Neighbors get a cancel code word but not your arming code, unless you want them to have a key and let in a repair crew. They are listed after you if you are usually home and before you if you are often away. And we tell everyone, in writing, that nobody should walk into a house with an alarm sounding; that is what the police dispatch is for. If two households agree to watch for each other, each system can list the other as a contact at no extra cost.

We are often out on the Sound for the day with patchy phone signal. Will the app hold our alarm alerts until we are back in range, and who should get them while we are out?

Push notifications and text alerts are delivered when your phone reconnects, so you will see what happened once you are back within signal. What you lose is time. A notification that arrives two hours late cannot help anyone decide whether to send police to your house, which is why an owner who is often on the water should not be the first contact.

For boating households we put someone ashore at the top of the list for the hours you are usually out, and you second. The app shows every arming, disarming and alarm event with the time and the user, so when you dock you can see whether the neighbor checked, whether the station called, and whether anything was opened. If you want, the system can arm itself on a schedule on summer weekend mornings, so leaving for the marina in a hurry does not leave the house open.

The water side of the house

Our house is the last one on the street before the seawall, with neighbors on one side only. Which side is a burglar most likely to come from?

From the side nobody overlooks, which on a street-end lot is the water side and the open side yard. On a normal block the houses on either side and across the street provide a lot of free watching. A street-end house loses half of that: the yard runs to the seawall, the side yard faces the water or a street end with no houses, and the only people who might glance over are on boats.

That changes the order of the sensor list. The front door still gets a contact, but the first money goes to the bay-facing slider, the rear and side doors, every ground-floor window on the open side, and a motion detector inside that covers the route from those openings to the rest of the house. An outdoor camera covering the side yard and the stairs to the seawall is worth more here than one aimed at the street, which your neighbors already see.

A generic street-end lot seen from above, with the usual order in which openings get sensors when the budget comes in stages. Not a real address.

Our deck slider has a sliding screen and, in winter, a storm panel. Which of the three gets the sensor?

The glass door. The screen is not a barrier and a contact on it would only tell you when someone wanted fresh air. A storm panel is a seasonal layer that comes and goes, so a sensor on it would be missing half the year. The sliding glass panel is the one that has to move for anyone to get in, so that is where the contact goes, normally at the top of the frame where feet, pets and furniture do not reach it.

Two details matter on a slider facing the bay. If you keep a bar or a dowel in the track, we place the contact so the door reads closed with the bar in and still reports if the bar is lifted and the door forced. And because these doors take wind, we use a contact with a wide gap tolerance so a door that rattles in its frame during a blow does not flicker between open and closed. For the glass itself, a glass-break detector inside the room covers the slider and the windows beside it at once.

When a surge or a heavy storm puts water in our walkout basement, the sensor on that door may fail. Can the rest of the house still be armed that night?

Yes, if the system is laid out for it, which is why we put the lowest floor in its own area. A panel will normally refuse to arm, or warn you, when a zone reports a fault. If the basement door contact is the only device on that floor and sits on its own area, you can bypass that one area from the keypad or the app and arm the rest of the house as usual. The station sees the bypass, so nobody is surprised.

We also choose devices for that floor knowing they may be lost. A wireless contact on a walkout door costs little to replace; a hardwired one with its cable running back through a flooded wall costs a wall. After a flood we replace any device that sat in salt water rather than drying it, and the panel upstairs, which never got wet, keeps its programming, so the repair is a short visit rather than a new system.

High tide plus a strong easterly sometimes leaves a puddle at our basement door without any real flooding. Will a water sensor keep crying wolf?

It will if it is placed on the floor right inside the door, where a puddle from a wet doormat or wind-driven rain collects. Water sensors trip on contact; they do not know the difference between a quarter-inch of rainwater and the start of a surge. Placement is the fix. We put the first sensor where water would mean a real problem, beside the boiler or water heater, in a low corner, or in a sump if there is one, and we ask you to show us where water has stood before.

We also set water alerts to reach you by text or app, not to send anyone. A flood alert is information: it tells you to check the basement, move things upstairs or call a plumber. If you want a second level, a sensor mounted a few inches up the wall can be set as a higher-priority alert that tells you the water is rising rather than sitting, which is the moment most owners want a phone call rather than a text.

Weather, ground and lost equipment

After Sandy a senator watched a whole dock float past a Locust Point house. If a storm carries off a sensor on our dock gate or shed, will the system even notice?

Yes. Wireless sensors from the brands we install check in with the panel at regular intervals, and if a device stops reporting, the panel flags it as missing or in trouble. That is called supervision, and it is the reason a sensor washed away with a dock gate does not just go quiet. You see the trouble on the keypad and in the app, with the zone name, so you know exactly which device is gone.

The account of the dock comes from the Bronx Times’ November 2012 round-up of Sandy stories, in which Sen. Jeff Klein described seeing a whole dock break loose from its supports and drift past a neighbor’s home. A storm like that will take outdoor devices with it no matter how they are mounted. What we can control is that the loss shows up, that the rest of the system keeps working, and that a replacement is a matter of pairing a new sensor to an existing zone rather than reprogramming the house.

Our house stands on land that was once the channel between Locust Island and the mainland. Does filled ground change anything about how an alarm is installed?

Not the panel or the sensors themselves, but it can change how doors behave, and door contacts depend on doors. Wikipedia says the channel that made the point an island was filled and built on; houses on filled ground and houses that have been raised or extended often have frames that shift a little with the seasons. A door that closes tight in summer can hang slightly open at the top in winter, and a contact set to a tight gap will read that as open.

During the walkthrough we close every door we plan to sensor and look at how it meets the frame. Doors that already sit unevenly get a contact with a wider gap tolerance and a magnet on the side that moves least. If a door does not latch properly, we tell you; no alarm fixes a door that a push opens, and it is cheaper to have a carpenter plane it before we mount anything than after.

Our boat sits on a trailer in the driveway all winter under a cover. Can the house alarm tell us if someone climbs aboard or starts taking it apart?

Sometimes, with limits worth knowing. A boat on a trailer within radio range of the panel can carry a battery-powered vibration or tilt sensor inside the cover or on the cabin hatch, reporting to the house as an outbuildings zone. In the cold, batteries in those devices drain faster, and a cover flapping in a gale can shake a sensitive vibration sensor, so we set it on the low side and test it on a windy day before relying on it.

The more dependable tools for a boat in the driveway are an outdoor camera with a clear view of it and a motion-activated light, both of which you can check from bed. We do not wire alarms into a boat’s own electrical system, and we say so plainly; the boat’s builder or a marine electrician is the right person for that. The 2015 yacht club break-ins happened while boats were laid up, which is the season a driveway boat is most exposed.

Clubs and community buildings on the point

On Election Day the poll site in our civic house opens before dawn. How do we let poll workers in without handing out the master code?

Give the site its own temporary user code, set to work only on that day and only during the hours the poll site is open. Most panels and apps we install let you create a code with a schedule and an expiry. The Board of Elections’ coordinator gets the code for the day, it disarms only the areas the poll site uses, and it stops working that night. The board’s officers keep their own codes, and the event log shows exactly when the temporary code was used.

The same approach suits a party rental, a contractor fixing the kitchen or a census worker using a room. The point is that a building with many users should never run on one shared code that has been passed around for years. When the association changes officers, codes can be removed from a phone in a minute, without anyone visiting the building.

Nineteen boats at the yacht club were broken into over one weekend while they were laid up for winter. Can every member see alerts for the clubhouse, or only the officers?

You can choose, and a club should choose deliberately. Alarm apps allow many users with different permissions. A typical club setup gives full control to two or three officers, alert-only access to a house committee, and nothing to the general membership, because an alert that goes to two hundred phones is an alert nobody acts on. The station still calls a short, ordered list of named people.

What members can usefully get is a camera view of the docks or the yard, which many clubs share more widely than the alarm itself. The Bronx Times reported in April 2015 that police reviewed the club’s surveillance cameras after the March break-ins and that the NYPD offered a $1,000 reward; it did not report whether any alarm or alert had gone off. For the clubhouse building itself, contacts on the doors, a motion detector in the hall and storage room, and a siren inside the building are the basic plan; the docks are a lighting and camera job.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Systems in Locust Point

We could not open Google’s AI Overview for these searches while writing this page, so nothing below is quoted from it. The claims are ones Locust Point owners repeat to us after looking things up in chat answers, on cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and on product pages. Each is followed by what we see on the point.

“An alarm costs about $750 installed”

HomeAdvisor gives a national average of $747 for an alarm system, with a range from about $55 to $2,500. That is a fair description of a typical American house with a handful of sensors, and the bottom of the range is a do-it-yourself kit.

A waterfront house on the point usually needs more than a typical house: a slider and view windows on the bay side, a walkout or basement level, a shed or garage, and often a camera that verifies trips. Our Bronx price list starts at $600 for a basic wireless system and $2,500 for complete home protection, and most Locust Point houses we price fall between the two depending on how many outbuildings and lower-floor openings there are.

The way to know is a sensor-by-sensor list. A national average cannot tell you whether your basement door needs a contact, whether your shed is in range, or whether the deck is the main way in. A two-sketch quote can.

“The basement or the utility closet is the natural home for the control box”

Plenty of installation guides suggest a basement, a utility room or a closet near the electrical panel, and in a dry inland house that is often fine. It keeps the box out of the living space and near power.

On a lot that floods, it is the worst place in the house. Sandy put two feet of water inside the civic house on Tierney Place, per the Bronx Times, and the City Planning Department’s 2017 briefing says new and substantially improved floodplain buildings must keep mechanical systems above the design flood elevation. A panel, battery and communicator in a flooded basement are a total loss and leave the house silent at exactly the moment it is most exposed.

We mount the panel on the main floor or higher and leave only cheap, replaceable devices on the lowest level. It costs nothing extra and it is the single decision that most affects how a Locust Point system survives a storm.

“Wireless sensors don’t work outdoors or near water”

This comes from cheap kits whose sensors are meant for indoor doors and windows, and from owners who stuck one on a shed and watched it fail. The batteries struggle in cold, and plastic housings let in damp.

Sensors built for outdoor or unheated use, sealed against moisture and paired with a panel that supervises them, do work on sheds, garages and gates, within a range that has to be tested rather than assumed. Water itself does not block radio across a yard; walls with foil insulation, metal sheds and masonry foundations can.

So the honest version is: some outdoor devices work well, some do not, and we test the signal from every proposed spot before we promise one. If a shed sixty feet away will not hold a reliable signal, we tell you and suggest a camera instead.

“A loud outdoor siren is the best deterrent”

Sirens do scare off some intruders, and a loud one is reassuring to buy. Many kit makers market decibel ratings as if more were always better.

On a dead-end street, the people a loud outdoor siren reaches are your neighbors, and city law caps a building alarm at fifteen minutes under Administrative Code §24-238. Outdoors on the bay side, a siren also takes salt and weather. An intruder inside the house hears an indoor siren perfectly well, and a neighbor who has been asked to look out the window does not need one to know something is wrong because the station has already called.

We usually fit the siren indoors, set it to stop inside the legal limit, and put the outdoor effort into lights and a verification camera. If you want an outdoor siren too, we mount it under cover with sealed fittings.

“Professional monitoring means police are there in minutes”

Monitoring company advertising often implies a fast, guaranteed response, and chat answers repeat it. Some quote national average response figures.

Monitoring guarantees that a trained person sees your signal and acts on it, quickly and in the order you set. It does not control how quickly a patrol car is free, and on Locust Point every responder comes along one road, which bridge traffic has been reported to block. No honest installer can promise an arrival time.

What we can do is make the first minutes count: a neighbor on the point first on the list, video verification so a confirmed break-in is reported as one, and indoor deterrence so an intruder leaves before anyone needs to arrive.

“A smart camera system replaces an alarm”

Camera makers increasingly sell their systems as complete home security, with motion alerts and app sirens, and many waterfront owners already have a camera on the deck.

A camera records and alerts; it does not know whether you are home, it rarely covers the inside of a door, and its motion alerts are full of boats, gulls, wind-blown tarps and headlights. On the water side of a house, an unfiltered camera alert is the alert people learn to ignore.

The combination works better than either alone. Door, window and motion sensors decide that something is wrong; the camera shows what it is. We tie the two together so an alarm trip arrives with the clip that explains it.

“Anyone can install an alarm, it’s just stick-on sensors”

Self-install kits are designed to be fitted by owners, and on your own house that is legal. Some handymen and general contractors therefore offer to fit alarms as a side job.

Installing alarm systems for other people as a business in New York requires a Security or Fire Alarm Installer license from the Department of State under General Business Law Article 6-D, with qualifying training, fingerprinting and an exam. A licensed installer also knows the siren rule, supervision settings and how to keep burglary devices out of a building fire alarm system.

Ask anyone you hire for their state license number. Ours is #12000287431, and it is printed on every quote.

Field Notes: Pricing a House From the Seawall Back

This is a note on how we work, not a story about any particular Locust Point house or owner. When someone on the point asks for an alarm, the first thing we do on site is walk to the back of the yard and look at the house from the water. That is the view a thief arriving by the seawall stairs, or across a neighbor’s open yard, gets, and it is usually a different house from the one you see from the street: more glass, a door at yard level, a shed with a padlock, a deck with furniture that could be stood on to reach a window.

Then we look down. We ask where water has stood and look for the marks it leaves, a tide line on a foundation, stained drywall, a replaced lower step. Wherever that line is, we draw it on the quote. Anything that costs real money goes above it; anything below it is chosen to be cheap to replace. Owners who lived through 2012 usually know the line to the inch. Newer owners often do not, and the conversation is worth having before the first storm rather than after.

Last, we ask who is on the point when you are not. The answer shapes the call list more than any piece of hardware, and on a peninsula with one road in, it is the answer that decides how useful the whole system is at 3 a.m.

Where two sources disagree we say so on the page: Wikipedia and CityNeighborhoods draw the western boundary differently, the Bronx Times gives the clubhouse street as Tierney Avenue in 2009 and Tierney Place in 2014, and the single access road appears as Locust Point Road in a 2018 report and as Locust Point Drive in listings. Google’s People Also Ask, related searches, AI Overview, Bing and DuckDuckGo suggestions, Answer the Public and Yelp could not be opened in our build environment, and Reddit returned no Locust Point threads; nothing on this page is drawn from them. Statements labeled as our practice describe how AESS works; they are not claims about any particular house.

  • Wikipedia, “Locust Point, Bronx” (opened October 2026): boundaries, area, 2011 population, single-family houses, Wright’s Island and Locust Island, the filled channel and bridge ramp, the yacht club’s block.
  • Wikipedia, “Throggs Neck”: 45th Precinct address and 2022 figures, fire companies, post office, Bx8 to Locust Point, Fort Schuyler, Ferry Point Park.
  • CityNeighborhoods.NYC, “Locust Point”: streets ending at seawalls and inlets, 1920s–1930s bungalow subdivision, yacht club founded around 1932, Hammond Creek boundary (a less formal source, used for color only).
  • Openigloo, “Buildings in Locust Point”: street names grouped under Locust Point by a rental site.
  • Nest Seekers listing on Tierney Place: single-family house, finished walkout basement, deck, Sound views, community beach access (house number not reproduced).
  • Bronx Times, December 16, 2011: civic association’s new president on one access road and “a little bit of the country in the big city.”
  • Bronx Times, September 13, 2009: the clubhouse lease with the city, its users and its waterfront spot by the bridge approach.
  • Bronx Times, January 21, 2014: civic center reopening, city ownership, use by bridge engineers, polling site, two feet of Sandy water, $300,000 rebuild.
  • Bronx Times, November 1, 2013: one year after Sandy; Locust Point among the hard-hit waterfront neighborhoods; roof and dock damage; seawall request.
  • Bronx Times, November 9, 2012: “Sandy’s Bronx Tales,” the dock seen drifting past a Locust Point home.
  • News 12 Bronx, October 25, 2017: “Effects of Sandy linger in Locust Point” (article body not available in our build environment; headline and summary only).
  • Bronx Times, January 11, 2015: NY Rising East Bronx committee; proposal to fortify the Locust Point Civic Center.
  • Bronx Times, April 13, 2015: nineteen boat break-ins at the Locust Point Yacht Club in one March weekend; Hammond Cove Marina also hit; $1,000 reward.
  • Bronx Times, January 25, 2011: the bridge’s 50th anniversary; ribbon cut January 11, 1961; the civic association on its impact.
  • Bronx Times, December 10, 2018: deck replacement project; traffic blocking Locust Point Road, the only access point.
  • Bronx Times, April 25, 2019: the work fence at the last Bronx exit and close calls for drivers turning toward the waterfront.
  • NYC Department of City Planning, flood-resiliency briefing for the Throggs Neck Homeowners Association, September 26, 2017: Community District 10 floodplain shares; mechanicals above the design flood elevation.
  • NYC Administrative Code §24-238: building burglar alarms must terminate within fifteen minutes.
  • NYS Department of State: Security or Fire Alarm Installer license, General Business Law Article 6-D.
  • NYC HPD: smoke and carbon monoxide detector requirements.
  • HomeAdvisor: national alarm installation cost average and range.
