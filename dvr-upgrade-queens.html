What to do in the first hour after an incident

  1. Do not power the recorder off. Many older DVRs keep only a few days. Turning one off and on rarely fixes anything and can corrupt the clip you need. Leave it running until the footage is copied.
  2. Write down the real time and the recorder's time. Look at the clock on the recorder's screen and on your phone together and note the difference. A detective or adjuster can work with an offset you have written down; an offset nobody noticed makes a clip look unreliable.
  3. Find the clip, then lock it. Most recorders let you protect or lock a segment from the playback screen so it is not overwritten. If you cannot find that option, note the camera name and the start and end time instead.
  4. Copy it twice. Export to a USB stick from the recorder's front port, and keep a second copy on a computer. The recorder's own player file, if it offers one, is worth including — some exports open only with it.
  5. Keep a short log. Who exported it, when, onto what, and who it was given to. Precinct detectives and insurance investigators both ask, and a three-line note on paper answers them.

If the recorder will not play back or export, stop there and call us. Pulling a drive out to "try it in another box" is how most footage gets lost for good.

How the work changes by building

Two-family houses. In most Queens two-families the old recorder was put wherever the original installer found an outlet, and the coax from each camera ends there in one bundle. We start by deciding with both households where the box should live, then bring the bundle to it with proper couplers or a short new run. Cameras are grouped — entrances both families use, the owner's yard and garage, the tenant's side door — and each group is assigned to the right accounts. If the two floors have separate internet services, the recorder goes on the owner's, and the tenant's access runs through the app, not through their router.

Attached rows and semi-detached pairs. With no side yard, the cable path from a rear camera to a front-room recorder runs through the basement ceiling or the attic crawl, or not at all. Old coax already in those spaces is the most valuable thing in the house; we test it before anything else. Where a new rear camera has no path, a short point-to-point wireless link from the back window to the front is often cleaner than drilling joists. On aluminum or vinyl siding we mount on blocks made for the siding profile so the screws do not open a leak.

Garden co-ops and condos. Here the paperwork comes first. The managing agent gets our scope, insurance certificate, camera list and a note on who will have viewing rights, and the board approves it before we schedule. In courtyard complexes the recorder usually stays in the super's office or a locked utility room, with live view for the super and a short list of board-approved users. Some co-ops write their own camera rules; Glen Oaks Village, for example, registers every outward-facing shareholder camera and does not permit cameras in common hallways, and we design to the house rules of the building in front of us. Where one courtyard is shared by several separate co-op corporations under a single managing agent — common in the older garden complexes — the rules can change from one building to the next, so we ask for the house rules of the exact building, not the complex.

Mixed-use and corridor buildings. Under the 7 train on Roosevelt Avenue or the J over Jamaica Avenue in Woodhaven, vibration, dust and noise are part of the job. We tighten mounts, use compression BNC fittings that do not shake loose, keep the recorder off the stockroom floor and away from the coffee urn, and put it on a battery backup so a tripped breaker does not end the recording.

Rules and programs a Queens business should know

  • NYPD camera sharing is opt-in. The program that began in Flushing's 109th Precinct lets a business choose to share chosen cameras and hours through the city's New York City Connect portal; nobody is required to join, and joining is separate from handing over a clip when a detective asks.
  • No cameras where people undress, and no sound by default. New York's unlawful-surveillance law covers places where a person would expect to be able to undress in private, and recording a conversation without at least one party's consent is eavesdropping under Penal Law § 250.05 — so staff bathrooms and changing areas stay out of view and audio stays off unless counsel says otherwise.
  • The state small-business security tax credit. New York's Commercial Security Tax Credit, for businesses with 50 or fewer employees, can return up to $3,000 per retail location for qualifying spending that includes surveillance cameras; a certificate must be applied for by October 31 each year.
  • Roadway and sidewalk dining. Under Dining Out NYC, sidewalk cafés can operate year-round and seasonal roadway cafés from April 1 to November 29, with a year-round roadway license now available. A camera covering a dining setup is mounted on the building, not the structure, which has to come down or be moved.
  • Your landlord's facade. Most corridor storefronts are leased, and the lease usually decides what can be fixed to the front of the building and what stays when you leave. Ask before drilling the sign band.

Stores that replace the recorder often add a door upgrade too; access control for Queens storefronts and back doors can share the same network and the same visit.

Your Questions Answered

DVR Upgrades in Queens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Queens homeowners, co-op boards and store owners bring to us about their recorders, grouped by the situation behind them: two households under one roof, building boards, busy corridors, staff and languages, and footage that someone else asks for.

Two Households, One Recorder

Upstairs and downstairs in our two-family each pay for their own internet — which connection should the recorder use?

The owner's, in almost every case. The recorder needs a connection that will not disappear when a lease ends or a tenant switches providers, and it should sit on a network the owner controls, so the router's settings and passwords are not in someone else's hands. The tenant still gets phone access: the app reaches the recorder through the maker's relay service over the owner's internet, so it doesn't matter which provider the tenant's phone or Wi-Fi uses. If the owner's router is on the other floor from where the recorder belongs, we run one network cable between them — through the basement ceiling or the same chase as the coax — rather than relying on Wi-Fi through two floors of plaster. The one thing we avoid is putting the recorder on the tenant's router "because it was closer"; when that tenant moves out and cancels, the owner loses remote viewing and often doesn't notice until they need it.

The second-floor tenant pays her own electric bill — whose circuit should the recorder and cameras run on?

The owner's meter, or the house meter if the building has one. A recorder and eight cameras draw a modest, steady load all year, and it is fair to both sides that the person who owns the system pays for it. Practically, it also stops the system from going dark if a tenant's breaker trips, or if the utility shuts off service to a unit at the end of a tenancy. Older Queens installs often have plug-in power bricks scattered across both apartments, each camera fed from whichever outlet was nearest; during the upgrade we replace those with one camera power supply next to the recorder, on a circuit the owner controls, with a small battery backup for the recorder and modem. If a camera at the tenant's entrance can only be powered from inside the tenant's apartment, we say so in writing so both parties agree before the work starts.

My neighbor and I own the two halves of a semi-detached house in Fresh Meadows — how do we share one recorder and its costs fairly?

You can, and in semi-detached pairs it is often cheaper than two systems, but agree the rules before the cable goes in. One recorder sits on one side of the party wall, so one owner holds the hardware and the admin login; the other gets an account limited to the cameras that cover their side and the shared areas. Each camera that shows both properties — the shared driveway, the joint front walk — should be named and listed in a short written note you both sign, along with who pays for the drive and repairs and what happens if one house is sold. The new buyer is not bound by your arrangement, so plan for the second recorder later: we leave each side's cables labelled and accessible, so splitting the system means adding a box, not rewiring. If either of you rents out a floor, that tenant's access is set up by the owner of their half.

We rent the basement unit to a family — can the camera at their side entrance be kept off our own live view?

Yes, and in many two-family and basement-unit houses it is the considerate way to set it up. A camera at a tenant's private entrance records who comes and goes from their home; you may want it recorded for the building's security, but you don't need to watch it live from your phone every evening. On the new recorder that camera can be left out of the owner's everyday account and kept only in the administrator account, which is used when something has actually happened. Recording continues the whole time, so the footage is there if the tenant reports a break-in or a package theft. Many owners also give the tenant view-only access to that one camera, so the tenant can see their own door. Aim it at the entrance and the steps, not into a window, and tell the tenant in writing that it is there and who can view it.

Our attached house in Middle Village has no side yard — is a wireless link reliable enough for a camera at the back?

Usually, if it is the right kind of wireless. A consumer Wi-Fi camera talking to a router two rooms away through brick and plaster will drop out; a pair of small point-to-point radios — one at a rear window or on the back wall, one at the front of the house — aimed at each other over a clear line of sight is a different thing, and carries a network camera's video steadily for a house length with room to spare. On attached rows the clear line is often through the house itself, window to window, or over the roof between two short masts. We check it with the radios in hand before committing. Where an old coax run already reaches the back of the house, we test that first, because a wire that works beats any radio. The rear camera itself is then a network camera on the hybrid recorder, alongside your existing coax cameras at the front.

Co-op and Condo Boards

Our garden co-op in Jackson Heights wants an alteration agreement before any camera work — what do you send the managing agent?

A short package the agent can forward to the board without rewriting it. It contains a one-page scope in plain language (replace the recorder in the super's room, keep the existing courtyard and entrance cameras, re-terminate their cables, no new wall openings unless listed); a camera list with each camera's location and what it points at; a sketch of any new cable route; our certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the managing agent as the board requires; our NYS low-voltage license number; the proposed work dates and hours; and a note on who will have viewing access afterwards. If the building sits inside the Jackson Heights Historic District and anything new would show on a street front, we say so, because landmark review may apply to that part. Most boards approve a like-for-like recorder swap quickly; it is new exterior cameras and new users that draw questions, so we answer those in writing up front.

Glen Oaks Village registers shareholders' outward-facing cameras — does replacing our recorder mean registering again?

Check with the management office, but plan on updating the registration if anything changes outside. Glen Oaks Village's posted form says every outward-facing shareholder camera must be registered whether or not the shareholder claims the co-op's rebate of up to $100, that unregistered cameras are subject to a $100 house-rule violation, that cameras may not be unreasonably directed toward another apartment, and that cameras are not permitted in common hallways. A recorder swap that keeps the same cameras in the same places changes none of that, but if the upgrade adds a camera, moves one, or replaces one with a different model, the registration should match what is actually on the building. We give you a list of every camera's location and direction after the work, which is what the form asks for, and we aim each one at your own door, walk or window line — not at a neighbor's — so it passes the co-op's inspection from outside.

The condo board says the lobby recorder belongs to the association — can a unit owner get a copy of footage from it?

That is the association's decision, and a good upgrade makes it easy for the board to say yes or no consistently. The recorder, the drive and the footage belong to the condominium, and access is set by the board, usually through a written camera policy: who may view, who may export, how long footage is kept and how an owner asks for a clip. We don't hand footage to individual owners on our own. What we do is build the system so the policy works — a managing-agent or board account that can export, a super's account that can only watch live, a log of exports, and retention long enough that a request made a week after a package went missing can still be met. If your building has no written policy, the board's attorney is the person to draft it; we can describe what the recorder can and cannot do so the policy matches the hardware.

Our co-op board wants to know exactly who will be able to watch the courtyard cameras — how is that set up so they can sign off?

With named accounts, each limited to what that person needs, written down before the work and checked after. A typical courtyard plan has one administrator account held by the managing agent or the board president and never used day to day; a super's account that sees the courtyard, the entrances and the laundry room door live but cannot delete or export; and one or two board accounts that can play back and export when the board authorizes it. Residents do not get recorder accounts. Each account has its own password, two-step sign-in where the app supports it, and a phone tied to a person rather than a shared tablet. The recorder keeps a log of logins and exports that the board can review. We hand the board a one-page list of accounts and permissions at the end, so the approval they signed and the system they own say the same thing.

Restaurants and Shops on Busy Corridors

Grease from the fryer coats our kitchen camera within weeks — where should a camera go in a working restaurant kitchen?

Out of the plume, and in a housing that wipes clean. A camera above the line, the fryer or the steam table collects a film of grease on its dome within weeks, and warm, wet air condenses inside cheap housings. A new recorder will show that blur in higher resolution. During the upgrade we move kitchen cameras out of the plume — to the side wall facing the line rather than above it — and fit models with a sealed, flat-front housing that wipes clean, rather than a dome that holds grease in its curve. We add a cleaning step to the closing checklist: a dry microfibre cloth, then one with a little glass cleaner, never a scrub pad. If the kitchen camera's job is to show who handled cash or food at the pass, we aim it at the pass, not at the burners. For the dining room and register, the recorder upgrade does help: wider dynamic range copes with bright windows behind the counter.

We run a small shop under the 7 train on Roosevelt Avenue — does the elevated line's rumble affect the cameras or the recorder?

It can, slowly, and mostly at the connections. Every passing train shakes the building a little, and over years that loosens camera mounts, lets a twist-on BNC connector work itself off, and makes an old recorder's hard drive work harder. The signs are a camera that drifts out of aim, a tile that flickers when a train passes, or a recording that has gaps. In an upgrade we re-fix mounts into solid material with anchors that resist vibration, replace twist-on connectors with compression fittings, secure cable so it can't swing, and stand the recorder on a rubber pad on a shelf rather than bolting it to a wall that carries the vibration. We also set the recorder to alert you on a disk error, so a drive starting to fail is caught early. The same applies under the J over Jamaica Avenue and the A over Liberty Avenue.

Our Flushing store is open until 2 a.m. with two shifts on the register — can the footage show who was on the register at any moment?

Yes, in two ways that work together. First, the register camera is aimed and framed to show the cashier's face and hands, the drawer and the customer side, not just the top of a head; that alone settles most shift questions. Second, many point-of-sale systems can send their transaction text to a recorder that accepts it, so each sale, void or no-sale appears as text over the register video and can be searched later. Whether that works depends on your register model and the recorder; we check both before quoting it. Simpler stores use a shift-change routine instead: the new cashier logs into the register at the start of the shift, and the recorder's timestamp is kept accurate to the second, so the register's login log and the video line up. We also give each manager a recorder login, so the playback history shows who looked at what.

Can one recorder cover our two takeout shops six blocks apart on Jamaica Avenue?

Not one box — but one app. Each shop keeps its own recorder, on its own drive, so that if one store's internet goes down or a recorder fails, the other keeps recording, and a break-in at one doesn't put both stores' footage at risk. Both recorders are then added to the same phone app and the same desktop software, so you see the two shops side by side, play back either one and export from either one in the same way. Staff accounts are set per store: the manager at one location sees only that location. Running a single recorder for both would mean streaming one shop's cameras over the internet to the other around the clock, which costs bandwidth, fails whenever the connection drops and leaves gaps exactly when something is going wrong. If you open a third location later, it simply joins the same app.

Staff, Languages and the Phone App

Half our staff read Chinese and half read Spanish — can the recorder and app be set up so everyone can use them?

Mostly, yes, and where the software falls short we make up for it on paper. Recorder menus and phone apps usually have a language setting, but which languages are offered depends on the maker, the model and the firmware region, so we check the exact recorder before you buy rather than promising. The phone app normally follows each phone's own language setting, so a cashier with a phone set to Chinese and a cook with a phone set to Spanish each see the app in their language. For the recorder's on-screen menu, which has one language at a time, we set the one your managers use. Then we leave a one-page picture guide beside the recorder — screenshots with arrows for "play back", "lock this clip" and "export to USB" — with short captions in the languages your staff read, which you supply or approve. Pictures do most of the work; staff remember where to tap better than what the menu says.

How do we give the night manager live view without letting him delete or export footage?

With his own account, set to view only. Every recorder we install lets the owner create user accounts and choose, for each one, which cameras it can see and whether it can play back, export, change settings or delete. A night manager typically gets live view and playback of the store cameras, no export, no settings, no access to the office or the owner's home cameras if they share a recorder, and no ability to delete anything. If you want him able to save a clip for police when you're not there, we give him export but not delete, and the recorder logs each export with his name and time. The point of separate accounts is accountability: the one shared login written on the wall, which we find in many Queens stores, means nobody can tell who changed a setting or removed a recording. We also change the recorder's admin password and keep it with the owner only.

Our family runs the store but the owner spends months abroad — can he watch the cameras from overseas?

Yes, with two caveats we check before the upgrade. Most recorder apps connect through the maker's relay servers, which generally work from abroad, but some apps sign up accounts to a regional server, and an account created in one region may not log in from another; we create the account deliberately so it works where the owner will be. Some countries also restrict or slow access to certain services, which is outside anyone's control here, so we test from a phone set to the owner's location before he leaves, if possible. The second caveat is bandwidth: watching several cameras live from far away is smoother on the recorder's lower-resolution sub-stream, so we set that as the default for remote viewing and leave full resolution for playback and export. We never open router ports for overseas viewing — that is exactly how old recorders end up in botnets.

Police, Insurers and Footage Requests

A detective from the 109th Precinct asked for a copy of our footage — what format, and how do we hand it over?

Ask the detective what they want, then give them a copy, not the recorder. Detectives usually ask for the clip on a USB stick or ask to come and copy it themselves. Export the time range they name plus a few minutes either side, from every camera that shows the scene, in the recorder's own format and, if it offers one, a common format such as MP4 too; include the recorder's player program if the export creates one. Write on a piece of paper the date, the cameras, the real time against the recorder's time and who made the copy, and keep your own copy. Do not edit, trim or screen-record the clip on a phone — that loses the original timestamp and quality. If the recorder can't export, don't pull the drive; call us. After the upgrade, every manager will have done one practice export with us, so the first real one isn't the first time.

Half the houses on our Corona block have cameras — can our recorders be linked so one clip follows a car past every house?

Not linked, but lined up — which works better. Each household's recorder stays its own: separate drives, separate logins, nobody on the block able to see into anyone else's system. What makes several recorders useful together is time. If every recorder's clock is synchronised to the internet time service, a detective or a neighbor can take a clip from house one at 2:14:05 and a clip from house four at 2:14:31 and know they show the same car twenty-six seconds apart. Old recorders drift by minutes or hours, and that is what makes a street's worth of footage hard to piece together. During the upgrade we set your recorder to synchronise automatically and show you where to check it; if neighbors upgrade with us, theirs are set the same way. When something happens, each owner exports their own clip and hands it over. Sharing logins between houses, however friendly the block, is how a recorder ends up controlled by someone who has moved away.

If we decide later to join the NYPD's business camera-sharing program, what does the upgraded recorder need?

Joining is entirely up to you, and the upgrade neither signs you up nor rules it out. The program — first tested in Flushing's 109th Precinct in 2023 and expanded in 2024 — is voluntary: businesses enroll through the city's New York City Connect portal and can share all cameras or limit which cameras and which hours are shared. Enrollment, and any device or software the program supplies, is arranged between you and the program, not by us. What we can do is make sure the upgraded recorder is ready if you decide to join: current firmware, a stable network connection, cameras named clearly, the clock synchronised, and a network that is closed to the outside world except for what you choose to allow. If you are unsure, start with good footage and a reliable export routine; that is what helps most when a detective calls, enrolled or not.

An insurer's letter told us to preserve footage of a customer's fall in our store — how do we stop the recorder overwriting it?

Lock it and copy it today, before the drive cycles round. Recorders overwrite the oldest video when the drive fills, so a fall recorded two weeks ago may be days from disappearing on an older box. Find the clip in playback, use the lock or protect function so the recorder skips it when overwriting, then export the full time range from every camera that shows the area — the fall itself, the entrance and the minutes before and after — to two USB sticks. Keep one, send one as the insurer or your lawyer directs, and write down what you copied and when. Don't trim it to "just the fall"; the condition of the floor before and the response after are often what matters. If the letter arrives after the footage is gone, tell the insurer that in writing. As part of an upgrade we size the drive so the recorder holds at least the weeks your insurer asks you to keep.

What AI Gets Wrong About DVR Upgrades in Queens

"Thirty days of footage is the standard — set it and forget it"

Retention guides from camera makers start here. Digital Watchdog, for example, writes that thirty days is a common starting point, then adds that the right period depends on regulations, risks and storage capacity.

The qualifier is the part that matters in Queens. A slip in a restaurant may not reach an insurer for weeks, a co-op board may set its own retention in a camera policy, and a cargo claim at a JFK warehouse can stay open far longer than a month. Thirty days is a floor, not a standard, and for a two-family whose owner travels it may be too short to notice a problem at all.

We size the drive the other way round: first the longest period someone may ask you to produce footage — insurer, board, landlord or your own lawyer — then the cameras' resolution and recording mode, then the drive that holds it with room to spare.

"Police can tap into any business's cameras whenever they want"

This one circulates whenever camera sharing is in the news. When the NYPD's business camera program was expanded in May 2024, coverage explained that it had been unveiled in Flushing's 109th Precinct the June before.

The same coverage was clear that businesses are not required to give access: they opt in through a city portal and can limit which cameras and hours are shared. Nobody connects to your recorder because you upgraded it.

What police do routinely is ask. After a robbery or burglary, detectives canvass the block and request clips. Whether you can hand one over in ten minutes depends on your export routine, not on any program — which is why we rehearse it with you.

"Doorbell and Wi-Fi cameras have made the DVR obsolete"

Forum threads often treat the two as rivals. On one home-security forum, an owner described running six video doorbells alongside two conventional cameras recording around the clock to a DVR — the two kinds doing different jobs.

That is what we see in Queens too. A doorbell camera at a tenant's door is convenient; it records events to a subscription and depends on the household's Wi-Fi. A recorder keeps every camera continuously, on a drive in the building, and keeps going when the internet drops.

"Any new recorder will work with the cameras you already have"

Online retailers sell recorders as if cameras and recorders were universal. They are not: analog cameras send video in one of several formats, and a recorder has to accept that format on that channel.

Penta-brid recorder data sheets list five inputs — HDCVI, AHD, TVI, older CVBS analog and IP — precisely because Queens basements hold a mix of all of them, sometimes on the same box, after twenty years of piecemeal camera replacements.

"A shareholder can put any camera they like on their own co-op apartment"

Advice aimed at renters and homeowners often says a camera on your own door is your business. Co-op lawyers point out that New York City has no law against hallway doorbell cameras, but that a co-op's rules may still prohibit them in common areas.

Boards also write rules on angles, recording and storage time. Glen Oaks Village, for one, requires registration of every outward-facing shareholder camera, bars cameras in common hallways and fines unregistered ones.

"Rentals should skip DVRs and go straight to network cameras"

Landlord forums repeat this — one investor advised another to use an NVR rather than a DVR for a multi-family, with cameras on the parking lot and front door and all tenants informed.

The tenant-notice part is sound. The hardware part ignores what is already in a Queens two-family: coax buried in plaster, cameras that still work, and an owner who does not want to open walls in an occupied apartment.

A hybrid recorder gives the same benefits — separate accounts, remote access, HD — on the existing cable, and accepts network cameras where they help. Starting over is a choice, not a requirement.

"Camera apps come in every language your staff speak"

App store listings and maker websites make language look like a solved problem. When we looked, the language menu on the Hik-Connect website listed English, Italian, Spanish, German, French, Polish, Dutch and a run of other European languages.

FAQ

Queens DVR Upgrade FAQ

Do we have to tell the tenants in our two-family before the upgraded cameras start recording?
+

We are not aware of a New York City rule that requires a small landlord to give notice before cameras record a building's own entrances, yards and shared halls, and your lease or a lawyer is the place to confirm that for your building. Whatever the rule, telling tenants in writing is what we recommend, and many owners already do it. A short note saying which areas are recorded, that nothing inside their apartment is, that the cameras record video only and not sound, and who can view the footage avoids most disputes before they start. It also makes it easier to ask a tenant for access when a camera needs attention. If the upgrade adds a camera that sees a tenant's door or windows, raise it with them before it goes up, not after.

Can you provide a certificate of insurance naming our co-op, condo or managing agent?
+

Yes. Most Queens co-op and condo boards ask for one before work starts, usually naming the corporation or association and the managing agent as additional insureds, with specific wording the agent sends us. Send us the building's insurance requirements with the alteration agreement or work authorization and we have the certificate issued to match, together with our NYS low-voltage license number. For larger buildings the agent may also want the work hours, the areas we will enter and the name of the technician; we include those in the package. Getting the certificate right the first time is the main thing that keeps a board approval from slipping a week.

Should a store's recorder go inside a lock box, and will it overheat in there?
+

In most stores it should, and it won't overheat if the box is the right kind. A recorder on an open shelf can be switched off, unplugged or carried out by anyone who gets behind the counter, and a thief who knows about cameras goes for the recorder first. We fit a lockable enclosure made for recorders, with vents or a small fan, mounted off the floor and away from coffee machines, fryers and space heaters. The drive's temperature can be read on the recorder's health screen, and we check it after the box has been closed for an hour. Keys stay with the owner and one manager. If the store has a back office with a lockable door, that can be enough on its own.

Our co-op apartment's window faces the street — can a camera behind the glass record clearly at night?
+

It can, if you deal with the reflection. A camera indoors looking out through a window works well in daylight, and some co-ops even suggest a window-ledge camera to shareholders because nothing is fixed to the building. At night the camera's own infrared lights bounce off the glass and fill the picture with glare, and a lit room behind the camera reflects in the pane too. The fixes are simple: turn the camera's infrared off and rely on the street light outside or a camera that records colour in low light, press the lens right up to the glass with a soft hood around it, and keep the room light behind it off or dimmed. If your building's rules require registering cameras that face out, as some Queens co-ops do, register it like any other.

Can the office computer at our other location show the cameras, not only phones?
+

Yes. The recorder makers we install provide desktop software for Windows and, for most models, Mac, which shows several cameras at once on a large screen and plays back and exports the same way the recorder does. We install it on the office computer, sign in with an account that has only the permissions that office needs, and test it before we leave. Some recorders can also be viewed in a web browser on the local network. Viewing from another location goes through the maker's relay or a VPN, never through ports opened on the store's router. If the office already watches another site with the same app, the upgraded recorder simply joins that list.

Is a deposit due before the swap, and when is the balance paid?
+

Yes. Our service agreement sets out a 50% deposit when you approve the written quote, which lets us order the recorder, drive and any cameras, with the balance due when the work is finished and you have seen every camera recording and played back. The agreement also covers the one-year parts-only warranty on equipment we supply and the late-fee schedule. If a board package or an approval is pending, we hold the deposit invoice until the board has signed off, so you are not paying for a job the building has not yet approved. Labor-only installs of a recorder you bought follow the same terms.

Can a camera watch our restaurant's roadway dining setup without being mounted on it?
+

Yes, and that is how we would do it. Under the city's Dining Out NYC rules, roadway cafés are built to be moved, set up and taken down, and seasonal ones run from April 1 to November 29, so a camera fixed to the structure would have to come down with it each season. Instead we mount the camera on the building facade or under the awning, aimed out over the dining area and the curb, with its cable run inside the building. A wide turret placed high usually covers the whole setup and the sidewalk between it and the door. If you hold a year-round roadway license, the same camera simply stays aimed at the structure all year.

Our shop's Wi-Fi is shared with customers — should the recorder be on that network?
+

No. A recorder on the same network as customers' phones can be found and probed by anyone who joins, and a busy guest network slows its uploads to your phone. During the upgrade we put the recorder on its own wired connection to the router, and where the router allows it, on a separate network from the customer Wi-Fi, with the guest network unable to reach it. Staff phones view the cameras through the recorder's app over the internet like any other remote viewer, so nobody needs to join a special network to check a camera. If the router is a basic provider unit that can't separate networks, a small business router or switch costs little and fixes it. Changing the guest Wi-Fi password regularly is still good practice, but it is not what protects the recorder.

Can the store's monitor by the door show customers they are on camera?
+

Yes. Most recorders have a second video output or can send a live view to a separate display, so a monitor near the entrance can show the door camera or a grid of the shop floor while the main screen in the office stays private. We set the public monitor to live view only, with no menus reachable from it, and keep the register camera and the office off it. A public-view monitor tells people they are being recorded the moment they walk in, and many owners find it deters casual theft better than a sign. It is wired back to the recorder, so it doesn't need its own recorder or drive.

Can our old analog cameras be moved to better spots during the upgrade, or must they stay where they were?
+

They can be moved, and an upgrade is the cheapest time to do it. Many Queens systems were laid out years ago for a different counter, a different door or a tenant who has left, and a camera aimed at a wall that no longer matters is wasted. If the new spot is within reach of the camera's existing cable, moving it is usually a short re-termination; if not, we run a new length of coax or switch that camera to a network model on the hybrid recorder. We walk the property with you before the swap, mark which cameras stay, which move and which are retired, and list them in the written plan so the price covers it.
