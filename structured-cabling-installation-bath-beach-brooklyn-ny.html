The Neighborhood

Why Bath Beach Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Wikipedia sums up Bath Beach in one line: a working-class community of semi-attached houses and small apartment houses. The city's own sales figures show what that means house by house. In the Department of Finance's 2022 summary for the neighborhood, 74 two-family houses changed hands against 19 one-family houses, along with 32 three-family houses. The 2024 summary tells the same story on a smaller scale, with 44 two-family sales, 17 three-family sales and only 12 one-family sales. A typical Bath Beach house is not a home for one household. It is a building with an owner on one floor and a tenant or relatives on another, sometimes three households stacked one above the next, all sharing a front door, a stair and a cellar.

That arrangement is the starting point for every cabling plan we draw here. In a one-family house, a single router in the right spot can sometimes cover the whole place. In a Bath Beach two-family, the router belongs to one household, sits in that household's apartment or in the cellar, and has to push its signal through brick, plaster and a floor or two before it reaches anyone else. Often each household pays for its own internet service, from a different company, and each provider's technician has come and gone without much thought for the last one. The cellar ends up with a modem on a shelf, a second one on a nail, a fiber box low on the wall and a tangle of coax and phone wire from companies that once served the house. Upstairs, the tenant runs a long cord down the stairs or lives with a weak signal.

Structured cabling replaces that improvisation with a plan. Each provider's line ends at one labeled spot on the cellar wall. From there, a cable climbs to each apartment through a route chosen for the house, and inside each apartment it ends at the jacks and ceiling access points that household actually uses. Each family's network stays its own, nobody needs to enter another apartment to reset a router, and when a tenant moves out, the next one plugs into a cable that is already there and already tested. The same principles apply on a larger scale to the walk-ups and garden apartments along Cropsey Avenue and to the stores below apartments on 86th Street.

The neighborhood is also changing fast near the water, where towers of 24 to 31 stories have risen on Cropsey Avenue since 2023, and on the side streets, where small new condos are replacing single houses. Each has a telecom room a developer designed, and each will need someone to add, trace and repair cable long after the builder has gone; the new-buildings section below goes into them.

Then there is the question of what arrives at the street. A third-party broadband directory, bestneighborhood.org, estimates that fiber service from Verizon reaches about 97 percent of the 11214 ZIP code and Optimum's fiber about 95 percent. A speed-test map published by Untapped Cities in 2017, using tests from May 2016 to April 2017, put Bath Beach's 90th-percentile average at about 109 megabits per second. Plans sold today are many times faster than that, and the bottleneck in an older house is now often not the provider's line but the inside wiring: a router in a cellar, a mesh unit on a radiator, old phone wire that was never meant to carry data. Fiber at the curb only pays off when the cable inside the house can carry it to the rooms where people work, study and watch.

Upstairs Lost Its Connection, the Store's Card Reader Went Dark, or Nobody Knows Which Cellar Cable Is Live: Fast Cable Help in Bath Beach

Cable problems in a shared house rarely announce themselves as cable problems. The tenant upstairs says the internet is down, the owner downstairs says it works fine, and both are right. A provider technician swapped the modem last week and moved the wrong cord. A plumber working on a cellar line pushed a bundle off the joists and pinched it against a pipe. A contractor renovating the second-floor kitchen closed a wall over a junction nobody marked. On 86th Street the story tends to be a card reader or a register that stopped talking to the back office after someone rearranged the counter, or a camera recorder that lost its link the night a breaker tripped.

Finding the fault is methodical work, and it usually takes less time than people expect. A tone generator on the dead jack and a probe in the cellar tell us which cable we are following. A tester tells us whether the copper still passes and roughly how far along the run it fails. A look at that spot usually shows the damage: a staple driven through the jacket, a cable crushed under a shelf, a connector pulled half out of its jack, water in a box near the floor. You get a short written list of what can be re-terminated on the spot, what needs a new pull, and what is dead cable better removed so it stops confusing the next person. If the damage came from another trade's work, the findings are written down so you can raise it with them.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling for Bath Beach's Architecture

Almost every Bath Beach call comes from one of six kinds of building. For a cabling crew, what separates them is where the provider's line comes in, how the cable gets from there to the floors above, how many households it has to serve, and who besides the owner has a say over the walls.

The semi-attached brick two-family

The neighborhood's most common house: two stories over a cellar, one apartment per floor, a shared front door and stair, and a party wall on one side.

The three-family house

Three stacked apartments over a cellar, often with a separate entrance for the top floor, three households and sometimes three different internet providers.

The older frame house

Wood-framed survivors from Bath Beach's resort era, such as the Victorian at 122 Bay 26th Street that BKLYNER described, with porches, wood siding and irregular plans.

The prewar walk-up

Four-story brick buildings like 2100–2106 Cropsey Avenue, two buildings from 1915 with 44 apartments between them, with a cellar, a single stair and masonry walls.

Garden apartments and postwar mid-rises

Low buildings around open grounds, such as the 84-unit garden complex at 1758 Cropsey Avenue from 1951, and six-story postwar buildings like 2036 Cropsey Avenue.

The new tower or small new condo

Concrete towers of 24 to 31 stories near the bay and three-story condos of six to twelve units on the side streets, each with a developer-designed telecom room or closet.

The semi-attached two-family: one cellar, two households, a party wall

The defining feature of these houses, for cabling, is that two networks have to begin in the same small space and end in two separate homes. The provider's lines usually reach the cellar through the front or the side of the house, and the cellar is shared ground: the boiler, the meters, the laundry and storage for both families. A good plan claims one clean piece of wall near the service entry for a small board or cabinet, gives each provider's handoff its own labeled spot on it, and starts each apartment's cable from there. The first-floor apartment is the easy one, since cable can come up through the floor into a closet or behind a baseboard almost anywhere along the cellar ceiling. The second floor is where the plan is earned.

Getting cable to the second floor of a semi-attached brick house means finding a hollow path past the first floor without opening the first-floor apartment's finished walls. The usual candidates are a closet on the first floor with a closet stacked above it, the space beside a plumbing stack in a kitchen or bathroom wall, the cavity behind the stair stringer in the shared hall, or a chase left by an old chimney that no longer serves a flue. Because the house is attached on one side, the party wall is never a candidate: it is solid masonry shared with the neighbor, and nothing we do goes into it. We check each possible route with a small hole and an inspection camera before relying on it, and once the cable reaches the second floor it spreads out to that apartment's jacks and ceiling access point the same way the first floor's did.

The three-family house: three apartments, one route up

A third floor turns a routing problem into a planning one. If the cable for the top apartment has to pass through both lower apartments, the route should be chosen so it passes through each of them once, in a place the household agrees to, and never again. That usually means pulling all three apartments' cables in the same visit, up the same vertical route, with spare capacity left in that route for the future. A closet stack that reaches all three floors is ideal. Where it stops at the second floor, the top floor is often fed through a separate route on the stair side of the house, or from the roof space if one is reachable and safe. Three-family houses also tend to have three internet accounts, and sometimes three different companies, so the cellar board needs room for three handoffs, each labeled with the apartment it serves.

The older frame house: porches, siding and a plan that wanders

A few wood-frame houses survive from the era when, as the Brooklyn Eagle tells it, wealthy families built villas, yacht clubs and mansions along this shore, long before the Shore Parkway paved over the beach. BKLYNER wrote about one of the best known, a Victorian at 122 Bay 26th Street between Benson and Bath Avenues, and the Brooklyn Eagle has written about the mansions that were abandoned after the 1929 crash. Houses like these, along with later frame houses that have been sided over, behave differently from brick. Wall cavities in wood framing often run further and more freely between floors, which can make the vertical route easier, but they may also hold old wiring, gas lines and additions from many owners. Porches and bay windows make plans irregular, so a single access point in the middle of a floor often misses a front porch room or a rear addition.

The prewar walk-up: masonry, one stair and a cellar room

Cropsey Avenue has a run of prewar apartment buildings with bay fronts; Forgotten NY singled out a handsome group between Bay 20th Street and 19th Avenue and more between Bay 22nd and Bay 23rd Streets. StreetEasy lists 2100–2106 Cropsey Avenue as two four-story prewar walk-ups with 44 apartments, built in 1915. In buildings like these the providers' lines arrive in a cellar room, each apartment's service climbs a riser the building or the provider controls, and the owner or managing agent decides what any contractor may open in the hall or the stair. A resident who wants a jack in a back bedroom works inside their own apartment and usually needs the owner's permission for anything beyond a slim surface raceway. An owner who wants a common-area network for a laundry room, cameras or an entry panel works in the building's own spaces.

Garden apartments and postwar mid-rises: several buildings or several floors from one room

StreetEasy lists 1758 Cropsey Avenue as a 1951 garden-apartment complex of three stories and 84 units, and OpenIgloo lists 2036 Cropsey Avenue as a six-story postwar building of 42 rent-stabilized apartments built in 1948. Garden complexes spread their units across several low buildings around open grounds, which means a common network has to travel between buildings, either through a connecting cellar, through conduit under a walk, or in a pair of cabled wireless bridges where digging is unwelcome. Postwar mid-rises stack their apartments on an elevator core, with a cellar or ground-floor room where the providers land.

The new tower and the small new condo: rooms the developer designed

The newest buildings in Bath Beach start their networks in a telecom room designed by the developer's engineers. In a tower like The Shoreline or Bayview Courtyard, that room feeds each floor through conduit or sleeves in concrete, and each unit through a small panel or a single outlet. Concrete ceilings and metal-stud partitions set the rules for added jacks: cable travels inside the partitions, through spare conduit where the building has it, or in a slim surface raceway where the concrete cannot be touched. Small new condos of three stories are usually wood or light-gauge framing with fire-rated walls between units, so cable that serves one unit stays inside that unit, and anything crossing a rated wall is sealed and recorded.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in a Bath Beach Two-Family Before Drawing a Route

These notes describe how our technicians survey the shared houses that make up most of Bath Beach. They describe our method, not a particular address or customer.

Ask who pays for what. Before anyone looks at a wall, the technician asks how many households live in the house, which of them pays for which internet service, and whether any household shares a connection with another. The answer decides how many networks the cellar has to start, and whether any of them need to stay private from the others.

Find every line that comes into the cellar. The technician follows each provider's line from where it enters to where it ends, notes the modem or fiber box on it and which apartment it serves, and marks any cable that ends nowhere. In houses that have had several providers, it is common to find two live services and three dead ones.

Look up at the cellar ceiling. Joist direction, heating pipes, gas lines, old knob-and-tube remnants, water lines and the path from the front of the house to the back all show up overhead. The technician looks for the clearest route from the service entry to the bottom of each possible vertical route and notes where cable must stay clear of the boiler and the flue.

Stand on each floor and look for anything that lines up. A closet over a closet, a kitchen wall over a kitchen wall, the hall beside the stair, an unused chimney chase: any of these can carry cable upward without opening a finished room. The party wall is ruled out at once.

Agree on access before leaving. If the route passes through a tenant's apartment, the technician notes exactly which closet or wall it uses, so the owner can tell the tenant what will happen, on which day, and for how long.

Power and Backup

When the Lights Go Out in Bath Beach: Keeping a Bath Beach Network Up Through a Con Edison Outage

Bath Beach has a recent history of summer outages that every network plan here should take into account. On a Sunday night in July 2019, the Brooklyn Eagle reported, more than 31,000 Brooklyn households lost power during a heat wave, about 3,600 of them in Bath Beach, and a Con Edison spokesperson explained that "the wires were really cooking." Power came back to most of the neighborhood by 11 p.m. Three weeks later, on August 10, about 3,800 customers in Bath Beach and Gravesend lost power again for about an hour and a half, and the Eagle noted that the earlier outage had left thousands in the dark for more than five hours. The Brooklyn Paper separately reported an outage that cut 1,172 customers in Coney Island, Gravesend and Bath Beach on a hot morning after power lines came down at Stillwell Avenue and Avenue Z, with the last of them restored around 2 p.m.

A network is only as reliable as its power. When the lights go out, the modem, the switch, the access points and every camera that draws power through its network cable all go dark at once, and so do internet phones and card terminals that depend on them. Most families accept that during a long outage. What they do not accept is the network failing to come back properly afterward: a switch that restarts before the modem and never finds its connection, an access point stuck in a half-started state, a camera recorder that lost its settings. In a store, the cost is clearer still: the card reader at the counter is down for as long as the network is.

The answer is a battery backup unit sized for the equipment it protects, placed in the cabinet or rack with the network gear, and a plan for what it should carry. For a house, that usually means the modem or fiber box, the main switch and the access points, which together draw little power and can stay up through the kind of short outage the Eagle described. For a store, it adds the register and card reader circuits, the camera recorder and any internet phones. For a building's common network, it covers the cameras and the entry panel first. We write the battery's job into the quote so you know what stays up and what does not, label the equipment it protects, and test the switchover before we leave. A battery will not carry a network through a five-hour outage on its own, and we do not pretend it will; it will carry the short interruptions that otherwise knock a network offline and leave it confused when power returns.

Where the equipment lives in a Bath Beach house

Wikipedia records that the southwest half of the neighborhood is filled land, made from earth excavated for the Shore Parkway in the early 1940s, and StreetEasy lists some addresses on Cropsey Avenue and 86th Street in hurricane evacuation zone 4. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in October 2012 that Hurricane Sandy breached the seawall on Shore Parkway, and CBS New York reported in November 2025 that the Shore Parkway Promenade's seawall is still being repaired, with Community Board 11's district manager blaming wave action for "significant flooding and damage to our infrastructure." None of that tells us which cellars take water, and we do not guess. What it does mean is that on any block toward the bay, we ask the owner whether the cellar has ever had water, and if the answer is yes or unsure, we mount the cabinet high on the cellar wall or move the main equipment to a closet upstairs, with a single cable down to the provider's handoff. The provider decides where its own box sits; everything after it is yours to place.

New Buildings

Twenty-Four to Thirty-One Stories by the Bay: Cabling the New Towers and Small Condos of Bath Beach

For most of the twentieth century, Bath Beach's tallest residential buildings were the co-op towers of the late 1950s and 1960s along Shore Parkway and Cropsey Avenue, such as Waterview Towers at 1935 Shore Parkway, which Homes.com lists at 17 stories and 325 units built in 1959. The new generation is taller and more complicated. CityRealty's account of The Shoreline at 2230 Cropsey Avenue lists a swimming pool and deck, a fitness center, a laundry room, bike and resident storage, a lounge and terrace on the 23rd floor, and lower floors planned for a daycare center and an ambulatory care facility. YIMBY's report on plans for 2300 Cropsey Avenue described 24 stories and 154 condominium units over a community facility and a garage for 270 cars. On Bay Parkway, 8800 Bay Parkway brings 40 rentals over a ground-floor community facility.

Every one of those spaces has its own network needs. A fitness room wants screens and music. A lounge wants strong Wi-Fi for a room full of phones. A garage wants cameras and, increasingly, a connection for the gate and any chargers. A daycare needs a check-in station, staff devices and cameras that parents may be invited to view. A medical suite needs separate networks for staff, patients and equipment. The developer's telecom room is the starting point for all of it, and the building's management or the commercial tenant usually brings in its own contractor once the developer has gone. That is where we come in, with a plan that uses the conduit and sleeves the building already has before proposing anything new.

The small condos on the side streets

The three-story condos that BrickUnderground describes, built on lots where single houses once stood, are a different job again. Marketproof's permit notices give a sense of the pattern: a three-story, three-unit building filed in April 2024 for 183 Bay 25th Street to replace a two-story house from 1920, and a five-story building with a commercial space filed in March 2023 for 8620 20th Avenue. Buildings like these often have one small closet or cellar room where the providers land and a single outlet in each unit. Owners who buy into them frequently find they want more: a jack at a desk, a cable to the TV, an access point in a bedroom at the far end of the unit. Because each unit is separated from its neighbors by fire-rated walls and floors, cable for one unit stays inside that unit, and anything that must pass through a rated assembly is sealed with listed firestop and recorded.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Licenses, Boards and Landlords: What Governs a Bath Beach Cabling Job

We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the published rules say and how we apply them, so you know what to ask before work begins.

The low-voltage permit rule. The city's Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024 and in effect since December 21, 2025, says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Industry websites still disagree about what that means in practice, so on large or unusual commercial jobs we confirm with the Department of Buildings rather than rely on any website, including this one.

Fire alarm wiring is separate. Fire alarm wiring sits outside that exemption and has its own licensing and filings. In the walk-ups, mid-rises and new towers, our data cable never shares a fire alarm pathway, and where both trades are working we coordinate with the fire alarm contractor.

Compliance notes for Bath Beach stores and buildings

  • Low-voltage permits. Under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, in effect since December 21, 2025, data and communication wiring under 50 volts does not need an electrical permit when installed by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Fire alarm wiring is outside that exemption.
  • Licensed security work. Cameras, access control and burglar alarms are installed under AESS's NYS license #12000287431.
  • Sealed openings. Every opening through a rated wall, floor or ceiling between a store and the apartments above, or between units in a new building, is closed with listed firestop once the cable is through and recorded on the drawing.
  • Separation from power. Data cable never shares a raceway with electrical wiring, crosses it at right angles where the two must meet, and stays clear of compressors, sign transformers and kitchen equipment.
  • Ceiling space. Where a store has a suspended ceiling, cable rides on its own supports rather than on the ceiling grid, sprinkler pipes or ductwork, and plenum-rated cable is used where the space above the ceiling carries return air.
  • Exits and walking paths. Nothing we install blocks an exit sign or path, crosses a floor where customers walk, or hangs where a delivery cart could catch it.
Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Bath Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bath Beach owners, boards and business people raise: households sharing one house, tenants coming and going, new buildings handed over by developers, and stores along the commercial streets. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Houses with more than one household

Our first-floor tenant is moving out and we will use that apartment ourselves. Can the jacks already in it be switched over to our network instead of being rewired?

Usually yes, and it can take minutes rather than a day. If the apartment's cables come back to a labeled panel in the cellar, changing which network they belong to is a matter of moving patch cords at the panel from the tenant's equipment to yours. If the cables run straight to the tenant's old router with no panel in between, we terminate them on a panel first, test each one, and then connect them to your network. Either way the walls stay closed. While we are there, we confirm that no cable from your new space still leads to anything of the tenant's, and we update the port map so the labels match how the house is now used.

Two of our three apartments are rented to relatives and one to a stranger. Can the relatives share a printer and files while the third apartment stays completely separate?

Yes. The cable plan stays the same, one bundle to each apartment from the cellar panel, and the separation is done in the equipment. The two related households can sit on one network, or on two networks that are allowed to reach each other's printer, while the third apartment's network has no path to either. If the third household has its own internet service, its cable simply connects to its own provider's equipment at the panel and never touches the others at all. If everyone shares one internet line, a managed switch and router keep the third apartment isolated while still giving it the connection. We label the panel so it is obvious which ports belong to which arrangement.

Each apartment in our two-family wants its own video doorbell at the shared front door. Can that wiring be part of the cabling job?

It can, and doing it together saves opening the same walls twice. A wired video doorbell or entry station needs a cable from the door to wherever its power and network come from, and with two households, each unit needs to reach the right apartment. The cleanest arrangement is a small two-button entry station at the front door, wired to the cellar panel, with each apartment's indoor station or app on its own network. Two separate consumer doorbells side by side also work if each gets its own cable. Entry stations and cameras are installed under our NYS license, and we show you both options with prices before you choose.

New buildings and their boards

Our condo board just took control of a new Cropsey Avenue building from the sponsor, and nobody can log in to the lobby network equipment. What should we get from the sponsor before their team leaves?

Four things, in writing. First, the administrator logins for every switch, router, firewall, camera recorder and entry panel the building owns, changed to accounts the board controls. Second, the port map: which panel port feeds which room, camera, reader or amenity space. Third, the test results for the cable, if the installer certified it, which tell you whether a later fault is new or was there from the start. Fourth, the contracts and account numbers for the building's internet service and any monitoring. If any of that is missing, we can trace and label the telecom room, test the existing runs and reset the equipment to the board's accounts, so the building is not dependent on a developer's contractor who has moved on.

We are opening a daycare on the ground floor of a new Bath Beach building. What should be cabled for check-in, staff tablets and cameras parents can view?

Plan for three separate networks on one cable plant. The office and check-in station need wired connections for the computer, the printer and the check-in tablet's charging spot. The classrooms need strong Wi-Fi for staff tablets, which means a ceiling access point in each room or between pairs of rooms, each on its own cable. Cameras need their own cable and their own network, with the recorder locked in the office or the telecom room and viewing access set up so parents see only what you choose to share. We start from the developer's telecom room or the cabinet in your space, keep the guest Wi-Fi apart from staff devices, and install the cameras under our license.

Our store in a strip near Ceasar's Bay shares one ceiling space with the stores on either side. How do we keep our cable separate and identifiable from theirs?

By giving it its own support and its own labels from end to end. Where a ceiling space runs over several tenants, cable from different stores tends to end up lying together on the same tiles and pipes, and when one tenant leaves, nobody can tell which bundle to remove. We hang your cable on its own J-hooks, label each cable at both ends and at the point where it enters your space, and record the route on a drawing. If the landlord has a designated pathway for tenant cabling, we use it. When you move, the next contractor can find and remove exactly what is yours, and your neighbors' work never disturbs it.

Stores and offices on the commercial streets

Our shop on 86th Street is rebuilding its front after a window was smashed. What cable should go in before the new glass and gate are installed?

Anything that will live on or near the front. A camera inside the window looking out and one at the door, each with its own cable back to a recorder locked away from the counter. A cable to the register and card reader position if the counter is moving. A cable for an alarm keypad or glass-break sensor if the alarm is being redone. And a spare cable with a pull string to the top of the storefront, in case you add a sign display or a second camera later. While the front is open, those runs can be hidden in the new framing instead of stapled across the finished wall afterward. We coordinate with the glazier and the gate installer so nothing is drilled through their work.

Our office on Bay Parkway is switching to internet desk phones. Does each phone need its own cable, or can the computer plug in through the phone?

Most internet desk phones have a second network port, so a computer can plug into the phone and share one cable to the wall. It works, and it is the right choice when a desk has only one jack and opening the wall would be hard. When we are pulling cable anyway, we suggest two cables to each desk: one for the phone, powered from a switch so the phone needs no power brick, and one for the computer. That keeps the phone working if the computer's cable is unplugged, and lets the phones stay up on the battery backup when the power drops. The powered switch sits in the cabinet with the rest of the network.

Our shop has a refrigeration compressor and a sign transformer near where the cable would run. Does that call for shielded cable?

Usually not, if the cable can keep its distance. Standard unshielded cable resists ordinary interference well, and the first fix is routing: keeping data cable away from motors, compressors and transformers and crossing power wiring at right angles. Shielded cable makes sense where the run has no choice but to pass close to heavy electrical equipment for a long stretch, as in some kitchens and back rooms. It costs more, needs shielded jacks and panels, and must be grounded correctly or it can do more harm than good. Our price list adds $50 per drop for shielded cable, and we recommend it only for the runs that need it, not for the whole store.

Getting the most from fiber

A fiber installation is booked for next week. Should the inside cabling be done before or after the provider comes?

Ideally the planning comes before and the final connection after. If we have already decided where your panel or cabinet will sit, you can ask the provider's technician to put the fiber box and router near it, which saves a cable later. If the inside cable is already pulled and terminated, the provider's technician can connect straight into it on the day. What we would not do is leave it to the provider to decide where the box goes with no plan, because providers tend to choose the nearest convenient wall, often a front room or a low spot in the cellar. If your installation date is close, send us a photo of the cellar and the rooms you care about and we will tell you what to ask for.

These are claims that recur in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased or quoted where we read them, each followed by what Bath Beach houses and buildings actually show.

"Wiring a home for Ethernet costs about $3,800"

Angi's article on who can install Ethernet wiring says the average cost of professional installation is $3,800, against about $410 to do it yourself. Figures like that circulate widely in AI answers because they are simple and quotable.

An average hides the two things that decide a Bath Beach price: how many drops the house needs and how many households it serves. "Call an electrician to run your Ethernet"

Angi's highlights say electricians, not internet providers, are responsible for installing Ethernet cables, and that hiring a local electrician is typically the best option. Many AI answers repeat the advice.

Electricians can do this work, and some do it well, but data cabling is its own trade with its own testing, labeling and termination standards. The city's Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 now says no electrical permit is needed for wiring under 50 volts for communication and data when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. What matters for your house is whether the installer tests and documents every run, and whether the same company can install the cameras and entry stations that ride on the cable, which in New York is licensed security work.

"One good router covers a typical house"

Consumer advice often says a single modern router, placed centrally, covers an ordinary house, and a mesh kit fills any gaps.

The typical Bath Beach house is a semi-attached brick building with two or three apartments, and city sales data shows two-family houses changing hands about four times as often as one-family houses. The router sits in one household's apartment or in the cellar, and its signal has to cross floors and brick to reach anyone else. Mesh units pass their signal from one to the next through the air, losing strength with each floor. For the upstairs household, the answer is almost always its own cable from the cellar, ending at its own access point.

We plan the network around the households in the house, not around a floor plan that assumes one family.

"Faster internet service fixes slow Wi-Fi"

When Wi-Fi feels slow, generic answers often suggest upgrading the internet plan.

But a gigabit line ending at a router in a cellar cannot deliver gigabit speeds to a back bedroom two floors up through brick, and the slow part is the last thirty feet, not the line from the street. People upgrade, pay more, and see no change upstairs.

Before you pay for a faster plan, a cable to the rooms that matter is usually the better spend, and we can show you where the speed is being lost.

"Cat6 carries 10 gigabits, so cable type is not worth worrying about"

Angi's guide says Cat6 carries 10 Gbps over 180 feet, and AI answers often stop there, as if any Cat6 cable from any installer performs the same.

The rating describes what a correctly installed run can do. A run that has been kinked around a cellar pipe, untwisted too far at the jack, stapled flat or left next to a compressor may not reach it. In a three-family house where one cable climbs past two apartments to reach the third, the only way to know the run performs is to test it. For ceiling access points and long runs, Cat6A at $250 a drop gives more headroom.

We test every drop's wiremap as standard and certify runs at $20 per drop when you want a report that proves what each cable can carry.

"Wireless means you can skip the cable"

Many answers present wireless cameras, wireless doorbells and Wi-Fi access points as the modern alternative to running cable at all.

Access points are wireless to your phone, but the good ones get their data and power from a cable. Wireless cameras need a power source and a strong signal, and they go blind when the Wi-Fi drops or the router is unplugged, which matters on commercial streets where News 12 reported 19 window-smash burglaries across Brooklyn in six weeks of 2026, Bath Beach among the neighborhoods hit. Why does an access point drop cost $300 when a data drop starts at $175?

The demarc is the point where the provider's responsibility ends, usually the box or modem where its line enters the cellar. A demarc extension carries that handoff by cable to where your equipment should actually live, such as a closet upstairs away from cellar water or a back office in a store. Is a wall-mounted cabinet enough, or does a store need a floor-standing rack?
