The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Lefferts Gardens Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Prospect Lefferts Gardens sits between Empire Boulevard and Clarkson Avenue, with Prospect Park along its western edge and New York Avenue on the east, and almost everything inside those lines was built long before anyone imagined a home network. The heart of the neighborhood is Lefferts Manor, where James Lefferts divided his family's land into roughly 600 lots in 1893 and attached a deed rule that each one carry a single-family house of brick or stone, at least two stories tall. Around the Manor are row houses, prewar elevator apartment buildings along Ocean Avenue and Flatbush Avenue, walk-ups on the side streets, a handful of postwar co-ops and, since 2015, a new generation of towers. None of these buildings came with a wire in the wall for anything but a telephone and, later, cable television. That is the first reason a network here has to be planned rather than improvised.

The second reason is that the neighborhood's households now ask much more of their connection than the buildings were designed to carry. A Manor house that once had one phone jack in the front hall may now hold two adults working from home on different floors, a teenager on a game console in the top-floor bedroom, a television streaming in the parlor and a doorbell camera at the stoop. Wi-Fi from a single router on the parlor floor rarely reaches all of that cleanly. Brick party walls, plaster on wood lath or metal lath, and floors stacked four high absorb the signal long before it gets to the far rooms. Adding wireless boxes in a chain helps a little and costs speed at every hop. The fix that lasts is a cable to every place that needs a dependable connection, including cables up to well-placed access points on each floor.

The third reason is that so many Prospect Lefferts Gardens addresses hold more than one household or more than one use. The neighborhood is full of two-family houses, owner-occupied row houses with a tenant upstairs, brownstones split into apartments and storefronts with homes above them. On Parkside Avenue the Kinko houses of the Melrose Parkside Historic District were built with a separate entrance for each duplex unit. Those buildings need two networks that do not interfere with each other, with a clear answer to who owns which cable, which box and which bill. Sorting that out at the start avoids the familiar arrangement where the downstairs household's router quietly serves the upstairs tenant through the floor and nobody can say whose problem an outage is.

The fourth reason is the historic districts themselves. Most of the neighborhood's house blocks lie inside the Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District, designated in 1979, and the smaller Ocean on the Park, Chester Court and Melrose Parkside districts were added later. The Lefferts Manor Association puts it plainly: the district "applies to the exterior of the buildings," and any change to the outside of a home must be approved by the Landmarks Preservation Commission before work begins. For cabling, that means the quick contractor's habit of running a wire up the front of a house, stapling it along a cornice or drilling a fresh hole through the stoop wall is the wrong answer here. The route has to go inside, through the cellar, closets and floors, and that takes someone who knows how these houses are put together.

Put those four facts together and a Prospect Lefferts Gardens cabling job tends to have the same shape. It starts in the cellar, where the provider's line usually arrives and where open joists give us a long horizontal path from the front of the house to the back. It climbs through a chase we find or create, often a closet stacked floor above floor, the space beside an old chimney breast or a disused dumbwaiter or laundry chute. It ends at outlets placed for how the household actually lives, with a labeled panel in the cellar or a closet that anyone can understand later. In apartment buildings the shape is smaller but the same: the provider's wire enters at the hall, and the job is getting cable from there to the rooms without tearing up a prewar apartment.

The Lefferts Manor single-family house: a cellar highway and a hunt for the vertical path

A Manor house is, for cabling, a gift and a puzzle at once. The gift is the cellar. In most of these houses the cellar ceiling is open joists running from the front wall to the back, and that gives us a clean, reachable horizontal path for every run in the house. The provider's line nearly always arrives there too, usually through the front wall below the stoop. The panel, switch and battery backup can go on a cellar wall, high enough to stay dry and low enough to reach, and every cable in the house can begin from that one point.

The puzzle is getting up. A four-story Manor house has plaster walls on every floor, wood trim nobody wants disturbed and floors that line up only roughly from story to story. We look for a natural vertical path before we consider opening anything: closets that stack directly above each other, the space beside a chimney breast, a disused dumbwaiter, an old laundry chute, the cavity behind a built-in bookcase or a pipe chase that a previous renovation left with room to spare. One good vertical path can carry every cable for the upper floors. When none exists, we make one inside a closet, where the patch afterward is out of sight, and we tell you that before the work is priced.

Within each floor the route is usually short, because the cables arrive in the center of the house. From a closet on the third floor we can reach the front and rear bedrooms with short fishes through the walls, often dropping the outlet into a baseboard or the wall just above it so the plaster stays sound. On the parlor floor, where the woodwork and plaster are often at their best, we come up from the cellar directly below the outlet instead of crossing the room. The result is a house with wired desks, televisions and access points on every floor and nothing visible except faceplates.

The Melrose Parkside Kinko house: one building, two front doors, two networks

The Kinko houses on Parkside Avenue were built as two-family homes with a separate entrance for each duplex unit, and that is exactly how they should be cabled. Each unit gets its own provider line, its own router and its own panel, and the cables for each unit run only through that unit's space wherever the building makes it possible. When the owner lives in one duplex and rents the other, this keeps the tenant's internet account, equipment and outages entirely separate from the owner's, which is what both sides usually want once they have lived with the alternative.

The awkward part is that both units usually share a cellar and the provider's lines arrive at the same front wall. We bring both lines in through one planned opening, mount each unit's equipment in its own small cabinet, and label every cable with the unit it belongs to, so a technician from either provider can find the right box without guessing. Where an upper duplex needs a vertical path, we look for one that stays within that unit or within a shared hall, rather than passing through the lower unit's bedroom closet.

Because these rows were designated in 2022, the outside of the houses is now under the Landmarks Preservation Commission. That rules out the quick fix of running a second provider's line up the facade to the upper unit. We plan both units' routes inside from the beginning, and if anything at all would change the exterior, we raise it before work is priced, so the owner can decide whether to seek a permit or choose an inside route.

The side-street row house with a tenant upstairs

Outside the Manor's covenant, many row houses in the neighborhood are owner-occupied on the lower floors with one or two rental apartments above. The cabling question in these houses is ownership. The owner controls the building and the cellar where the cables gather; the tenant controls the apartment and has a lease that may say nothing about network wiring. We cable the owner's floors as a home, and if the owner wants to offer the upstairs apartment a wired connection, we run a separate cable or a small backbone to a jack inside that apartment, terminated in a box the tenant can reach without entering the owner's space.

Where the tenant arranges and pays for the work, we ask for the owner's written approval of the route, because any opening through a floor or a shared wall is a change to the building. That letter protects the tenant when the lease ends and the owner when the next tenant moves in. We also label the upstairs cables in the cellar with the apartment's letter or number, so they are not mistaken for the owner's runs during the next renovation.

The prewar elevator building on Ocean Avenue or Flatbush Avenue

The 1920s apartment buildings along Ocean Avenue, facing the park, and along Flatbush Avenue are solid structures: masonry walls, plaster on metal or clay tile, concrete or tile arch floors and long interior halls. A building like 115 Ocean Avenue, six stories and 90 apartments built in 1925, was not designed for any wiring beyond what fit in the walls when it opened. Inside an apartment, the provider's wire usually enters near the front door or the hall closet. The job is getting cable from there to a bedroom office or a living room television, which is often the longest walk in the apartment.

Fishing through clay tile and plaster is slower and riskier than fishing through wood, so we look first for existing paths: an old telephone conduit, the space above a hall closet, a hollow chase beside a radiator riser, or a dropped soffit from a past kitchen renovation. Where no hidden path exists, a slim surface raceway painted to the wall along a baseboard or the top of a picture rail is often the cleanest and most reversible answer, and in a rental it is usually the only one a landlord will approve. We will tell you honestly which of those your apartment needs before you agree to anything.

Many of these buildings are co-ops, and the board or managing agent will want to know what is being done to the walls and whether anything leaves the apartment. Work that stays entirely inside a unit is usually a simple alteration notice and an insurance certificate. Work that crosses a hall, uses a riser or reaches the roof is a building decision, and we prepare a short scope with photos so the board can approve it at one meeting.

The newer tower or condo: start from what the building already built

The towers that have gone up since 2015 are a different world. The Parkline at 626 Flatbush Avenue, Hello Lenox at 271 Lenox Road and smaller condos like the eight-story building at 185 Ocean Avenue are concrete buildings with a telecom room, a management office and provider equipment already installed on each floor. Inside a unit, cable usually travels in metal-stud walls that are easy to fish, but the wall that separates the unit from the hall belongs to the building, and anything beyond the apartment door is management's decision.

The work in these buildings is mostly about finishing what the developer started. A unit may have a single outlet for internet and one per television, which leaves a home office or a far bedroom on Wi-Fi alone. We add wired outlets and an access point inside the unit, starting from the panel or outlet the building provided, and we send the management office a certificate of insurance and a short description of the work beforehand. Hello Lenox's keyed elevator access and The Parkline's doorman and package room mean our crew is scheduled through the front desk, not just the resident.

Ground-floor businesses in these buildings, and in the new condo planned at 757 Flatbush Avenue just south of the neighborhood line, with 125 feet of retail frontage and a business center listed among its amenities, are separate commercial jobs. They usually need their own provider line from the building's telecom room, coordinated with the developer's engineer before the store's walls are closed.

The storefront with homes above

The commercial avenues are lined with buildings that hold a store or restaurant at street level and apartments or an office upstairs, often sharing a single cellar. The store's network and the upstairs network should be physically separate: separate provider lines where possible, separate panels and separate labels, so a tenant upstairs cannot accidentally unplug the store's register on the way to the boiler. We route the store's cables along its own part of the cellar ceiling and bring them up into the shop through its own floor openings.

Kitchens and back rooms get extra care. Cable is kept well away from hoods, fryers, steam tables and dishwashers, and outlets in a prep area are placed high and protected so that a wet mop or a spill cannot reach them. In salons, the outlets for the front-desk computer and card terminal stay away from wash stations and dryers. These details sound small, but they are the difference between a network that lasts a decade and one that fails within a year.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Prospect Lefferts Gardens Cellar

On a survey in a Prospect Lefferts Gardens house, the technician rarely starts in the room that has the bad Wi-Fi. The first stop is the cellar, flashlight pointed at the ceiling, following the provider's line from where it comes through the front wall. That line tells most of the story. If it is a fiber drop ending at a small terminal, the house already has a fast service and the trouble is distribution. If it is a coaxial cable feeding a modem on the floor of the cellar, the first recommendation is usually moving that equipment up off the floor and onto a proper wall panel. If three or four different providers' lines come through three or four holes, the house has a history worth cleaning up before anything new goes in.

The second thing we check is what else lives on the cellar ceiling. In these houses that can mean old steam pipes, newer water lines, gas piping, electrical cable from several decades of work, and sometimes a forgotten bundle of telephone wire from when the house was divided. Network cable has to keep its distance from electrical runs and away from the heat of steam pipes, and it must never be tied to gas piping. Then we look up through the house for the vertical path: we open closets on each floor, look for stacked closets or a chimney chase, and note where the floors above line up with the joists below. By the end of those fifteen minutes we can usually describe the route aloud, room by room, and the quote is written from that walk, not from a formula.

The Line From the Street

Fiber, Cable or a Box in the Window: How the Internet Reaches a Prospect Lefferts Gardens Home, and Why It Changes the Wiring

Prospect Lefferts Gardens is well served by the standards of the city. BestNeighborhood counts nine residential internet providers covering 99.64% of the neighborhood and an average of 6.5 providers per home. Optimum's cable network is the most widely available. Verizon's fiber and EarthLink's fiber, which rides on the same lines, each reach 95.54% of residents by that count, while Optimum's newer fiber service reaches about 30.75%, concentrated in the southeast, east, south and central parts of the neighborhood, and Astound's fiber about 14.96%, mostly in the southeast and south. Fixed wireless services that put a receiver in a window or on a roof, from T-Mobile, Verizon and Starry, are listed at 96% to 99% coverage.

Those numbers matter for cabling because each kind of service arrives in a different place and asks for a different starting point. A fiber service usually ends at a small optical terminal near where the line enters the building, which in a house is typically the cellar's front wall. A cable service ends at a modem fed by coaxial cable, which can be placed almost anywhere the coax reaches. A fixed wireless service starts wherever the receiver gets the best signal, which may be a top-floor front window, a rear window or the roof. In each case, the best place for the network's panel and switch is not always where the provider's installer left the box. Part of our job is moving the starting point to where it serves the whole house.

Fiber terminals and the cellar wall

When fiber arrives at the cellar, we mount the provider's terminal and the router on a backboard next to the network panel, so a single short cable joins the service to the house wiring. That keeps the equipment off the floor, away from the boiler and the washing machine, and within reach of a battery backup. Every cable in the house then runs from the panel, and the provider's technician, when they come back for a repair, sees a tidy wall with labels instead of a router on a shelf with wires running everywhere.

In apartment buildings the fiber usually reaches each unit through a riser the provider installed, ending at a small box inside the apartment near the entry. We work from that box. Where the box sits in an inconvenient spot, such as behind a coat closet door, we run one cable from it to a better location, usually a closet or a cabinet more central to the apartment, and distribute the rest of the cabling from there.

Window and rooftop receivers

Fixed wireless receivers bring their own wiring question: they need one outdoor-rated cable from the receiver to the inside, and the receiver's best spot is rarely near the room where the network should live. For a top-floor window unit, we run the cable inside the window frame and down through the house to the panel. For a roof-mounted receiver or a community network node, the cable comes through the roof or a bulkhead and down a vertical path, and the roof penetration is sealed by the trade responsible for the roof. NYC Mesh, the volunteer community network, describes its standard building install as "5GHz wifi on the roof" and offers to help with "Lease and insurance paperwork if needed," which is a reminder that rooftop work in a co-op or rental needs the building's approval first.

On a landmarked house, a receiver or antenna that can be seen from the street is an exterior change. Rules and Approvals

Permits, Landmarks, Boards and Covenants: What Governs a Prospect Lefferts Gardens Cabling Job

The rules for data cabling in New York City changed recently, and much of what is written online has not caught up. Local Law 128 of 2024 added a section to the city's Administrative Code providing that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. It took effect on December 21, 2025, with the 2025 New York City Electrical Code. For nearly every network cabling job in a Prospect Lefferts Gardens house, apartment or store, that means no Department of Buildings electrical filing for the cable itself.

No permit does not mean no rules. Cable that passes through a floor or a fire-rated wall in an apartment building must be carried in a sleeve and sealed with listed firestop afterward. Cable in a ceiling space that serves as an air return needs a plenum-rated jacket, and cable climbing between floors in a shared riser needs a riser rating. Fire alarm wiring is a separate licensed trade and is never part of a data cabling job. And any new electrical outlet for a rack, a switch or an access point's power supply is electrical work for a licensed electrician, which we coordinate but do not perform.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission and the outside of the house

Most of the neighborhood's house blocks are inside the Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District, designated on October 9, 1979, and the later Ocean on the Park (2009), Chester Court (2014) and Melrose Parkside (2022) districts cover additional rows. The Commission's jurisdiction covers the outside of designated buildings. For cabling, that means a new hole through the facade, a wire run up the front of the house, a box on the stoop or a visible antenna is an exterior change that needs the Commission's approval first, and its permit guidebook calls for conduit to be concealed where an installation needs it.

Inside work is outside the Commission's reach, which is why our routes in the historic districts are designed to stay inside: from the existing provider entry, through the cellar, up through closets and chases, to the rooms. When a job truly needs something exterior, for example a new provider entry where none exists or a receiver on the roof, we describe it in writing before work is priced, so the owner can apply for a permit or decide to change the plan. Commission staff indicated when the Melrose Parkside rows were designated that minor changes would likely be approvable at the staff level, but approval is the owner's application, not something an installer can assume.

The Lefferts Manor covenant

The Manor's deed restriction, still enforced by the Lefferts Manor Association, requires single-family use. That has no effect on network wiring as such, but it does shape what we are asked to do. We are sometimes asked to wire a Manor house as if it were a two-family, with separate networks and entrances for a second household. We will build separate networks for family members, a home office or a live-in relative within a single-family home, but the covenant question is the owner's, not ours, and we do not design the job around a use the deed does not allow.

Co-op boards, condo managers and landlords

In the neighborhood's co-ops and condos, the board or managing agent usually wants a certificate of insurance naming the building and its agent, a short scope of work, and work scheduled within the building's hours. Anything that leaves the apartment, a hall run, a riser, a roof penetration, needs the building's specific approval. Renters need the landlord's approval for any opening through a wall or floor; surface raceway that can be removed at move-out is often the route a landlord will accept. We prepare the paperwork the building asks for, so the approval comes the first time it is requested.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Prospect Lefferts Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the situations Prospect Lefferts Gardens owners, tenants, boards and businesses describe when they call: getting a connection to every floor of a tall house, splitting one building into two networks, working inside a prewar co-op, and keeping a store online through a long day.

Manor houses and other tall homes

Our Manor house has a chimney breast running up through every floor. Can the network cables climb beside it instead of through the walls?

Often, yes. The space between a chimney breast and the side wall, or behind a closet built against it, is one of the most common vertical paths in these houses, because it lines up from the cellar to the top floor. We check that the space is clear, that the cable keeps its distance from the flue itself and any working fireplace, and that it is not already carrying electrical cable, then bring one bundle up to each floor and branch out from there.

We want wired desks and a television jack on the parlor floor without disturbing the original plaster moldings and wainscoting. Can it be done?

Yes, and the parlor floor is usually the easiest floor to do it on, because the cellar is directly below. We place outlets in the baseboard or the wall just above it and come straight up from the cellar ceiling, so the cable never crosses the room inside the wall and the moldings above stay untouched. Where a desk sits in the middle of a room, a floor box or a jack inside a built-in can work, and we show you the options before anything is drilled.

Our Manor house was one of the last built, in the early 1950s. Is it simpler to wire than the older houses on the block?

Usually somewhat. Houses built at the tail end of the Manor's development tend to have more regular framing, walls that line up better floor to floor and sometimes an existing chase for later utilities, which makes fishing cable faster. They still have plaster or early drywall and a cellar route, so the approach is the same, but the survey often finds a cleaner vertical path and fewer surprises behind the walls.

The cellar in our house gets damp during heavy rain. Where should the network equipment go?

Not on the cellar floor, and ideally not in the cellar at all if it is wet. We can mount the panel high on a dry cellar wall with the cables entering from above, or move the router, switch and battery backup up to a closet on the first floor and run a single cable from the provider's entry to it. The decision depends on where the provider's line arrives and how damp the space really gets, which is why we ask about it on the survey.

Two-family houses and owners with tenants

We own a Kinko house on Parkside Avenue and rent out the upper duplex. Can the tenant have a separate provider and network without sharing our modem?

Yes, and that is how these houses work best. We bring a second provider line through the same planned opening in the front wall, mount it in its own small cabinet labeled for the upper unit, and run the cables for that unit only through its own space or a shared hall. The tenant opens an account in their own name, and an outage or a password change on one side never affects the other.

The upstairs tenant in our row house wants a wired connection for remote work. Who should pay, and who owns the cable when they move out?

That is for the owner and the tenant to agree, and we recommend putting it in writing before work begins. Cable that is built into the walls usually becomes part of the building, so most owners either pay for it as an improvement or approve the tenant's work with the understanding that it stays. Surface raceway inside the apartment can be removed at move-out if the lease requires it. Either way, we need the owner's written approval for any opening through a floor or shared wall.

Our two-family house has one cable line feeding both floors through a splitter in the cellar. Should each unit have its own line?

If both households subscribe separately, yes. A splitter that feeds two modems from one line can work, but it divides the signal and makes it hard to tell whose equipment is causing trouble. We replace it with separate lines from the provider's entry to each unit's equipment, labeled by unit, so each provider's technician can see exactly what serves which apartment.

Prewar apartments, co-ops and towers

Our Ocean Avenue co-op has clay tile walls under the plaster. Will a raceway look out of place in a prewar living room?

Not if it is chosen and placed well. A slim raceway painted to the wall and run along the top of a baseboard, the edge of a door casing or a picture rail disappears in most prewar rooms. Before we suggest it, we look for hidden paths, such as an old telephone conduit, a hall closet ceiling or a soffit from a past kitchen renovation. Raceway is the fallback, not the default, and the quote says which one your apartment gets.

The fiber box in our tower apartment ended up inside the coat closet by the door. Can the network start somewhere more useful?

Yes. We leave the provider's box where it is and run a single cable from it to a better spot, usually a closet or cabinet near the middle of the apartment, where the router and a small switch can sit. The rest of the apartment's jacks and access points then run from there. Inside your unit that is usually a simple job, and the building's management office only needs a certificate of insurance and a short description before we arrive.

Our co-op board asked for a written scope before approving a cable through the hallway closet. What should that scope contain?

The route on a simple floor plan, the cable type and its jacket rating, how many openings will be made and how each will be sealed, the hours the crew will work, and a certificate of insurance naming the cooperative and its managing agent. Photos of the closet and hallway before work help. We prepare that document for the board, because a clear one usually gets approved at the first meeting rather than coming back with questions.

Stores, offices and rooftops

Our salon on Rogers Avenue needs a card terminal, a front-desk computer and Wi-Fi for clients. How many cables is that?

Usually three or four: one to the card terminal, one to the front-desk computer, one to a ceiling access point that carries both the staff and client networks, and often a spare at the desk for a printer or a second terminal. Each one runs to its own labeled port on a small switch in a cabinet at the back, so a fault in one never takes down the others, and the client network is kept apart from the payment terminal.

Our restaurant on Flatbush Avenue shares a cellar with the apartments above. Where should our network equipment live?

In a locked cabinet in the restaurant's own space, ideally on the ground floor or high on a wall in the part of the cellar the lease gives you. The provider's line comes in, the router and switch sit in the cabinet on a battery backup, and the cables run along your part of the cellar ceiling and up through your floor. Keeping it separate from the building's own equipment means no tenant or super can unplug your terminals by accident.

We want to join a rooftop community network on our building. Can you run the cable from the roof down to our apartment?

Yes, once the building has approved the rooftop equipment. We run an outdoor-rated cable from the roof unit through the bulkhead or an approved opening, down a vertical path to your apartment, and terminate it at your router. On a landmarked building we also check that the equipment and cable cannot be seen from the street, because a visible antenna is an exterior change that needs the Commission's approval first.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Prospect Lefferts Gardens

Ask an AI assistant, Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr about wiring a Brooklyn home for ethernet and you will get confident, general answers. Some of them hold up. Several do not survive contact with a Manor house, a Parkside two-family or a prewar co-op. These are the claims we hear most often, and what the work looks like on the ground.

"Just get faster internet; the plan is the problem"

Most general advice starts with the provider: upgrade the plan, switch to fiber, buy a bigger router. In much of the country that is reasonable. In Prospect Lefferts Gardens, BestNeighborhood lists fiber as the fastest available type for 82.21% of homes, with Verizon's fiber reaching 95.54% of residents.

So the plan is rarely the limit. A gigabit service delivered to a cellar wall does nothing for a bedroom four floors up behind brick and plaster. Households in these houses often pay for speed they never see on the top floor, then pay again for a faster plan that changes nothing.

The correction is to measure the house, not the plan. Test speed at the router and in the far rooms. If the router is fast and the rooms are slow, the answer is cable and access points, not a new contract.

"Mesh Wi-Fi solves coverage in any home"

AI answers often recommend a mesh kit as the universal fix. Mesh works well in wood-frame homes with open floor plans, and it is easy to set up.

In a row house with brick party walls and four stacked floors, each wireless hop between mesh units loses a share of the speed, and the units have to be placed where the signal from the previous one still arrives, which is rarely where the household needs them.

Mesh units that are wired back to the router over cable behave like proper access points and keep their full speed. In these houses the cable is what makes the Wi-Fi work.

"Any electrician can run your ethernet"

Angi says plainly that "A local electrician should tackle this task because it requires significant electrical work." Network cable is not electrical work in the sense that statement implies; under Local Law 128 of 2024, data wiring under 50 volts needs no electrical permit in New York City.

An electrician can certainly pull cable. What is often missing is termination to standard, testing with a certification analyzer, labeling and a port map. Without those, a cable that looks fine may fail at gigabit speeds, and nobody will know which wall plate leads where.

The better question for any installer is simple: will every link be certified, and will I get the results? If the answer is no, the job is not finished.

"Cabling a historic home means tearing open the walls"

Some general answers warn that wiring an older home means major demolition, and others assume a wire can simply be run along the outside of the house. Both are wrong here.

A Manor house's cellar gives a long open path, and closets, chimney chases and old dumbwaiters usually provide a way up. Meanwhile, the outside of the house belongs to the historic district: the Lefferts Manor Association notes that exterior alterations need the Commission's approval before work begins.

The real answer is an inside route planned with care. In most houses we open a few small, hidden spots, not entire walls, and we leave the facade exactly as it was.

"A national cost guide tells you what your job costs"

Fixr puts the average price per Cat6 data drop at "$125 to $250, including materials and labor," and Angi gives an average of $3,800 for professional ethernet installation. Those figures are national averages built from many kinds of homes.

They say nothing about how long a fish through clay tile takes, whether a co-op requires work in limited hours, or whether a Kinko house needs two separate panels. The same number of outlets can be a short day or a long one depending on the route.

The only number worth planning around is a written quote from someone who has walked your house. Ours lists every outlet and every opening, at the Brooklyn rate.

"Renters can't add wired connections"

AI answers sometimes tell renters to forget about cable and rely on Wi-Fi. In a large prewar apartment, that leaves a home office in the back bedroom on a weak signal.

With the landlord's written approval, a renter can usually have cable run in slim raceway that is removed at move-out, or through a hidden path that becomes part of the building. Many owners approve this readily when the work is clean and documented.

The step people skip is the written approval. We ask for it before work begins, which protects the renter at the end of the lease.

"Put the equipment in the basement, out of the way"

The common advice is to tuck the router and switch in the basement. In many houses here the cellar is the right place to start, because the provider's line arrives there.

But a router on the cellar floor next to the boiler and the washing machine is exposed to heat, dust and water, and the Wi-Fi from it serves the cellar best of all.

We mount equipment on a dry wall panel off the floor, on battery backup, and serve the house from wired access points on each floor rather than one router below everyone's feet.
