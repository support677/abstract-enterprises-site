The Neighborhood

Why Wyckoff Heights Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

Wyckoff Heights is a neighborhood that even its own sources argue about. Wikipedia describes it as an area that straddles the border of Bushwick in Brooklyn and the southwest corner of Ridgewood in Queens, mostly in ZIP codes 11237 and 11385, and named for the Wyckoff family whose farmland it was built on from the late nineteenth century. Forgotten New York, writing about Wyckoff Avenue in 2008, wondered aloud whether the name had been coined by the hospital that carries it. The same site notes that the borough line runs down the middle of Wyckoff Avenue for about a mile, and Wikipedia's article on the Myrtle–Wyckoff Avenues station places the border along Wyckoff between Gates Avenue and Eldert Street. History adds one more twist: because the street grid ran at an angle to the old boundary near Arbitration Rock, some houses were built across the county line, and their owners were at times taxed by both counties. For a door buzzer, none of that changes the repair. The housing rules that govern an entrance are city rules, and a strike, a transformer or a smart lock behaves the same on either side of the avenue. What the border does change is how people describe where they live, which is why we ask for the street and the nearest corner rather than the neighborhood name.

The buildings tell the more useful story. The city's Bushwick Neighborhood Plan describes the side streets of Bushwick as dominated by owner-occupied two- and three-family rowhouses and says the city wants to keep that rowhouse character, while Broadway, Myrtle Avenue and Wyckoff Avenue were planned for denser building. Wikipedia's Wyckoff Heights article says the first buildings in the area were attached wood-frame three- and six-family rows, that masonry construction arrived around 1900, and that the last open land was filled in the 1940s with attached houses served by alleys and rear garages. Forgotten New York's 2016 walk describes Wyckoff Avenue near Myrtle as lined with handsome apartment buildings from the 1910s or earlier with businesses on the ground floor, and a more classic walk-up apartment house between Greene Avenue and Bleecker Street. A rental listing at 293 Wyckoff Avenue describes a railroad-style apartment with its own separate entrance to the building's hallway. A listing at 287 Wyckoff Avenue advertises a virtual doorman and smart locks on all doors. Further north, Forgotten New York found the stretch from Flushing Avenue to about Starr Street still largely industrial in 2008, its lofts increasingly taken over by artists and musicians. Just across the line in Ridgewood are the Mathews Model Flats, three-story brick tenements built between 1908 and 1914, two families to a floor around large air shafts.

Put those sources together and a Wyckoff Heights buzzer call usually comes from one of four very different doors. The first is the residents' door of a walk-up over a shop, a narrow door that carries every apartment's security and takes the traffic of a busy avenue. Its bell plate and release serve only the upstairs flats, while the shop keeps its own entrance, gate and often its own alarm. The second is the street door of a brick walk-up or a wood-frame row on a side street, where a single small transformer in the cellar powers every bell and the strike. The third is the front door of a two- or three-family house, where the owner downstairs answers in person and the tenants upstairs depend on a button. The fourth is the entrance of a newer building that has replaced buttons with an app, a keypad or a smart lock, where the fault may sit in a battery, a network or an account rather than a wire. A technician who walks in expecting one of these and finds another loses time, so the first questions on the phone are always about the door and the building, not the brand on the plate.

Most Wyckoff Heights buzzer calls fall into one of four patterns, and each tells the technician where to start. In a walk-up over a storefront, the common complaint is that the residents' door buzzes but will not open, or opens only when someone leans on it. That points first to the door: a latch pressed against a keeper that has shifted, a closer that slams the door hard enough to bend the strike, or a frame knocked out of line by hand trucks and deliveries. In a brick walk-up or a frame row, the common complaint is that some apartments still ring and others have gone quiet, which points to the split in the cellar or the hall and the run to the silent flats. In a two- or three-family house, the usual story is that the owner's bell still works and the tenant's does not, which tells us the transformer is alive and the fault is on the tenant's branch. In a smart-lock building, the usual story is that the app, keypad or lock stopped answering, and the first checks are the lock's own batteries, the hub, the network and the account. In every type of building there is one call nobody should sit on: a street door that will not close or will not lock.

We get that door closing and locking before anything else, even when a part for the rest of the system has to be ordered. The walk-up over a shop: one narrow door carrying a whole building

The buildings Forgotten New York describes near Myrtle and Wyckoff were built with businesses on the street and apartments above, and the residents' entrance is usually a separate, narrow door between or beside the shopfronts. The listing at 293 Wyckoff Avenue describes exactly that: a separate entrance to the hallway of the building. That door carries all of the building's security, so it is also the door residents are most tempted to wedge when the buzzer dies. It faces a busy sidewalk and absorbs the knocks of deliveries for the shop as well as the apartments. Its strike, keeper, closer and hinges wear together, and a closer set hard enough to beat the wind will slam the door until the keeper bends and the latch no longer seats. When we are called to one of these doors, we watch it close half a dozen times before we touch a wire. We look at whether the latch enters the strike cleanly, whether the door bounces, and whether the frame has been patched around old hardware.

The cellar is the other half of the story. In many of these buildings the shop and the apartments share a single cellar, and the transformer for the residents' bells may be screwed to a joist beside the shop's stock, its compressor lines or its own alarm panel. Shop renovations are a common reason a buzzer stops working: a new ceiling, a new sign, a relocated outlet or a cleared-out cellar can cut a pair that nobody knew was there. We find the supply that actually feeds the residents' door, label it, and note what else shares its circuit. Any work on the line-voltage side, such as giving the transformer its own circuit or moving an outlet, goes to a licensed electrician, because the Department of Buildings requires a license and a permit for electrical work. Our work stays on the low-voltage side and at the door. Before a visit, we ask the owner to arrange access to the cellar with the shop, since a locked cellar is the most common reason a visit runs long.

The early brick walk-up: two families a floor, one supply for all

The brick walk-ups of the 1910s, on Wyckoff Avenue and on the blocks across the line, were built to the tenement standards of their time, and the Landmarks Commission's reports on the nearby Mathews Model Flats describe three-story brick buildings with large air shafts, private bathrooms and two families on each floor. We have no source describing how any particular Wyckoff Heights walk-up was wired. What we see in buildings of this kind across Brooklyn is a single plate at the street door, a single small supply in the cellar and a wire that rises into the building at one or two points before splitting for each flat. When one apartment falls silent, the break is usually between the split and that apartment. When every button goes faint together, the supply is struggling, often because something has been added to it, such as a video doorbell, a hallway light or a second chime.

In a building with two flats on each landing, the stations for a floor are often close together and may share a run for part of the way. That is why we test both apartments on a floor even when only one is reported, and why we ask tenants which apartments are empty or under renovation. A gutted apartment between tenants is a common place for a pair to be cut or a station to be left disconnected. We also ask whether the building has a vestibule. Many walk-ups of this age open straight into the stair hall, so the street door carries everything, and the closer has to be set to latch reliably without slamming. When the owner wants it, we leave a card in the cellar showing which button goes to which apartment, where the split is and which transformer feeds it.

The attached wood-frame row: doors and frames that move

Wikipedia's article names attached wood-frame three- and six-family rows as the first buildings in Wyckoff Heights. A frame building settles and moves differently from a brick one. Floors deflect, door frames rack, and a door that latched cleanly in March can bind in August. For a buzzer, that movement usually shows up as a strike that buzzes while the door stays shut, because the latch is pressed hard against one edge of the keeper and the strike cannot free it. The fix is often at the door, not in the wire: a hinge screw replaced with a longer one, a keeper moved or shimmed, or a strike chosen for the frame's actual profile. We check how the door hangs in its frame before deciding that a strike has failed.

The wiring in these buildings tends to run through the cellar joists and up through the frame walls, often along the stair. A run in a frame wall is easier to reach than one in solid masonry, but it is also easier for later work to disturb, since every new outlet, pipe or layer of siding is a chance to nick a pair. In a six-family the supply has to serve six stations and a strike from one small transformer, so we measure it under load. Where an owner wants the system more resilient, we can suggest separating the run that feeds the strike from the runs that feed the stations, so one damaged branch does not silence the whole house.

The side-street two- or three-family: an owner downstairs, tenants upstairs

The city's Bushwick plan describes the side streets as owner-occupied two- and three-family rowhouses, and that arrangement shapes every buzzer repair in them. The owner often lives on the ground floor and answers the door in person, while the tenants upstairs rely on a button that releases the street door. When the tenant's bell goes silent while the owner's still rings, the transformer is rarely to blame, since it is plainly still running the owner's half. The fault is on the tenant's branch: the button, the run up the stair, a splice nobody has opened for years, or the station itself. When the bell rings upstairs but the release does nothing, the suspects are the return pair, the contact in the station, or a strike that cannot free a latch pressed against it. We test every household's branch even when only one is reported.

These houses are where video doorbells most often appear, and where they most often cause trouble. A video doorbell on the owner's door can work on the existing pair if the transformer can supply it, but it does not replace a tenant's release on a shared street door, and it may starve an undersized transformer that also feeds the strike. If the owner wants one, we check the transformer first and point out where the camera would also see a neighbor's stoop. Wikipedia also notes the 1940s attached houses with alleys and rear garages. In those houses the bell wire may run through a garage ceiling, and the transformer may share an outlet with garage equipment, so we check the outlet and its protection along with the bell.

The loft at the north end: heavy doors and equipment nobody documented

Forgotten New York's 2008 walk found Wyckoff Avenue from Flushing Avenue to about Starr Street still largely industrial, with its lofts increasingly occupied by artists and musicians. Loft buildings put different demands on a buzzer. The street door is often a heavy steel door in a steel or masonry frame, sometimes a former loading entrance, and it needs a strike rated for its weight and a closer strong enough to pull it shut against a strong wind without slamming it off the frame. The runs from the street to the units can be long, and in buildings converted piece by piece the stations may come from several makers and several decades. Some tenants add their own bells or cameras at their unit doors, which can confuse anyone tracing the building's own system.

The smart-entry building: batteries, a hub, a network and a login

The listing at 287 Wyckoff Avenue advertises a virtual doorman and smart locks on all doors. In a building like that, the chain between a visitor and the door is longer than in a walk-up. A courier or guest may reach a remote attendant or a resident's phone, the release command may travel over the internet, and the lock itself may run on batteries with its own wireless link to a hub. When the door stops opening, the cause can be a dead lock battery, a hub that lost power, a router replaced by the internet provider, an expired account or a door that no longer lines up with the lock. We check the door hardware and the lock's batteries first, because those are the cheapest faults to fix and the easiest to miss, then the hub and network path back to the closet, and then the account settings with whoever holds the administrator login.

Smart locks also change what happens when something fails. A lock that runs on its own batteries may keep working through a power cut but stop when its batteries run out, and its behavior then depends on how it was installed and set. We test what the residents' door does with the lock's batteries low, with the hub unplugged and with the building's internet down, and we confirm that people inside can always get out without a key, a phone or a code. In a rental, the management company normally holds the account and approves the work. We ask for the login before the visit, because some repairs cannot be finished without it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Residents' Door on Wyckoff Avenue Before Touching the Cellar

This is not a story about one particular job. It describes how our technicians approach the kind of door Wyckoff Avenue has more of than most Brooklyn streets: the narrow residents' door beside a shopfront, leading to a stair hall and a few floors of flats. The checks are the same ones we use everywhere. The order is what matters, because on this kind of door the most likely fault is usually the one nobody looks at first.

First, the door, with no electricity involved. The technician closes the door gently and watches the latch go into the strike, then lets the closer shut it and watches again. On a door that has been slammed for decades, the keeper is often bent, the latch often catches on one edge, and the frame often shows patches around older hardware. If the latch is pinned, the strike will buzz and the door will stay shut until someone shoves it, and residents will report a broken buzzer. A hinge, keeper or closer adjustment fixes that without any electrical part changing hands.

Second, the plate and the stations. Only then does the technician press each button and listen at each station, ideally with a resident or the owner upstairs. The pattern of silent and working apartments points to where the break is. One dead flat points to its own run or station. Every flat dead points to the supply or the release circuit. Every flat faint points to a supply carrying more than it was meant to carry.

Third, the cellar, with the shop's agreement. Downstairs, the technician finds every transformer on the wall, not just the obvious one, and measures the one feeding the door while someone upstairs holds the door button. He looks at what else shares its outlet or circuit, and at any recent work in the cellar, such as a new ceiling, new shelving or a moved outlet, that might have disturbed the run. If the shop has its own alarm or camera equipment nearby, he leaves it alone and tells the owner if it appears to share anything with the residents' system.

Last, the same final test every time. Every button is pressed, every station answered, the door released from every flat that has a release, and then the door is watched closing and latching on its own several times. A buzzer that works on a door that does not lock has not been fixed, and on a door that opens onto a busy avenue, that difference matters.

A business door wears differently from a residents' door because of how often and how hard it is used. Compliance notes for Wyckoff Heights buildings and businesses

  • The 1968 date and the eight-apartment count.   • The minimum system. The Rules at the Door

Who Keeps a Wyckoff Heights Buzzer Working, Who Pays, and What the Sources Disagree About

The same dead button can mean very different things depending on the building behind it: a lease problem in a two-family, an owner's obligation in a walk-up whose tenants once asked for an intercom, or a code requirement in a newer building of eight or more apartments. This section sets out what the public sources say and where they disagree. We are not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What we can do is repair the system and leave a written record of what failed and what was fixed.

Most of the neighborhood's stock predates 1968. The walk-ups from the 1910s or earlier that Forgotten New York describes, the frame rows Wikipedia names as the first buildings, and the side-street rowhouses all date from long before January 1, 1968. Under the Attorney General's summary of Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, an older multiple dwelling must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request it. Parker Custom Security's guide puts the request as a written one by tenants occupying a majority of apartments. Whether a particular old building's intercom exists because tenants asked for it, because an owner chose it, or because of a later renovation is a question about that building's own history, and the answer changes how strongly the law reaches the system.

Who pays: a real conflict between sources. The Attorney General's guide says that when tenants in an older building request an intercom, the landlord may recover the cost. Brick Underground's December 2014 article on broken buzzers describes the same rule as an intercom required "if requested by a majority of tenants," at the tenants' expense. Those are not quite the same thing, and an owner and a tenant association negotiating over a new system should read the statute or ask counsel rather than rely on either summary, including this one. For a repair of a system that already exists, the question usually does not arise. Parker Custom Security's guide says that once an intercom exists, a dead buzzer is treated as a hazardous violation, and HPD's own violation wording describes the bell buzzer system at the entrance as something the owner must repair.

Two-families and owner-occupied threes. The side streets the city's Bushwick plan describes are full of two- and three-family houses. A two-family is not a multiple dwelling, so the apartment-building intercom rules do not reach it in the same way. A three-family can be, and the threshold for the firmest requirement is a separate question answered further down this page. In practice, an owner who rents the upper floors has every reason, and often a lease clause, to keep the tenants' bells and release working. A tenant who cannot let in a home aide, a delivery or a guest has a real problem whatever the statute says.

Newer buildings and smart entry. A building on a rezoned stretch of Myrtle or Wyckoff Avenue finished in recent years, with eight or more apartments, is on its face inside the post-1968 rule. Whether a particular app-based or virtual-doorman arrangement satisfies the two-way voice requirement is a question for the building's counsel and, if a complaint is made, for HPD. Our job is narrower: making sure that whatever system the building has can call each apartment, carry voice both ways where it is meant to, release the door, and leave the door self-closing and self-locking, with free exit from inside.

The door is the stricter issue. HPD's self-closing door page leaves no room for doubt: a defective self-closing door is a class C violation with 14 days to correct it. Its FY25 program report describes notices posted in buildings telling tenants to report a failed door to the owner and then to 311. When a release fails, residents start wedging the door, taping the latch or asking for the closer to be loosened, and a building can go from a buzzer complaint to a hazardous-door violation quickly. That is why every buzzer visit we make ends at the door.

Whether your building has an open violation is a question about one address, best answered from HPD's public records or your managing agent.

The line, the code and the paperwork

Our building is on the Queens side of the Wyckoff Avenue centerline, while the listing called it Bushwick. Does HPD apply a different housing code or a different inspection process in Queens?

No. The Housing Maintenance Code and the Multiple Dwelling Law are city and state rules that apply the same way in every borough, and HPD inspects in Brooklyn and Queens alike. A 311 complaint about a broken buzzer is taken the same way on either side of the avenue, and the Department of Buildings' rules on electrical work are citywide. What the borough does change is the record itself. Your building's address, block and lot are filed in one borough or the other, and violations, complaints and permits are listed under that official address, not the neighborhood name a listing uses. Forgotten New York notes that the line runs down the middle of Wyckoff Avenue for about a mile, and history records houses built across the county line whose owners were at times taxed by both counties, so confusion about which side a building sits on is nothing new. If you are trying to look up your building's violations or complaints, use the address on the deed or the tax bill. For the repair itself, nothing changes: the same checks, the same parts on the van and the same final test at the door.

Years ago an inspector accepted the old bell-and-release system in our 1910s walk-up as it was. Can the owner keep relying on that acceptance, or does it run out?

An old acceptance is worth less than it sounds once the system stops working. Administrative Code section 25-151(g), in the text republished on hpdfiling.nyc, describes the minimum acceptable system as a bell or buzzer, or a speaking and listening device, together with a return buzzer that releases the door lock, and it says previously installed devices in good condition may be accepted at the department's discretion. Two parts of that sentence matter. "In good condition" means the acceptance assumes the system works. A plate whose buttons ring nowhere, or a release that hums without opening the door, is not in good condition, whatever an inspector decided about it years ago. And "at the department's discretion" means the decision belongs to HPD, case by case, rather than being a permanent pass attached to the building. In practice, an owner of an old bell-and-release system has two choices when it fails: repair it so that each apartment's bell rings and each release opens the door, or replace it. Repair is usually far cheaper, and in buildings of this age the failure is very often a single transformer, a single splice or the strike at the door. We test the system against what the minimum describes, every bell and every release, and write down the result so the owner has a record of the system's condition after the repair.

One security company says the city's intercom rule covers buildings with three or more apartments, and another says eight. Which applies to our six-family?

The sources do disagree, and it is worth knowing why before you act on either number. Seneca Security cites Administrative Code section 27-2045 and says the requirement applies to most buildings with three or more units. The Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide, summarizing Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, draws its firmest line at multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 that have eight or more apartments, which must have a two-way voice intercom with a buzzer that opens the door. Parker Custom Security says the same, adding that older buildings fall under the rule once tenants occupying a majority of apartments request it in writing. These are different provisions describing different things, some about locks and some about intercoms, and vendor summaries tend to blur them. For a six-family built before 1968, which describes most of the frame rows and walk-ups in Wyckoff Heights, the Attorney General's summary suggests the post-1968 intercom rule does not apply on its face, while the majority-request provision does. That leaves the building's own history as the deciding factor. If the question has real consequences, such as a dispute with tenants or a violation, read the statute itself or ask counsel. For a repair, the numbers do not change the method. If the building has a system, tenants depend on it, and we fix the link that failed.

Locks, gates and shared apartments

The smart lock on our street door runs on its own batteries. What happens when they run down, and whose job is it to change them?

It depends on the lock, so the first step is to find out exactly what is on the door. Many battery-powered smart locks warn through their app or a light well before the batteries are exhausted, and many keep a mechanical way in, such as a key cylinder, for when they finally die. Others behave differently. Some stay locked with dead batteries, which is safe for security but leaves residents with phones and codes that no longer work. What must never change is free exit: a person inside must always be able to open the street door without a key, a phone or a code, whatever state the batteries are in. In a rental building such as the one listed at 287 Wyckoff Avenue with smart locks on every door, the batteries are part of the building's entry system, so changing them falls to whoever maintains that system, normally the management company or its vendor, not individual tenants. We can test the lock, its batteries, its hub and the door it is fitted to, set a sensible battery schedule with management, and confirm what the door does when the lock goes dead. If the door has drifted out of line with the lock, the lock may drain its batteries straining against a binding bolt, which is a door repair rather than a battery problem. Tenants who see repeated low-battery warnings should report them in writing to management, with dates.

When the store below us pulls down its roll-down gate at night, it covers half of our residents' button plate. Can the plate or the gate be changed so visitors can still reach us?

Yes, and it is worth doing, because a plate that visitors cannot reach after the shop closes is a working buzzer that functions as a broken one. On buildings with businesses on the ground floor, such as those Forgotten New York describes along Wyckoff Avenue, the residents' door and the shopfront are often a few inches apart, and a gate added or replaced years after the building went up can end up overlapping the residents' side. There are usually three ways to solve it. The plate can be moved to the residents' door frame or to a flat surface on the residents' side of the opening, outside the gate's path, using the existing wire where it reaches or a short new run where it does not. The gate's guide or housing can sometimes be adjusted by the gate company so it stops short of the residents' door, which is the shop's or owner's job rather than ours. Or, where neither is possible, the call point can move inside a vestibule if the building has one and the outer door can be handled separately. Before moving anything, we check that the new spot is within reach, that the residents' door still opens freely from inside, and that the shop's gate, alarm and lights are untouched. The owner should agree the arrangement with the shop in writing, since the gate is theirs.

The utility shut off the meter that feeds our walk-up's hallway lights, and the buzzer went dead at the same moment. Can you get the buzzer working without the power back?

Not in a way we would leave in place. In many walk-ups, the transformer that runs the bells and the strike is plugged into, or wired from, the same house circuit that runs the hallway lights, so when that service is cut, the buzzer dies with it. That is not a buzzer fault, and no part we fit will fix it. The safe sequence is for the owner to restore the service, after which we check that the transformer and the strike came back undamaged, since a sudden shutoff or restoration can sometimes take out a tired transformer. Running the buzzer from an outlet in a tenant's apartment, or from the shop's supply, is the kind of improvised fix that causes trouble later, and changes to line-voltage circuits belong to a licensed electrician in any case. In the meantime, the important thing is the door. Without power, an electric strike usually stays locked and the door can still be opened from inside by its lever or knob, which is the safe condition, but visitors cannot be let in. A building with a magnetic lock is different, and its door must still release for people leaving. Tenants who lose hallway lighting and the buzzer together can report the conditions to 311, and the owner should treat restoring the common-area service as urgent. Once the power is back, a short visit confirms that every station rings and the door releases and locks.

Three of us share a railroad apartment, and each of us has visitors and deliveries, but the plate has one button for the whole apartment. What are the options?

A building's buzzer is wired one button and one station per apartment, so an apartment shared by roommates, such as the railroad-style flat listed at 293 Wyckoff Avenue, gets one call however many people live there. There are a few practical ways to make that work better without changing the building's system. The simplest is a clear label on the button that the whole household agrees on, so couriers know they have the right apartment, together with a household habit of answering for one another. In a railroad flat, the station is often near one end, so the real problem is usually that the roommates at the far end cannot hear it. A louder station, or a second sounder elsewhere in the apartment fed from the same station, can fix that, and the owner has to approve any change to the building's equipment. In buildings with app-based entry, management may be able to add more than one phone to an apartment's call list, which is a setting rather than a repair. What we would not do is add extra buttons to the plate for individual roommates, because that changes the building's directory and wiring for an arrangement that may change at the next lease. If the station itself is faint or intermittent, that is a repair the owner can book, and we test it alongside the rest of the line.

Our walk-up is described as a 1910s "new law" tenement. Does that label change anything about the front door or the intercom?
+

Not directly. "New law" refers to the tenement standards of that era for light, air and plumbing, which is why the Mathews flats across the line in Ridgewood have large air shafts and private bathrooms. The rules for the entrance come from the Multiple Dwelling Law and the Housing Maintenance Code, which apply according to the building's age, apartment count and history, not its tenement label. The label does tell us roughly what to expect inside: brick walls, a stair hall and wiring that has been changed many times over a century.

Does the city's rezoning plan for Myrtle and Wyckoff Avenues require anything new at the door of our existing walk-up?
+

No. The city's plans allow denser new buildings along Broadway, Myrtle Avenue and Wyckoff Avenue, with affordable housing required in new construction there. Zoning governs what can be built, not how an existing building's door must work. Your walk-up's entrance is still governed by the same housing rules as before. The rezoning can matter indirectly, since construction next door can shift a frame or cut a run, so a door that starts misbehaving when a neighbor breaks ground is worth checking early.

While you are in the building for the buzzer, can you also look at the self-closing hinges or closers on the apartment entrance doors along the hall?
+

Yes, if the owner asks. We adjust closers and hinges on doors that are part of the path from the street to the apartments, and we can check apartment entrance doors on the same visit at the same hourly rate. HPD treats a defective self-closing door as an immediately hazardous violation, so it is sensible to deal with them together.
