The Neighborhood

Why Forest Hills Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Very little in Forest Hills was built by accident, and that is the first thing a soundbar installer has to understand here. In 1906 the Cord Meyer Development Company bought roughly 600 acres of farmland in central Queens and gave it the name Forest Hills, after the park on its southern edge. Two or three years later Margaret Sage's Russell Sage Foundation took 142 of those acres and hired Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. and the architect Grosvenor Atterbury to plan a model village around a railroad station. When the IND subway opened under Queens Boulevard in 1936, a third plan arrived: blocks of mostly six-storey apartment buildings, and after the war, towers. Each plan left a different kind of wall behind, and a different set of people who expect to be consulted before anyone drills into it.

South of the Long Island Rail Road, in Forest Hills Gardens, the walls are often not what a stud finder expects. Atterbury built many of the early houses from standardized precast concrete panels — about 170 of them per house, cast off site with hollow insulating chambers and lifted into place by crane. Later Gardens houses are brick and stucco Tudors with leaded glass, slate or red clay tile roofs and plaster inside. The whole enclave is governed by the Forest Hills Gardens Corporation under a covenant dating to 1913 that bars exterior alterations without approval, looks after its own streets and parking, and expects contractors' vehicles to carry a resident's guest sticker. None of that stops a soundbar going up in a living room, but it does change how the visit is planned.

North of the tracks and along the boulevard, the conditions flip. The pre-war buildings on 108th Street, Yellowstone Boulevard and the side streets off Queens Boulevard are co-ops for the most part, with plaster partitions, elevators, a live-in superintendent and a managing agent who wants a description of any work and a certificate of insurance before a technician arrives. The towers — the three 20-storey buildings of Parker Towers toward the Rego Park end of the boulevard, the 12-storey Forest Hills Co-op Houses at 108th Street and 62nd Drive — are concrete, where a TV wall needs a hammer drill and every apartment has neighbors above, below and beside. Between the two sit the 1920s garden courts, Arbor Close and Forest Close, where attached Tudor houses share walls around a common green.

The sound problem here usually runs in the opposite direction from the rest of Queens. Forest Hills is a quiet, leafy place, and on most evenings the loudest thing on an apartment's floor is somebody's television. Residents say so. In an r/ForestHills thread asking about co-op life, one shareholder wrote about an upstairs neighbor who “is still loud at all hours, blasting their terrible music, their TV”; a renter in another thread described upstairs neighbors as “needlessly noisy.” A soundbar with a wireless subwoofer, left on its factory settings, is exactly the kind of equipment that produces those posts. So in a Forest Hills co-op the installation is as much about where the bass goes as about where the bar hangs.

The exception is summer. Forest Hills Stadium, the 1923 horseshoe beside the West Side Tennis Club that hosted the U.S. national championships for decades, has been an outdoor concert venue since 2013, with room for up to 14,000 people and 37 performances in 2024. City inspectors issued nine noise violations at the stadium during the 2025 season, and the Gardens Corporation's board president told Columbia News Service that the concerts send “vibrations through residents' houses for hours at a time.” Households within earshot have a seasonal version of the problem everyone else in Queens has year-round: an outside noise that competes with the dialogue. We cover both directions further down the page.

Forest Hills figures that shape a soundbar job

  • 122,344 residents in the Forest Hills and Rego Park community district, with a median age of 43.6 and a median household income of $96,845 (Census American Community Survey 2022, via Data USA). An older, settled population that watches a lot of television and expects the setup to be simple.
  • 48.4 percent of homes in the district are owner-occupied, and in 61.2 percent of households the language everyone shares at home is not English (Data USA, 2022). Many owners here are co-op shareholders, which is why paperwork comes up on so many calls.
  • Over 800 houses and 11 apartment buildings make up Forest Hills Gardens, home to about 4,500 people, according to a 2014 6sqft profile. Each house in the early phase used about 170 precast concrete panels (Southampton Historical Museum archive on Grosvenor Atterbury).
  • 1931, six storeys, 50 apartments: a typical 108th Street co-op as listed by CityRealty. Buildings of this vintage, with plaster walls and an elevator, make up much of the apartment stock around 108th Street.
  • 1,327 apartments in three 20-storey towers at Parker Towers on Queens Boulevard (The Real Deal, 2018), and 432 co-op apartments in three 12-storey buildings at the Forest Hills Co-op Houses, the scaled-down result of Mario Cuomo's 1972 mediation (Queens Daily Eagle).
  • 55 decibels measured by inspectors inside a home near the stadium during a September 2025 concert, against the city noise code's 42-decibel limit for commercial music inside a residence; the stadium runs to a 10 p.m. curfew (Queens Daily Eagle, October 2025).

Put together: an older, often multilingual population, half of it owning the walls it lives behind, split between a private Tudor village, a belt of co-ops and a handful of very large towers, with a concert venue in the middle. That is the brief for a soundbar in Forest Hills, and it is different enough from Astoria or Jamaica to deserve its own page.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Forest Hills' Architecture

The bar is the light part; the building is what we plan around. Six kinds of home account for almost every Forest Hills call, and they ask for six different approaches to the fixing, the cable and the sound.

Atterbury's Concrete-Panel Houses

The early Gardens houses, assembled around 1910–1917 from factory-cast concrete panels with hollow chambers. Solid where the web of the panel is, hollow where the chamber is — so the drill tells us which we have found before an anchor goes in.

Gardens Tudors in Brick and Stucco

The later houses on streets such as Greenway North and Markwood Place: brick or stucco outside, plaster inside, leaded glass, wood-burning or gas fireplaces and, in some, beamed sunrooms. Exterior changes need the Corporation's approval; interiors are the owner's.

Garden-Court Rows

Arbor Close (1925) and Forest Close (1927), attached neo-Tudor houses grouped around shared gardens; Forest Close's 38 houses sit on Austin Street between 75th and 76th Avenues. Party walls on one or both sides, compact front rooms and neighbors who are also friends across the green.

Detached Houses North of the Tracks

The Cord Meyer lots, where Robert Tappan and William Patterson designed houses to complement each other, plus newer rebuilds. Usually wood framing with plaster or drywall — the best chance in the neighborhood for fully concealed cabling.

Pre-War Elevator Co-ops

The six-storey buildings that followed the 1936 subway along 108th Street and the streets off Queens Boulevard. Plaster partitions, a superintendent, a managing agent with forms, and apartments stacked tight above and below.

Concrete Towers

Parker Towers' three 20-storey buildings, the Forest Hills Co-op Houses and the high-rises along the boulevard. Poured concrete or block walls, conduit inside them, management work rules, and bass that travels through the frame.

What each wall asks of the bracket

Precast concrete panels. We start with a small pilot hole at the bracket's fixing points, using a masonry bit without the hammer function until we know what is behind the surface. Steady resistance all the way to depth means the solid web of the panel, and a concrete sleeve or wedge anchor goes in. A sudden drop means the bit has broken into one of the panel's hollow chambers; there we either slide the bracket along to find solid concrete or use a hollow-wall anchor designed for cavities in masonry. No cable goes inside these walls — the chambers were cast as insulation, not as conduits — so the finish is a painted raceway or a route down behind the furniture.

Brick and stucco Tudors. Inside, these are usually plaster over masonry on the outside walls and plaster on lath or block on the interior ones. Plaster over masonry gets a pilot through the plaster and masonry anchors into the brick behind; plaster on lath gets toggles behind the lath or fixings into the framing. Fireplace walls and chimney breasts are left to the end of the conversation, because they bring heat, flue questions and often a mantel that dictates the TV height.

Garden-court party walls. When the TV wall is the one shared with the next house, we check its construction with a pilot hole before committing, keep anchor depths short of the wall's full thickness, and usually move the subwoofer to the opposite side of the room. The front-room layouts in Arbor Close and Forest Close are compact, so a mid-size bar often suits them better than a long Atmos bar.

Wood framing north of the tracks. Stud-located fixings, a scanner run across the whole bay to find pipes and cables, and, where the homeowner wants nothing visible, an in-wall power and cable kit from behind the TV down to an outlet on the same wall. A basement under the living room can make the route even simpler.

Pre-war co-op plaster. Plaster that is still keyed to its lath holds a bracket well if it is drilled without hammer action; plaster that sounds hollow when tapped gets fixings that reach the framing or a TV-attached bracket instead. We stay away from radiator risers and from any wall the managing agent has ruled off limits.

Concrete towers. Hammer drill, carbide bit, dust extraction at the hole and concrete anchors, booked inside the building's permitted work hours because it is loud. Before the first hole we scan for embedded conduit and keep clear of the vertical lines above and below outlets and switches.

Fireplace walls in the Gardens. Many Gardens living rooms are arranged around a wood-burning or gas fireplace, and the wall above the mantel is the obvious place for a TV even when it is not the best one. A screen over a mantel sits higher than seated ear height, and a bar on the mantel shelf can block the lower edge of the picture or sit in rising warm air when the fire is lit. We look at the alternatives first — the wall beside the fireplace, a corner on an arm — and if the mantel wins, we mount the bar on the wall or the TV rather than on the shelf, angled toward the seats where the bracket allows, and keep cables out of the firebox surround.

Owners in the Gardens and the bigger co-ops often ask for a wired network point behind the TV while we are there. AESS structured cabling in Queens can run that cable on the same visit, which keeps a networked bar and a streaming box off crowded Wi-Fi.

The offices above the shops around 71st Avenue and Continental Avenue bring a different request: a meeting-room display that people can actually hear on a video call. A bar on the display's eARC socket, or connected to the room's computer, lifts far-end voices above the hum of a small office, and a sensible fixed level keeps the room next door out of the meeting. For larger rooms with microphones around a table, ceiling audio is the better answer and our AV team takes that on.

Compliance notes for Forest Hills businesses

  • Apartments upstairs. The city's noise code, as summarized by the Department of Environmental Protection, limits commercial music to 42 decibels inside a nearby residence and, between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m., to 7 decibels above the street's background level at 15 feet, with a separate limit on pervasive bass. A soundbar in a Queens Boulevard storefront is set with that ceiling in mind.
  • Corridors and waiting rooms. A long bar on a bracket can project past the 4-inch limit for wall-mounted objects between 27 and 80 inches off the floor in the 2010 ADA Standards. In a clinic hallway we mount above that band, recess it, or keep it off the circulation route.
  • Outlets are electrical work. Connecting a soundbar is low-voltage work and needs no Department of Buildings permit of its own; a new outlet behind the screen does need a licensed electrician.
  • Landlord paperwork. Commercial landlords typically ask for a contractor's certificate of insurance; ours costs nothing and names whoever your lease requires.
  • Inside the Gardens. For premises on or near Station Square, anything that changes the outside of a building — a speaker under an arcade, a cable through a façade — belongs to the Corporation and the landlord to approve before we touch it.

Practice managers who call about a waiting-room screen often add door entry or cameras to the list. Low-voltage installation from AESS in Queens covers sound, data and security under the same license.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Forest Hills: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from Forest Hills addresses in particular — Gardens houses, garden courts, co-ops, towers and the businesses around Austin Street. General questions about booking and equipment are in the FAQ near the end.

Houses in Forest Hills Gardens

Our Gardens house is one of Atterbury's precast concrete houses — what actually holds a soundbar bracket in a panel wall with hollow chambers inside?

A concrete anchor in the solid part of the panel, once we have found it. Atterbury's panels were cast with hollow chambers for insulation, so the same wall is solid concrete in some places and a skin of concrete over an air space in others. A soundbar weighs a few pounds to a little over a dozen, which concrete carries easily — the risk is not strength but an anchor set into a thin face over a void, where it can spin or crack the face. We drill a small pilot at each fixing point, without hammer action at first, and feel for the change: steady resistance all the way in is solid, a sudden give is a chamber.

Where the bracket's holes fall on solid concrete we use sleeve or wedge anchors sized to the bracket. Where they fall on a chamber, we shift the bracket a little sideways, use a bracket with more mounting slots, or switch to an anchor made for hollow masonry. The cables can't be fished through these walls, so we run a slim painted raceway from the bar to the outlet or route everything down behind the console. On the price list this is the concrete-wall line, and we tell you before drilling that a century-old surface can spall around a hole, which is why we start small.

We live inside Forest Hills Gardens — which parts of a soundbar job touch the Corporation's rules on exterior changes?

For a bar on an inside wall, usually none; for anything that shows on the outside of the house, possibly all of it. The 1913 covenant that every Gardens buyer signs prevents exterior alterations without the Corporation's express approval. A soundbar, its bracket and its cables inside your living room are not exterior work. A speaker or TV on a porch, a cable drilled through an outside wall to reach a terrace, or anything fixed to the façade is the kind of change the Corporation reviews, and we won't do that part until you have its answer in writing. We can describe the work for you, but only the Corporation can interpret its own rules.

The visit itself needs one bit of planning. The Corporation looks after the Gardens' streets and parking, and visitors' vehicles are expected to show a resident's guest sticker. When you book, tell us where we can stop and arrange the guest pass the way you would for any contractor, so the technician isn't circling with a van full of tools or carrying a drill case three blocks.

Will a subwoofer make the leaded glass windows in our Tudor living room rattle?

It can, if the glass is loose in its lead or the sash is loose in its frame. Leaded windows are made of small panes held in lead strips, and over a century some of those joints relax. Low bass carries energy that can set a loose pane or a loose casement buzzing at particular notes, which sounds like a fault in the soundbar when it isn't. It is more likely with the subwoofer close to the window wall and turned up.

We place the sub away from the window wall, ideally on the inside wall of the room, and set its level so it fills out the bottom without shaking the glass. On the day we play a bass-heavy scene and listen at the windows. If a particular pane still buzzes, the cure is with the window — re-cementing or tightening by someone who restores leaded glass — and we'll point out exactly which one so you can show them.

Garden courts and older houses

Before you drill, should we photograph the hairline cracks already in our 1920s plaster?

Yes, and we do the same. Plaster in a house from the 1920s has usually moved with a hundred winters, and fine cracks around doorways, ceilings and windows are normal. When a bracket goes up on a cracked wall, nobody wants an argument later about which cracks were there first. A few photos taken in daylight before the visit, with the TV wall and the walls either side in frame, settle it.

On arrival the technician looks over the TV wall with you, notes any existing cracks or loose areas on the work order and photographs them. Where plaster sounds hollow or a crack runs through the spot the bracket wants, we move the fixings to sound plaster or to the framing, or recommend a TV-attached bracket so the wall isn't asked to do more than it can.

Co-ops and towers

Our co-op's managing agent wants a written description of the work before approving it — what should it say for a soundbar?

Short and specific: what is being installed, where, how it is fixed, and what it does not involve. For a typical job that means the bar's make and model, the room and wall, the number and type of anchors (for example, four concrete anchors or two toggles behind plaster), the cable finish (surface raceway, no openings in the wall), and a line confirming there is no electrical, plumbing or structural work. Add the expected duration, the contractor's name and license number, and the date you want.

We write that description for you from the photo and models you send, and our certificate of insurance goes to the agent at no charge with whatever additional insureds the building lists. If the alteration agreement calls for anything we don't do — a new outlet, for instance — the description says so plainly, so the approval covers exactly what happens on the day.

The TV wall in our tower apartment is solid concrete — could the drill hit electrical conduit buried inside it?

It could, which is why we look before we drill. Concrete buildings often carry wiring in conduit cast into the walls, running mostly straight up and down from outlets and switches or across at their height. A hole that meets a conduit can damage the cable inside and cut power to part of the apartment, or worse. The bracket only needs anchors a couple of inches deep, but that is enough to reach conduit near the surface.

So we scan the area with a detector that finds metal and live wiring, mark the likely conduit routes above and below the outlets and switches, keep the anchors clear of those lines, and use a depth stop on the drill. If the scan is uncertain at a fixing point, we move the bracket rather than gamble. Where the building has drawings showing conduit runs, the superintendent can often tell us where to avoid.

Households that watch in more than one language

Our parents watch Russian-language channels through an internet TV box — does the soundbar's dialogue mode work as well for Russian as for English?

Yes. Dialogue and speech modes work on sound, not on words: they lift the range of frequencies where human voices sit and, on some bars, turn down the effects and music around them. A Russian news anchor, a Spanish-language drama and an English sitcom all have voices in that same range, so the mode helps each of them in the same way. What changes clarity more than language is how the channel was mixed — some imported programs arrive with music loud under the speech — and that is where a center-channel bar and its voice setting earn their keep.

The box itself connects like any streaming player: HDMI into the TV, with the TV passing the sound to the bar over eARC or ARC so the TV remote still controls the volume. If the box has its own remote that older viewers prefer, we check that its volume buttons can be set to drive the TV or the bar, and we leave a short note of the settings in whichever language the household gives us, so a grandchild or a neighbor can put things right after an update.

Concert nights

Is it a problem to book the installation on a day when the stadium has a concert?

Not for the work, but it can be for getting there. On show days residents have described street closures and parking taken up around the stadium, and the Gardens streets nearest the venue are the busiest. A technician with a van of tools and a bar to carry needs a place to stop near your door, which is harder in the hours before a show.

When you book, tell us if there is a concert that day and we will pick an early arrival window, well before crowds gather, or another day. Shows end by the stadium's 10 p.m. curfew, so an evening appointment that day is the one to avoid. Everything else — mounting, wiring and tuning — is indifferent to what is happening on the stage.

Austin Street businesses

Can the soundbar in our waiting room help keep conversations at the reception desk from carrying to patients in the chairs?

A little, but it shouldn't be relied on for privacy. A TV playing at a comfortable level does give the room some background sound, which makes nearby speech slightly harder to follow. But a soundbar is designed to make speech clear, which is the opposite of what masking needs, and the gaps between programs leave the desk exposed. Turning the bar up to cover conversations only annoys the people waiting.

What helps is placement: the screen and bar on the far side of the waiting area, aimed at the seats and away from the desk, at a moderate fixed level. For genuine speech privacy at a reception counter, a dedicated sound-masking system with small ceiling emitters is the right tool, and our AV and sound team for Queens can price one. Anything the practice needs for legal compliance is a question for its own advisers, not for an installer.

When our Austin Street restaurant opens its front for the Sunday open street, can the soundbar's sound stay inside?

Mostly, with the right wall and the right level. A soundbar throws its sound forward from the bar, so the first choice is to mount the screen on the back or side wall facing the tables, not above the window facing the street. Aimed inward, the bar reaches the diners and the open frontage gets the spill rather than the full output. A bar mounted over the door aims straight out at the pavement.

We set a sensible maximum volume where the bar or TV allows it, leave the subwoofer low or off, because bass escapes through any opening, and show staff a quick way to lower the level when the front is open. The city's commercial-music rules don't pause for an open street, and the apartments over Austin Street's storefronts hear the difference.

Before you buy a bar for a Forest Hills room

Measure the space under the screen. Note the TV's width, the gap between the bottom of the screen and whatever sits below it, and how far the nearest window, door frame or radiator is from each end. Long bars want open space at their ends, and many Gardens and garden-court rooms have a window or door close to the TV wall.

Look at the back of the TV. An HDMI socket marked eARC or ARC means one cable carries the sound and one remote runs everything. An older second-room TV with only an optical output still works, with a few compromises on control.

Think about the ceiling before paying for Atmos. Up-firing height speakers need a flat, reflective ceiling over the seats. A plaster living-room ceiling suits them; beams, slopes and glass do not.

Decide where a subwoofer could go before buying one. In a co-op or a garden-court house the honest answer is sometimes "nowhere it won't bother someone," and then a bar with good built-in bass is the better purchase.

Concert Season

Quiet Streets, Loud Summers: Sound Settings for Forest Hills

For ten months a year the main sound question in Forest Hills is how not to be the noisy neighbor. From late spring to early autumn, near the stadium, it is also how to hear the film. The two call for different settings, and a well-set-up bar can switch between them in a couple of button presses. This is how we approach both.

Keeping your sound in your apartment

  • Bass is the part that travels. Treble and speech fade quickly through plaster and concrete; low bass passes through floors and walls far more easily. Most of the complaint posts on neighborhood forums are about the thump, not the words, so the subwoofer is where we spend our attention.
  • Subwoofer off the shared surfaces. In a co-op, the sub goes on the floor away from the party wall and off any spot directly over the neighbor's bedroom where we can tell, on a dense pad rather than straight on the floorboards.
  • Corners avoided. A sub pushed into a corner sounds louder in the room and excites the structure more; a position along a wall, out of the corner, gives an even sound at a lower setting.
  • Night mode found and labeled. Most bars have a night or late-night mode that compresses loud peaks. We set it up, show it to everyone who uses the TV and, if the household wants, set the bar's default so it wakes up at a sensible level.
  • Speech first, volume second. A bar with a center channel and its dialogue mode on can be played several notches lower for the same intelligibility, which helps everyone on the floor.

Hearing the dialogue when the stadium is playing

On show nights the problem is reversed. A concert carries a broad wash of low-frequency sound that fills the gaps in speech, and turning the TV up mostly makes the room louder without making voices clearer. What helps is the same discipline as the rest of Queens uses against traffic: the bar mounted close under the screen at seated ear height, its speech mode on, any automatic leveling mode tried, and captions one press away. If the household sits with its back to the windows that face the venue, the bar's sound reaches them before the concert does.

The stadium's season and its 10 p.m. curfew mean the overlap is a few hours on a few dozen evenings a year. We don't design the whole room around it, but we do make sure that the person who watches most knows which two settings to change on those nights and how to change them back.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Forest Hills

A note on method: the tools used to research this page could not display Google's AI Overview, so nothing below is presented as its words. Each heading is a claim published by a manufacturer, a cost guide or an installer — the kind of line answer engines summarize and repeat. Under each is what it leaves out once the wall is in Forest Hills.

“Find your studs — drywall anchors can work, but one screw into a stud is better”

That is the first step in one New York TV-mounting company's soundbar guide, and it is the first step in most DIY instructions. In a framed house with drywall it is sound advice.

In a good share of Forest Hills there are no studs to find. Atterbury's Gardens houses are precast concrete panels, the towers are poured concrete, and many pre-war co-op partitions are plaster on lath or over block. A stud finder on those walls either reads nothing or reads nonsense, and a drywall anchor has nothing to grip.

The question to ask first is not where the studs are but what the wall is made of. We answer it with a pilot hole and choose the anchor afterwards.

“Install a soundbar without a stud finder — quick, safe and damage-free”

No-stud hanging systems are marketed as a way to put up a soundbar quickly without searching for framing, and for the walls they are designed for they may do just that.

The difficulty is the word “wall”. A system rated for gypsum board says nothing about a concrete panel with a hollow chamber behind its face, or crumbly 1930s plaster, or a poured-concrete tower wall. Using a fixing outside its rating is how bars end up on the floor.

If you want to try a no-stud product, check that its maker lists your wall type. If they don't, a pair of correctly chosen anchors is a small cost against a dropped bar.

“Align the template's center line with the center of your TV”

Samsung's instructions for its soundbar wall mount have you tape a paper template to the wall and line it up with the middle of the television. It is the right way to get the bar centered on the screen.

It assumes the TV itself is in the right place. In many Tudor living rooms the obvious wall has a window, a door or a fireplace that pushes the screen off the room's center line, and in garden-court front rooms the seating may not face the middle of the wall at all.

We settle the TV's position against the seating first, then center the bar on the TV using the template. Doing it in that order avoids a perfectly centered bar on a badly placed screen.

“Avoid putting the subwoofer directly against a wall or in a corner”

Polk Audio's placement advice warns that bass reflections build up against walls and in corners. That is accurate, and it is about sound quality in your own room.

In a Forest Hills co-op or a garden-court row house the same advice has a second reason. A sub against the party wall or in a corner shared with the next apartment pushes more energy into the structure the neighbors live in, and that is what starts noise disputes.

We follow Polk's rule for your sake and add one for the neighbors': the sub goes off shared walls and on an isolation pad, at the lowest level that still sounds full from the sofa.

“With an adjustable TV mount, put the soundbar on a bracket attached to the mount”

Dolby's guide to soundbar placement suggests that if the TV is on an adjustable mount, the bar can ride on a bracket connected to that mount so the two stay together.

It is a good idea with a condition attached: the arm then carries the TV and the bar. A full-motion arm swung out from a plaster wall in a corner places the combined weight a long way from the wall, and the arm's rating and its anchors have to allow for both.

Before fitting a TV-attached bracket we add the bar's weight to the TV's, check it against the arm's published limit, and confirm the arm's own anchors suit the wall.

“Mount the soundbar closest to 42.5 inches off the ground, where a seated listener's ears fall”

Yamaha's guide to mounting a TV and soundbar gives about 42.5 inches off the floor as the height where a seated listener's ears fall, and the principle — sound at ear level — is right.

Forest Hills rooms don't always cooperate. A Gardens living room with a fireplace and mantel puts the TV higher than ideal, a sunroom with a low window sill puts it lower, and an older relative in a high-backed armchair sits at a different height from a family on a low sofa.

We measure the actual seats, then choose between bar under the screen, bar on the TV, or a slight downward angle where the bracket allows, so voices arrive at the heads of the people watching.

“Installing a soundbar costs $58 to $231”

Homewyse's cost estimator put the national average cost to install a soundbar at $58.43 to $231 in September 2026, and a range like that is easy for a summary to repeat as the going rate.

A national range can't know that your wall is a concrete panel or that your building needs an insurance certificate. In Forest Hills the bar on the wall is $135.45 on our Queens list, a concrete or block wall adds $78.75, plaster adds $47.25, and the certificate is free.

Field Notes on Forest Hills Walls

Habits our crews follow on these walls — general practice, not the story of any one home

On a Gardens street, assume nothing about the wall until the first pilot hole. FAQ

Forest Hills Soundbar Installation FAQ

Will mounting the soundbar on the wall void the manufacturer's warranty on the bar or the TV?
+

Not by itself. Wall-mounting with a bracket made for the bar, or with the maker's own bracket, is normal use. What can cause trouble is damage from a fixing that failed or from drilling into the product. We use brackets rated for your model, and brackets and parts carry their own manufacturer's warranty.

Can a soundbar go on a mirrored wall?
+

Not by drilling through the mirror. Old mirror panels can crack under a drill and are often glued to the wall behind. The usual answers are to fix the bar to the TV or its mount, place it on a console in front of the mirror, or mount on a section of wall beside or below the glass.

How should a wall-mounted soundbar be cleaned?
+

Switch it off and wipe the case with a dry microfiber cloth. Use a soft brush or a vacuum on low with a brush tool for the grille, and never spray cleaner onto the bar, because liquid can get inside. If it sits over a radiator, clean it more often in winter, when rising air carries dust into the grille.

Does a wall-mounted soundbar need space behind it for air?
+

Most bars don't run hot, but they do need their vents and ports clear. Leave the gap the maker's bracket sets, keep rear ports uncovered, and never box the bar into a recess or shelf with no air around it. Bars with a rear port or an up-firing driver need that clearance most.

Should I set up the soundbar maker's app account before you arrive?
+

It helps. If the bar uses an app, install it on your phone and create the account with your own email beforehand, and have your Wi-Fi password ready. We then add the bar to your account during the visit so updates and settings stay under your control, not ours.

If I decide the soundbar is wrong after it's mounted, can it still go back to the store?
+

Usually, if you keep the box, the packing and every part, and you are inside the retailer's return period. We take the bar off the bracket undamaged and repack it. The bracket and the holes stay, so if you are unsure of the model, a console setup first is the cheaper way to try it.

Our TV is on a rolling floor stand — can the soundbar travel with it?
+

Yes, if the stand has a shelf or a bracket point below the screen. We fix the bar to the stand or the TV, route the HDMI and power down the stand's column, and leave one plug to move between rooms. A wireless sub stays behind or moves separately.

Can the soundbar be mounted upside down or on its side to fit the space?
+

Usually not. Most bars are designed to sit level with the drivers facing forward and any up-firing drivers on top; turned over or on end, the left and right channels and height effects no longer land where they should. We follow the mounting directions in your model's manual, and if the space is tight, a slimmer bar or a bracket on the TV mount is the usual fix.
