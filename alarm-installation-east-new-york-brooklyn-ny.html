The Neighborhood

Why East New York Properties Need Alarm Installation

East New York is large and it is mostly houses. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as running from the Cemetery Belt and the Queens line in the north to Jamaica Bay in the south, with the Canarsie Line, the Bay Ridge Branch tracks and Van Sinderen Avenue on the west, and Linden Boulevard, Pennsylvania Avenue and Atlantic Avenue as its main roads. It describes the land use as primarily semi-detached homes, two-to-four family houses and multi-unit apartment buildings, including condos and co-ops. Three ZIP codes cover it: 11207 and 11208 for most of the neighborhood, and 11239 for Starrett City. That mix matters for an alarm, because most of the people who call us here own the building they live in, and a house has more ways in than an apartment.

The houses also have a history that shapes where an alarm should look. In the 1980s, East Brooklyn Congregations, a coalition of local churches, persuaded the city to give vacant land for owner-occupied houses, and the Nehemiah plan began building on the empty lots left by the decline of the 1960s and 1970s. The Gotham Center’s history of the plan describes what the organizers asked for: full basements, double-glazed windows, natural gas furnaces and parking pads, so residents did not have to depend on street parking. It also records a detail that we use on every survey. After hundreds of house meetings, residents asked that the kitchen “be placed in the front so that homeowners might watch their front yard, parking pad, and street as they prepared meals.” The front of a Nehemiah house was designed to be watched. The back was not.

That is why our plan for a rowhouse here usually begins at the rear. The front door faces a parking pad, the street and the kitchen window, and on a block of owner-occupied houses neighbors notice a stranger at a front door. The back of the house faces a small yard, a fence and the yards of the next street, and is overlooked by nobody in particular. The basement door, the rear door to the yard and the ground-floor windows at the back are where we put the first contacts, and the motion detector goes where a person coming up from the basement or in from the yard has to pass. The front still gets a contact and often a video doorbell, but we do not spend the budget there first.

Crime has fallen here, and we will say so plainly. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 75th Precinct, which patrols East New York, shows 155 burglaries reported in 2026 through November 8, against 210 in the same period of 2025, a drop of 26.2 percent. The same report records 2,433 burglaries in the precinct in all of 1990 and 247 in all of 2025. So this page does not tell you the neighborhood is dangerous, and we do not sell alarms with crime maps. A burglary rate is an average across tens of thousands of homes; what a homeowner needs to know is how exposed their own back door is, whether anybody would find out if it were forced, and how quickly. Those are questions an installer can answer for one house.

The break-ins that do make the news here tend to share two features. They happen when nobody is watching, and they happen fast. In June 2022, CBS New York showed surveillance video of a man pulling an ATM off a deli wall on Fulton Street in East New York and dragging it toward a broken window, part of a series police tied to a five-person crew that hit 42 businesses in Brooklyn and Queens over six months, all overnight while the shops were closed. In September 2022, amNY reported a bodega at 416 Glenmore Avenue entered at 2:50 a.m. by three people who carried out an ATM, the cash register and about $13,000 in merchandise. At home, in February 2026, News 12 reported that a couple near Vandalia and Fountain avenues opened their door just before 9:30 p.m. to a woman claiming to be from a cleaning agency, and three armed people forced their way in. Those are three very different problems, and an alarm answers each of them differently, which is what most of this page is about.

Six facts about East New York that shape an alarm

  • Burglary in the 75th Precinct is down sharply. 155 reported in 2026 through November 8, against 210 a year earlier (-26.2%); 9 in the last 28 days, against 27; down 90.3% over 33 years (NYPD CompStat, week of November 2–8, 2026).
  • Car theft is still common. The same report shows 329 grand larcenies of autos in 2026 to date, against 399 in 2025, which is why so many owners here want the parking pad or driveway on camera as well as the house on the alarm.
  • Nehemiah houses are a large share of the housing. The Brooklyn Nehemiah Homeowners Associations put the count at more than three thousand homes in Brownsville and East New York since the early 1980s, and the Gotham Center history says Nehemiah houses made up 77 percent of the increase in East New York’s single-family houses in the 1980s.
  • Eleven public housing developments. Wikipedia lists eleven NYCHA developments in East New York, from the thirty-three three-story buildings of East New York City Line to the twenty-two eight-story buildings of the Pink Houses; several have moved to private management under the PACT program.
  • A new layer of apartment buildings. The 2016 rezoning allowed medium-density mixed-use buildings along key corridors, and Chestnut Commons at 110 Dinsmore Place, fourteen stories and 275 apartments, was the largest public site designated after it.
  • Thirteen bodegas for every supermarket. The city’s 2018 community health profile, cited by Wikipedia, counted 13 bodegas for every supermarket in East New York. Small food stores with cash, cigarettes and ATMs are the commercial alarm customers we see most.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for East New York’s Architecture

Most of East New York was built twice. The first time was the railroad-and-elevated boom that followed the Fulton Street elevated line in 1889 and the subway’s arrival at New Lots in 1922; the second was the rebuilding that started with Nehemiah in the 1980s and continues with the apartment buildings of the rezoning. Six kinds of property cover almost everything we are asked to alarm.

The Nehemiah rowhouse

Two- and three-bedroom brick houses of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 square feet, according to the Gotham Center history, sold to working families from 1982 onward, with a parking pad, a full basement and the kitchen at the front. Attached in long rows, with small back yards.

The Spring Creek modular house

The Nehemiah houses built in Spring Creek in the 2000s were modular homes designed by the architect Alexander Gorlin and assembled at the Brooklyn Navy Yard, painted from a palette of thirteen colors so no two neighbors match. Newer frames, newer windows, the same back-yard logic.

The older two-family frame house

Older two-family and three-family houses, brick and modest wood frame, of the kind that historically lined Liberty, Pitkin and Sutter avenues and the streets between them. More doors, a shared hall, a basement with its own entrance, and often a rental floor.

The rezoning-era apartment building

Mid-rise and taller buildings on and near Atlantic Avenue, such as Chestnut Commons at 110 Dinsmore Place. Elevators, a staffed or camera-watched lobby, fob entry, and apartments whose owners or tenants want only their own door and windows on an alarm.

NYCHA and Starrett City apartments

Public housing from the 1950s onward and Starrett City’s 46 buildings and 5,881 apartments, which opened in 1974. The building systems belong to the landlord; a tenant’s alarm stays inside the apartment door and never touches them.

The storefront with apartments above

Two- and three-story mixed buildings on Pitkin, Fulton, Atlantic and Liberty: a store at street level behind a roll-down gate, a cellar below, and one or two apartments upstairs with their own street door.

The Nehemiah rowhouse: a short list, in the right order

A Nehemiah house is one of the easiest houses in Brooklyn to alarm well, because it is compact and consistent. A typical house has a front door off the parking pad, a rear door to the yard, a basement, and a modest number of windows, most of them double-glazed units from the original construction or from later replacement. The survey takes the same course every time. We stand on the parking pad and look at the front door, the kitchen window and the windows above; then we go through the house to the back yard and look at the rear door, the back windows on both floors, the basement windows if there are any at grade, and anything near the back wall a person could stand on, such as a shed, a grill platform or an air-conditioning unit on a bracket. Finally we go down to the basement and look at its door, its windows and the boiler or furnace area.

The result is usually a short list. Contacts on the front door, the rear door and the basement door. Contacts or a glass-break detector covering the ground-floor back windows. One motion detector in the hallway or living room that anyone coming from the basement stair or the yard must cross. A second motion detector or a contact on the upper back windows only if something outside makes them reachable. That list sits comfortably inside our basic residential system or a wireless system in the $600 to $1,200 range, depending on how many back windows there are.

Two details are particular to these houses. Because the houses are attached, a siren in one house is a siren for the whole row, so we fit an indoor siren rather than a loud exterior bell, set the cutoff well inside the city’s fifteen-minute limit, and make sure the neighbors on both sides know whose alarm it is. And because the houses are owned by the people who live in them and many have been occupied by the same family for decades, the alarm often has to work for several generations at once: a grandparent at home all day, adults who leave at six, and teenagers who come home at three.

The two-family frame house: more doors, more people

The older two- and three-family houses on the side streets are a different job. They are larger, many of them are wood frame, often re-sided over the years, and they usually have more entrances: a front door into a shared hall, a separate door to the upper floor in some layouts, a side or rear door to the yard, a basement entrance under the stoop or at the back, and sometimes a door from a basement apartment. Windows have usually been replaced at different times by different owners, so the survey checks each one rather than assuming they match. On frame houses we prefer wireless contacts on doors and windows, because fishing wire through old frame walls with plaster inside means patching, and wireless sensors report just as reliably.

The bigger question in a two-family is who the alarm belongs to. An owner who lives on one floor and rents the other usually wants the basement, the boiler room and the yard door protected all the time, their own floor armed on their own code, and the tenant offered the same protection for the rental floor with a separate code. A partitioned panel does exactly that: one system, separate areas, separate codes, separate arming. The tenant decides whether to use their area. The owner can see whether the shared doors are protected, but not when the tenant comes and goes, unless both sides agree otherwise.

The rezoning-era apartment building: your door, your windows, your app

The buildings that went up after 2016, such as Chestnut Commons near Atlantic Avenue and Conduit Boulevard, arrive with building systems already in place: fob or keypad entry at the street door, cameras in the lobby and halls, sometimes a staffed desk, and a fire alarm system that the owner maintains. None of that protects the inside of an individual apartment, and none of it should be touched by a resident’s alarm. An apartment system here is small: a panel with a cellular communicator in a closet, a contact on the apartment door, contacts on any window or terrace door that can be reached from a setback roof, a courtyard or a neighboring terrace, and one motion detector covering the hall. Everything is wireless, held on with adhesive or small screws, and comes out cleanly when you move.

NYCHA developments and Starrett City: inside the apartment only

East New York has eleven public housing developments, and Starrett City, also called Spring Creek Towers, is the largest subsidized rental complex in the United States according to Wikipedia, with its own Department of Public Safety. In all of them the building, the hallways, the doors and the windows belong to the landlord, and in the developments that moved to PACT management, such as Linden Houses and the Pennsylvania Avenue–Wortman Avenue buildings, the property manager is a private company working with NYCHA. A resident who wants an alarm can still have one, as long as it is a tenant’s alarm: battery-powered wireless sensors fixed lightly to the inside of the apartment door and the inside of any reachable window, a small panel and keypad, and phone alerts or monitoring. Nothing is wired into the building, nothing is mounted in the hall, and everything comes out without a mark.

The Kitchen at the Front

Planning an Alarm Around a House That Was Designed to Watch the Street

The Nehemiah organizers solved a real problem in the 1980s. Their residents came from blocks where nobody could see the street from inside the house, and they asked for a kitchen at the front so that whoever was cooking could keep an eye on the yard, the car and the children. Forty years later that decision still works. On a Nehemiah block in the early evening, the street is watched from dozens of front windows, and a stranger trying a front door or a car door is likely to be seen.

The design has a consequence the organizers did not need to think about: the rear of the house is the least watched part of it. Nobody cooks at the back. The back yard is small and fenced, and the yards behind it belong to houses facing the next street, whose kitchens also face away. So in daylight, when the adults of a working household are out and the front windows are empty, and at night, when the kitchen light is off, the back of the house is where a person can work at a door or a window without being seen by anyone on the street. That is the reasoning behind the order of our survey and the order of our price list for these houses.

It also shapes the choice of devices. At the front, the deterrent is already there: the neighbors, the street lights, the parking pad in full view. A video doorbell adds a record of who came to the door and lets the owner answer from work, which matters here for another reason covered further down. At the back, the deterrent has to be supplied. A contact on the rear door and the basement door, a glass-break detector in a back room with several windows, and a motion detector inside the hall make sure that anything that happens at the back is reported within seconds.

The basement deserves its own paragraph. Nehemiah houses were built with full basements, and today they serve as laundry rooms, storage, home offices and, in some houses, finished bedrooms. Basement doors to the yard and basement windows at grade are on our list for every house, because they are low, out of sight and often forgotten when the family locks up. A basement that holds the boiler or furnace is also the right place for a carbon monoxide detector and a water sensor on the floor beside the water heater, both of which report through the same panel and the same app. Some Nehemiah homeowners associations manage shared sewer systems for their blocks, according to the Brooklyn Nehemiah Homeowners Associations, and a water sensor in the basement is a cheap way to hear about a backup before it ruins a finished floor.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in East New York

The city counts four main commercial corridors in East New York: Fulton Street, Atlantic Avenue, Liberty Avenue and Pitkin Avenue, with a business improvement district on Pitkin. Add the shopping around Pennsylvania Avenue and Linden Boulevard, Gateway Center by Jamaica Bay, and the East Brooklyn Industrial Park, and most of the neighborhood’s businesses fall into one of six settings. The stores that call us most are small food stores, which is not surprising in a neighborhood the city counted as having thirteen bodegas for every supermarket.

Fulton Street under the el

East of Broadway Junction the J and Z elevated runs above Fulton Street, and the storefronts beneath it include delis and small groceries. In 2022 CBS New York showed video of an ATM dragged through a Fulton Street deli in East New York to a broken window, overnight, while the store was shut.

Pitkin Avenue

Named for John Pitkin, who named East New York in 1835 and opened a shoe factory at Williams Street and Pitkin Avenue. Today a shopping street with its own business improvement district, where most stores close behind a gate, with glass behind it and a back room behind that.

Atlantic Avenue

A 120-foot-wide road on the city’s own description, with commercial and industrial frontage and, since the rezoning, new mixed-use buildings with stores on the ground floor. Wide frontages and long side walls.

Liberty Avenue and City Line

The main commercial street of City Line, home to restaurants, food markets and shops serving Bangladeshi, Guyanese and Dominican families, according to Wikipedia. Our City Line page covers this strip in more detail.

Pennsylvania Avenue, Linden Boulevard and Gateway Center

The two big roads across the south of the neighborhood, and Gateway Center, a suburban-style shopping complex whose two buildings opened in 2002 and 2014. Independent shops and offices around them have the same overnight needs as stores anywhere else in the neighborhood.

The East Brooklyn Industrial Park

Forty-four blocks set up in 1980, bounded by Atlantic Avenue, Sheffield Avenue, Sutter Avenue and Powell Street. Industrial and warehouse buildings with roll-up doors, roof hatches and small offices inside.

What the overnight break-ins here have in common

The commercial burglaries reported in East New York in recent years follow a pattern that tells us exactly where a store alarm has to be strong. In the 2022 series that CBS New York covered, police said every theft happened overnight when the shops were closed, and the targets were smoke shops, delis, nail salons, barbershops, grocery stores and donut shops. The method was force at the front: broken windows, broken doors. In the September 2022 series that amNY reported, the crew either cut through locks with tools or smashed open glass front doors; at the Glenmore Avenue bodega, three people were inside at 2:50 a.m., unplugged the ATM and carried it out the front door along with the register, and loaded everything into a van. None of that takes long. The value of an alarm in that situation is not that it stops a determined crew, because a gate cut or a door broken is already done, but that it reports the very first second of the attack to a monitoring station and to the owner’s phone, sounds inside the store so the crew knows it has been heard, and produces a timed record that police and insurers can use.

So a store alarm on Fulton, Pitkin or Glenmore starts at the gate. A heavy-duty contact on the roll-down gate reports when the gate is lifted. A contact on the door behind the gate, and a glass-break detector covering the front window, report the second stage. Motion detectors inside cover the floor, with one aimed at the counter and the cash area. A shock sensor on the wall or floor plate behind the ATM or the safe reports anyone working on it, even if they came in some other way. The rear door to the alley or yard gets a contact, and the cellar door or sidewalk hatch, the roof hatch and any skylight get theirs. The panel reports over cellular, with battery backup, so cutting a phone or internet line outside does nothing.

Delis and bodegas: the counter, the cooler and the ATM

A small food store has a particular layout: a front door and window, a counter and register near the door, aisles and coolers along the walls, a back room or cellar for stock, and often an ATM near the front. Two points matter for the alarm. First, the coolers and their compressors move air and change temperature, so motion detectors are aimed away from cooler doors and fans, and we choose detectors designed to ignore that kind of movement. Second, the cash and the cigarettes sit behind the counter, which should be covered by a detector of its own, and the cash itself belongs in a safe that is bolted down.

Warehouses and shops in the industrial park

The East Brooklyn Industrial Park, set up in 1980 between Atlantic Avenue, Sheffield Avenue, Sutter Avenue and Powell Street, has the opposite problem: large spaces, few people, and long empty nights and weekends. Roll-up loading doors get heavy-duty contacts, man doors get standard contacts, roof hatches and skylights are always included, and the stock area, the office and any cage holding valuable goods are separate zones, so an alert tells the owner which part of the building is involved. Many owners also want an alert when a door is left open after hours or the system was never armed at all, which the app can send automatically.

Compliance notes for East New York businesses and buildings

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to be able to stop, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. On a commercial street where the store has apartments upstairs, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • Licensing. Alarm installation businesses in New York State are licensed by the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours is #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A burglar alarm is not a code fire alarm system. Monitored smoke detectors on a burglar alarm are useful, but they do not replace a fire alarm system where the building code requires one. The FDNY’s rules (3 RCNY § 907-01) make it unlawful to transmit two or more unnecessary or unwarranted fire alarms in a three-month period, so smoke devices are placed away from kitchens and fryers.
  • Detectors in rental housing. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling and sets carbon monoxide rules for owners and tenants, including in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances. Adding monitored devices to an alarm does not replace those duties.
  • No citywide alarm permit that we could find. Many suburban towns in New York require an alarm permit and charge for false alarms. We found no general New York City permit for a burglar alarm in a home or store. Avoiding false alarms still matters, because every unnecessary dispatch costs the police time and costs the owner credibility.

Alarm Installation in East New York: Questions Answered

These questions come from the way East New York is built and from what has happened here: the Nehemiah house with its basement and parking pad, the modular houses of Spring Creek, the stores that are empty for a few hours a night, and the industrial park that is empty all weekend. Each is answered in full here, and none of them is repeated in the FAQ further down.

Nehemiah houses and other rowhouses

Our grown son sleeps in the finished basement of our Nehemiah house and comes home late through the door to the yard. How do we alarm a basement that is also somebody’s front door?

By giving the basement its own area on the panel and your son his own code. Some Nehemiah basements have been finished over the years, and when one becomes a bedroom, its yard door stops being a door nobody uses and becomes the way one person comes and goes at odd hours. If that door sits on the same zone as the rest of the house, either the house is left unarmed until he gets in or he sets off the siren every night. A partitioned panel solves both. The basement is one area, the upstairs is another. When the family goes to bed, the upstairs is armed at night with the bedrooms left free, and the basement stays armed with a normal entry delay on the yard door. Your son opens the door, enters his code on a small keypad at the bottom of the stair or on his phone, and only the basement disarms. The interior door at the top of the basement stair gets a contact on the upstairs area, so the rest of the house still knows if someone comes up from below. You get a notification with his name when he disarms, if you want one, and nothing at all if you do not.

Every Nehemiah house on our block has the same layout. Doesn’t that tell a burglar exactly where our keypad and motion detector will be?

It tells him where the rooms are, which he could guess anyway, but it should not tell him where the alarm is. Identical houses tempt installers to put every panel in the same closet and every motion detector in the same corner, and on a block of similar houses a visitor who has seen one system has seen them all. We avoid that in three ways. The panel goes somewhere that is not the obvious first closet inside the front door, with the keypad by the door as a separate device, so pulling the keypad off the wall does not silence the system and the panel is not where an intruder looks first. The motion detector is placed for your family’s routes rather than at a standard height in a standard corner, and in many of these houses a second detector covering the stair is worth more than a perfect first one. And the cellular communicator reports an entry as soon as the door opens, not only when the entry delay expires, so the event is already on its way to the monitoring station whatever happens to the keypad in the next thirty seconds.

We park on the pad right outside our front door. If we add a camera for the car, should it be tied to the alarm or kept separate?

Tied to the same app, but not to the siren. A camera over the parking pad records who comes and goes and sends a clip when someone stands by the car at night, which is what most owners want; the CompStat report for the precinct still shows 329 grand larcenies of autos in 2026 through November 8, so the worry is reasonable. But the pad is next to the sidewalk, and people walk past it all night. If the camera’s detection could sound the house siren, the family would be woken by every late walker and would soon stop arming the alarm. So the camera sends a notification and saves a clip, the alarm stays armed on the doors and windows, and both appear in one app. If somebody goes from the car to the front door and opens it, the door contact trips the alarm in the ordinary way, and the camera clip from the same minute shows who it was. We draw the camera’s detection area to cover the pad and the front steps and to leave out the sidewalk and the street.

Who is at the door

In February 2026 armed robbers got into a home near Vandalia and Fountain avenues after a woman knocked and said she was from a cleaning agency. How can we check a caller before we open the door?

By making sure you never have to open the door to find out. News 12 reported that a couple answered a knock just before 9:30 on a Friday night, that the woman at the door claimed to represent a cleaning agency, and that three armed people then forced their way in. An alarm system cannot stop someone who is let in, and we will not claim otherwise. What it can do is put a camera and a speaker between you and the caller. A video doorbell at the front door lets you see and talk to the person from your phone or from a screen inside, whether you are upstairs, at the back of the house or at work, and it records the visit. You can tell an unexpected caller to leave a card, ask for the company’s number and call it yourself, or simply not answer. If you do open the door and something goes wrong, a panic button carried on a key ring or fixed beside the door sends an emergency signal to the monitoring station, and a duress code, a separate code that disarms the system normally but silently reports that you are being forced, gives you a way to call for help that the people in front of you cannot see. We set those up and practise them with the household before we leave.

Crime figures and whether to install

Burglaries in the 75th Precinct are down by about a quarter this year and by about ninety percent since 1990. Is an alarm still worth installing now?

That depends on your house, not on the precinct, and we would rather you decide it that way. The figures are real: the CompStat report for the week of November 2 to 8, 2026, shows 155 burglaries in the precinct this year to date against 210 last year, and 2,433 in the whole of 1990 against 247 in the whole of 2025. A falling average is good news for everyone, but the burglaries that still happen are not spread evenly. They fall on houses and stores whose weak points are out of sight, whose owners are out at predictable hours, and which nobody would notice being entered for some time. If your back door faces a yard that nobody overlooks, if the house is empty from seven in the morning to six at night, or if you run a store that is empty overnight, an alarm still changes what happens on the day somebody tries. If your house is rarely empty and every door is in plain view, you may need very little, and the survey will say so.

Stores, delis and bodegas

When a crew breaks into a deli overnight for the ATM, is a loud siren or a silent alarm the better choice?

For most East New York stores, both: a silent report first, and a loud siren a few seconds later inside the store. The crews described in the 2022 reports worked fast. At the Glenmore Avenue bodega, amNY reported, three people were in and out with the ATM, the register and the merchandise, and the Fulton Street video showed a man already dragging the machine to the window. A completely silent alarm gives the police the best chance of arriving while people are still inside, but in a burglary that lasts a few minutes, it often means the crew finishes undisturbed. A loud siren the instant the gate lifts usually makes a crew leave, but it gives them warning before anyone has been told. What we usually set is a report to the monitoring station and the owner’s phone the moment the gate or door opens, followed by the interior siren after a short delay, with the siren cutting off well inside the city’s fifteen-minute limit because there are apartments over most of these stores. The owner, who knows the store and the block, can change that balance, and we explain the trade-off before deciding.

We own a building on Pitkin Avenue with our store on the ground floor and a rented apartment upstairs. Should the building have one alarm or two?

Two, sharing nothing but the building. The store and the apartment have different occupants, different hours and different risks, and the tenant is entitled to privacy about when they come and go. A store alarm on its own panel protects the gate, the glass, the store door, the rear door, the cellar and the roof, and it reports to you. If the tenant wants an alarm, the apartment gets a separate small panel, with its own codes, reporting to the tenant. The one place where the two meet is the street door to the stair, which usually sits beside the store gate. We suggest a contact on that door reporting to the apartment’s alarm and, with the tenant’s agreement, a camera covering both the gate and the stair door, so that a person working at either at night is recorded.

The industrial park

Our building in the East Brooklyn Industrial Park has a fenced yard where our trucks stay overnight. Can the alarm cover the yard as well as the building?

Yes, but the yard needs different equipment and different rules from the building. Inside, the building gets ordinary contacts and motion detectors, and every door, hatch and skylight goes on the alarm. Outside, ordinary motion detectors are the wrong tool, because wind, rain, birds, headlights and the trucks themselves trigger them. In a fenced yard we use outdoor beam detectors along the inside of the fence line, which only trip when something breaks the beam, and cameras whose detection is drawn to the yard and not to the street beyond the fence. The yard is set as its own area that arms only when the last truck is in and the gate is shut, and it sends alerts with a camera clip rather than sounding the building siren immediately, so the owner or the monitoring station can see whether it is a person or a stray dog before anyone is dispatched. A contact on the vehicle gate tells you if it is opened after hours.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in East New York

These are claims that turn up again and again in generic online advice and machine-written answers about burglar alarms. They are paraphrased, not quoted, and each is set beside what we actually find on East New York jobs.

“Burglars use the front door, so protect the front first.”

Advice written for suburban houses often starts at the front door, because that is where a lot of suburban break-ins begin. On a Nehemiah block, the front door opens onto a parking pad in full view of the street and of a kitchen window that was put there, at the residents’ own request, so that someone could watch the yard and the street.

The part of these houses that nobody watches is the back: a small fenced yard, a rear door, a basement door and back windows facing other people’s yards. That is where we start the survey and where the first sensors go.

The front still gets a contact, and usually a video doorbell. It simply is not where the budget goes first.

“Crime here is high, so you need the biggest package.”

Online neighborhood scores often describe East New York by its past. The NYPD’s own figures for the 75th Precinct show burglary down 26.2 percent this year to date and about ninety percent over 33 years. Selling a large system on fear is easy and not honest.

A compact rowhouse with three doors and a handful of reachable windows needs a short list of sensors, which usually fits our basic residential system or a wireless system in the $600 to $1,200 range.

The size of the system should follow the house: how many doors, how many reachable windows, whether there is a rental floor, and whether anyone is home during the day.

“You need a landline for an alarm to call the monitoring company.”

That was true of the dialers in older alarm systems, including many we find in East New York houses, and it is why some of those systems now ring only inside the house. When the household moved to cable or fiber, the dialer lost its line.

Current panels report over their own cellular link, with battery backup, and need no phone line at all. They can use the home internet as a second path if the owner wants one.

An old system with sound wiring can often be rescued with a new panel and communicator on the existing wiring, which is listed on our price sheet at $800 to $1,500.

“Every alarm needs a big outdoor siren so the neighbors hear it.”

In detached suburbs, an outside bell may be the only way anyone hears an alarm. In East New York most houses are attached, and an interior siren in one rowhouse is heard clearly next door.

New York City’s Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to stop automatically within fifteen minutes. An outdoor bell that rings for the full fifteen minutes at 3 a.m. on a row of attached houses mostly teaches the block to resent alarms.

We fit interior sirens, set a shorter cutoff, and rely on the monitoring station and the owner’s phone to get the message to a person.

“Apartment residents can’t have an alarm because they don’t own the building.”

Tenants in East New York, whether in a rezoning-era building, a NYCHA development or Starrett City, are often told an alarm is not possible. What is not possible is wiring into the landlord’s building or mounting devices in the hallway.

A tenant’s alarm is wireless, battery-powered and fixed lightly to the inside of the apartment door and windows, with a small panel that plugs into an outlet. It comes out cleanly when the tenant moves.

The lease and the management office still have their say, and we put the plan in writing for them before the visit.

“A store alarm stops smash-and-grab burglaries.”

The 2022 ATM thefts in East New York were done by crews who cut locks or broke glass and were in and out in minutes. No sensor stops a person who has already broken a window.

What an alarm does is report the first second of the attack to a monitoring station and to the owner, sound inside the store so the crew knows it has been heard, and leave a timed record that matches the camera footage. That is worth having, but it is not the same as prevention.

Prevention is physical: a bolted safe, an ATM anchored as its owner requires, a solid rear door, and cash taken off the premises at night. We say so on every commercial survey.

“Professional installation always means a long monitoring contract.”

Many national dealers finance equipment through monitoring agreements of several years, and homeowners on forums such as Reddit describe exactly that arrangement. It is a business model, not something the technology requires.

We sell the equipment at our published Brooklyn list price. Monitoring through our partners is optional, month to month, from $30 a month, and many East New York owners start with phone alerts only.

If you later move, sell or decide you do not want monitoring, the equipment is still yours.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start in the Back Yard, Finish on the Parking Pad

This describes how our technicians survey an East New York rowhouse, not a story about one particular job. The visit starts with a short walk past the front: the parking pad, the front door, the kitchen window and the windows above it. Then the technician goes through the house and out to the back yard, which is where the real work is. He stands with his back to the fence and looks at the house: the rear door and its frame, the back windows on both floors, any basement door or window at grade, and anything near the wall that a person could climb on, from a shed roof to an air-conditioner bracket. He looks over the fence too, because the yards behind belong to houses on the next street, and how open or overgrown they are says a lot about who could approach unseen.

Then he goes down to the basement, tries its door and windows, notes where the boiler or furnace and the water heater stand, and asks who uses the basement and how. Only after that does he walk the routes from each door to the bedrooms to choose where the motion detector belongs, and ask about the household: who is home in the day, who comes in late and by which door, whether anyone rents a floor. The written price follows that walk line by line. On stores the order is the same: the gate and the glass first, then the back door, the cellar and the roof, then the counter and the ATM.

What does an alarm for a warehouse or shop in the East Brooklyn Industrial Park cost on your Brooklyn list?

Can we tell the NYPD where our store’s cameras are, so detectives know to ask for the footage after a break-in nearby?
+

Yes. The city’s 311 service explains that you can register your security camera locations with the NYPD through NewYorkCityConnect.org. Registering tells the police that a camera exists at your address; it does not give them live access. We can help you note the camera positions when we install them alongside the alarm.

Does a Nehemiah homeowners association have to approve an alarm before it goes into our house?
+

We found no published rule from the Brooklyn Nehemiah homeowners associations about alarms, and an alarm mounted inside your own house usually raises no association question at all. If you want an outdoor camera or anything on a shared wall or fence, ask the association first; we can send a short description of the work and our certificate of insurance for them to see.

Will anything have to be fixed to the brick outside of our Nehemiah house?
+

Usually nothing for the alarm itself. The panel, keypad, siren and cellular communicator all sit inside, and contacts go on the inside of doors and windows. Only optional extras such as a floodlight camera over the back door or a video doorbell at the front are mounted outside, with small fixings into the mortar joints where possible rather than the face of the brick.

Can one glass-break detector in the hall hear a window break behind a closed bedroom door?
+

Not reliably. Acoustic glass-break detectors are rated for a distance in open air, and a closed door cuts the sound a great deal. In a small rowhouse we place a glass-break detector inside the room that has the glass, usually a back room with two windows, and use contacts or the hall motion detector for the rest of the house.

Our East New York house was broken into this week. How quickly can an alarm go in?
+

Requests are booked within 2 hours, and a technician can usually survey the house soon after, with the written price given on that visit. Installation follows once the price is agreed and any damaged door or window has been made secure. If you need something the same night, tell us on the phone and we will say honestly what is possible.

The burglar damaged our back door frame. Should it be repaired before you put a sensor on it?
+

Yes. A contact reports whether a door is open or closed, so it needs a door that closes properly in a frame that holds. Have a carpenter or locksmith repair or replace the frame, the strike and the lock first. We can fit the rest of the system before that and add the back door contact as soon as the door is sound.

Does the alarm’s cellular connection depend on our own phone plan or carrier?
+

No. The communicator inside the panel has its own cellular connection, which is part of the monitoring or app service, not your personal phone plan. Changing your phone carrier, losing your phone or letting a family plan lapse does not affect how the alarm reports. Your phone only matters for receiving the alerts and using the app.

How many sensors can one panel handle? Is it enough for a three-family house?
+

The panels we install take far more wireless sensors than a three-family house normally needs, and they can be divided into separate areas for each floor with their own codes. Capacity is rarely the limit in East New York houses. The practical limits are the number of keypads you want and how the floors and the basement are shared.

Our basement has a furnace and a dehumidifier running. Will they set off a motion detector down there?
+

They can if the detector is aimed at them, because passive infrared detectors react to warm air moving across their view. We mount basement detectors so they look along the stair and the door rather than at the furnace, the dehumidifier or a dryer vent, and we walk-test them with the equipment running before we leave.

My mother already wears a medical alert pendant from another company. Will a new alarm interfere with it?
+

They are separate systems and normally work side by side without trouble. If she would rather have one system, a panic pendant on the new alarm can send an emergency signal through the same panel and monitoring station, but we suggest keeping her existing service until the new one has been tested and she is comfortable with it.

Does an alarm need yearly maintenance, and what does it cost if something needs fixing later?
+

There is no required yearly service for a home alarm. The panel reports low batteries and faults on its own, and most owners only need to replace sensor batteries when told to.
