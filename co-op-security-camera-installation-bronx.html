Bronx Co-ops

The Bronx Holds the World's Largest Housing Co-op and Some of the City's Smallest

A camera plan for a Bronx cooperative starts with a question most other boroughs never have to ask: which kind of co-op is this? The borough runs from a single development of tens of thousands of residents to six-unit HDFC walk-ups that are run by whichever shareholders have time on a Tuesday night. Both are housing cooperatives. Both have a board of directors. They need very different camera systems, and they buy them in very different ways.

At one end sits Co-op City. Riverbay Corporation describes it as a state Mitchell-Lama cooperative with 15,372 apartments in 35 high-rise buildings and seven townhouse clusters, plus three shopping centers, three community centers and eight parking garages with more than 10,000 spaces. Only about a fifth of its 320 acres is built on. Wikipedia calls it the largest housing cooperative in the world. A camera project there is a campus project, measured in buildings and garage levels, not in cameras.

At the other end are the HDFC co-ops. A Bronx real estate practice that handles co-op closings describes the borough as holding "one of the densest bands of HDFC buildings in the city, through Mott Haven, Highbridge, Morrisania and the Grand Concourse." These are limited-equity buildings formed from city-owned property, supervised by HPD, often with no super on site and a small reserve. A good camera plan for one of them may be three cameras and a recorder.

Between the two is most of the Bronx co-op market. The same firm says Bronx co-ops "cluster in the Riverdale and Spuyten Duyvil belt," with elevator buildings along the Henry Hudson Parkway and central Riverdale, large complexes along Pelham Parkway and the Grand Concourse that converted from rentals, and garden co-ops in Throggs Neck and Country Club that read as low-rise rentals from the street. CooperatorNews put it plainly in a Riverdale market report: "co-ops tend to predominate in Riverdale." A Homes.com guide to Pelham Parkway notes that prewar buildings there "range from mid-rise elevator buildings to garden-style complexes with landscaped courtyards, and midcentury brick co-ops are also common."

Then there is the limited-equity tradition that began here. The Amalgamated Housing Cooperative, on Van Cortlandt Park South, says it was founded in 1927 and is "the oldest limited equity housing cooperative in the United States." Lehman College's Bronx public art survey describes the Amalgamated houses as eleven apartment buildings "scattered over six blocks just south of Van Cortlandt Park," built between 1927 and 1970, ranging from six-story mock-Tudor buildings to a pair of 24-story towers, with a garden common reached through a tunnel-like portal. That one description holds every camera problem a Bronx board meets: walk-ups, towers, slopes, courtyards and passages, all under one corporation.

One correction we make often: Parkchester

Boards and buyers regularly call Parkchester a co-op. It isn't. Its buildings were converted to condominiums, about a third in 1974 and the rest as Parkchester South Condominium in 1986. Owners there are unit owners, not shareholders, and the association's documents work differently. If your building is in Parkchester, our page on condo security camera installation in the Bronx is the one written for you.

Bronx co-op facts that shape a camera plan

  • Co-op City: 15,372 apartments, 35 high-rises, seven townhouse clusters, eight garages with more than 10,000 spaces, about 160 residential elevators (Riverbay Corporation).
  • Co-op City is supervised by the state Division of Housing and Community Renewal (HCR), like all Mitchell-Lama cooperatives (Riverbay Corporation).
  • The Amalgamated Housing Cooperative dates to 1927 and calls itself the oldest limited-equity housing co-op in the country (Amalgamated; Lehman College).
  • HDFC co-ops are limited-equity buildings supervised by HPD, with income limits up to 165% of area median income (NYC HPD).
  • Parkchester converted to condominiums in 1974 and 1986; it is not a co-op (Wikipedia, Parkchester).
  • The Grand Concourse Historic District, designated in 2011, covers 78 properties, many with original mosaic and terrazzo vestibules (CityLand; LPC designation report).
Approvals

Who Signs Off on Cameras Depends on What Kind of Bronx Co-op You Are

In every cooperative, the corporation owns the building and the shareholders hold proprietary leases. Lobbies, halls, cellars, garages and grounds are corporate property, and decisions about them belong to the board of directors acting under the bylaws. New York's highest court set the standard in Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue Apartment Corp. (1990): courts generally defer to a board's good-faith decisions made within its authority and for the purposes of the cooperative. That is why a camera plan is usually a board vote, not a shareholder vote. What sits around that vote changes a great deal from one Bronx co-op to the next.

Market-rate co-ops in Riverdale, Pelham Parkway and the Concourse

Most of these buildings have a managing agent and a board of five to nine directors. The agent collects proposals, the board votes, the agent signs and schedules. The practical steps are the ones in the rest of this page: a survey, a written plan the board can read in ten minutes, a vote, a notice to shareholders. Some boards send the plan to the co-op's attorney first, especially for hallway cameras, and we write the plan so it can be read that way.

State-supervised Mitchell-Lama co-ops

Co-op City and other state Mitchell-Lama developments answer to the state Division of Housing and Community Renewal (HCR) as well as to their own boards. Riverbay notes that, like all Mitchell-Lama cooperatives, Co-op City is supervised by HCR. In practice that can mean purchasing procedures, bid formats and approval steps that a market-rate building does not have. We respond to whatever format the bid package sets and leave the rules to the management office, which knows them better than any contractor.

City-supervised Mitchell-Lama co-ops

Bronx developments in the city's Mitchell-Lama program deal with HPD. A 2018 State Comptroller audit summarized HPD's Mitchell-Lama rules as requiring competitive bids and HPD approval for contracts over $100,000 for building services, repairs and replacements. Most camera projects come in well below that line, but a full campus replacement may not, and a board should know before it asks for prices.

HDFC co-ops

HPD describes HDFC co-ops as limited-equity cooperatives incorporated under the Business Corporation Law and Article XI of the Private Housing Finance Law, with shareholders electing a board and HPD supervising under a regulatory agreement. Many small Bronx HDFCs are self-managed. The treasurer is often the person who calls us, and the camera plan has to fit a budget measured in hundreds or low thousands of dollars, not tens of thousands.

Buildings where a sponsor still holds apartments

Many Bronx co-ops converted from rentals in the 1980s, and some sponsors still hold blocks of apartments rented to tenants. The sponsor's apartments are still shares in the corporation, and the board still decides about common areas, but the sponsor may sit on the board or pay a large share of maintenance. Renters in sponsor apartments see the same cameras as shareholders, so the notice the board sends should reach them too.

High-Rise Co-ops

Cameras for Bronx Co-op Towers: Riverdale Elevator Buildings and Campus High-Rises

A tall co-op has a short list of places where almost everything happens: the front door, the mail and package area, the elevator lobby on the ground floor, the garage, the service entrance and the trash room. The upper floors matter much less than people expect. A 20-story building does not need 20 floors of hallway cameras. It needs the handful of points every person must pass to get in, get out or carry something away, each covered well enough to identify a face.

The entrance and vestibule

The camera that does the most work in any Bronx tower looks at the inner vestibule door from the inside, at about head height, so it sees the face of everyone who comes through. A second camera covers the outer door and the sidewalk approach in a wider view. Riverdale buildings often face west over the river, and that afternoon light is brutal on a camera looking out through glass. We mount the face camera to look in toward the lobby rather than out toward the street wherever the layout allows, and use wide dynamic range cameras where it doesn't.

Mailrooms and package rooms

Package theft in a staffed tower usually happens in the gap: the doorman steps away, the porter is on another floor, a courier leaves a stack by the desk. A camera over the package shelves, aimed so it sees the person reaching for the box and not just the top of their head, settles most disputes. In unstaffed buildings, the package room door matters more than the shelves.

The ground-floor elevator lobby and the cabs

Riverbay says it modernized all 160 residential elevators in Co-op City's high-rises. Elevator work like that is a good moment to plan cab cameras, because the elevator contractor is already in the car. We supply the cab camera and the wireless link that carries its picture down the hoistway to the recorder; anything inside the car's controls or traveling cable stays with the elevator company. A camera at the ground-floor elevator lobby is cheaper, needs no elevator contractor and catches most of what a cab camera would.

Compactor and trash rooms

Towers built in the 1960s and 1970s often have a compactor or chute room on every floor. Bags left on the floor beside the chute, furniture left on the landing, and the occasional fire in a chute are among the most common shareholder complaints we hear in big co-ops. A camera in every chute room is rarely worth it. Cameras on the floors with repeat problems, placed by the super's records, usually are.

Stairwells and roof doors

Fire stairs in a high-rise are long, quiet and rarely used, which is why people end up in them who shouldn't. We cover the stair doors at the lobby level and the roof door, and leave the intermediate landings alone unless the board has a specific problem. Cameras never go on the latch or the door hardware itself; exit doors stay exactly as the fire code requires.

Garages & Parking

Co-op Garages and Parking Fields in the Bronx

Parking is a bigger part of Bronx co-op life than anywhere else in the city. Co-op City alone has eight garages with more than 10,000 spaces. Riverdale elevator co-ops often have a garage cut into the hillside under the building. Garden co-ops in Throggs Neck, Country Club and along Pelham Parkway have open lots behind the buildings. Each needs a different camera layout, but the order of priorities is the same.

Entrances and exits first

Every car and every person that enters a garage passes the ramp and the pedestrian door. Cameras there, set to show the front of the vehicle and the face of the driver or walker, are worth more than any number of cameras along the parking rows. On a sloped Riverdale ramp, the camera goes where the car is level and slow, usually at the gate or just past it, not halfway down the incline.

The pedestrian door and the stairs to the lobby

Many garage break-ins in residential buildings are not about cars at all. Someone walks in behind a car, then uses the garage stair to reach the building. A camera on the inside of the garage-to-building door, aimed at head height, is often the single most useful camera in a co-op garage.

Rows, ramps and columns

Inside a structured garage, columns hide most of the floor from any one point. We cover the drive aisles with bullet cameras looking along the aisle, so a car moving through is in view for several seconds, and accept that not every parked car will be in frame. Concrete garage ceilings take anchors well, but they are often damp or spalling near the ramps, and we pick mounting points on sound concrete away from leaks.

Open lots behind garden co-ops

An open lot needs fewer cameras than people expect and more light than most have. A camera on the building wall looking down the length of the lot, with a second at the lot entrance, covers most garden co-ops. Where the lot is dark, color cameras with their own small white lights, or infrared models, make the picture usable without new pole lighting, which is electrical work we don't do.

License plates

Boards often ask for every plate on every car. A plate is readable only when a camera is set up for it: a narrow view at the gate, the right angle, a slow car, and enough light. We can set one camera up that way at a garage entrance. General overview cameras will show the car, its color and its direction, but not reliably its plate.

Multi-Building Campuses

Long Campuses, Many Doors: Planning Cameras for Multi-Building Bronx Co-ops

Several of the largest Bronx co-ops are not buildings at all but campuses: towers and townhouse clusters on one property, or a string of buildings on a slope like the Amalgamated houses south of Van Cortlandt Park. Camera plans for these properties are mostly about distance, consistency and who watches.

One recorder per building, or one central room

Network cable for cameras has a practical limit of about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, from switch to camera. On a campus, that usually means a recorder or a network switch in each building, connected back to a central point. Some boards prefer each building's video to stay in that building, so a fault in one never takes down the others. Others want everything in one locked room near the management office. Both work. What matters is that the board decides once, and every building follows the same pattern.

Getting video between buildings

Between buildings, we use fiber where a conduit or an existing cable path already runs, and point-to-point wireless bridges where it doesn't. Fiber doesn't care about weather or trees. Wireless bridges are fast to install and cost far less than trenching, but need a clear line of sight from roof to roof or wall to wall. On a hillside campus, a bridge mounted on the uphill building can usually see the roofs below it.

The same cameras and settings everywhere

A campus that grows building by building over ten years ends up with five different camera brands and five different apps, and nobody knows all of them. We set one standard on the first building: the same camera models for the same jobs, the same recording settings, the same naming scheme for cameras, the same retention. Each later building plugs into that standard, and staff only learn one system.

Working beside a campus public safety department

Co-op City runs its own public safety department. Its website describes sworn New York City and State certified peace officers, a patrol services unit and an on-site 24-hour dispatch center. Where a co-op has staff like that, the camera plan should be written with them: which cameras their dispatchers see live, which they can play back, and who exports clips. Our job is the cameras, the recorders and the accounts; how the officers use them is the co-op's.

City cameras on the streets around the campus

State Senator Jamaal T. Bailey put $1 million toward NYPD cameras in the 45th Precinct, aimed at Co-op City, according to a Bronx Times story from December 2025, which quoted him: "These tools will help law enforcement better prevent crime, respond more effectively, and provide residents with greater peace of mind." Police cameras watch public streets for the police. They do not show the inside of a co-op lobby, garage or laundry room, and the board has no access to them. A co-op's own cameras are still the only record of what happens on its property.

Prewar Co-ops

Prewar Co-ops on the Grand Concourse and Pelham Parkway

Below the towers, the Bronx has a deep stock of five- and six-story prewar buildings, many of them now co-ops: Art Deco apartment houses along the Grand Concourse, mid-rise elevator buildings and garden courts along Pelham Parkway, and older walk-ups on the side streets. They share features that change how cameras go in.

Decorated vestibules

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's report on the Grand Concourse Historic District, designated in 2011 with 78 properties, describes buildings with a "concave main-entry vestibule featuring beige and gold mosaic tile and terrazzo flooring." Nobody wants a camera bolted into that tile. We look first for the plain surfaces: the ceiling, a painted plaster band above the tile, a door frame, a steel transom. Cable comes in from above, through the ceiling or a closet, not across the mosaic. On buildings inside the historic district, exterior cameras follow the LPC's permit guidance, which we cover below.

Courtyards and side entrances

Many Pelham Parkway and Concourse buildings are built around a courtyard, with two, three or four entrances off it, each leading to a separate stair hall. Each of those doors is a front door to the people who use it. A camera at each stair-hall door, aimed at the person entering, is the core of the plan. A wide courtyard camera on top of that is nice to have, not essential.

Cable paths in old buildings

Prewar buildings hide useful paths: abandoned dumbwaiter shafts, old intercom risers, gas pipe chases, the space behind a stair stringer. Our survey tech looks for these first. A camera cable that rides an existing riser to the lobby costs a fraction of one that needs a new channel through plaster, and leaves nothing for the board to repaint.

Walk-ups with no elevator and no super on site

Walk-up co-ops have fewer places to watch and less money to watch them with. The front door, the cellar door and the mailboxes cover most of them. The harder question is where the recorder lives. It should be in a locked common room with power and an internet connection, not in the treasurer's apartment. If no such room exists, a small lockable wall cabinet in the cellar or the first-floor hall is better than keeping the recorder in someone's home.

HDFC & Self-Managed

HDFC and Self-Managed Co-ops: Cameras on a Small Budget

HPD's own description of HDFC co-ops sets the frame: shareholders' household income is generally capped at up to 165% of area median income, the building is supervised by HPD, and money from flip taxes goes back into capital repairs and reserves. For a 10- to 30-unit HDFC in Mott Haven, Highbridge or Morrisania, a camera system is a real expense, and the board needs to see exactly what it is paying for.

Start with the doors that matter

In most small Bronx HDFCs we suggest starting with the front door from the inside, the mail area, and the cellar or rear door. That is three cameras. Our Bronx starting system has four cameras and a recorder for $950 installed, which leaves one camera for whatever the board's biggest problem is, often the stairs to the roof or the yard where the trash goes out. Concealed cable inside finished walls is priced separately, and in walk-ups we usually avoid it by running cable in the stair hall in painted raceway.

Who holds the passwords when there is no staff

Self-managed co-ops lose access to their own cameras more often than any other kind of building, usually because the one person who set them up moved away. We hand the main password to the board in writing, and set up at least two personal logins for officers, so the building never depends on one shareholder's phone. When officers change, the outgoing ones lose access and the new ones get theirs.

Paying for it

Boards fund camera work from the operating budget, from reserves, or by assessment under the bylaws; the treasurer and the co-op's accountant decide which. Because equipment is ordered only after the deposit, and the 50% deposit is the only money due before work starts, a small board can plan the purchase around its next maintenance cycle. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Across the Bronx

Co-op Neighborhoods We Cover, and the Camera Question Each One Raises

We work in co-ops across the whole borough from our office on East Fordham Road. These are the neighborhoods where Bronx co-ops are concentrated, and the first thing a camera plan has to settle in each.

Riverdale and Spuyten Duyvil

Elevator co-ops with doormen, garages under the building and lobbies facing the river. The first question is glare: entrance cameras have to cope with bright western light through lobby glass, and garage cameras with a steep ramp. Desk staff usually want a live monitor, which shapes where the recorder and screen go.

Van Cortlandt Park South and Kingsbridge

Home of the Amalgamated and neighboring limited-equity co-ops, with buildings of different ages spread over several blocks of hillside. The first question is distance: which buildings share a recorder, and how video crosses streets and courtyards between them.

Co-op City and Baychester

Towers, townhouse clusters and garages on a single campus with its own management and public safety staff. The first question is standards and phasing: one camera standard for every building, rolled out in a sequence the capital program can carry.

Pelham Parkway, Allerton and Morris Park

Prewar mid-rise and garden courtyard co-ops and midcentury brick buildings. The first question is the number of entrances: each courtyard stair hall is its own front door, and each needs its own camera before the courtyard itself.

The Grand Concourse and Fordham

Art Deco buildings, many converted to co-ops, with decorated vestibules and some inside the historic district. The first question is surfaces: where a camera and its cable can go without touching mosaic, terrazzo or ornament, and what the LPC will approve outside.

Throggs Neck, Country Club and Pelham Bay

Garden co-ops that look like low-rise rentals from the street, with lots and grounds behind and the Sound close by. The first question is weather and light: outdoor cameras that survive salt wind and still give a usable picture of a dark lot.

Mott Haven, Highbridge and Morrisania

The heart of the borough's HDFC stock, mostly walk-ups and small elevator buildings, many self-managed. The first question is budget and custody: the fewest cameras that cover the doors, and a password plan that survives the next board election.

Problems Cameras Solve

The Problems Bronx Co-op Boards Call About, and What a Camera Can Do for Each

Boards rarely call to say they want cameras. They call because something keeps happening and nobody can say who did it. Here is what we hear most from Bronx co-ops, and what a camera can and can't do about each.

Packages disappearing from the lobby

A camera over the drop area and a camera at the door, both at a height that shows faces, turn "my package was stolen" into a clip the managing agent can give to the shareholder and, if needed, to the local precinct. The camera does not stop the theft. Moving drop-off into a locked room does that, and the camera makes the locked room accountable.

Strangers following cars into the garage

The usual pattern is a person on foot walking in under a closing gate. A camera on the ramp and one on the garage-to-building door show it happening and when. The fix for the gate itself, a faster close or a sensor adjustment, is the gate company's job; we can tell the board which cameras will prove whether the change worked.

Damage and arguments in the laundry room

Broken machine doors, missing clothes and arguments over who left what in the dryer. A vandal-resistant dome that covers the door and the machines, with no audio, settles most of it. Signs at the door saying the room is recorded prevent much of the rest.

Elevator damage on move-in day

Scraped cab walls and dented doors are a constant cost in co-op towers. A camera at the ground-floor elevator lobby, plus a cab camera where the board wants one, tells the managing agent which move caused the damage so the moving deposit can cover it.

Doors propped open

Side and cellar doors get wedged open for deliveries, aides, contractors and smokers. A camera on the inside of each problem door shows who opened it and how long it stayed open. Some recorders can send an alert when a door camera sees movement at night; the board decides who receives it.

Dumping in lots and by the trash enclosure

Mattresses and construction debris left behind garden co-ops and by garage entrances cost the co-op money to haul away. A bullet camera looking along the approach to the enclosure, with enough light at night, shows the vehicle and the person.

Slip-and-fall and damage claims

When someone says they fell on the front steps or a car was dented in the garage, the first thing the insurer asks is whether there is video. A camera on the steps and the garage aisles, with retention long enough to outlast the time it takes a claim to arrive, gives the board an answer.

Contractor damage during capital projects

Facade work, boiler replacements and elevator modernizations bring outside crews through the lobby and cellar for months. Cameras on the service entrance and the routes those crews use show what was already damaged before the project and what was damaged during it.

Camera shapes and where each fits

Domes go on ceilings in lobbies, laundry rooms and garages; the vandal-resistant versions take a kick or a broom handle. Turrets go over doors and on outside corners; they keep their lens clear of rain and don't collect spider webs over the glass like domes can outdoors. Bullets go on long views, such as a garage aisle, a lot or a path between buildings, and their shape tells people they are being recorded. Pan-tilt-zoom cameras are rarely the right answer for a co-op: when one is looking left, nothing is recording on the right. We suggest them only for very large open grounds, and always alongside fixed cameras.

DVR or NVR

Our Bronx price list starts with an analog high-definition system on a digital video recorder: four 2MP eyeball cameras and a 1080p DVR, installed, for $950. That system is a good fit when a building already has coax cable in good condition, because the new cameras can reuse it. Network systems, where each camera gets one network cable that carries power and video to an NVR, start at $984 for four 2MP IP eyeball cameras. Four 4MP dome cameras on a 4K NVR are $1,157, and four 5MP eyeball cameras on a 4K NVR with a monitor are $1,479. Most new co-op systems we install are network systems, because they scale more easily across a building.

Resolution and lenses

Higher resolution helps when one camera must cover a wide space. At a vestibule door, where the person is a few feet away, a 2MP or 4MP camera with the right lens shows a face as clearly as an 8MP camera with the wrong one. We pick the lens for the distance first, then the resolution.

Night pictures

Infrared cameras see in the dark in black and white, with no visible light. Color night cameras use a small built-in white light, or a very sensitive sensor, to keep color after dark. For a co-op garage or a lot where the board wants to know the color of a car, color night models are worth the difference. For a cellar corridor, infrared is enough.

How long the recordings last

Retention is set by the size of the hard drives, the number of cameras and the quality setting. The trade groups that advise New York co-ops suggest 30 to 90 days. We size drives for the number of days the board chooses, and write that number on the handover sheet so nobody assumes footage is kept longer than it is.

Power, backup and the electrical line we don't cross

Network cameras draw power through their data cable from a PoE switch or the recorder, so most cameras need no outlet at all. The recorder and switch plug into an existing outlet in the equipment room, on a battery backup that rides out short outages and shuts the system down cleanly in long ones. We are a low-voltage contractor and do not do electrical work. If a location truly needs a new outlet, the co-op's electrician installs it.

Taking Over a Camera System the Co-op Already Has

Most Bronx co-ops that call us already have cameras: an analog system from the 2000s, a recorder from a company that no longer answers the phone, or a mix of both after a few repairs. Before we propose anything new, we look at what is there.

Keep what works

Cameras that still give a clear picture can stay. Coax that tests clean can carry new high-definition analog cameras, and a newer recorder can often run old and new cameras side by side while the board phases in the rest. Our survey notes, camera by camera, which ones are worth keeping.

Working With Other Systems

How Co-op Cameras Fit With the Intercom, Fobs and Elevators

Cameras are rarely the only security system in a Bronx co-op, and the plan works better when it knows what else is in the building.

The lobby intercom

Many video intercoms have their own small camera at the entry panel, which shows the visitor to the apartment being called. That camera usually does not record to the building's recorder. Co-ops that restrict elevator access by floor often pair it with a cab or elevator-lobby camera. The elevator contractor handles anything inside the car's controls; our elevator access control work and our cameras stay on our side of that line.

One equipment room

Where the building already has a locked room for the intercom and access control equipment, the camera recorder usually belongs there too, on the same battery backup and the same internet line, with one labeled rack instead of three loose boxes.

New York Rules

What New York Law Says About Cameras in a Bronx Co-op

This section summarizes public sources so a board knows what to ask its attorney. It is not legal advice, and the co-op's counsel is the right person to apply it to your building's documents.

Common areas are the board's to manage

Ricardo M. Vera of Newman Ferrara told CooperatorNews in 2025 that New York "does not recognize a common law right of privacy in public or common areas." Combined with the board's authority under the bylaws and the business judgment rule from Levandusky, that is why boards can generally place cameras in lobbies, halls, laundry rooms and garages without a shareholder vote, as long as they follow their own documents.

Video only, never sound

Under New York Penal Law § 250.05, eavesdropping is a class E felony, and § 250.00 counts the mechanical overhearing of a conversation as eavesdropping unless at least one person in it consents. Many cameras come with microphones. On every co-op job we turn audio off and note it on the handover sheet.

Rooms that are off-limits

Penal Law § 250.45, unlawful surveillance, covers recording people in places such as bedrooms, changing rooms, restrooms and bathrooms. New York Labor Law § 203-c separately bars employers from video recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms and changing areas, which matters for the super's and porters' spaces in a co-op cellar.

Shareholders' own cameras in the hall

Video doorbells and peephole cameras on apartment doors look into common hallways. Habitat quoted attorney Debra Guzov in 2023: "you can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways." Many boards now write a house rule that either allows such devices with conditions or prohibits them. That is a board decision, made with counsel.

Landmarked buildings

Parts of the Bronx sit in historic districts, including the Grand Concourse, Riverdale and Fieldston districts. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidance asks that security cameras stay off the primary facade where possible, be small and painted to match, and be "well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," attached at mortar joints or plain surfaces rather than ornament. We include the camera cut sheets a permit application needs and mount to the approved locations.

Permits and licensing

New York City's Administrative Code § 28-105.4.7 exempts signaling and data wiring under 50 volts from an electrical permit, with exceptions for life-safety systems, so a low-voltage camera system normally needs no DOB electrical permit. New York State licenses installers of security systems, including CCTV and video surveillance used to detect or monitor intrusion, under 19 NYCRR Part 195. Our state license number, #12000287431, is on every proposal.

Footage Policy

A Footage Policy Written for How Bronx Co-ops Are Actually Run

FirstService Residential's 2024 guidance for New York co-ops and condos recommends keeping footage 30 to 90 days, logging every request and every viewing, and not giving residents access without prior approval from the board and legal counsel. Christina Forbes of FirstService put the reason simply: "Limiting access protects the privacy of residents and helps prevent potential misuse." The details of a good policy depend on who actually runs the building.

In a staffed tower with a managing agent

The agent's office is usually the custodian. The doorman or desk sees live views of the entrance and packages; the super sees cellar and garage views; playback and export are limited to the agent and one or two board officers. Requests from shareholders go to the agent in writing.

On a campus with its own public safety staff

Dispatchers need live views to send officers. Playback and export are often limited to supervisors. The policy should say how a clip is preserved when an incident report is written, so the video is still there when an insurer or the police ask a month later.

In a self-managed HDFC or small co-op

Without staff, the board itself is the custodian. Two officers hold logins, one of them exports clips, and the minutes record each request. Keeping the log in the board's minutes is simple and leaves a record when officers change.

Lines every Bronx co-op policy should include

  • Which cameras exist and what each one covers, attached as the camera map.
  • That no camera records sound.
  • How many days footage is kept before it is overwritten.
  • Who sees live views, who can play back, and who can export.
  • How shareholders, renters in sponsor apartments, insurers and police request a clip.
  • That every request and export is logged with date, requester and reason.
  • What happens to logins when staff, officers or the managing agent change.

Bronx Conditions

Wind, Water, Settling Ground and Old Cellars

The Bronx has its own set of physical conditions, and a co-op camera that ignores them starts failing in the second winter.

Wind and spray on the east side

Co-op City sits between the Hutchinson River and Eastchester Bay, and garden co-ops in Throggs Neck and Country Club face Long Island Sound. Exposed walls there need weather-rated housings, corrosion-resistant brackets and cable entries that point down. We check that each outdoor camera is tight on its mount at handover, because a camera that slowly turns in the wind is the most common outdoor failure we see.

Ground that moves

Wikipedia's account of Co-op City notes that the buildings stand on pilings to bedrock while the land around them settles a fraction of an inch a year, cracking sidewalks and building entrances. Anywhere a cable crosses from a building onto settling ground, or into an entrance slab, we leave slack and use flexible conduit so movement pulls on the conduit and not the camera.

Hills and slopes in the west Bronx

Riverdale, Spuyten Duyvil and the Amalgamated blocks sit on slopes. Garage ramps are steep, buildings step down the hill, and a camera on the uphill side can see over the roofs of the downhill buildings. We use that height for wireless bridges between buildings, and keep entrance cameras low enough to see faces rather than hats.

Old cellars, boilers and damp

Bronx prewar cellars are often warm near the boiler and damp near the street wall. Recorders hate both. We put the recorder in the coolest, driest locked space available, raise it off the floor, and keep it away from steam lines. In buildings that have flooded, it goes above the high-water line, even if that means a higher floor.

Snow and power cuts

Snow piles against ground-level cameras near lots and drives, and plows throw slush. We mount lot cameras on building walls well above plow height. The battery backup on the recorder covers short outages, and the system restarts on its own when power comes back after a long one.

Questions

Bronx Co-op Camera Questions, Answered

Shareholders on our Mitchell-Lama waiting list keep asking whether cameras will raise carrying charges. What can the board say?

The board can give them the number. A camera system is a one-time capital cost with no monthly fee from us, and the board can show exactly what it costs and where the money comes from: operating budget, reserves or an assessment. In a supervised Mitchell-Lama, any change to carrying charges goes through the supervising agency anyway, so a camera purchase on its own rarely moves them. Showing the proposal, with its total, usually ends the rumor.

Bags keep piling up beside the trash chute on two floors of our tower. Is a camera in the chute room worth it?

On those two floors, often yes. A small dome inside the chute room, aimed at the door, shows who comes in and what they carry. Put it only where the super's records show a repeat problem. Covering every chute room on every floor adds cost and gives the board footage nobody will ever review.

Deliveries stack up in our Riverdale lobby while the doorman is on break. Where should the package camera point?

At the place where hands meet boxes. That is usually the shelf or table where couriers drop parcels, seen from slightly above and in front, so the camera shows the face of the person reaching in. A second camera at the door shows them leaving with it. A camera that looks straight down on the shelf mostly records the tops of heads.

Our only locked room in the cellar is the boiler room. Can the recorder live there?

We'd rather not put it there. Recorders and hard drives fail early in heat and damp, and a boiler room can be both. A small locked, ventilated wall cabinet in a cooler part of the cellar or the first-floor hall is usually better. If the boiler room really is the only option, we mount the recorder high, far from the boiler and steam lines, and the board should plan on checking its drive health more often.

Our new LED lobby lights make the camera picture flicker. Can that be fixed?

Usually, in the camera's settings. Some LED fixtures pulse at a rate that shows up as banding or flicker on video. Most cameras have an anti-flicker setting matched to 60 Hz power, and adjusting the exposure setting usually clears the rest. If the fixtures are very cheap, the fix is a different driver in the light, which is the electrician's job, not ours.

A director wants to post clips of people leaving trash in the courtyard on the lobby bulletin board. Is that wise?

Talk to the co-op's attorney first. Posting identifiable footage of shareholders, guests or staff can start disputes the board doesn't need and may raise legal issues the attorney should weigh. Most boards get better results by handling the matter privately through the managing agent, with the clip kept in the request log, and posting a general reminder instead.

Older shareholders use a side ramp because the front steps are hard for them. Should the ramp door get its own camera?

Yes, in most buildings. An accessible side entrance is a front door for the people who use it, and often the door that gets propped open for deliveries and aides. A turret above the ramp door, aimed at head height, treats it the same as the main entrance.

Our board is debating whether to leave the Mitchell-Lama program. Should we put off buying cameras until that's settled?

Not necessarily. Cameras protect the building whatever its financing, and the equipment stays with the corporation either way. The part that may change is purchasing procedure: while the co-op is supervised, its bidding and approval rules still apply. If the board waits, nothing it has spent on a survey or plan is lost, because nothing is ordered until the deposit is paid.

Should the camera notice to shareholders go out in more than one language?

In many Bronx co-ops it should. The notice only works if every household can read it, and many buildings already send maintenance notices in English and Spanish, or other languages their residents use. We supply plain-language wording for the board to translate, covering which areas have cameras, that nothing records sound, who sees the video and how long it is kept.

The security guard company the co-op hires wants to bring its own cameras. Should the board let it?

Usually not. If the guard company owns the cameras and the recorder, the footage and the passwords leave when the contract ends. A better arrangement is a system the corporation owns, with a personal login for the guard company that the board can switch off on the last day of the contract.

Reality Check

Five Things Bronx Co-op Boards Hear About Cameras That Aren't Quite Right

“A Mitchell-Lama co-op can't buy cameras without the state's permission”

Supervision affects how a supervised co-op buys, and large contracts may need bids or agency approval. It doesn't stop a board from deciding to install cameras. The managing agent or management office knows which steps apply to your development.

“Camera installation needs an electrician and a permit”

Network cameras run on low-voltage cable that carries their power, and the city's code exempts most wiring under 50 volts from an electrical permit. The recorder plugs into an existing outlet. An electrician is needed only if a location genuinely has no outlet nearby.

“Buy the highest-resolution camera and every face will be clear”

A face is clear when the camera is at the right height and distance, with the right lens and the light behind it. An 8MP camera looking down from a 12-foot ceiling into a backlit vestibule shows a dark scalp. A 2MP camera at head height facing the door shows the face.

“Our old coax has to be ripped out”

Coax in good condition can carry high-definition analog cameras to a modern DVR. We test it first and keep the runs that pass, which is often the cheapest way to upgrade a Concourse or Pelham Parkway building.

“Once the cameras are in, the building is secure”

Cameras record what happened. They don't lock doors. A lobby door that doesn't close on its own, or a garage gate that stays open, needs a hardware repair; the camera only shows who walked through. We often point out door problems on the survey and refer the board to the right page for them.
