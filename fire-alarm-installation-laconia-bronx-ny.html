Why Laconia's Store Rows, NYCHA Buildings and New Tower Need Fire Alarms Designed for How They Are Built

Laconia is the northern part of Allerton in the northeast Bronx. Wikipedia's description of Allerton draws it between East Gun Hill Road on the north, the IRT Dyre Avenue Line on the east, Allerton Avenue on the south and Bronxwood Avenue on the west. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws a different line, with Boston Road to the northwest and Williamsbridge Road to the west, and news outlets have called the same block Laconia, Allerton or Williamsbridge depending on the reporter. This page uses the area those definitions share: the streets between Allerton Avenue and Gun Hill Road, from Bronxwood Avenue east to the Dyre line, with Boston Road cutting across on its old colonial diagonal. Laconia Avenue and Barnes Avenue were laid out in the early 1920s, when developers sold lots as "Laconia Park" and "Gun Hill Heights" (CityNeighborhoods.nyc), and most of what they built is still here: detached brick homes, two- and three-family houses and small apartment buildings.

That housing is not what this page is about. Laconia's commercial fire alarm work sits in a short list of buildings, and each one carries a lot of weight. The first is the store row. At 11:53 p.m. on March 5, 2026, the FDNY was called to a fire in a deli at 1234 East Gun Hill Road, between Pearsall and Throop Avenues. It went to a fourth alarm by 12:24 a.m., brought 74 units and 231 fire and EMS personnel, and was not under control until 2:23 a.m. (Norwood News, March 6, 2026). Firefighters fought it from the roof and the street because it had spread through the cockloft, the closed void between the top ceiling and the roof that runs over every store in a row. "When units opened up the store, they encountered fire in the cockloft that had spread across several adjoining stores," FDNY Deputy Assistant Chief Paul Miller said (ABC7 New York). Eleven businesses were hit: a deli, a convenience store, a Chinese restaurant, a salon, a laundromat, several storefront churches, a driving school and an insurance agency (ABC7 New York), plus a printing shop that had been on the block for 29 years (News 12 Bronx). A month later all eleven storefronts were still under plywood and vacate orders, and News 12 reported the fire as an accident caused by electrical wiring (News 12 Bronx, April 13, 2026). "We're still in business, but it's not like before," said printer Nicholas Aklassou.

The second is public housing. Eastchester Gardens, which NYCHA lists at 1130 Burke Avenue, is a superblock of ten brick buildings bounded generally by Burke, Bouck, Adee and Yates Avenues. Harrison and Abramovitz designed it and it was finished in 1950: five eight-story single-cruciform buildings and five seven-story double-cruciform buildings, each with one elevator core and two internal stairwells, set around a central green (New York State Historic Preservation Office, National Register nomination). In March 2025 NYCHA and its partners closed $391 million in financing to renovate all 877 apartments under the PACT program, a job expected to take about four years; the published scope includes hardwired smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, modernized elevators, lobby and hallway upgrades and new security installations (NYCHA Journal, March 27, 2025). It does not mention a building fire alarm, and this page assumes nothing about one. Nine months later, at about 3:13 a.m. on December 30, 2025, a fire in a fourth-floor apartment at 1166 Burke Avenue, between Hering and Tenbroeck Avenues, killed a 70-year-old man (Norwood News; ABC7 New York). ABC7 reported that he was the 14th person aged 65 or older to die in a city fire since November 9 of that year.

The third is height. In April 2026, permits were filed for a 13-story mixed-use building at the corner of Boston Road and Bouck Avenue, 3374 Boston Road: 124 feet tall, about 69,000 square feet, 97 apartments over 20,636 square feet of commercial space, with 23 enclosed parking spaces (New York YIMBY, via 100% Bronx, April 14, 2026). At 124 feet it is well past the 75-foot line where New York City treats a building as high-rise, which brings voice evacuation and a fire command station into the design.

Around those three sit the smaller jobs: two-story corner buildings on Boston Road with offices under apartments, such as 3366 Boston Road, whose 2021 listing described a dental office of about 2,200 square feet under two apartments (StreetEasy); one-story store buildings on Boston Road dating from the 1930s to the 1950s (StreetEasy); P.S. 76 The Bennington School, a four-story school building from 1931 at 900 Adee Avenue (PropertyShark; InsideSchools); and churches such as the one-story 1989 building at 1271 Burke Avenue (PropertyShark).

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for the Buildings Laconia Actually Has

When does a building need a fire alarm system? In New York City the answer comes from the Building Code's occupancy rules, read by the design professional against your Certificate of Occupancy. A summary of NYC Building Code section 907.2 by NY Engineers gives the main triggers: every school (Group E) and every institutional building (Group I); assembly spaces with an occupant load of 300 or more; business occupancies with 500 or more occupants or 100 or more above or below the exit level; mercantile buildings with a fire area over 12,000 square feet, more than three stories, or a below-grade sales floor over 1,500 square feet; and apartment buildings (R-2) with units three or more stories above the exit level or 16 or more units.

One-story store rows

The row on East Gun Hill Road between Pearsall and Throop Avenues and the one-story store buildings on Boston Road, built between the mid-1930s and early 1950s (StreetEasy), were put up long before modern fire alarm rules. Many have no building system at all; each store may have only stand-alone smoke alarms. That can be legal for the building as it stands, but it changes the moment a row is rebuilt, combined into one larger store, converted to a use with a lower trigger, or substantially altered. After the March 2026 fire, rebuilding the damaged stores will mean new filings, and the design professional decides what the code now requires. For many rows the best answer is commercial fire alarm system installation that treats the cockloft and the party walls as part of the design.

Seven- and eight-story apartment buildings

Eastchester Gardens' buildings each have one elevator core and two stairs (NY State Historic Preservation Office). Buildings of that shape and height need hallway, elevator lobby and machine room coverage that matches how the stairs and elevator actually work, plus elevator recall. Inside the apartments, smoke and carbon monoxide detection is often handled under separate housing rules, which is why the PACT scope lists hardwired smoke and CO detectors as their own line (NYCHA Journal). Whether a building-wide system is also required or upgraded during a renovation is a question for NYCHA's design team and the FDNY filing, not for a website.

The filed high-rise on Boston Road

A building of 124 feet (New York YIMBY) is a high-rise under New York City's definition of more than 75 feet, as summarized by the National Fire Sprinkler Association. That means an emergency voice communication system with speakers and speaker strobes, a fire command station for the FDNY, two-way communication, elevator recall and firefighter service, and interfaces to every fan and damper. With 20,636 square feet of commercial space and an enclosed garage under 97 apartments, it also needs a clear split between the residential system and whatever tenants build out at street level.

Two-story mixed-use corners

A building like 3366 Boston Road, with a dental office under two apartments (StreetEasy), often falls below the fire alarm triggers as a whole, but a change of use, a renovation, an insurer or a landlord can still call for a system or for monitored detection in the commercial space.

Schools, churches and halls

Every school needs a fire alarm, and P.S. 76 at 900 Adee Avenue is a four-story building from 1931 with about 589 students in kindergarten through grade five (PropertyShark; InsideSchools). Work in a school building of that age is planned around classes, plaster walls and the city's own school construction rules. Churches are assembly occupancies, so the occupant load of the largest room decides most of it: the one-story church at 1271 Burke Avenue and the religious building at 3301 Laconia Avenue (PropertyShark; LoopNet) are the kind of buildings where that calculation matters, and the storefront congregations on Gun Hill Road are the kind where it is easy to overlook.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Roof Void Is the Building's Biggest Room

When we survey a one-story store row like the ones on Gun Hill Road and Boston Road, the first question isn't where the panel goes. It is how to get into the cockloft. Most stores have a dropped ceiling, sometimes two, and above that a void that can run the full length of the row. Party walls that were supposed to stop at the roof deck often stop short, have been opened for ducts and cable, or were never fire-rated at all.

So we bring a ladder, a light, and the owner's permission to lift tiles in more than one store. We look for where the party walls end, how the roof is framed, what wiring is up there, and whether anything is already fastened to the joists. The answer changes the design: heat detection in the void, separate zones for each store, or a recommendation to the design professional to repair the separations first.

The March 2026 fire on East Gun Hill Road was fought from the roof and the street because it was in that void (Norwood News). A rebuilt row is the one chance to make that space visible to a fire alarm. We would rather spend an extra hour above the ceilings at the survey than find the problem in the middle of installation.

Your Questions Answered

Laconia Fire Alarm Questions From Store Owners, Housing Managers, Developers and Congregations

These are the questions we expect from people running Laconia's store rows, apartment buildings, construction projects, schools and churches, answered with what the FDNY rules and the local record actually say.

Rebuilding the East Gun Hill Road row

Our storefront on East Gun Hill Road has been boarded up since the March fire. When we rebuild, does the store need a fire alarm it never had before?

Possibly, and you will not know until the design professional reviews the rebuilt layout against the current code. Repairing a fire-damaged store is filed as new work, and new work is measured against today's NYC Building Code, not the code the row was built under. A store that ran for years with stand-alone smoke alarms may still be allowed to, if its use and size stay below the triggers; a store that grows, merges with the space next door, adds cooking, or becomes an assembly space for a congregation may not. Ask your architect or engineer to state in writing what fire alarm, if any, the rebuild requires before anyone orders equipment. We can survey with them.

When the burned row is rebuilt, should the cockloft be closed off with new fire walls or watched by detectors?

Usually both questions get answered, by different people. Whether the roof void must be divided by fire-rated walls or draft stops is a Building Code and structural decision for the architect or engineer. Whether the void also gets detection is a fire alarm design decision that follows from it. Walls slow a fire's spread; detectors report it sooner. In a row where the March 2026 fire spread through the cockloft into adjoining stores (ABC7 New York), we recommend raising both at the first design meeting, because once the new ceilings go up, reaching that space again is expensive.

Investigators looked at a church in the middle of our row as the possible origin. Can our landlord require every tenant in the row to connect to one fire alarm panel?

A landlord can put that requirement in a lease, and for a rebuilt row it often makes sense: one panel sees the whole building, one central station account reports it, and one company tests it. The design still has to be filed with the FDNY and approved before tenants' devices are added. What a landlord cannot do is skip the code requirements that apply to a particular tenant, such as an assembly space needing notification for its occupant load. If your lease is silent, ask the landlord for the device map and the testing contract before you sign a renewal.

The burned row held storefront churches, a laundromat, a salon and a restaurant side by side. Does mixing those uses in one rebuilt building change the fire alarm?

It can. The Building Code treats a church, a restaurant and a laundromat as different occupancies, and the design professional decides whether they are separated from each other or treated as one mixed building. A congregation is an assembly use with its own occupant load; a restaurant may bring kitchen hood suppression; a laundromat brings dryers and lint. Each can change what detection and notification its own space needs, and the strictest one can drive the design of a shared system. The row the FDNY described in March 2026 held exactly that mix (ABC7 New York).

Apartment buildings on Burke and Bouck Avenues

Our building on Burke Avenue is getting hardwired smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in every apartment. Is that the same thing as a building fire alarm?

No. Hardwired smoke and CO detectors inside apartments warn the people in that apartment. A building fire alarm is a separate, supervised system with a control panel, devices in hallways, lobbies, elevator machine rooms and service spaces, a central station connection and an FDNY approval. Some buildings have both, some only the apartment detectors. The renovation scope NYCHA published for Eastchester Gardens lists the apartment detectors (NYCHA Journal), and residents who want to know about building-wide equipment should ask the property manager or resident association for the scope of work.

In a cruciform apartment building with one elevator core and two stairs, why does the fire alarm design start with the elevator?

Because the elevator is the one shared path that everyone uses and firefighters need to control. Detectors in the elevator lobbies, machine room and hoistway send the cars to a safe floor and keep them there for firefighters; the FDNY lists elevator recall among the functions it checks at the acceptance test (FDNY, Tips for Fire Alarm Systems). In a cruciform plan, the arms of the building meet at that core, so hallway devices are laid out arm by arm from the center. Elevators being modernized, as at Eastchester Gardens (NYCHA Journal), have to be tested with the fire alarm again when the work is done.

The senior center in one of our Burke Avenue buildings shares the ground floor with offices. Is it treated as its own space for the fire alarm?

Usually the design professional treats a senior center as its own use inside the building, because a room where many older adults gather is an assembly or community space rather than apartments. That affects notification, exits and sometimes door hardware. At Eastchester Gardens, a senior center occupies Building 4, converted from a children's center in the 1960s (NY State Historic Preservation Office), and the renovation includes upgrades to the community and senior centers (NYCHA Journal). Any change to such a room's layout is a moment to check its fire alarm coverage against the drawings.

Building tall on Boston Road

The 3374 Boston Road filing lists a community facility of only 168 square feet. Can a room that small change the building's fire alarm?

Its use matters more than its size. A small community room used as a lounge or meeting space is usually treated as part of the building around it. If it becomes a day program, a clinic room or a place of worship, its occupancy can change what detection and notification it needs and how it is separated from the apartments. The filing for 3374 Boston Road lists 168 square feet of community facility space beside 20,636 square feet of commercial space (New York YIMBY, via 100% Bronx), so the design should note what each space is expected to become, and the tenants should know that a change of use can mean a fire alarm filing.

The 3374 Boston Road filing describes a concrete building. Does concrete construction change how fire alarm cable is run?

Yes. In a concrete building, many conduits and back boxes are set into the slabs and walls before the concrete is poured, so the fire alarm layout has to be ready floor by floor ahead of each pour. Missing a box means drilling or surface-mounting later. Risers usually run in shafts planned with the architect, and the survivability rating of each circuit on the drawings decides how its cable is protected. The filing for 3374 Boston Road lists a concrete structure (New York YIMBY, via 100% Bronx).

Our dental office is on the ground floor of a two-story Boston Road building with apartments upstairs. Does our office need a fire alarm?

Not automatically. A small office under a couple of apartments often falls below the code triggers for a building-wide system. But a renovation, a change of use, oxygen storage, or a landlord or insurer requirement can bring one in, and a monitored detector in the office can protect the apartments above when the office is closed. The design professional checks your Certificate of Occupancy and the planned work.

Schools and churches

Why does every school need a fire alarm no matter its size, when many stores and offices don't?

Because the Building Code puts all educational occupancies in the group that requires a fire alarm, regardless of size (NY Engineers summary of BC 907.2). Children need to be warned and moved by adults, and a whole school must be told at once. Stores and offices, by contrast, cross the line only at certain sizes or occupant counts. A four-story school building from 1931 like P.S. 76 at 900 Adee Avenue (PropertyShark) has carried that requirement throughout its life, and any program using rooms in a school building works within the school's system.

How does someone figure out the occupant load of our one-story church on Burke Avenue?

The design professional measures the rooms and applies the occupant load factors in the NYC Building Code for the way each room is used: fixed pews, open chairs, standing room, classrooms or a kitchen. The total tells you whether the building crosses the assembly trigger of 300 people for a fire alarm (NY Engineers summary of BC 907.2) and how many exits it needs. A church that holds weddings, funerals and community meals may be calculated differently from one that only holds Sunday services. Ask for the calculation in writing and keep it with your Certificate of Occupancy.

Equipment

Types of Commercial Fire Alarm Systems We Install in Laconia

What is a commercial fire alarm system? It is a supervised set of devices that sense fire, a control panel that decides what happens next, appliances that warn people, and connections that recall elevators, shut down fans, release doors and report to a central station. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? The panel constantly checks every device and wire. When a detector senses smoke or heat, a sprinkler flows water, or someone pulls a station, the panel sounds the alarm, operates the building's interfaces and sends a signal for the FDNY. "Every store in a row is its own building"

Generic answers treat each storefront as a separate fire risk. A one-story row is usually one structure with one roof, and the space above the ceilings is often continuous or barely divided.

"Apartment smoke detectors and a building fire alarm are the same thing"

They are not. Smoke and CO detectors in an apartment protect that apartment; a building fire alarm is a separately approved, supervised system that monitors common areas and reports to a central station.

"Approval takes a few weeks"

Sprinklers put water on a fire. They do not, by themselves, warn every floor, recall elevators, shut down fans or call the FDNY through a central station.

"If the panel says normal, the building is protected"

A panel showing normal means it sees no alarm, trouble or supervisory condition on the devices it knows about. It cannot see devices that were removed, painted, covered or never connected.

Can fire alarm design start on a Laconia store while the building is still under a vacate order?
+

Usually, yes. A vacate order keeps people from occupying the building; it does not stop a design professional from surveying with permission, measuring and drawing a new system. What happens on site is controlled by the Department of Buildings and the owner's engineer, so the survey is scheduled with them. Who should Eastchester Gardens residents ask about the detectors being installed in their apartments?
+

The property manager and the resident association first. Can a driving school or insurance office in a store row rely on a stand-alone smoke alarm?
+

Often it can, if the code does not require a building system for that space and the landlord and insurer accept it. A battery or plug-in smoke alarm only warns whoever is in the room, though, and an office in a row of closed stores at night has no one in the room. A single monitored detector tied to a small panel or the building system reports to a central station even when the office is empty.

What is a cockloft, and why does it keep coming up in Laconia fire reports?
+

A cockloft is the closed space between the top-floor ceiling and the roof. Norwood News described it as typically uninhabited and often inaccessible. In a one-story store row, that space often runs over every store, so a fire that gets into it can travel sideways out of sight. That is how the March 2026 fire on East Gun Hill Road reached several adjoining stores, according to the FDNY (ABC7 New York). For fire alarm design, it is a space that may need its own detection.

When I rent a store in a row, is the cockloft above my ceiling part of my space?
+

Usually not. Most store leases give the tenant the space below the ceiling and keep the roof, the structure and the void above for the landlord. That means detection in the cockloft, if the design calls for it, is normally base-building work the landlord owns and maintains, even though it sits over your store. Read your lease's description of the premises and its repair clause, and ask the landlord to confirm in writing who is responsible for anything installed above the ceiling.

Can the Gun Hill Road stores reopen one at a time, or must the whole rebuilt row pass its fire alarm test together?
+

It depends on how the row is rebuilt and filed. If each store has its own system, each can be tested and approved on its own schedule. If the row shares one building system, the base system has to be installed and accepted first, and stores whose devices are added later are filed and tested as additions. Ask the design professional to plan the filing around the order in which owners expect to reopen.

Who pays the FDNY filing fees when a tenant adds fire alarm devices in a landlord's building?
+

Whoever the lease says pays. The FDNY fees themselves are fixed: NYC Business lists $420 for a fire alarm plan application, or $525 when a more complex analysis is needed. Many commercial leases put tenant improvement filings on the tenant and base-building work on the landlord. Check your lease's alterations clause, and put the answer in writing before the design professional files.

Does a printing shop's equipment need special fire detection?
+

Sometimes. Printers, cutters and laminators throw off paper dust and heat, and stored paper is a heavy fuel load. Smoke detectors near the presses can foul or false-alarm, so the design professional may choose heat detection or a different location, plus regular cleaning on the inspection schedule. Electrical loads matter too: News 12 reported that the March 2026 fire on East Gun Hill Road was an accident caused by electrical wiring.

What should a Laconia store owner do when the fire alarm shows trouble at night and the landlord can't be reached?
+

Do not reset or silence it repeatedly. Photograph the display, call the central station that monitors the panel to report the trouble, and call a fire alarm service company. If the panel shows an actual alarm or you smell smoke, call 911 first. If the system is out of service, the FDNY must be told and a fire watch kept.

Do the stores in a rebuilt row need strobes in their restrooms?
+

Where a store has a fire alarm with visual notification, public restrooms and other spaces people use are generally covered so people who cannot hear the horn still get the warning. The design professional decides which rooms need strobes under the Building Code and the accessibility rules. A single-user staff toilet may be treated differently from a public restroom, so show the design professional how each room is actually used.

Does a store's old FDNY fire alarm approval still count after the store burns and is rebuilt?
+

Not for the rebuilt work. An FDNY Letter of Approval covers the system as it was approved and tested. When a store is rebuilt, the new or replaced devices are filed and tested again, using the old approval and drawings as the starting record if they still exist. Find the old Letter of Approval, drawings and central station contract now, before debris removal, and give copies to your design professional.

Does the FDNY test a new high-rise fire alarm one floor at a time?
+

The inspection covers every floor, but the work is organized by system, not floor by floor. Inspectors check the fire command station, voice messages, speakers and strobes, elevator recall, fans and dampers, and sprinkler connections throughout the building. For a building like the 13-story one filed at 3374 Boston Road, the pre-test should cover every floor before the FDNY is called. A missing device on one floor can stop the test.
