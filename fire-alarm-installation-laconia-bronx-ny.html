The Neighborhood

Why Laconia Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

The housing is what sets Laconia apart from the apartment districts further south and west. By the early 1920s Laconia Avenue and Barnes Avenue were laid out, and modest one- and two-family houses began to go up, built for Italian-American, Jewish and Irish families; West Indian and Dominican households arrived in the 1980s and after, and NeighborhoodScout now counts more than a quarter of residents as born in another country. City-Data's counts for the neighborhood show the building history clearly: about 748 housing units built in 1939 or earlier, 298 in the 1940s, 940 in the 1950s, the single largest group, and 189 in the 1960s, with 445 newer units from 2010 to 2019. Listings give a picture of the typical house. A brick building at 2450 Laconia Avenue, built in 1940, is listed as a multiple-occupancy home. A house at 2546 Laconia Avenue is listed as a fully detached brick legal two-family. A house on Hone Avenue is listed as a three-bedroom apartment over a two-bedroom apartment with a full basement. That layout, two households stacked over a basement full of mechanical equipment and storage, is the most common fire alarm job we see in neighborhoods like this one.

Six Laconia facts that shape a fire alarm plan

  • The houses are mostly old enough to have been rewired more than once. City-Data counts roughly 2,000 units built before 1960, and NeighborhoodScout says many residences date from 1940 to 1969. Original circuits, later additions and battery alarms of every age often sit side by side in the same house.
  • Two households under one roof is normal. Detached and attached brick two-family houses, multiple-occupancy homes and stacked apartments over a full basement dominate the side streets. Redfin showed 13 multi-family homes for sale in late November 2025 at a median listing price of $850,000.
  • The store rows are low and continuous. One-story buildings with two or three stores each, built between 1935 and 1951, line Boston Road. When stores share a roof, they may share a cockloft, and the March 2026 Gun Hill Road fire spread exactly that way into neighboring businesses.
  • Some buildings are both a store and a home. Prewar corner buildings like the one at 3366 Boston Road put apartments over an office or shop, and a few blocks south a January 2026 fire at 700 Adee Avenue, which FDNY described as a three-story non-fireproof building with a deli below and two apartments above, was noticed by the families upstairs when they smelled smoke.
  • Walk-ups cluster near the El. Wikipedia notes that tenement buildings are scattered through the area, mostly within five blocks of the elevated line on White Plains Road, and Redfin lists apartment units on Matthews and Barnes Avenues. These are the buildings most likely to have a building panel.
  • The largest public housing development in the Bronx borders the area. NYCHA's Edenwald Houses covers 48.88 acres, with Laconia Avenue as one of its boundaries. NYCHA owns and manages it, so fire alarm work inside it is NYCHA's to arrange; we did not research NYCHA's procedures and do not describe them here.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Laconia's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up almost every fire alarm call we get from Laconia. What separates them is not brand or budget but how many households and businesses share the building, how the roof and basement connect the parts, and whether the law treats the building as a house with alarms or a building with a system.

The detached brick two-family

Two apartments in a free-standing brick house with a basement, like the legal two-family listed at 2546 Laconia Avenue. Walls on every side, a driveway, and usually one heating plant for both units.

The stacked two-family over a full basement

A larger apartment over a smaller one, as in the three-bedroom over two-bedroom layout listed on Hone Avenue, with the basement used for storage, laundry or a finished room.

The 1940s multiple-occupancy home

Houses like 2450 Laconia Avenue, built in 1940 and listed as multiple occupancy, where the number of households has changed over the decades and the alarms have been added piece by piece.

The one-story store row

Boston Road buildings from 1935 to 1951 with one to three stores each, flat roofs and a shared ceiling space, and the longer Gun Hill Road strips with their mix of food, services and storefront churches.

The two-story corner building

Prewar corner buildings with a business at street level and apartments above, like 3366 Boston Road with its dental office and two two-bedroom apartments.

The walk-up near the El

Apartment buildings within a few blocks of White Plains Road, the stock most likely to have a building panel, a super and a monitoring account.

The detached brick two-family: two homes, one heating plant, one plan

In a detached two-family, the owner often lives in one apartment and rents the other, and the alarms in each apartment were usually bought at different times by different people. The result we find most often is a house where the upper apartment has two battery alarms from one decade, the lower apartment has a hardwired alarm from another, the basement has nothing, and none of them can hear each other. Our plan treats the house as one building with two households. Smoke alarms go on every level, in the hall outside each sleeping area, and in or near each bedroom as the layout requires, and carbon monoxide alarms go on each level with sleeping rooms and near the fuel-burning equipment. Then we link them, with hardwired interconnect where the walls and ceilings allow cable to travel, or with wireless interconnect where opening plaster would be destructive. Linking matters most in exactly the case the Laconia fires describe: a fire that starts at night on the floor nobody is on. Because the boiler and water heater usually serve both apartments from the basement, we put CO detection where its warning reaches both households, not only the one closest to the boiler room door.

The stacked two-family over a full basement: where the fire is most likely to start out of sight

The basement is the part of a Laconia two-family that nobody watches. It holds the boiler, the water heater, the meters and the electrical panel, often a washer and dryer, sometimes a finished room or a workshop, and in many houses decades of stored furniture, paint and boxes. A fire there grows under both apartments, and the first sign upstairs may be smoke coming through the floor. We plan the basement first. Heat detection usually suits the boiler and laundry corner, where steam, lint and the occasional puff of combustion air would make an ordinary smoke detector unreliable. Smoke detection goes at the top of the basement stair, where smoke would travel on its way up, and in any finished room someone uses. A CO alarm goes near the fuel-burning equipment. All of it links to the alarms in both apartments. When the basement has its own entrance from the side of the house, we ask who uses it, because a basement that has become a workspace or a storage room for a tenant's business changes what belongs down there.

The 1940s multiple-occupancy home: undoing years of piecemeal alarms

A house built in 1940 and listed today as multiple occupancy has probably been divided, combined and rewired more than once. We find alarms mounted where a wall used to be, alarms in rooms that are now closets, a hardwired alarm on a circuit that also feeds a refrigerator, and smoke alarms that were painted over during a renovation. Before adding anything, we map what is there: each alarm, its date code, whether it is battery, plug-in or hardwired, which circuit feeds it, and whether it links to anything. Smoke alarms carry a manufacture date, and an alarm past the age its manufacturer specifies should be replaced, not tested and left. Then we design the house as it is used now, with each household's sleeping rooms and exits as they actually are, rather than as a previous owner arranged them. If the house's legal occupancy is unclear, we point the owner to the Certificate of Occupancy and keep our scope to protecting the people living there.

The one-story store row: the space above the ceiling that ties the businesses together

A one-story building on Boston Road with three stores was built as one structure, with one roof, and very often one open roof space above the store ceilings. The demising walls between stores may stop at the ceiling, or rise partway into the cockloft, or rise to the roof and have been breached over seventy years by cable, ductwork and plumbing. Whatever the original intent, the result we look for is whether smoke and fire in that space could move from one store to the next, the way the Gun Hill Road fire moved. Detection inside each store watches the store. It does not watch the cockloft, because a fire above a ceiling has to burn through the ceiling before a device below it notices. Where the owner and, on a filed system, the design professional decide the roof space should be watched, we install detection suited to it, usually heat detection, since that space can be dusty and swings through extreme temperatures, and we zone it separately at the panel so a signal from the roof space is not mistaken for a signal from one store. On every store row survey we look into the cockloft where it can be reached safely, note what penetrates the demising walls, and tell the owner what we see, including openings that should be sealed by the right trade.

The two-story corner building: a store and a home that must warn each other

The prewar corner building with two apartments over a 2,200-square-foot dental office is a pattern, not an exception. In buildings like it, the business has its own lease, keys and hours, and the families upstairs have theirs. A fire in the office at night threatens the people upstairs, who may not smell it until it is serious, and the Adee Avenue fire a few blocks south was found when the families upstairs smelled smoke. A fire upstairs during business hours threatens the patients and staff below. So our plan connects the two halves. Detection in the business, especially in back rooms with compressors, sterilizers, water heaters or storage, is tied to alarms that the upstairs households will hear. The shared stair, if there is one, is protected as the escape route for both. Then we put three things in writing with the owner: who owns and maintains each device, who is called when the building signals after hours, and who can open the business for a technician or a firefighter. Whether a two-story building like this needs a full fire alarm system depends on how it is separated and how its units exit; the 2022 Building Code exempts certain buildings of two stories or fewer from a manual fire alarm system when each dwelling unit has one-hour fire partitions and its own direct exit, and the design professional decides whether that applies.

The walk-up near the El: a building panel and a super who needs to read it

Walk-ups within a few blocks of White Plains Road are the Laconia buildings most likely to have a real fire alarm panel in the basement or the lobby, and the most likely to have changed owners without anyone handing over the records. Our first visit to a building like this is a survey, not a sales call: we identify the panel and its condition, trace the circuits, match each device to a location, check the standby batteries, and compare what we find with any FDNY paperwork the owner has. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules sets out the boundary that shapes the rest of the job: replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel with something different needs new plans filed with the FDNY. Because owners are expected to keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years, we leave the building with a device list, test results and a short explanation of the panel's common messages that the super can keep at the panel and the owner can keep with the building's files.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Surveying a Laconia Store Row Before Anyone Picks a Device

Count the storefronts, then count the roofs. From the sidewalk, the technician notes how many stores share the building, whether the parapet or roofline breaks between them, and where a ladder or hatch reaches the roof. A row that looks like three buildings is sometimes one, and a row that looks like one is sometimes three.

Ask every tenant the same questions. What hours are you open, who has the keys, what runs overnight, where do you charge batteries, and what is stored in the back? A laundromat's dryers, a restaurant's fryer and a church's evening service each change the plan.

Look up, past the ceiling. Where a tile or hatch allows it safely, the technician looks into the cockloft with a light, notes whether the demising walls reach the roof, and photographs every unsealed penetration. Those photos go to the owner whether or not we get the job.

Find the panel and the paperwork. If there is a panel, its screen is photographed, its batteries checked for a date, and its monitoring account identified. If there is none, we note where one could live: indoors, dry, reachable, and visible to the person who will answer it.

Walk out the way a customer would. Finally the technician leaves each store by its exits, front and back, and notes what a person would see and hear on the way. Only then do we start drawing.

Compliance notes for Laconia buildings and businesses

  • Plans come first on a filed system. Design and installation documents for fire alarm systems go to the FDNY for review and approval before installation begins.
  • Who has to be on the plan. The NYC Business portal says the plans must be approved by a New York State registered professional engineer or registered architect, and the fire alarm plan involves a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder. We install, label and pre-test; we do not act as the design professional.
  • Authorization before work. The FDNY issues a Project Authorization before work starts on a filed system, and after installation the Fire Alarm Inspection Unit inspects it.
  • Complete before the inspection. The system must be 100 percent complete and pre-tested before the inspection is requested.
  • Repair versus redesign. Identical replacement of detectors, pull stations or panels with no design change is a repair; moving or replacing a control panel with different equipment needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Monitoring and records. Monitoring companies that report to the FDNY must work from an FDNY-approved central station, and owners keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years.
  • Homes need alarms regardless. Even where a building fire alarm system is not required, smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are required in almost all residential occupancies, which covers Laconia's two-family houses and the apartments above its stores.
  • Small buildings may be exempt from a manual system. The 2022 Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard. The design professional decides whether a building qualifies.
  • No landmark constraints found. We found no landmark district in Laconia, so exterior devices are a question of code and good sense rather than Landmarks approval. If your building is individually designated, tell us.
One Roof, Eleven Stores

What the March 2026 Gun Hill Road Fire Teaches About Detecting Fire in a Store Row

The fire at 1234 East Gun Hill Road reached four alarms. Firehouse.com placed the block near Givan Avenue, and reports listed the businesses on it: a deli, a convenience store, a Chinese restaurant, a salon, a laundromat, several storefront churches, a driving school and an insurance agency. Shore News Network reported that crews found fire spreading through the cockloft and into adjoining businesses. CBS New York counted 74 FDNY units and more than 230 first responders, and officials said 11 stores were affected. ABC7 reported that investigators were focusing on a church in the middle of the block as the likely origin. We do not know what fire detection any of those stores had, how the fire was first noticed, or what the final cause was, and we make no claims about any of it. The pattern, though, is one every owner of a Laconia store row should understand, because the buildings on Boston Road are built the same way.

A shared roof space is a shared risk. In a one-story row, the cockloft is the void between the store ceilings and the roof deck. If it runs the length of the building, or if the walls that divide it have been opened over the years, a fire that gets into it can travel above every store while the stores below look normal. That is why a detector on each store ceiling, however good, is not the whole answer. Detection belongs where fire is likely to start and where it is likely to travel, and in a row building the second place is above the ceiling.

The businesses are different, and so are the hazards. A laundromat runs dryers that throw lint and heat. A restaurant cooks with open flame and oil. A salon runs hot tools and uses aerosols. A church may host evening services with candles and a kitchen for meals. An office may run space heaters in winter. A deli may run refrigeration compressors around the clock. Each of those calls for its own detection choice inside the store, and each is a reason the roof space above should be watched as one continuous risk rather than as one store's problem.

Night and empty stores. Most of a store row closes at night. A fire that starts after closing, in a space nobody is in, can grow before anyone outside smells it. Detection only helps if it reaches a person: through monitoring that calls the fire department, or through alarms that sound in apartments above or next door. In a row where only some tenants have monitoring, the owner should know which stores report to someone and which do not.

Coordination is the hard part. One owner and several tenants means several opinions about cost, several sets of keys, and several schedules. We handle store rows by putting the roof space and any building-wide equipment on the owner's side of the plan, each store's interior devices on a list tied to that store, and a single call sheet that every tenant gets a copy of. That way a signal from the roof space at three in the morning goes to someone who can open every door.

Late at Night on the Side Streets

Throop Avenue, Burke Avenue and the Hours When Nobody Is Awake

The two residential fires we found in the reporting on Laconia's side streets both happened at night. The Throop Avenue house fire in July 2024 was reported around 11:30 p.m., and two people were in critical condition afterward. The Burke Avenue fire happened on a Tuesday in December; one person died. We do not know what alarms were in either house or whether they sounded, and nothing in the sources says. We mention them because they describe the condition every residential plan here has to meet: people asleep, doors closed, a fire that may start on another floor or in the basement, and seconds that matter.

Sleeping rooms first. In a two-family house the bedrooms are spread over two or three levels and belong to two households. An alarm in the hall of the owner's apartment does nothing for the tenant asleep one floor up unless it is linked. Our plans put smoke alarms where the code and the manufacturer require them near each sleeping area and link them through the whole house, so the alarm that hears the fire is not the only one that sounds.

Fuel-burning equipment and winter. A December night in a Laconia house means the boiler is running, the windows are shut, and sometimes a space heater or an oven is helping in a cold room. Carbon monoxide gives no warning anyone can smell, so CO alarms belong on every level with sleeping rooms and near the boiler and water heater, and their end-of-life dates get written down at installation.

Batteries in the house. At the Throop Avenue fire, FDNY hazardous materials resources evaluated an e-bike found at the scene, and the reports did not say whether it was involved. Across the city, Firehouse.com reported that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent in 2026. In a house, that means asking where e-bikes, scooters and large tool batteries are charged, and putting detection where it will notice a battery fire early, which is never a reason to charge in a doorway, a stair or a bedroom.

Test it the way it will be used. Our technicians test every new alarm with the household listening from the farthest bedroom with the door closed, in both apartments. If the sound is not clearly audible there, the plan changes before we leave.

Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Store rows and shared roofs

We own a one-story building on Boston Road with three storefronts under one roof. Should each tenant have separate detection, or can one panel watch the whole row?

One panel serving the whole building is usually the better design for a row like that, with each store and the roof space identified separately at that panel. A single panel sees the building as it really is: one structure, one roof, one cockloft, three businesses. It can tell you that the signal came from the middle store, or from the roof space above the last one, and one monitoring account can cover all of it. Separate equipment in each store, by contrast, often means three systems of different ages, three monitoring arrangements or none, and nothing at all watching the space the stores share. That said, the right answer depends on whether the building needs a filed system at all, which depends on its occupancy and size, and on what the tenants' leases say. If a filed system is required, the registered design professional decides the layout. If it is not, we can still build a building-wide arrangement for the owner, with interior devices in each store and detection in the roof space, so the row protects itself as one building. We put the panel where the owner or a responsible tenant will see it, never in the back of a stockroom that is locked at night.

The roof space over our stores is below freezing in January and much hotter than the stores in August. Which detector holds up in a space like that?

Heat detection is usually the dependable choice for a cockloft, chosen from devices listed for the temperature range that space actually reaches. Ordinary smoke detectors are designed for conditioned rooms. In an uninsulated roof space they can be pushed past their rated temperatures, fouled by dust and debris, and set off by condensation, which in a row of businesses means nuisance alarms that tenants soon learn to ignore. Heat detectors come in fixed-temperature and rate-of-rise types, and the choice for a roof space has to account for how hot it gets on a summer afternoon, so a device does not mistake a heat wave for a fire. Where the design calls for earlier warning than heat detection gives, there are other detection technologies listed for harsh spaces, and on a filed system that decision belongs to the design professional. We also plan for maintenance from the start: a device in a cockloft has to be reachable for testing, so we note the access point on the device list and leave a clear route to it.

If the store next door catches fire, will our own alarm do anything to save our stock, or does it only warn us?

An alarm detects and warns; it does not put out a fire. What it can do for your stock is shorten the time between the start of a fire and the arrival of firefighters, and in a row of stores that sometimes decides whether the fire stays in one unit. If your store's detection is monitored, a fire that reaches your space at night sends a signal to the central station, which notifies the fire department, even if nobody is on the block. If the roof space above the row is watched, a fire travelling through the cockloft toward your store can be detected before it comes through your ceiling. Neither is a substitute for sprinklers, fire-rated separations or a sound roof, and neither replaces insurance. Our job is to make sure the detection you have is placed where fire would actually reach it and that its signal goes to someone who acts. For a store that holds valuable stock, we also suggest asking your insurer what they expect, since some carriers ask for proof of monitoring.

Reports said investigators were looking at a storefront church in the middle of the Gun Hill Road block. We lead a small storefront congregation. What should we check first?

Start with the things a congregation controls every week, and do not assume anything about the cause of that fire, which we do not know. First, look at your back rooms: kitchens used for fellowship meals, storage of chairs, hymnals and decorations, and any space heaters or extension cords that have become permanent. Those rooms are often closed and empty during services, which is exactly when a fire there can grow unnoticed, so they need detection that suits them, usually heat detection near cooking and smoke detection elsewhere. Second, look at candles and any open flame, and agree on who puts them out and when. Third, find out whether your space has any detection that reports to anyone when the room is empty, which for most congregations is six days a week. Fourth, ask how many people gather at your largest service, because a room that holds a crowd is treated differently by the code, and whether your space needs a full system depends on its occupancy, its size and the building. A registered design professional makes that determination on a filed design. We can survey the space, tell you what is there, and price what it needs.

Shops with heat, lint, fumes and machinery

Our laundromat runs a long bank of gas dryers. What detection works with lint in the air, heat behind the machines and a store full of customers?

The answer is usually a mix: heat detection behind and above the dryers where lint and heat would fool a smoke detector, smoke detection in the customer area and back rooms where the air is cleaner, and carbon monoxide detection because gas dryers burn fuel and vent combustion gases. Lint is the main hazard and the main nuisance in a laundromat. It builds up in the space behind the dryers, in the venting and on every horizontal surface, and it burns readily, which is why the area behind the machines deserves attention. Smoke detectors placed in that airborne lint tend to foul and false alarm, so we choose devices for each zone based on what the air there is like during a busy Saturday. CO detection goes where a failed vent or a blocked flue would show up first, and where the attendant will hear it. If the laundromat sits in a row with a shared roof space, the dryer venting usually passes through that space, and we look at it on the survey. We also suggest the owner keep the lint cleaning schedule posted, because no detector replaces clean ducts.

Our salon uses hair spray, hot irons and dryers all day. Will a smoke detector over the chairs keep going off?

It can, if it is placed by habit in the middle of the ceiling over the stations. Aerosol hair spray, the fine mist from finishing products and the heat from styling tools can all trigger certain smoke detectors, especially in a small salon with a low ceiling and little air movement. The fix is placement and device choice, not removing protection. We keep smoke detection out of the direct drift of the styling stations, use photoelectric devices where smoke detection is needed in the work area, and look at heat detection for spots where products and heat concentrate. Back rooms with towels, dryers and stock, and any area where hot tools are left to cool, get attention too, since those are the places a small fire starts while everyone is with a client. If your salon shares a roof space with the neighbors, that space is part of the plan as well. We test every device before leaving, and we ask you to run a normal busy hour's worth of styling near the stations while we watch, so the layout is proven before we go.

Our dental office downstairs has a compressor and a sterilizer in a back room, with apartments above. Is that room better served by heat or smoke detection?

A mechanical back room like that is usually better served by heat detection, with smoke detection in the treatment rooms, the halls and the reception area where the air is clean and people are present. Compressors run hot and cycle on their own, sterilizers release steam and heat, and both rooms are often small, closed and full of equipment, which are conditions that make ordinary smoke detectors prone to nuisance alarms. Heat detection there will notice a real fire without sounding every time the sterilizer opens. The more important question in a corner building like this is who hears it. The office closes at night; the families upstairs do not. So the detection in the back room and the rest of the office should be tied to alarms the apartments above will hear, or to monitoring that sends a response, preferably both. We write down who receives a signal after hours and who can open the office, because a fire in a locked ground-floor office under two apartments is exactly the situation the plan exists for.

Brick two-family houses

Our two-family was built in 1940 and still has some original wiring. Can new hardwired alarms go on those circuits, or do they need a circuit of their own?

We do not put new hardwired alarms on a circuit we have not tested, and in a house with original wiring we check what feeds each alarm before connecting anything. Hardwired smoke alarms are designed to run on household power with a battery for backup, and they are connected according to the manufacturer's instructions and the electrical code. In a house built in 1940, the question is not only whether the circuit works today but whether it is in a condition we would trust, and whether it also feeds something that gets switched off, overloaded or tripped regularly. If the circuit tests sound and is suitable, alarms can often be connected to it. If the wiring is in poor condition, or the only available circuit is shared with a kitchen appliance or controlled by a switch, the house is better served by a suitable dedicated circuit, which is work for a licensed electrician, or by sealed long-life battery alarms with wireless interconnect, which need no household wiring at all. We tell you which path we recommend, why, and what each costs, before any device goes up.

Our upstairs tenant does deliveries and charges an e-bike battery in the shared stairway overnight. What should go there, and what do we say to the tenant?

Detection in the stair is worth having, but the real answer is that the battery should not be charging in the stairway at all, because that stair is how both households get out. A fire in a stair blocks the exit for everyone above it, and battery fires grow fast; at the Throop Avenue house fire in 2024, FDNY hazmat resources evaluated an e-bike found at the scene, and across the city the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent in 2026. Talk to the tenant plainly and in writing: charge in an open area of the apartment, away from the exit door and bedrooms, not overnight unattended, with the charger that came with the battery. On our side, the stairway gets smoke detection linked to both apartments, so a fire there sounds in every bedroom in the house, and the charging location the tenant agrees to gets detection suited to it. If the tenant relies on the bike for work and has nowhere safe to charge, that conversation is better had now than after a fire. We do not set a household's charging rules, but we can tell you where detection should go once you have agreed them.

The hardwired alarm in the upstairs hall goes off whenever someone showers. What is causing it, and how is it fixed without losing protection?

Steam from the bathroom is the likely cause, especially if the alarm sits close to the bathroom door and the bathroom has no working exhaust fan. Many smoke alarms cannot tell dense water vapor from smoke, and in the narrow upstairs halls of Laconia two-family houses the alarm that protects the bedrooms often ends up a few feet from the shower. Because the alarms are linked, one steamed alarm can set off the whole house, and households start pulling batteries or disconnecting devices, which is the dangerous part. The fix is to move the hall alarm to a spot that still covers the sleeping area but sits outside the path of steam, as the manufacturer's spacing guidance allows, and to make sure the bathroom exhaust is working and used. In some layouts a different device type suits that location better. What we do not do is remove protection from the hall or leave a disconnected alarm in place. After the change, we run a hot shower with the door open and watch, so both the household and the technician know the problem is solved.

Paperwork, filings and the neighborhood name

The FDNY issues a Project Authorization before work starts on a filed system. What can happen on our building while we wait for it?

Survey, documentation and preparation can happen before the authorization; installation of the filed system should not. The NYC Business portal describes the order clearly: a registered design professional approves the plans, the fire alarm plan involves a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder, the FDNY issues a Project Authorization, the work is installed, and the Fire Alarm Inspection Unit inspects it once the system is 100 percent complete and pre-tested. While the filing is under review, we can survey the existing conditions for the designer, trace and label what is already there, identify the cable routes and the panel location, order equipment once the design is settled, and plan the schedule around the tenants' hours. Anything that keeps people protected in the meantime, such as repairing a failed household alarm in an apartment or restoring an existing detector, is maintenance, not filed work, and should not wait. Ask your designer how long the review is expected to take, and plan store opening dates and lease starts around that, not around the day you would like the job finished.

Owners are supposed to keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years. For a small store building, what actually counts as a record?

Anything that shows what was inspected, tested, repaired or changed, when, by whom, and with what result. For a store building with a panel, that means the reports from each inspection and test, the service tickets from every repair visit, records of any device that was replaced and with what, monitoring company notices about signals and trouble conditions, and any correspondence with the FDNY about the system. For a building on the household side, without a panel, the useful records are the installation date and model of each alarm, its replacement date, and notes of each test. Keep them in one place that stays with the building, not in a tenant's drawer or on one person's phone, and keep a copy offsite. At the end of our work we hand over a device list matched to the labels, the test results and a call sheet, which gives the owner a clean starting point. If you are buying or inheriting a building and the seller left nothing, a documented survey of the system as it stands becomes the first page of that file.

Do we need a permit just to replace the smoke alarms in our own two-family house?
