The Neighborhood

Why East New York Properties Need Access Control

East New York is large, and it is not one kind of place. Wikipedia draws its edges at the cemeteries and the Queens line to the north and east, Jamaica Bay to the south, and the Canarsie Line, the Bay Ridge Branch tracks and Van Sinderen Avenue to the west. It covers three ZIP codes, 11207, 11208 and 11239, all inside Brooklyn Community District 5, and it is policed by the 75th Precinct from its station house at 1000 Sutter Avenue. Inside those lines sit City Line, New Lots, Spring Creek, Starrett City and Cypress Hills, and Wikipedia’s summary of the housing is plain: mostly semi-detached homes, two-to-four family houses, and multi-unit apartment buildings including condominiums and co-ops. Every one of those has a different kind of front door, and a different person who decides what goes on it.

The single most common door we are asked about here belongs to a Nehemiah house. The Gotham Center’s history of the plan describes how East Brooklyn Congregations, a coalition of churches, broke ground in October 1982 and went on to build some 4,500 houses over about three decades. The first two-bedroom houses were about 1,000 square feet and sold for $30,000; three-bedroom houses were about 1,200 square feet and sold for $35,000. The builders gave them full basements, natural-gas furnaces, double-glazed windows, parking pads and staggered building lines. Those last three details matter to us more than anything else in the history. A Nehemiah house typically presents a front door, a parking pad and, below the front steps or inside the house, a basement, all within a few strides of the sidewalk. A good door plan treats them as one entrance with three parts, not as three unrelated gadgets.

The second generation of Nehemiah houses is built differently again. Architectural Record’s account of Nehemiah Spring Creek, designed by Alexander Gorlin Architects on about 30 acres of former landfill near Jamaica Bay, explains that each house was assembled from 20-by-40-foot factory modules with steel frames, fiber-cement siding and concrete floors, stacked two, three or four high, with the front door raised so that every unit has its own stoop to the sidewalk. The first phase was finished in June 2009 and the second in June 2011. A steel-framed modular wall does not take a strike, a station or a cable the way a brick or wood-framed wall does, so these houses get their own method on this page.

Then there are the basements. Between 2019 and the closing of applications, the city ran its Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program only in Community District 5, East New York and Cypress Hills. HPD’s page describes it as open to owner-occupied one- to three-unit homes, with low- or no-interest loans of up to $120,000 and technical help from the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation, and it requires each converted apartment to receive a Certificate of Occupancy from the Department of Buildings. A legal basement apartment has its own entrance and its own tenant. That turns a family house into a house with a landlord and a renter under one roof, and the door system has to respect both: the tenant’s privacy, the owner’s right to reach the boiler and the meters, and the fire exit that the basement unit depends on.

North of Linden Boulevard, the buildings are newer and much taller. The City Council approved the East New York Neighborhood Plan on April 20, 2016, the city’s first rezoning under Mandatory Inclusionary Housing, and local reporting counts nearly 6,000 new homes since, more than 60 percent of them affordable. Brownstoner describes The Highland at 268 Barbey Street, fourteen stories and 320 apartments attached to the landmarked Empire State Dairy on Atlantic Avenue; a thirteen-story, 229-unit building at 2683 Atlantic Avenue whose lottery closed in June 2026; and the first fifteen-story building of the Innovative Urban Village at Flatlands and Pennsylvania Avenues, 386 apartments in a plan for 1,975. In December 2025 the Council approved Herkimer-Williams at Broadway Junction, with up to 1,000 more affordable homes. Each of these buildings fills its lobby with hundreds of households in a few months. None of the reporting we read describes their entry hardware, so we do not guess at it; what we know is that a lobby of that size lives or dies by how credentials are issued, changed and taken back.

The fourth East New York is industrial. City Limits describes the Industrial Business Zone as 57 blocks running from Atlantic Avenue down to Sutter Avenue between Sheffield Avenue and Powell Street, with a further spur along Van Sinderen Avenue, and quotes the East Brooklyn Business Improvement District’s 2019 count of 6,265 employees at more than 100 businesses. The BID’s own directory reads like a list of door problems we solve: steel and iron works, a precast concrete plant, auto shops, a school bus company, carting and recycling yards, self-storage, pharmacies and check cashing. Those are roll-up doors, chain-link and steel gates, heavy hollow-metal personnel doors and counters that hold cash. Access control there is less about convenience and more about who has a key to a yard at 4:30 in the morning, and whether anyone can prove it.

We do not sell any of this with fear. The 75th Precinct’s own CompStat report for the week ending September 13, 2026, shows 124 burglaries so far that year against 167 at the same point in 2025, a fall of about a quarter, and in October the Police Commissioner said shootings in the precinct were down 47 percent on five years earlier. Good news, and we repeat it. But the stories that reach us are about doors, not statistics: a News 12 report from January 2025 in which a Boulevard Houses resident said her building’s entrance lock did not work and the intercom was out, and a CBS report from early 2026 about an armed home invasion near Vandalia and Fountain Avenues by a man disguised as a cleaner. A door that locks every time, and that lets you see and speak to whoever is outside before it opens, answers both of those.

Keypad Dead, Fob Reader Blinking Red or a Gate Stuck Open: Repair in East New York

Not every call is a new system. If a keypad on a Nehemiah front door has gone dark, a lobby reader has dropped offline, or a yard gate will not latch behind the last truck, ring (347) 934-8335 and describe what the door is doing, or send a short video of it. A visit is booked within two hours of the call. Many faults turn out to be a flat backup battery, a door that has dropped on its hinges so the latch no longer meets the strike, or a power supply that was never on a dedicated circuit; those are usually put right on the first visit. We work on systems other companies installed as well as our own, and we tell you honestly when a part is no longer worth repairing.

Building Specialists

Access Control for East New York’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware. Below are the six kinds of property we see most here, with what each one usually needs and why.

Original Nehemiah houses

Single-family houses built from the 1980s onward with full basements and parking pads, set on staggered building lines. Usually one controlled front door, a gate across the pad, and a contact on the basement door, with the controller in the basement near the panel.

Nehemiah Spring Creek townhouses

Two- to four-story houses built from steel-framed modules with fiber-cement siding and concrete floors, each with a raised front door and a stoop. Stations are surface-mounted and cable follows the module seams rather than cutting through steel.

Two- to four-family houses

Brick and frame houses on the side streets off Liberty, Pitkin and Sutter Avenues, often with an owner downstairs and tenants above. A shared street door with a call button per household, and separate codes for each apartment door.

Houses with a basement unit

Homes where the cellar became a legal apartment, through the Community District 5 pilot or otherwise. A second entrance with its own credentials and its own administrator, kept apart from the family’s system.

Rezoning-era towers

Thirteen- to fifteen-story mixed-income buildings on Atlantic Avenue and near Pennsylvania Avenue with hundreds of apartments, ground-floor retail and community space. Networked readers, video entry panels, elevator and amenity control, and careful privacy paperwork.

Industrial buildings and yards

Single-story and loft industrial buildings in the zone between Atlantic and Sutter Avenues, with roll-up doors, steel personnel doors and fenced lots. Gate readers, vehicle detection, hardened strikes and schedules built around shifts.

What the materials mean for the install

Brick is the easy case: readers and stations are fixed into the mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so a later change leaves a joint to repoint, not a broken brick. Wood frames on older two-families need a solid section of jamb for a strike, and where a jamb has been bored for three or four generations of locks we fill or replace that section first. Steel-stud walls in the Spring Creek modules take toggle-style anchors and surface boxes; we do not cut the steel framing, and we follow existing chases between floors where the modules meet.

Industrial doors are the hardest on hardware. A hollow-metal frame filled with grout or concrete, which is common where a frame is set in a masonry wall, has to be cut cleanly for a strike body, and the strike itself has to be rated for a door that gets kicked, slammed by wind off a loading area and pulled by a forklift driver in a hurry. Aluminum storefront entrances in new buildings have narrow stiles with little room for a lock, so the lock usually goes into the panic bar or the frame header instead of the door edge.

One Entrance in Three Parts: Planning a Nehemiah House

The Gotham Center’s list of what the Nehemiah builders put into every house, full basement, parking pad, staggered building line, reads almost like a site plan for an access system. The layout varies from block to block, but the pattern we plan for is a front door a few steps up, a parking pad beside it, and a basement below. Because the building lines were staggered, one house’s door may sit a little forward or back of its neighbor’s, which changes what a camera at the door can see.

We start with the front door, because it is the one the whole family uses every day. A strike in the frame and a keypad or one-button station beside the door means the door relocks every time it shuts, whether or not anyone remembers to turn a key, and each person gets their own code or fob that can be cancelled on its own. Next comes the gate across the parking pad, if there is one: either a simple electric lock on a walk gate, or a motor on a swing or slide gate for the car, opened from the same app. Last is the basement, which in most houses is reached from inside but sometimes has a door of its own to the outside. That door gets a contact so the house knows when it opens, and a lock only if someone other than the family needs to use it.

The cable route is usually the pleasant surprise. A full basement under the whole house means the controller and its battery can sit on a basement wall, out of sight and out of reach, and cable can run along the basement ceiling to a point under the front door and up inside the wall. A run to a gate post across the pad goes in a shallow trench or along an existing fence line in conduit. We keep the controller well away from the furnace and water heater, and off the floor in case water ever comes in.

What we leave in place

The original key lock stays working, so nobody is ever locked out by a dead battery or a forgotten code. The doorbell, if it still works, can stay or be replaced by the station’s call button. And nothing is mounted where it records the neighbor’s door, yard or parking pad; on a staggered line that often means mounting the station on the far side of the door from the next house.

A Second Front Door Below the Steps: Basement Apartments

The Basement Apartment Conversion Pilot Program made Community District 5 the only part of the city where the city itself financed and guided owners through legalizing a cellar or basement apartment. It is no longer taking applications, but the units it helped create are lived in, and other owners have converted basements on their own. Either way, the house now has two households and two front doors, and the door plan has to answer three questions that a one-family house never asks.

First, whose door is it? Once a tenant lives in the basement unit, its entrance is the tenant’s front door. The tenant should hold the only everyday credentials for it, should be able to change their own code, and should not have their entries logged into an app the family upstairs reads. We set the basement entrance up as a separate system or a separate account with the tenant as its user, and give the owner a sealed emergency method, such as a key kept in a lockbox or an override that can be used only after notice, rather than a live feed of the tenant’s door.

Second, how does the owner still reach the boiler, the meters and the shut-off valves? In many houses those sit in the part of the basement the family kept. If the only way to them is through the tenant’s unit, that is a lease and building question for the owner, not something hardware should paper over. Where the utility space has its own door, it gets its own lock on the owner’s system.

Third, and most important, the basement unit’s entrance is its way out in a fire. A lock on that door must open from the inside with one simple movement and no key, code or special knowledge, and it must not fail locked on the inside when the power goes. We fit strikes that keep the inside lever free at all times, and never a magnetic lock or a double-cylinder deadbolt on an exit door from a sleeping area.

Two Hundred Households in a Season: Lobbies in the New Buildings

A new East New York building does not fill the way an older one did, one tenant at a time over decades. A lottery building fills in waves: hundreds of applicants, a few dozen leases signed in a week, households moving in over a single summer. The Innovative Urban Village’s first building, as Brownstoner described it in May 2026, has 386 apartments, 291 of them offered through the lottery and the rest held for households who need on-site supportive services and for survivors of domestic violence. That mix alone tells you the lobby system needs more than a stack of fobs at the management office.

What it needs is a credential process that keeps up. Each household should get its credentials the day its lease begins, not on a rollout day weeks later; each credential should belong to a named person in the household, so a lost fob can be cancelled without changing anyone else’s; and every credential should stop working on move-out without someone remembering to do it. Package rooms, bike rooms, laundry rooms and amenity floors each need their own rules. A building with apartments held for survivors needs management staff trained to block a named person at the door and to keep entry records tightly limited, and every smart-access building in the city needs the written privacy policy, consent process and data limits required by the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law.

We work for developers, general contractors and managing agents on these buildings, from the hardware schedule through to the training of the first super. Where the building already has a video entry platform from another company, we can add readers, extend it to new doors, or take over its service.

Older buildings in the neighborhood face the same questions on a smaller scale. A six- or twelve-unit walk-up off Pennsylvania Avenue may never see two hundred move-ins in a season, but it still turns over apartments, still has a super who needs a credential for the cellar and the roof, and still has to keep its street door self-closing and self-locking under the Multiple Dwelling Law. There, the usual first step is a reader and strike on the street door wired into the existing intercom, so tenants who prefer to buzz guests in can keep doing so, followed by readers on the cellar and package doors once the building is used to the system. The same privacy paperwork applies at six units as at three hundred.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in East New York

The city’s own studies name Fulton Street, Atlantic Avenue, Liberty Avenue and Pitkin Avenue as East New York’s commercial corridors, and Wikipedia adds Linden Boulevard and Pennsylvania Avenue as the main roads through the neighborhood. Each carries a different kind of business, and so a different kind of door.

Atlantic Avenue

A 120-foot-wide road, by the city planning department’s measure, lined with auto shops, wholesalers and now the ground floors of new apartment towers. Back doors to service lanes, staff-only stockrooms and after-hours delivery doors are the usual requests.

Pitkin Avenue

The shopping street with its own business improvement district, lined with storefront retail and food. Mostly storefronts with a roll-down gate in front and a back or basement door that needs a staff schedule and an alert when it opens after closing.

Liberty Avenue

Smaller storefronts, many with apartments above, near the old 75th Precinct station house at 484 Liberty Avenue. The common job is splitting a shop door and a residential street door so each runs on its own schedule.

Fulton Street

The northern corridor toward Cypress Hills and Broadway Junction, where the Herkimer-Williams plan will add retail and community space. Storefronts and community spaces with side doors that should open only for certain hours.

Linden Boulevard and Pennsylvania Avenue

Wide roads that carry much of the neighborhood’s traffic, with storefronts, offices and new buildings, running south toward Gateway Center, whose two malls opened in 2002 and 2014. Office and clinic suites with separate staff and visitor doors are the usual job.

The Industrial Business Zone

Fifty-seven blocks of fabricators, iron works, a precast plant, bus and truck yards, carting, recycling and storage between Atlantic and Sutter Avenues. Gates, roll-ups and steel personnel doors that open before dawn.

Inside the Industrial Business Zone

City Limits’ 2021 review of the zone recorded the city’s 2016 promise to add 3,900 jobs there, and a $16.7 million municipal investment that included $8.2 million for street safety on Van Sinderen Avenue and $2.5 million for broadband. It also recorded businesses that have been here a very long time, such as a metals fabricator in its 125th year, and school bus companies with large parking lots. Those two kinds of business, long-established shops and large vehicle yards, account for most of the access control we are asked about in the zone.

A long-established shop usually has a key problem that has grown with it: keys cut for former employees, a padlock combination everyone knows, a gate remote in a truck that was sold. The fix is to move every door and gate to individual credentials, cancel the old keys by rekeying the cylinders that remain, and keep one key-operated override in a safe. A vehicle yard has a timing problem: dozens of drivers arriving within the same half hour, often before 6 a.m., and a gate that should close behind each vehicle. That calls for a reader drivers can reach from the cab, vehicle tags for the regular fleet, and a loop or beam detector so the gate knows a vehicle has cleared it.

Rules that apply to East New York business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on a path of exit has to release when the power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Exit doors from shops and offices always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above shops. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling must be self-closing and self-locking, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and remote release. A shop that shares a building with apartments cannot leave the residential street door on a daytime unlock schedule unless an attendant is on duty.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law requires a written policy, consent, limits on what is collected, and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized. A shop’s own staff door is not covered, but a shared residential door is.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
  • Tenant locks. Multiple Dwelling Law section 51-c gives a residential tenant the right to add their own lock to their apartment entrance door alongside the owner’s, as long as the owner is given a key on request. An access system on the apartment door does not take that right away.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in East New York: Questions Answered

Nehemiah houses and other owner-occupied homes

On our Nehemiah house the front door, the parking pad and the basement all face the street within a few steps of each other. How do we plan one system around all three?

Treat them as one entrance and decide what each part is for. The front door is the everyday door, so it gets the controlled lock and the credentials: a strike in the frame, and a keypad or station beside the door. The parking pad gate, if you have one, is the second door; it can open on the same code or the same app, and a gate contact tells you when it has been left open. The basement is usually reached from inside the house, so it only needs a contact to report when an outside basement door opens.

One controller in the basement runs all three, with a battery that keeps the door working through an outage. If you want only the front door this year, we install that controller anyway, so the gate and the basement can be added later on the same system without replacing anything.

Our Spring Creek townhouse was built from factory-made steel modules. Can a door station be wired without cutting into the frame?

Yes. Architectural Record describes these houses as stacks of 20-by-40-foot modules with steel frames, fiber-cement siding and concrete floors, so we work with that construction rather than against it. The station is surface-mounted on the siding beside the door, fixed with anchors made for thin steel studs and sealed against the weather. Cable enters the wall through one small sealed hole and runs down inside the stud bay, or follows the joint where two modules meet, to a controller in a closet or utility space.

We do not cut or notch the steel members, and we do not drill the concrete floor slab to pass a cable between stories. Where no chase exists, a neat surface raceway inside a closet is better than a damaged frame.

Our basement apartment was legalized through the East New York pilot. As the owner, can I keep a way into that unit for emergencies without being able to track my tenant?

Yes, and that is the right way to set it up. The basement entrance gets its own lock and its own credentials, issued to the tenant, and its entry log is either switched off or visible only to the tenant’s own account. Your emergency access is a separate method that does not report everyday comings and goings: a key in a lockbox, or an override code that is used only when you have given notice or there is an emergency, and that shows in the log when it is used.

This keeps the arrangement fair to both sides and easy to explain in a lease. We do not set up an owner’s live view of a tenant’s front door, and the unit’s door always opens freely from inside.

Neighbors on our block of Nehemiah homes look out for each other. Can an alert that one of our doors was forced go to a neighbor as well as to us?

It can. Most systems we install let the owner add other people to a notification list without giving them a code to the door, so a forced-door or door-held-open alert can reach a trusted neighbor’s phone at the same moment it reaches yours. You choose which alerts they get; most families share only the forced-door alert and keep ordinary openings private.

A neighbor on the list should know what you want them to do, which is usually to call you and, if they see something wrong, call 911, not to go and check the door. Taking a neighbor off the list is a single tap.

The gas furnace and water heater take up one wall of our Nehemiah basement. Where should the controller and its battery go?

On a different wall, at chest height or higher, ideally close to the electrical panel and the route up to the front door. Electronics and backup batteries last longer away from the heat and exhaust of fuel-burning appliances, and keeping them off the floor protects them if the basement ever takes water. The power supply gets its own circuit, labeled at the panel, so that a breaker tripped by the washer or a space heater does not take the front door down with it.

We also leave clear working space in front of the furnace and the panel. A controller is never mounted where it would block service to either.

Apartment buildings, old and new

The phone app for my new building’s front door keeps asking for my location all the time. Is the building allowed to do that?

The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which covers smart-access systems in multiple dwellings, forbids owners from using those systems to track a tenant’s location outside the building, and limits the data collected to what the system needs. Some entry apps ask for location so that a phone can unlock the door only when it is actually near the door, and that can be set to “while using the app” or replaced by Bluetooth proximity. Being asked for location at all times is worth raising with management in writing.

When we set up phone entry for a building, we choose settings that work without constant location access, and we give the owner a copy of the privacy policy the law requires so tenants can see what is collected and for how long.

Our condo’s entry system runs on one company’s cloud service. If we change providers, do all the readers have to come out?

Not always, but it depends on choices made when the system was bought. Many readers speak a standard wiring protocol and can be connected to a different controller; others are tied to one company’s cloud and fobs. The fobs themselves may or may not work on a new system, depending on their format. We survey the existing readers, controllers and fobs, list which can be kept and which cannot, and price both a partial and a full changeover.

For new buildings we recommend readers and credentials that are not locked to a single cloud provider, and a contract that hands the condo its own administrator account and its data on request.

The industrial zone and the commercial corridors

Our fabrication shop’s personnel door is a dented steel door in a frame filled with concrete. Can an electric strike still go in?

Usually yes. We cut a clean pocket in the frame for the strike body, chip out the grout or concrete behind it, and fit a heavy-duty strike rated for the door’s weight and abuse. If the door has sagged or the frame is bent so the latch no longer lands squarely, that is corrected first, because a strike will not fix a door that does not close true. Where a frame is too damaged to hold a strike, an electrified lock in the door itself, with power passed through a hinge, is the alternative.

On shop doors that take real punishment we add a door closer sized for the door and a contact that reports a door left open, so you know if a door was propped for a delivery and never shut.

We lease our yard to a school bus company whose drivers change every school year. Can their access end in June and come back in September?

Yes. Each driver gets a credential with a start date, an end date and the hours the yard is open to them, so a driver hired for the school year stops getting in on the last day of the year without anyone remembering to cancel them. Credentials for returning drivers can be renewed in a batch before the first day back, and new drivers are added as they are hired. The bus company can be given its own administrator account for its drivers, while you keep control of the gate and see every opening.

This also settles a common argument between landlord and tenant: who let a vehicle in on a Sunday in July. The gate log answers it.

Trucks come into our carting yard one at a time all night. How do we stop a second vehicle following the first through the gate?

The gate has to know when a vehicle has passed and close right behind it. A vehicle detector in the drive, either a loop cut into the pavement or a beam across the opening, tells the gate operator the first truck has cleared, and the gate starts closing at once instead of waiting on a timer. A second detector on the yard side keeps the gate from closing on a vehicle that is still in the opening. A camera aimed at the gate, tied to the gate log, shows which credential opened it and how many vehicles went through.

Nothing physical stops a determined driver from tailgating a slow gate, so speed matters: a faster operator and a short opening time do more than any sign.

Our check-cashing counter handles cash all day. Can access control run a two-door entry so only one door is open at a time?

Yes. Two doors in a vestibule can be interlocked so that the inner door will not release while the outer door is open, and staff at the counter release the inner door only once they have seen who is inside. The design has to keep both doors free to open from inside in a fire or a power failure, which the Building Code requires for exits, so the interlock controls who comes in, not who can get out. Any rules your regulator or insurer sets for the counter itself come first, and we design the doors around them.

Staff-only doors behind the counter get their own readers and schedules, so the back room is open to the people working that shift and nobody else.

Burglaries in the 75th Precinct are down this year. Is access control still worth paying for on a Liberty Avenue store?

It depends on what you want it for. The precinct’s CompStat report showed burglaries down about a quarter year to date in September 2026, and we would not sell a system on the idea that a break-in is coming. What access control does every day, whatever the crime numbers, is end the key problem: no more keys with former staff, a schedule that locks the back door at closing time, and a record of who opened which door and when.

If your store has had keys go missing, staff turnover, or a back door that is sometimes found unlocked in the morning, one controlled door often pays for itself in the locksmith visits it saves. If none of that applies, a good lock and an alarm may be enough, and we will tell you so.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in East New York

These are the generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what an East New York door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“Fobs make every building more secure.”

A fob is only as good as the door it controls and the list of people who hold one. A fob reader on a lobby door that does not close by itself, or a list that still includes last year’s tenants, adds cost without adding security.

The Boulevard Houses resident who told News 12 in January 2025 that her entrance lock did not work was describing the most common failure we see anywhere: hardware that stopped being maintained. No credential helps a door that cannot lock.

We start every job by making each door close and latch on its own, and we hand over a simple routine for keeping the credential list current.

“Access control is for apartment buildings and offices.”

Much of East New York is houses: Nehemiah homes, Spring Creek townhouses, and two- to four-family houses on the side streets. Generic answers skip them.

A single-family house with a front door, a parking pad gate and a basement has exactly the problems access control solves: keys given out over decades, relatives and aides who need to get in, and a door that someone forgets to lock.

A one-door house system on the Brooklyn list starts at $1,500, the same price as one door in a large building, and it can grow one door at a time.

“Wireless smart locks are the easy answer for houses.”

A battery smart lock avoids cutting walls, and on an interior door it may be all you need. Consumer guides recommend them for that reason.

But an original Nehemiah house has a full basement under the front door, which makes a wired run unusually easy, and a wired strike with a battery-backed controller does not depend on someone changing batteries in January. Owners on Reddit’s landlord forums also describe fob systems that “lock tenants out when they freeze”; the same is true of cheap outdoor hardware of any kind.

We wire the doors that matter and use wireless only where cable genuinely cannot go.

“A landlord can see who comes and goes.”

Generic answers treat a landlord’s access logs as a perk. In New York City, a smart-access system in a multiple dwelling comes with legal limits on what is collected, how long it is kept and what it can be used for.

In a house with a legal basement apartment, the same sense applies even where the law’s exact reach depends on the number of units: the tenant’s front door is the tenant’s, and the owner does not need a feed of it.

We set up separate accounts for separate households and keep logs short by default.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of a lock off the shelf and call it a door. That is the price of one part.

A controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the cable, a programmed controller and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500 on our Brooklyn list. A yard or building with several doors and gates on one cloud-managed system is $4,000 to $15,000.

“More cameras mean you don’t need access control.”

East New York already has a lot of cameras. Amnesty International’s 2021 study called it the most surveilled neighborhood in the three boroughs it examined, with 577 cameras counted at intersections.

Cameras record what happened; they do not decide who gets through the door. The early-2026 home invasion near Vandalia and Fountain Avenues was recorded on a neighbor’s doorbell camera, and the man got in by posing as a cleaner.

The useful combination is a door that stays locked until someone inside has seen and spoken to the visitor, and a camera that shows the door when it opens.

“Crime statistics tell you what to install.”

Some answers open with neighborhood crime rankings. The 75th Precinct’s 2026 burglary count was down about a quarter on 2025 by September, and shootings were down 47 percent on five years earlier.

None of those numbers tells you which of your doors is left unlocked, who still has a key, or whether your gate closes behind the last truck.

We plan from the doors and the people who use them, and we leave forecasting to the precinct.

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before the Doors

This describes how we approach a house that has been in one family for a long time, not a particular customer. Many original Nehemiah houses are now in their fourth decade, and over that time keys travel. Before we talk about hardware, we ask the family to make a list: who has a key today, who had one once, and who might have copied one. Grown children, a home attendant from years ago, a neighbor who watered the plants, a contractor who redid the kitchen. The list is almost always longer than anyone expected, and it is the single best argument for individual codes.

Then we go down to the basement, because it decides the price. We look for the panel, a clear wall away from the furnace, and a straight run along the joists to the spot under the front door. We check the door itself last: does it close and latch on its own, and is the jamb solid where the strike will go? Only when all three are right do we choose the keypad or station. A good lock on a door that does not latch protects nothing.

FAQ

East New York Access Control FAQ

At Brooklyn rates, what does one wired keypad door on a Nehemiah house cost?
+

On our Brooklyn list a single controlled door with a keypad or reader, an electric strike, the wiring and ten fobs runs $1,500 to $2,500. Most Nehemiah front doors land toward the lower end because the full basement makes the cable run short and simple. A finished basement ceiling, a damaged jamb or a station with video calling moves it up. Sales tax is added, and the written figure is fixed after the visit.

If my phone dies on the walk home from the train, how do I get through my own front door?
+

We never set up a door that opens only with a phone. Every system we install in a house or a building gives each person a second way in, usually a keypad code or a fob on the key ring, and house doors keep a working mechanical key as a last resort. Many people use the phone for convenience and keep the fob for exactly this situation, coming home late from Broadway Junction or Pennsylvania Avenue with a dead battery.

Someone posted our gate code in a group chat. How fast can it be changed without giving everyone a new one?
+

Within minutes, from the app, as long as each person has their own code rather than one shared code. Then you only cancel the code that leaked and issue its owner a new one, and nobody else is affected. If your gate still runs on one shared code, this is the moment to move to individual codes, which also shows in the log whose code was used. We can set that up remotely on most systems we install.

Do you work on industrial buildings and yards in the East New York industrial zone, or only on homes and apartment buildings?
+

Both. A large share of our East New York work is commercial and industrial: steel personnel doors, roll-up doors with a person door beside them, yard gates for trucks and buses, offices inside warehouses, and counters that handle cash. We bring heavy-duty strikes and gate hardware for those doors, schedule work around your shifts, and send insurance certificates to your landlord or general contractor before the first visit.

There is no cell signal at our basement apartment door. Will phone unlocking still work there?
+

It can, if the reader unlocks by Bluetooth from the phone rather than over the internet, because Bluetooth does not need a signal at the door. We test signal at every door on the site visit. Where phone unlocking is unreliable, the tenant uses a code or a fob as the main credential and the app only for messages and remote release from somewhere with a signal. The door itself keeps working offline either way.

In our two-family, the upstairs tenant pays a separate electric bill. Whose power runs the front door lock?
+

The owner’s. We take power for the controller and the lock from the owner’s or common circuit, labeled at the panel, never from a tenant’s meter. That way a tenant’s shutoff, an unpaid bill or a tripped breaker in an apartment cannot leave the shared door unpowered, and nobody pays for a door that serves both households. The controller’s battery covers the door during an outage.

Some of our warehouse staff don’t want a company app on their personal phones. What do they use instead?
+

A card or a fob. Every system we install accepts physical credentials alongside phone keys, so phone entry can be offered as an option rather than a requirement. Cards can carry a photo and serve as an ID badge as well. Managers who want app alerts can still have the app, and drivers or staff who prefer a card are set up the same way, with the same schedules and the same end dates.

The door into our basement apartment is also its way out in a fire. What kind of lock is allowed on it?
+

One that always opens from the inside with a single simple movement, without a key, a code or any special knowledge, and that never traps anyone when the power fails. In practice that means an electric strike or electrified lever that leaves the inside handle free at all times. We do not fit magnetic locks or double-cylinder deadbolts on an exit from a sleeping area, and if the unit has an approved second exit we treat that door the same way.

We are ordering a new steel front door next month. Should anything be done to the door before it arrives?
+

Yes, and it saves money. If the door and frame are ordered with the frame prepared for an electric strike, or with a hinge that passes power into the door, the access hardware goes in without cutting the new steel on site. Send us the door supplier’s quote or drawing before you order, and we will tell you which preparation to ask for. If the door is already installed, we can still fit hardware; it just takes more cutting.

What makes a good keypad code for a family, and how often should we change it?
+

Give each person their own code of at least five or six digits, and avoid the house number, birthdays, repeated digits and simple runs like 1234. With individual codes you rarely need to change everyone’s; you change one when someone leaves, loses a phone note, or shares it by mistake. Wipe the keypad face now and then too, because worn keys can hint at the digits. Temporary codes for workers should always carry an end date.

Is a fingerprint lock a good idea for our own family’s house?
+

It can be, for a one-family house where only the family uses the door and everyone agrees to enroll. Fingerprint readers outdoors can struggle with cold, wet or worn fingers, so each person should also have a code or a fob. If any part of the house is rented, keep biometrics off the tenant’s doors altogether; the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law treats biometric data in apartment buildings with particular caution, and a code or fob does the job without it.

Our kids leave the front door unlatched. Can the lock relock itself?
+

Yes. With an electric strike the door relocks the moment it closes, because the latch drops into a locked strike every time, so there is no thumb-turn to forget. What it cannot do is close a door left standing open, so we add a door contact that sends an alert if the door stays open longer than you choose, and a closer if the door does not swing shut on its own.
