Putnam's Elevator Stock

A County of Two- and Three-Story Elevators: What Putnam's Buildings Mean for Access Control

If you drive from Brewster to Cold Spring you can go a long time without passing a building tall enough to need an elevator. Putnam is a county of houses, reservoirs, state parkland and small hamlets. The elevators that do exist were mostly installed for one reason: so that older residents and people with disabilities could get from the lobby to a second or third floor.

Why Small Elevators Get Secured

Why a Building With One Small Elevator Still Needs a Gatekeeper

The most common reaction we hear from a Putnam board is that elevator security sounds like something for big buildings. The buildings we are asked to secure here are small, but the reasons are concrete, and most of them come from how small buildings are actually used day to day.

  • Front doors that do not stay shut. Two-story buildings often have a single entry that is propped open for deliveries, for a moving van or simply on a warm afternoon. Once someone is inside, a car that answers every button takes them to the residential floor. A reader in the car is a second line that does not depend on the front door being closed.
  • Community rooms and shared spaces. Many senior complexes have a community room, game room, library or laundry on the ground floor that outside groups and visitors use. Those visitors have every reason to be in the lobby and no reason to be upstairs. Elevator access control lets the building keep the ground floor welcoming while the residential floor stays private.
  • Keys and codes that nobody can take back. An elevator key switch for the upper floor, or a keypad code that has been the same for years, cannot be cancelled when a resident moves out or a contractor finishes. Individual credentials can be cancelled from a screen in under a minute.
  • Medical and office floors after hours. In a two- or three-story professional building, the ground floor may be a pharmacy or a physical therapy practice open into the evening while the upper floor offices close at five. A car that runs freely after hours undoes the locked suite doors upstairs.
  • Not knowing who rode, or when. After a theft, a fall or a complaint, a manager with no access log has nothing to go on. An elevator access control system with audit trail records every credential use with the time and the credential, so the board can track elevator access activity.

To prevent unauthorized elevator access in most Putnam buildings, one good reader in the car, a well-kept credential list and a clear rule about what happens when something fails are enough. Where a building has a real need to keep particular floors private — a records floor in a municipal building, a staff floor in a medical office, an owner's floor in a condominium — we add floor-by-floor control for those floors only. We would rather sell a Putnam building the smallest system that stops its actual problem than the largest one it can be talked into.

Inside the Car

How an Elevator Reader Talks to a Hydraulic or Traction Controller

Every elevator access control system answers one question many times a day: may the person standing in front of this button make the elevator go there? Our equipment does not move the car, open the doors or touch the safety circuits. It decides whether a floor button in the car, or a call button at a landing, is allowed to register for the rider in front of it. The elevator controller still does everything else exactly as it did before.

Most elevators in Putnam's low-rise buildings are likely to be hydraulic. Elevator manufacturers and designers describe hydraulic elevators as the usual choice for buildings of about five stories or fewer, because they are economical for short rises and run at slower speeds than traction elevators. Taller or newer buildings may have traction or machine-room-less elevators. From our side the principle is the same for both: the elevator controller has inputs for its car and hall buttons, and our system places a contact — a relay output, an I/O module point or a dry contact on an interface board — where the elevator contractor tells us it can safely sit, so that a button only works when our panel says it may.

Three ways to make the decision

  • Car enable (Level 1 or Level 2). One reader in the car. A valid fob, card, PIN, phone or face wakes the car buttons for a few seconds and the rider chooses any floor the car serves. In a two-stop building this is all the control there is to have, and it is usually all that is needed.
  • Floor-by-floor restriction (Level 3). Each controlled floor button has its own relay output. A credential unlocks only the floors it is assigned, and the other buttons stay dark. This suits condominiums with private floors, offices with tenant floors, and municipal buildings with staff-only levels. It is how elevator floor restriction, an elevator floor lockout system and tenant floor restriction are actually built.
  • Hall-call control (Level 3). A reader beside the call button at a particular landing — the parking deck, a lower level, the lobby — so the car cannot even be called there without a credential. This is useful where the weak point is a garage or a side entrance rather than the car itself.

The equipment list on our side is similar whichever design you choose: the reader or readers, an access control panel (sometimes called the elevator access control controller), the elevator interface (a relay board, I/O module or interface board with one point per controlled floor per car), a power supply with a battery, the cable between them, and the software where credentials are added and cancelled. That software can be a cloud-based elevator access control platform or a local one, which matters a great deal in Putnam for reasons we explain in the rural section below.

What our equipment never touches is just as important. The car's two-way emergency communication — the phone button that lets a trapped rider reach help — is required by the elevator code, ASME A17.1, section 2.27.1, and has been part of that code since long before card readers existed, according to the National Elevator Industry, Inc. Firefighters' emergency operation, door circuits and leveling are elevator systems. Our readers are mounted and wired so that none of those depend on our equipment, and our outputs are designed to drop out of the way when the elevator goes into fire service.

Elevator access control relay interface, elevator floor enable relay, elevator access control I/O module and dry contact are all names for that point where our panel's decision reaches the button. Their number — floors times cars times any controlled hall stations — is the biggest driver of a Level 3 price.

Small Conveyances

LULA Cars, Platform Lifts and the Small Conveyances in Putnam Churches, Libraries and Town Buildings

A good share of the elevators in a county like Putnam are not full-size passenger elevators at all. Churches, small office buildings, libraries, clubs and older public buildings often added accessibility with a LULA (limited use/limited application) elevator or a vertical platform lift. Elevator suppliers describe the LULA as a class of elevator for low-rise buildings that sits between a wheelchair lift and a full commercial elevator, commonly used in churches, small offices and public buildings of up to about three levels, with a small car and limited travel.

These units raise their own access question. Many of them are fitted with a key switch so that the public cannot ride them unattended, and the key ends up on a hook in an office or in the pocket of one volunteer. When that person is away, a visitor who uses a wheelchair waits. Lift access control installation — replacing the key with a reader or keypad — with credentials for staff, regular volunteers and the people who rely on the lift — — often makes a LULA both more secure and more accessible at the same time.

The licensing is different too, and it matters. New York State's Department of Labor issues an Accessibility Lift Technician license for work on accessibility lifts, and an Elevator Accessibility Technician license that can be substituted for it where the work is restricted to accessibility platform lifts covered by ASME A18.1, alongside the Elevator Mechanic and Elevator Contractor licenses for elevators and other conveyances. So the person who connects our reader to a LULA or platform lift controller is the licensed technician or mechanic who services that unit, not us. We supply and mount the reader or keypad, run the low-voltage cable, set up the panel and the credentials, and hand the licensed technician a clean dry-contact output to connect.

  • What usually fits a LULA: a keypad or a fob reader beside the call station on each level, or a single reader in the car, depending on how the unit's controls are arranged and what its servicing technician will accept.
  • What we ask first: the make of the lift or LULA, the company that services it, and whether the key switch is on the call stations, in the car or both. A photo of each is a great help.
  • What we never do: open the lift controller, change its wiring or bypass a key switch ourselves. That is conveyance work under Article 33 of the Labor Law.
Rural Realities

Internet Gaps, Cell Dead Zones, Generators and Distance: Designing for Putnam's Conditions

Putnam's geography is the reason most people live here and the reason some security systems disappoint. Hills, reservoirs and forest that make the county beautiful also make internet service, cell coverage and quick service calls harder than they are in a city. An elevator access system that assumes perfect connectivity and an installer ten minutes away will let a Putnam building down. We design around four local conditions.

1. The machine room may have no internet

Most households in Putnam do have a broadband subscription — the 2023 American Community Survey puts it at 94.6% of households, as recorded in our county research file — but an elevator machine room or electrical closet is not a household. A cloud-based elevator access control panel there either needs a network cable run from the building's internet service, a cellular modem, or a design that does not need the internet at all for everyday operation. We check this on the survey and price any new network line or cable run as its own line, because it is outside our per-car price.

2. Phones lose signal inside a steel car

Putnam has a long record of complaints about cell dead zones; in 2015 Senator Schumer called for a federal investigation after independent testing found that carriers' coverage maps overstated reception in parts of the region, Putnam included. Inside an elevator car, the steel walls make things worse. That is why, for mobile credentials, we prefer systems where the phone talks to the reader directly over Bluetooth or NFC, so the credential works whether or not the phone has a signal at that moment, and why we always keep a fob or PIN option available for residents who do not use smartphones.

3. Power goes out, and many buildings have generators

Putnam has had long outages after storms; local reporting recorded about 13,000 Putnam customers out after an October 2017 storm and more than 28,000 after the May 2018 storms. Several senior buildings say they have a generator on the premises; the Putnam County Housing Corporation lists generators at its Gleneida Court buildings. Our panel has its own battery, but the real question is what the elevator does on generator power and what our system should do while the building is switching over. We agree that with the board and the elevator contractor in writing before installation, and we put the access panel on the same emergency circuit as the elevator wherever the electrician and the building allow.

4. Distance changes how a system should be run

Our technicians are not around the corner from every Putnam building, and neither is your manager. So we set up elevator access control with remote management wherever the building has a connection, so managers can manage elevator permissions remotely: add and cancel credentials, change a schedule, look at the event log and get an alert when a reader or panel stops reporting, all from a browser or an app. Where there is no connection, the panel stores the credential list and the schedules locally, so the elevator keeps working exactly as programmed, and changes are made on site or through a scheduled connection.

Building by Building

Putnam's Elevator Buildings, Type by Type, and the Access Design Each One Tends to Get

The buildings named below are examples from public listings of each building type; naming them does not mean they are our customers.

Two- and three-story senior complexes in Carmel and Mahopac

The Putnam County Housing Corporation's buildings are a good picture of the type: two-story buildings on Gleneida Court in Carmel with an elevator, a community room and laundry, and a three-story, 48-unit building at 170 Route 6 in Mahopac with an elevator, a community room and a game room. The Putnam County Office for Senior Resources also lists the 62+ apartments at 50 Main Street in Brewster. In buildings like these, apartment building elevator access control starts with a Level 1 reader in the car that reads the same fob residents use at the front door is usually the right first step. The community room stays easy to reach; the residential floor does not. Staff and approved aides get their own credentials, and we set up visitor access through the entry intercom so family members do not need a fob of their own.

Larger 55+ and senior communities with several buildings

Stonecrest in Brewster, with 136 apartments and elevators in each residential building, and the Hillcrest Commons and Hughson Commons communities in Carmel are examples of a different scale: several buildings, an on-site management office, a clubhouse or community suite. Here the design priority is one system for all of the cars, with one credential per resident that works in their own building and the common areas, an on-site administrator at the management office, and readers that all report to the same event log. An elevator access control system with centralized management keeps the manager from juggling separate lists for every building.

Cold Spring condominiums near the river and the train

Butterfield in Cold Spring is described in its listing as 49 condominium residences for buyers 55 and over in two elevator buildings, with a reserved space for each unit in a parking deck, next to the Philipstown Friendship Center and a short walk from Main Street. A condominium like this tends to want each owner to reach their own floor, the lobby and the deck, but not other owners' floors, which is floor-by-floor restriction at Level 3, plus a hall-call reader at the deck landing so the car cannot be called from the garage without a credential. Owners who are away for weeks at a time are a real consideration; we cover seasonal credentials in the FAQ below.

Medical offices around Putnam Hospital and along Route 6

Putnam Hospital at 670 Stoneleigh Avenue in Carmel anchors a cluster of medical and professional offices, and others line Route 6 through Mahopac and Carmel and Route 22 in Southeast and Patterson. We name the hospital only as a landmark; we do not claim it as a client. Medical building elevator access control usually means elevator access control with time-based floor permissions: patient floors open to anyone while practices are open, staff-only floors locked all the time, and every floor credential-only after hours. A small building with a pharmacy on the ground floor and offices above may need nothing more than car-enable after five o'clock.

County, town and village buildings

The Putnam County Office Building at 40 Gleneida Avenue in Carmel, town halls, highway department offices and libraries all mix public counters with staff-only space. The usual answer is to add the elevator to the badge or fob system staff already carry at the doors, so one credential runs both, and to keep records floors, server rooms and offices restricted while public floors follow public hours. Municipal building elevator security also needs clear audit records, which every system we install keeps.

Brewster's Main Street and the commuter-rail villages

Brewster has Putnam's densest village centre; its mayor said in 2026 that the village already has the highest population density of any community in the county. Main Street has older mixed-use buildings with apartments over shops, a short walk from the Brewster Metro-North station, and ideas for more housing keep returning: the Brewster Crossing transit-oriented plan is listed by the county's economic development corporation as dormant, and a concept for affordable housing at the former Garden Street School was under discussion in 2026 with no formal application, according to the Putnam County Press. Where buildings like these have an elevator, keeping shoppers and commuters off the residential floor is a classic car-enable job.

New construction on Route 6 and Route 22

The county's economic development corporation lists Overlook (Gateway Summit) on Route 6 in Carmel as actively under construction, a 60-unit senior housing building with a grocery store at 1534 Route 22 in Southeast as approved, a concept for two three-story buildings with apartments over retail at 25 and 31 Front Street in Patterson, and an approximately 120-room, four-story hotel on Route 311 in Patterson as pending approval. For any new building with an elevator, the time to plan elevator access is before the elevator is ordered, so the contractor can provide controller inputs and a traveling-cable pair for the reader, and the architect can show a conduit from the access panel location to the machine room.

Office, flex and light-industrial buildings

The office and warehouse projects along Fields Lane and Independent Way in Southeast, and the commercial corridors on Route 6, Route 22 and Route 52, include two-story office and flex buildings, some with a single passenger or freight elevator. Commercial elevator access control here usually means car-enable with the company's existing badges, a schedule that matches business hours, and, where a building is shared, tenant floor restriction so each company's people reach only their own floor.

Who Touches What

Elevator Mechanics, Lift Technicians and Low-Voltage Installers: Dividing the Work on a Putnam Job

Every elevator access project in New York State is two jobs done by two trades, and the most useful thing a board can understand before it signs anything is where the line between them falls. New York's Labor Law Article 33 requires businesses and individuals that design, install, inspect, test, maintain, alter, service and repair elevators and other conveyances to be licensed by the Department of Labor. The Department's licensing page states that it is a violation of Article 33 for an unlicensed individual to alter or maintain a conveyance, or to install power lines from the mainline to the conveyance in existing buildings, and that only licensed elevator mechanics working under the direct supervision of a licensed elevator contractor may do that work.

Local Code

Code Enforcement Across Putnam's Six Towns and Three Villages

Outside New York City, the State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code is administered locally, and Putnam divides that job among its towns and villages. The office that may want to hear about elevator work depends on where the building sits. Our research for other Putnam projects found, for example, that the Town of Carmel's code includes a building construction and fire prevention chapter with fire inspectors; that the Village of Brewster's Code Enforcement Officer administers the Uniform Code and issues certificates of occupancy; that the Village of Cold Spring says projects follow the State code, the Energy Code, the Village Code and its Architectural and Historic District Design Standards; and that Philipstown's code refers to a Code Enforcement Official and a Fire Marshal.

For most elevator access projects the elevator contractor's work is what a local office, or the elevator inspector, is interested in, and it is the elevator contractor who knows what that municipality expects for a change at the controller. Our part is low-voltage work that we carry out under our state license. Where a building is in a village historic district, such as Cold Spring's, anything visible from outside may need review, but readers inside a car or a lobby rarely do. On every Putnam project we confirm with the elevator contractor and, where there is doubt, with the town or village building department before work starts, and we do not guess on your behalf.

  • Town of Carmel (including Mahopac and the hamlet of Carmel): Building Department and fire inspectors under the town code.
  • Village of Brewster: Code Enforcement Officer under the Uniform Code; the surrounding Town of Southeast has its own building department.
  • Village of Cold Spring and Village of Nelsonville: village building departments, with Cold Spring's historic district design standards; the surrounding Town of Philipstown (including Garrison) has its own Code Enforcement Official.
  • Towns of Kent, Patterson and Putnam Valley: each administers its own code enforcement; Patterson's code, for example, names a Town Fire Inspector and a Code Enforcement Officer.

One more point that city-based companies sometimes get wrong: New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021, the tenant data privacy law that regulates smart access systems in residential buildings in the five boroughs, does not apply in Putnam County. Nor do NYC Department of Buildings bulletins. We still recommend that any Putnam building using fobs, phones or face readers on the elevator adopts a short written policy on what the system records, who can see it and how long it is kept, because residents ask and boards should have an answer.

When Things Fail

Fire Recall, Generator Power and Equipment Faults: Deciding the Failure Mode Before Anything Is Wired

The most important decisions are about what happens when something goes wrong. We write them into every Putnam proposal as plain sentences the board signs off on.

Fire alarm and firefighters' service

When a building's fire alarm recalls the elevator, or a firefighter turns the recall key, the elevator follows its own firefighters' emergency operation. Regulators and the elevator code treat add-on devices in the car and at hall stations the same way: they must not impede normal or emergency operation and must not have any effect while the elevator is on firefighters' service. NYC's Buildings Bulletin 2022-010 says this in so many words; it is New York City guidance, not Putnam law, but it states the principle the elevator code applies everywhere. Our outputs are arranged so the car ignores our system during recall, and we test that with the elevator contractor before the job is closed out.

Fail-safe or fail-secure

If our panel loses power or stops working, the elevator buttons can either all become free to use (fail-safe) or stay locked except for the lobby (fail-secure). In a senior building where residents depend on the elevator, fail-safe is almost always the right choice: a few hours of unrestricted riding is far better than residents unable to get home. In a building where a particular floor holds records or medications, the board might choose fail-secure for that floor only. Either way, it is a written decision, and the building's staff know how to put the car into independent or attendant service if they need to.

Outages and generators

Our panel's battery carries it through a short outage without any change. For longer outages, what matters is what the elevator does: some buildings' elevators run on generator power, some only lower to the lobby and open, and some stop. Where a generator powers the elevator, we ask the building's electrician to place our panel on the same emergency circuit, so access control and the elevator come back together. We test what happens when power returns either way.

Reader faults and communication failures

A reader can be damaged, a cable can be cut during unrelated renovation work, a panel can lose its network connection. An elevator access control communication failure between the reader and the panel should never strand a resident. Part of fail-safe design is an elevator access control emergency override that trusted staff can use, an alert to the manager when a reader or panel stops reporting (where the building has a connection), and a short written procedure that tells staff what to do and whom to call. The most common cause of an elevator card reader not working is mundane: a loose connection, a failed power supply or a battery at the end of its life.

Upgrades and Repairs

Retiring a Key Switch, a Shared Code or an Orphaned Reader in a Putnam Elevator

Many Putnam buildings already have some form of elevator control, usually one of three: a key switch in the car or at a landing, a keypad with a code everyone knows, or a card or fob reader put in years ago by a company that no longer answers the phone. Each one can be replaced or rescued, and an elevator access control retrofit is often simpler than a first installation because the elevator contractor has already provided a connection point once.

Key switches

A key switch is, electrically, a simple contact. In many cases our reader's output can take its place at the same point, so the controller sees the same signal it always did, now triggered by a credential instead of a key. The elevator contractor confirms that and makes the change at the controller end. Shared keypad codes

A single elevator keypad code works until the day it does not, usually because it has spread far beyond the people it was meant for. Replacing the keypad with one that supports individual codes, or with a fob reader, is a modest elevator keypad installation or replacement. Where the keypad hardware is sound, sometimes the controller behind it can be upgraded instead so the same keypad supports individual codes.

When an elevator card reader is not working, an elevator key fob is not working or the whole elevator access control system is not working, the cause is usually one of a short list: power supply or battery failure, a cable damaged during other work, a failed reader, a panel that lost its programming or its network connection, or a controller-side change made during elevator maintenance. Elevator access control troubleshooting starts on our side with power, the reader, the panel and the event log. Taking over an orphaned system depends on whether its software and credentials can be recovered. Myths

Five Mistaken Beliefs About Elevator Security in a Rural County

1. "Crime is low here, so the elevator does not need securing."

Putnam's crime numbers are low: New York State's index crime data record 519 index crimes across the whole county in 2024. But most requests we get are not about crime at all. They are about privacy for residents, control of who reaches upper floors, cancelling credentials that have walked out the door, and knowing who rode when something goes wrong. Those needs exist in a quiet village as much as in a city.

2. "The security installer will wire it into the elevator."

Not legally in New York State, and not by us. The controller connection is conveyance work licensed by the Department of Labor, and it is made by your elevator contractor or lift technician. Our part ends at a clean, labeled output that their licensed mechanic connects.

3. "A cloud system will not work out here."

It will, if it is designed properly. A well-chosen platform stores credentials and schedules on the local panel, so the elevator keeps working with no internet at all. The cloud is used for remote management when the connection is up. The mistake is choosing a system that needs the internet to make every decision.

4. "Residents will be trapped if the reader fails."

Only if nobody decided what should happen. Fail-safe operation, a battery, a staff override, firefighters' service that ignores our system and a written fault procedure mean a reader fault becomes a service call, not an emergency.

5. "Older residents will not cope with fobs."

Most do, with a little help on enrollment day and a credential that suits them. For residents who do not, a personal PIN, a large keypad or intercom release from staff covers the gap. The aim is that nobody's independence depends on one small object.

FAQ

Putnam County Elevator Access Control FAQ

Our building has a LULA elevator rather than a full-size passenger car. Can it take a card reader?
+

Usually, yes. A LULA (limited use/limited application) elevator has a controller and call stations just like a larger elevator, and many are fitted with key switches that a reader or keypad can replace. We supply and mount the reader or keypad, run the low-voltage cable and program the credentials; the connection at the LULA controller is made by the licensed elevator mechanic or accessibility lift technician who services it, because that is conveyance work under New York's Labor Law Article 33. Before the survey we ask for the make of the unit, the name of the company that maintains it and photos of the call stations and the car controls. For a LULA in a church, library or town building, a schedule often works well: free to use during public hours and credential-only at other times, with codes or fobs for staff and the people who rely on it.

Our small Putnam building has a hydraulic elevator. Does a hydraulic car change how the reader is connected?
+

Not in principle. Whether the elevator is hydraulic or traction, its controller has inputs for the car and hall buttons, and our system adds a relay or dry-contact output that the elevator contractor connects so a button only registers when our panel allows it. What differs from building to building is the controller's age and design, whether it has spare inputs, and whether the traveling cable to the car has spare pairs for a car-mounted reader. Older relay-logic controllers sometimes need a different connection method from newer microprocessor controllers, and the elevator contractor decides that. Hydraulic machine rooms are often small rooms next to the shaft at the lowest level, so we also check space, temperature and moisture for our panel. None of this changes our Putnam starting price for Level 1 or Level 2; it can change the elevator contractor's part of the work.

The machine room in our Putnam building has no internet. Can the elevator credentials still be managed from the office?
+

Yes, there are two good ways to do it. The first is to give the panel a connection: a network cable run from the building's internet service or a small cellular modem, which we price as its own line after the survey because it sits outside our per-car price. With that in place, your manager can add and cancel credentials, change schedules and read the event log from a browser or app anywhere. The second is a panel that stores credentials and schedules locally and does not need the internet to make decisions, with changes made on site or during a scheduled connection. Many buildings use both, so the elevator keeps working through an internet outage and still gets remote management when the line is up. What we avoid is a system that cannot decide whether to let a resident ride without reaching a server somewhere else.

Will a reader installed in the car interfere with the elevator's emergency phone?
+

No. The car's two-way emergency communication is required by the elevator code, ASME A17.1 section 2.27.1, and it belongs to the elevator and its monitoring service, not to the access control system. We do not wire into it, power our equipment from it or mount anything that blocks it. When we choose where a reader goes in the car operating panel or on the cab wall, we keep clear of the phone button, its speaker and any screen or camera the emergency communication system uses, and we follow the elevator contractor's guidance on what can be fixed to the cab. If your building is planning to upgrade the emergency phone to a system with video or text messaging, tell us during the survey so the two projects do not compete for the same space or the same spare cable pairs to the car.

Who supplies the electrical circuit that powers the elevator access panel?
+

Normally the building's electrician. Our panel needs a reliable 120-volt circuit near where it will be mounted, usually in or beside the machine room. We specify what is needed during the survey, and we prefer a dedicated circuit so that other equipment on the same circuit cannot trip it. Where the building has a generator that powers the elevator, we ask for the panel to be on the same emergency circuit, so access control and the elevator come back together after an outage. Remember that New York's elevator licensing law reserves running power lines from the mainline to the elevator itself to licensed elevator mechanics; our panel's circuit is separate from that and is ordinary building electrical work. If an electrician is not available, tell us and we can discuss options, but we will always put the arrangement in writing in the proposal so nothing is left between trades.

Can one floor in our Putnam County building require a PIN code on top of a fob?
+

Yes. Many readers support two-factor operation, where a fob or card must be followed by a personal PIN, and the requirement can be set for particular floors, readers or times. A common Putnam example is a professional or municipal building where most floors need only a badge but a records floor, a pharmacy store room or a server room needs badge plus PIN. In an elevator, two-factor control is usually set up at Level 3, with the floor's relay output released only after both checks pass, so that a lost or borrowed badge on its own does not open the sensitive floor. Face or fingerprint readers at Level 2 can play the same role without a PIN to remember. We recommend keeping two-factor for the few floors that genuinely need it; asking every resident for a PIN on every ride makes a system harder to live with.

Does a phone credential work inside a steel elevator car where there is no cell signal?
+

With the mobile credential systems we normally recommend, yes. The phone talks to the reader directly over Bluetooth or NFC, using a credential already stored on the phone, so the moment of entry does not depend on cell coverage or Wi-Fi inside the car. The phone needs a connection only when the credential is first issued or changed, and that happens wherever the resident is. That matters in Putnam, where coverage gaps have been documented for years and steel elevator cars weaken any signal further. Some systems do rely on a live connection or on the phone opening an app, so we check this for the specific platform before recommending it. And because batteries run flat and not every resident uses a smartphone, we always keep a fob, card or PIN available alongside phone credentials.

Our condominium has owners who leave for the winter. Can their elevator access be paused and switched back on in spring?
+

Yes. Most platforms let an administrator suspend a credential without deleting it, or give it start and end dates, so an owner's fob simply stops working while they are away and starts working again on the date they return. That is useful in a 55+ condominium where some owners spend months elsewhere, because a fob left in a drawer, lent to a relative or lost while traveling cannot be used to ride to their floor in the meantime. Owners who want a house-sitter, family member or contractor to check on their unit can have that person issued a separate, time-limited credential limited to their floor, which keeps the event log clear about who was in the building. On a cloud-managed system the manager can do all of this from wherever they are; on a locally managed panel, it is set up on site before the owner leaves.

Our lower elevator landing opens to an unheated garage. Will a reader there hold up through a Putnam winter?
+

It can, if the reader is chosen for the location. Readers and keypads carry a rated operating temperature range and an ingress rating for dust and moisture; for an unheated garage, parking deck or open lower lobby we specify outdoor-rated hardware rated for the cold, mount it out of the path of snowmelt and road salt splashed from cars, and seal the cable entry. We keep the access panel itself indoors in a conditioned space, never in the garage. Hall-call readers at garage landings are worth the care, because the garage is often the easiest way into a building without passing the front door. We also check that the reader does not interfere with any garage door controls, and we pick a reader whose light and sound are easy to notice in a dim, noisy space.

What routine upkeep does an elevator access system need after it is installed?
+

Light, regular attention rather than major work. Each month someone should review the credential list and remove people who have moved out or finished working at the building, and glance at the event log for repeated denied attempts at one reader. Each year the panel battery should be checked, readers cleaned, the software updated, and the bypass during firefighters' service confirmed again after any elevator maintenance work. Every few years the battery is replaced and the credential technology reviewed. Most of this can be done by a trained manager, and on a connected system much of it can be done remotely. Buildings that would rather not handle it can book scheduled maintenance visits from us, with on-call service in between. The main thing is that someone owns the job; systems fail through neglect far more often than through equipment faults.
