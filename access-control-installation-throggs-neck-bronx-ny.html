Throggs Neck, Bronx

Plan for the Peninsula You Will Live In · Garage and Gate Count as Doors · Free Exit Always

Throggs Neck is the last stretch of the Bronx before the water: a peninsula between Westchester Creek, the East River and Long Island Sound, reached by car far more often than by train, with the approaches to two bridges running through it. For most of a century it has been a place of houses, garages and small shops that residents describe as quiet. That is about to be tested. In December 2025 New York State licensed a casino-hotel for the golf course at Ferry Point Park, at the foot of the neighborhood, with a 500-room hotel, a 2,000-seat hall and garages for thousands of cars, and the project is expected to take years to build. We do not predict what the casino will do to the streets around it, and nobody honestly can. What we can do is help a household, a condo board or an East Tremont Avenue shop decide which of its doors, gates and garage doors matter, put each one on a credential that can be changed in a minute, and keep every door opening from inside without a key, before, during and after the build.

The Neighborhood

Why Throggs Neck Properties Need Access Control

Start with the map. Wikipedia describes Throggs Neck as a narrow spit of land in the southeastern Bronx marking the passage between the East River and Long Island Sound, and sets the neighborhood’s limits at Westchester Creek on the west, Baisley Avenue and the Bruckner Expressway on the north, and water on the other sides. It gives the area as about 1.9 square miles, puts it in Community District 10 and ZIP code 10465, and notes that the 45th Precinct, whose station house is at 2877 Barkley Avenue, patrols it. The spelling has never settled. The same article records that the traditional name has two Gs, that the bridge and the expressway carry one, and that a story credits Robert Moses with dropping the second G to save room on signs. We use both, the way residents do.

The peninsula was a long time becoming a neighborhood. Wikipedia’s history runs from a 1642 settlement under the Dutch, through a 1776 skirmish at the Westchester Creek bridge near today’s East Tremont Avenue, to the building of Fort Schuyler at the tip between 1833 and 1856, which brought laborers and craftsmen, many from Ireland, to live nearby. In the late nineteenth century it was a summer resort with beer gardens reached by steamboat. Large estates held most of the land until the 1920s, when, citing a 1922 New York Times notice of an auction of 1,600 waterfront bungalow lots from the Morris estates, the article says they were cut into row homes and densely built bungalow lots. Summer colonies such as Silver Beach and Edgewater Park grew up the same way and were later adapted for year-round living.

What changed the peninsula most was the road. The article notes that a 1929–1939 plan to bring a subway branch to Throggs Neck never happened, and that the building of the Throgs Neck Bridge and its parkways, finished by 1961, ended the area’s isolation without ending its dependence on cars. The approach to the Bronx–Whitestone Bridge runs through it too, and the Bruckner Interchange sits at its northern edge. When the city opened a ferry landing at Ferry Point Park in December 2021, the city’s announcement quoted the Community Board 10 district manager calling the district “a transit desert.” For doors, that history matters in a plain way: most households here own a car, many own a driveway and a garage, and those are entrances to the house that a front-door lock does nothing about.

The housing that resulted is mostly small. A 1984 New York Times profile, as Wikipedia quotes it, said the area “seems like a well-kept suburb”; the same article lists middle-class homes, waterfront condominiums and beach clubs alongside the Throggs Neck Houses, a public housing development built in 1953 and expanded twice. Twenty years later the neighbors went to the city about what was being built. City Planning’s summary of the Throgs Neck rezoning, adopted by the City Council on September 28, 2004, says residents had complained of new multifamily townhouses that were “bulkier and taller” than the homes around them, of waterfront buildings that blocked views of the water, and of too little parking; planners surveyed 242 blocks lot by lot and moved most of them to districts that do not allow attached townhouses. Two years later the Brush Avenue rezoning did the same for three blocks between Wenner Place and Jay Place, beside Ferry Point Park, limiting them to detached one- and two-family homes with room for two or three cars each.

So the typical Throggs Neck job is not a lobby with a hundred fobs. It is a house with a front door, a side gate, a driveway and a garage, perhaps with a tenant upstairs; a row of townhouses from before 2004 whose owners share a walk or a gate; a condominium building near the water with a garage under it; or a shop on East Tremont Avenue with a back door to an alley. Each has more ways in than its owner usually counts, and each opening is a separate decision about who should be able to use it and when.

The news from the peninsula is mostly about those secondary doors and about cars. In June 2025 News 12 reported that several cars in Throgs Neck had been broken into and their airbags stolen, that residents said all of them were Hondas, and that at least one car had been taken. In December 2022 the station showed a package thief on a homeowner’s camera taking parcels from a Throgs Neck house in daylight while the family was out. Neither story is about a door that failed; both are about what sits outside the door. That is a useful reminder of the limits of this work, and we come back to it below.

And then there is Ferry Point. In December 2025, the Bronx Times reported, the state awarded Bally’s a 15-year license for a casino with 3,500 slot machines, 210 table games and 40 poker tables, a 500-room hotel, two parking garages, a 2,000-seat event center, a nightclub, a spa and shops on the golf course at Ferry Point Park. The same report says residents near the site had raised concerns about crime and traffic, and that Community Board 10, which covers Throggs Neck, voted against the plan at a chaotic March meeting. News 12 reported that the company aimed to finish ahead of mid-2030. Whatever one thinks of it, the casino is the largest change to the peninsula since the bridges, and it will arrive in stages: years of construction first, then a destination that draws visitors at all hours. A sensible door plan is built for both stages, and it starts with what is already there.

Repairs

Gate Code Out in the Open or a Garage Door Left Up in Throggs Neck? We Fix That First

Most of the calls we get from houses like these are not about new systems. They are about a side gate whose latch no longer meets the keeper after a winter of frost, a keypad whose code was given to a contractor three summers ago and never changed, a garage door opener that nobody can program because the remote and the manual went with the last owner, or a battery lock that has been chirping for a week. In a shop, it is a back door reader that stopped talking to its controller or a lock that buzzes but does not release. We fix the door first, then the electronics, and we change the codes before we leave.

We work on hardware other people put in, including locks and openers from companies that no longer answer the phone. When a part cannot be had, we say so on the visit and price the replacement for that one opening rather than the whole house. A repair visit is billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), and the written report lists every door we touched.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Throggs Neck’s Architecture

Six kinds of property make up most of the peninsula outside its parks and campuses. We describe them from the sources cited on this page and from what a door survey looks for; the method under each is ours.

Houses on the old bungalow lots

Where the estates were cut into small lots in the 1920s, many original summer cottages have been enlarged or replaced. Lots are narrow, side yards are a few feet wide, and the side passage is often the only route to the back of the house. Doors are usually light wood frames that want a deadbolt-style keypad rather than a strike.

Detached one- and two-family houses

The bulk of the 242 blocks City Planning surveyed in 2004. A front door, a side door off the driveway, a garage at the back or under the house, a yard gate, and often a separate entrance for an upstairs or basement apartment. Each household needs its own way in.

Attached townhouse rows built before 2004

The “bulkier and taller” multifamily townhouses that prompted the rezoning still stand. Several owners share a front walk, a gate or a row of garages, while each has a private door, so the plan has a shared layer and a private one.

Waterfront condominium buildings

Condominium buildings near the bridges and the shore, such as those on Pennyfield Avenue that appear in current sale listings, with a lobby, a garage, a package area and a volunteer board. Wind, salt air and garage traffic shape the hardware.

Mixed-use buildings on East Tremont Avenue

The BID’s 2018 district plan describes ground-floor stores with offices or community rooms on the second floor in some buildings and apartments above in others. A store, an office and a family can share one front wall and need three separate entry rules.

Public and institutional buildings

The Throggs Neck Houses, SUNY Maritime College at Fort Schuyler, the parish schools and the high schools have their own operators and security staff. They appear on this page as landmarks; AESS does not install there unless asked by the institution itself.

How the peninsula changes the method

  • Count every opening, not just the front door. A typical house here has four to six: front, side, back, garage, garage-to-house and a gate. We list them all before choosing hardware.
  • Separate households, not just people. Owner and tenant, or two branches of one family, each get their own door or their own codes, with nothing logged about the other.
  • Plan for the car. The garage door and the driveway gate are opened from a moving vehicle, often in the dark, so the credential has to work from the driver’s seat without turning the car into a key that a thief can drive off with.
  • Keep the water and wind in mind. Near the shore, keypads, gate locks and fasteners are chosen for salt air, and gates are checked for wind load before a lock is fitted.
  • Leave room to tighten later. Wiring and controllers are sized for the doors you might add once the casino work starts, so changing a rule later costs a phone call, not a visit.

What to gather before the survey

  • Every key and remote you can find, including garage clickers, gate keys and the code someone wrote inside a cabinet door.
  • Who lives and works there: family members, tenants, a regular cleaner or aide, a dog walker, a snow-removal service.
  • The garage opener’s make and age, photographed on its label, and whether it has battery backup.
  • For a condo, the declaration and bylaws section on common elements, and the name of whoever holds the current system’s login.
  • For a store, the lease clause on alterations and the landlord’s contact, because the doors usually belong to the building.
The Casino Years

Before, During and After Ferry Point: Planning Doors for a Changing Peninsula

The casino has a public record, and it is worth reading before deciding anything about your own doors. On February 25, 2025, Community Board 10 held a public hearing on the land-use actions the project needed. According to the board’s minutes, 46 people spoke: 36 against, seven in favor, three asking for more information and one neutral. Speakers raised additional traffic through the Ferry Point Park community, air quality, increased crime and concern for children at a nearby school, the loss of parkland and possible future encroachment on Harding Avenue, disturbance of methane gases, and a lack of transparency about the promised benefits. The minutes describe the project as a 3.1-million-square-foot development with a gaming floor, hotel, event space and parking.

The approvals then went back and forth. 6sqft reported that in June 2025 the City Council voted 32 to 12 for the home rule resolution that let the state take roughly 16 acres out of parkland. In July, the same outlet reported, the Council voted 29 to 9, with four abstentions, to deny the rezoning, on a motion by the local Council Member, and the mayor vetoed that denial. In December the Gaming Commission awarded the license; News 12 reported that approval was conditional on an independent monitor for up to five years and that the company expected more than 12,000 union construction jobs. In March 2026, 6sqft reported that Bally’s had bought the 16-acre parcel from the city for $156.6 million and committed $4 billion to a three-million-square-foot complex.

None of that tells a homeowner on Lafayette Avenue or a shopkeeper on East Tremont what will happen on their block. The record shows fears and promises, not findings. The benefits package the Bronx Times describes includes NYPD substations, transit and infrastructure upgrades and funding for schools and organizations, many of them taking effect only after completion. The Gaming Facility Location Board itself, the paper reported, said the project’s scale made it hard to predict whether those promises would be delivered. We will not tell anyone that a casino brings burglaries, or that it does not. What we will say is that a plan for your doors should not depend on which way that argument goes.

Instead we plan in three stages. Before construction, the job is to know your own openings: replace shared and copied keys with individual codes, fix the gate and garage door that never quite close, and put the doors that matter on a system you can change from your phone. During construction, years of trucks, trade crews and new faces will pass through the western end of the peninsula. That is the time for dated codes for any work on your own house, alerts for a garage or gate left open, and a camera that shows who is at the side gate before anyone walks out to it. After opening, a destination that runs late will change when people are on the streets near Ferry Point and the ferry landing. Doors that face those streets may want a stricter schedule at night; doors that do not may need nothing more at all.

The point of staging is that each step is small and reversible. A keypad deadbolt and a garage controller bought now keep working if the casino is delayed, scaled back or never felt on your street. A controller sized for six doors costs little more than one sized for two, and it means that tightening a rule in 2030 is a setting rather than a purchase. And if the worst fears of the hearing do not come true, nothing has been wasted: you will still have doors that lock behind you, codes you can take back, and a record of who came in.

  • Now: list every opening; replace copied keys and shared codes; repair latches, closers and garage door safety eyes; choose one system for the whole property.
  • Construction: dated codes for your own contractors; open-door alerts on the garage and side gate; a video call station at the gate or side door; check that free exit works on every gate after any fence work nearby.
  • Opening and after: review the night schedule on doors facing busier streets; keep the paper record of codes and the manual release current; change nothing that is already working.
Next to the Park

The Blocks Beside Ferry Point Park

The houses nearest the casino site are a small, specific place. City Planning’s Brush Avenue rezoning covered three full blocks bounded by Wenner Place on the north, Jay Place on the south, the Hutchinson River Parkway on the east and Brush Avenue on the west, between Ferry Point Park and the parkway. The city’s reason, in its own words, was that “there is a mismatch between the built character of the area and the building type permitted by the existing zoning”: the blocks were detached houses, and attached and multifamily buildings had been going up on underused lots. Since February 2006 they have been limited to detached one- and two-family houses, with minimum lot widths of 30 and 33 feet and parking for two cars at a one-family house and three at a two-family.

Those figures describe the door problem exactly. A detached house on a 30-foot lot has a side yard on at least one side, a driveway, a garage or a parking pad, and more cars than the street can hold. Its owner already deals with drivers looking for parking on the way to the park and the golf course; the hearing record shows neighbors expect more of that, not less. The useful questions are about what sits between the sidewalk and the back of the house: whether the side passage has a gate and whether it latches, whether the garage door can be seen from the street and whether anyone knows when it is left up, whether the back door is a better way in than the front because nobody watches it.

NYC DOT’s September 2026 presentation to Community Board 10 adds one more change to plan around. Its proposed Throggs Neck bicycle network, about two miles long, runs on Brush Avenue, Lafayette Avenue, the Cross Bronx Expressway service road, Randall, Buttrick, Schley and Balcom Avenues, with new park connections at Schley and Balcom and at Harding Avenue and a ramp from Brush Avenue to the Hutchinson River Greenway. DOT proposes repurposing about 70 parking spaces and building the first phase in the fall of 2026, the second in the spring of 2027. It counted 700 northbound and 432 southbound vehicles on Brush Avenue in the peak hour. A driveway gate or garage door that opens across a new bike lane needs a slower, more visible opening, and a keypad post that a driver can reach without stopping in the lane.

For houses like these we usually recommend four things and stop: a code lock on the side or back door the family actually uses, a latching gate on the side passage with a lock that opens freely from the yard, a garage door controller that reports its position and closes itself after dark, and one video station where a visitor is seen and heard before anyone opens anything. A driveway gate is worth it only where the driveway is long enough to hold a car clear of the sidewalk while the gate opens, and where the family will actually close it.

What stays with the city, and what is yours

  • Yours: every door, gate and garage door on your lot; the codes and who holds them; the camera at your own entrance and what it is aimed at.
  • Your association’s, where there is one: gates and lanes in the private communities on the peninsula, such as Silver Beach and Edgewater Park, which have their own rules and their own pages on this site.
  • The city’s: public streets, sidewalks, curb cuts, traffic, parking enforcement, the parks and the bike lanes. No private gate goes across a public street, and no camera of ours is aimed at a neighbor’s windows or yard.
Cars and Garages

A Peninsula of Driveways: Garage Doors, Remotes and the Bridges

On a peninsula where most people drive, the garage is the door used most, and the one least thought about as a door. A typical opener is controlled by a clicker on the car’s sun visor, a wall button inside, and sometimes an old keypad on the jamb whose code has never been changed. Inside the garage is usually a door into the house with a lock nobody uses. That combination means the visor remote is, in effect, a house key that lives in the car, often next to a registration card with the home address on it.

The June 2025 break-ins News 12 covered were about airbags, not house keys. Still, a car opened on the street or in a driveway gives whoever opened it everything in the glovebox and on the visor. A resident’s post we found in a Queens community on Reddit, which we saw only as a search excerpt, describes thieves taking cars over the Whitestone and Throgs Neck bridges; we cite it as one person’s account, not a finding. The fix for the house is simple and cheap compared with the risk: take the house out of the car.

That means moving the garage door to a controller that answers to the owner’s phone or a code entered at a keypad, rather than a clicker that anyone holding it can use; giving the door from the garage into the house its own lock and its own code; fitting a position sensor that tells you when the garage door is up; and, where the family likes the convenience of a remote, choosing one that can be disabled from the app the moment a car is broken into. None of this needs new wiring through the house. Most of it fits on the opener and the door frame in an afternoon.

The same thinking applies to a condo garage. A building whose garage ramp opens onto a service road near the bridges usually runs on remotes or windshield tags. Remotes are copied and handed on; tags stay on the car they were issued to. We prefer tags or plate-matched readers for vehicles and fobs or phones for people, so that a stolen car opens nothing and a car sold to a stranger can be removed from the list in a minute. Every vehicle gate and garage door we install has the photo eyes or edges that keep it from closing on a person or a car, and a manual release that someone in the building knows how to use.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control on East Tremont Avenue

Throggs Neck’s main street is East Tremont Avenue, and since 2018 its central stretch has had a business improvement district. The City Planning Commission’s report approving the Throggs Neck BID sets its boundaries on both sides of East Tremont Avenue from Bruckner Boulevard to Miles Avenue and counts 180 properties: 141 wholly or partly commercial, about 35 residential, and four owned by government or nonprofits. It describes ground-floor commercial uses with offices or community facilities on some second floors, mixed-use buildings with apartments above, residential zoning with commercial overlays, and a commercial vacancy rate under ten percent. Its first-year budget of $340,000 went to sanitation and maintenance, marketing and events, and administration; the plan lists no security services. The doors on the avenue are the owners’ and tenants’ to manage.

Stores with apartments above

The shop has its front and back doors; the apartments have a street door, usually beside the shop. They should never share a credential. If the store’s staff need the building’s cellar or refuse area, that gets its own door rule, not a key to the residents’ hall.

Second-floor offices

The BID plan notes offices and community facilities on some second floors, usually reached up a flight of stairs from their own street door. A reader or code lock on the street door, a release button at the reception desk, and a schedule that locks the door when the last person leaves.

Back doors to the alley or lot

Deliveries come and garbage goes out the back, often early in the morning before anyone is in the front. A back door on a code that works only during delivery hours, with a contact that reports it left open, closes the gap nobody watches.

Stockrooms and cellars

The room with the stock, the cash or the controlled medicines gets its own lock and its own short list of codes, separate from the back door that every employee uses.

Restaurants and late openers

Kitchen doors propped for air, staff who close at different hours, and a front door that locks at a set time whether or not anyone remembers. Free exit stays in place for every occupant until the last person leaves.

Toward the Bruckner end

At the Bruckner Boulevard end the avenue meets the expressway. Where a property there has a lot, a service bay or a storage yard, its gate wants a vehicle-rated operator and a log of which code opened it.

The BID also matters to the casino story. The Community Board 10 minutes record that a bus was provided after a request from the Throggs Neck BID, running along East Tremont Avenue and not into the Ferry Point Park community. If the avenue does gain evening trade from casino visitors and workers, the change for most stores will be longer hours and more staff, which is a scheduling problem as much as a security one: more people with codes, more closing routines, more chances for a back door to be left on the latch.

The rules a Throggs Neck entry system has to meet

  • Free exit, always. Every electrically locked door must open from inside with one motion and no key, code or special knowledge. Where a door is unlocked by a motion sensor or a release button, the NYC Building Code’s sections on electrically locked egress doors (§§1010.1.9.8–1010.1.9.9) set how it must behave, including release on loss of power and on fire alarm where required.
  • Apartment buildings keep a self-locking street door. The Multiple Dwelling Law, §50-a, requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercom or buzzer system in many multiple dwellings; a new reader or keypad has to keep that working.
  • Tenant data has rules. In buildings with three or more apartments, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) governs keyless entry: a written policy, consent, collecting only what is needed, and deleting authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized. It does not reach an owner-occupied one- or two-family house, but we follow its spirit there anyway.
  • Gate operators need entrapment protection. Motorized vehicle gates and garage doors we install meet the UL 325 standard, with monitored photo eyes or edges in both directions of travel on swing and slide gates.
  • Housing Maintenance Code duties stay with the owner. Locks on entrance doors to rental apartments and building entrances remain the owner’s duty under the Housing Maintenance Code; a new system does not move that duty to anyone else.
  • Licensing. Anyone installing these systems in New York needs a state license under General Business Law §69-m; AESS holds #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Throggs Neck: Questions Answered

These are the questions Throggs Neck homeowners, condo boards and shopkeepers bring us now that the casino is licensed and the bike lanes are coming. Each is answered here, once, in full.

The Casino at the Foot of the Peninsula

The Ferry Point casino-hotel is not expected to be finished until around 2030. Should a Throggs Neck building upgrade its doors now or wait to see what the casino brings?

Do the inexpensive, reversible part now and leave the rest open. Replacing copied keys with individual codes, fixing gates and garage doors that do not close properly, and choosing one system that can grow are worth doing whatever the casino turns out to mean, because they deal with risks you already have. They also make the later decisions cheaper: a property with its doors already listed and on one controller can add a schedule or a camera in an afternoon.

What is worth waiting for is anything that depends on how the streets actually change, such as a driveway gate bought because of parking you expect but have not seen, or night schedules on doors that face streets nobody yet knows will be busy. The license was granted on conditions and the project has already survived a Council denial and a veto, so its timing is not something to build a door budget around.

Bally’s community benefits package lists NYPD substations. Does a promise like that change what a co-op or condo board near the site should plan for its own entrances?

No. Whatever police presence comes, it will patrol streets, not your lobby, garage or package room, and the Bronx Times reports that many of the promised benefits take effect only after completion. A board’s duty to keep its entrances self-closing and locking, and to know who holds a credential, is the same with or without a substation.

Where a promise like that does help is in the conversation with the precinct. Once there is a building system with a log and a video station, a board can answer an officer’s question about who entered at what time without guesswork. That is worth having on any timeline.

Our condominium garage exits onto a road that casino traffic may use. Should the garage door run on a timer at busy hours or open only for a resident’s tag?

Only for a tag, with the timer used for closing rather than opening. A garage door held open on a schedule lets anyone walk or drive in behind a resident during the busiest part of the day, which is exactly when the ramp is hardest to watch. A tag or plate-matched reader opens the door for each resident’s car, and a close timer and a contact sensor make sure it does not stay up.

If queuing on the ramp becomes a problem, the answer is a faster door and a reader placed far enough up the ramp that a car stops clear of the street, not an open door. A pedestrian door beside the garage door should have its own reader, so nobody needs to walk under a moving door.

Speakers at the community board hearing worried about more traffic and crime near Ferry Point Park. What can a homeowner on those blocks control on their own lot, and what stays the city’s job?

You control every opening on your property: the front, side and back doors, the passage gate, the driveway and the garage, and who holds a code to each. You can decide which of them are locked all the time, which report when they are left open, and where a visitor is seen before anyone answers. You can also keep a camera on your own entrance, aimed so it does not record a neighbor’s windows or yard.

The street, the sidewalk, the curb, the traffic and the parking are the city’s, and so is the park. A private gate cannot close a public street or sidewalk, and a gate on your lot should swing inward so it never blocks the walk. Concerns about traffic and enforcement belong with Community Board 10, the 45th Precinct’s community council and 311.

Cars, Garages and the Water’s Edge

Our garage remote is clipped to the car’s sun visor, and the registration in the glovebox carries our address. If the car is broken into in the driveway, what should change?

Assume the remote is gone, even if it is still on the visor, because it may have been copied or the thief may come back. Disable it in the opener or reset the opener’s remote memory that day, then change the code on any keypad by the garage. If the door from the garage into the house has no lock, or a lock nobody uses, that door is now the weakest one in the house.

The lasting fix is to stop keeping a house key in the car. Move the garage to a controller that opens from a phone or a code, give the house door in the garage its own lock and code, and add a sensor so you know when the garage door is up. If the family wants a remote, choose one that can be cancelled from the app on its own.

We keep a boat and its trailer in the side yard behind a chain-link gate that we open only a few times a year. What lock and sensor make sense for a gate used that rarely?

A heavy padlock or a keyed gate lock usually serves a gate opened a few times a season better than anything electric, because a keypad that sits idle for months is the one whose battery is flat the day you need it. What the gate does need is a way to know it has been opened: a wireless contact on the latch that sends an alert to your phone, powered by a battery that lasts years.

If the yard is also the route to the back door, treat that as a separate opening with its own lock, and keep the trailer gate on the outside of your plan. Check the chain-link post and hinges each spring; a gate that sags after winter is easier to lift off than to break.

The dead end of our street runs down to the water, and neighbors want to put a gate across the end of the block. Is that allowed?

Not on a public street. A mapped city street, its sidewalk and its end belong to the city, and a private gate across them would block public access and emergency vehicles. Some communities on the peninsula grew from private bungalow colonies and manage their own lanes under their own rules; check whether your street is mapped before anyone spends money.

What neighbors can do is coordinate their own lots: latching gates on each side passage, garage doors that close themselves at night, and video stations at front doors. If the worry is people using the street end at night, that is a matter for 311 and the precinct rather than a gate.

East Tremont Avenue After Dark

If evening trade on East Tremont Avenue grows once the casino opens, how should a shop that adds a late shift change its staff codes and its closing routine?

Give each person on the late shift their own code, valid only during their hours, so the log shows who closed. Set the front door to lock itself at closing time and the back door to report if it is still open fifteen minutes later. Then write the closing routine down: who checks the back door, who arms the alarm, who leaves last, and what they do if the lock will not engage.

A second shift also means more people who once had a code and no longer work there. Make removing a departing employee’s code part of their last day, not something done when someone remembers. The system should make that a single step.

The BID’s maintenance crew and our landlord’s porter both need the area behind our row of stores before we open. How do we let them in without handing out store keys?

Separate the shared area from the stores. If there is a gate or door into the rear yard or alley, put that opening on its own code that works only in the early-morning hours the crews need, and keep each store’s back door on its own code. The crews then have one credential that opens the shared space and nothing else.

Because the gate usually belongs to the landlord, the landlord should hold the administrator account and the tenants should be told who has a code. We put that in writing at handover so it does not depend on one porter’s memory.

Condos and Houses Near the Bridges

Our waterfront condominium is run by volunteer board members who change every year or two. How should the access system’s passwords and records pass from one board to the next?

The system should belong to the association, not to a board member. Put the administrator account in the association’s name on an email address the association controls, keep at least two administrators at all times, and record each change of administrator in the board minutes. When a member leaves the board, their personal login is removed the same week.

At every changeover, hand over a short written record: the controller’s location, the account details kept in the association’s safe or password manager, the list of credential groups, the installer’s contact and the date of the last battery test. We provide that record at installation and update it on any service visit.

Our front door faces the Cross Bronx Expressway service road and the traffic noise never stops. Will we hear a visitor at the door or the gate over it?

Probably not with a simple chime, which is why we would not rely on sound. A video station at the door or gate can ring every phone in the house and an indoor screen at the same time, so a visitor is seen wherever you are, and the station’s own speaker and microphone are chosen for a noisy street, with noise cancellation set on the outdoor side.

The same noise hides the sound of a door or gate being forced. A contact on the door that sends an alert is more reliable on a loud street than listening for it, and a light at the door that comes on with the station helps the camera at night.

Put the shared part in a short written agreement among the owners: who is administrator, how costs are split and what happens when a house is sold.

We plan to sell our house next spring. Is it worth installing access control first, or should we leave it to the buyer?
+

Fix what a buyer's inspector will notice, such as a gate that will not latch or a garage door that does not reverse, and keep the electronics simple. A battery keypad lock and a garage controller are inexpensive, and a buyer can reset them to their own codes in minutes; a larger wired system is better chosen by the people who will live with it. Whatever you install, hand over the administrator account and the manuals at closing.

If drivers headed for the park or the casino block our driveway, can the gate or door camera record it in a way that helps a 311 complaint?
+

It can give you a dated clip showing the car and the time, which is useful when you report a blocked driveway to 311. Aim the camera at your own driveway apron rather than across the street, and keep clips only as long as you need them. The camera does not make anyone arrive faster; enforcement stays with the city.

I see clients by appointment in a home office with its own side entrance. Can that entrance open only during booked hours while the family entrance stays private?
+

Yes. The office entrance gets its own lock on a schedule that matches your hours, with a video station so you can see and admit each client, and the door between the office and the rest of the house gets its own lock that clients never have a code for. Outside office hours the office entrance behaves like any other locked door of the house.

Can our garage door close by itself if it is left open after dark?
+

Yes. A controller with a position sensor can close the door at a set time or a set number of minutes after it opens, and send a notice to your phone when it does. The opener's photo eyes and auto-reverse stay in charge, so the door stops and reopens if anything is in its path, and the automatic close can be switched off for a night when you are working in the garage.
