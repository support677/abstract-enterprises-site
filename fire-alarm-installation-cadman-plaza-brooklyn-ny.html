The Neighborhood

Why Cadman Plaza Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

The housing that replaced them is the reason fire alarm work here looks so different from the brownstone blocks a few minutes' walk away. Between the late 1960s and the early 1970s, on the site of the old trolley terminal, three Mitchell-Lama cooperatives went up between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street. Cadman Plaza North, at 140 Cadman Plaza West, dates from 1967 and rises 27 stories; listings give it 250 or 350 apartments, and we have not settled which is right. Whitman Close, at 75 Henry Street, was completed in 1973; its tower is listed at 32 stories with 352 apartments or 33 stories with 370, and about sixteen two-story townhouses by the architect William Conklin sit at its foot. Cadman Towers, at 101 Clark and 10 Clinton Streets, was begun in 1969 and finished in 1971, with towers of 32 and 13 stories, 420 or 421 co-op apartments depending on the source, and 50 duplex townhouses added to keep the street at a human scale. NYC Urbanism describes saw-toothed poured concrete, six-foot concrete walls around the towers and townhouses, garages for about half of the units, and a supermarket, synagogue and stores along Pineapple Walk. These buildings were left out of the Brooklyn Heights Historic District when it became the city's first historic district in 1965. Their concrete matters to an installer: it decides how cable can be run, where a device can be fastened, and how long a job takes.

The fire reports from these blocks keep returning to two themes: what happens high in a tall building, and what happens when one apartment is a construction site. In March 2022, the Brooklyn Eagle reported a smoky fire on the fourth floor of 75 Henry Street that spread acrid smoke through the 32-story co-op. Worried residents went out into the hallways, and tenants near the affected apartment were alerted through the building intercom by the doorman. The apartment where it began had been under renovation for six months and held construction materials. In December 2022, a fire on the ninth floor of 140 Cadman Plaza West injured two people, both with minor injuries. And on July 28, 2025, a smoky fire broke out on the ninth floor of a 15-story office tower at 185 Montague Street, between Cadman Plaza West and Clinton Street. The main stair led down to a lobby full of smoke, the safety stair let out onto the sidewalk beside a Chipotle, and 26 units with 84 FDNY and EMS members responded. A tenant from a medical office told the Eagle, "We smelled it and then the alarm went off." None of these reports says how the detection in those buildings performed, and we do not suggest any of it failed. What they do show is the shape of the problem: smoke travels through corridors and stairs that many households or offices share, and how quickly each person hears about it depends on detection inside their own space as much as on the building's system.

Six Cadman Plaza facts that shape a fire alarm plan

  • The homes are high. Cadman Plaza North is 27 stories, the Whitman Close tower 32 or 33, the taller Cadman Towers building 32, and One Clinton 36 or 38 depending on the source. Most households here live far above the street, so the corridor and the stair, not a window, are the way out.
  • The towers are poured concrete. NYC Urbanism describes Cadman Towers as saw-toothed poured concrete behind six-foot concrete walls. Cable cannot be fished through a concrete ceiling the way it can through plaster and lath, which is why tower apartments usually get sealed or wireless-interconnected alarms rather than new hardwired runs.
  • Height crosses a code line. A DOB bulletin summary from SPARC says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. At 27 to 38 stories, the residential towers are well past that, so their building systems involve announcements as well as tones, while the townhouses at their feet do not.
  • Two firehouses are close by. Engine 205 and Ladder 118 share the firehouse at 74 Middagh Street, and Engine 224 is at 274 Hicks Street. A nearby firehouse shortens the drive, not the time it takes for someone to notice a fire and call.
  • Mechanical rooms sit below grade. In January 2024 a water-main break flooded the Cadman Towers basement mechanical room, and the Eagle reported the roughly 50-year-old pipes from the street into the building are the co-op's responsibility. Panels, power supplies and standby batteries in cellars need to be placed and checked with water in mind.
  • The towers sit outside the historic district; much of their neighborhood does not. The Brooklyn Heights Historic District uses Cadman Plaza West (Old Fulton Street) as its northern edge, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior changes visible from a public way. The Borough Hall Skyscraper District, created in 2011, protects 21 buildings nearby, and 8 Cadman Plaza West is an individual landmark. We check an address before anything touches a façade.

Inside a co-op tower apartment: your alarms, the corporation's system, and a concrete ceiling

An apartment in one of the three Mitchell-Lama towers is surrounded by somebody else's fire protection. The corridor outside the door has the building's detection and, in a tower this tall, the speakers or signals that carry announcements. That equipment belongs to the co-op, is maintained under its contracts, and is not ours or the shareholder's to move. Inside the apartment door, the job is different. FDNY guidance for residential buildings says smoke alarms go outside each sleeping room, within 15 feet of the room's entrance, and carbon monoxide alarms in the same locations. In a one-bedroom on a high floor that may mean two or three alarms; in a large apartment with bedrooms off a long hall, more. We plan them so they are interconnected, so a fire in the living room wakes someone asleep at the far end with the door shut.

The concrete settles most of the method questions. A poured slab ceiling cannot be fished for new cable, and surface raceway across a living room is something few shareholders want. So in these towers we usually install sealed long-life alarms that interconnect wirelessly, or replace existing hardwired alarms on their existing boxes where the apartment already has them. Fastening into concrete is done with the right anchors, slowly, with dust control, because the building's own corridor detectors sit just outside the door and dust from a hammer drill can travel. Before work starts, we tell the doorman or super what we are doing and when tests will sound, so a test in apartment 21C does not become a call to the lobby. If anything in the apartment is wired to the building system, such as a heat detector or a speaker, we leave it alone and point it out to the managing agent.

The duplex townhouses: a house plan inside a tower co-op

The townhouses at the bases of Cadman Towers and Whitman Close look like small houses and live like them, with an entry level, stairs inside the unit, and bedrooms upstairs. That interior stair is the main difference from a tower apartment. A fire on the lower level fills the stair with smoke on its way to the bedrooms, so the plan needs a smoke alarm on each level, one outside each sleeping room within the 15-foot distance the FDNY describes, carbon monoxide alarms in the same places, and interconnection so the lower level and the bedrooms sound together. If a townhouse has a kitchen at the entry level, the alarm near it is placed and chosen so that ordinary cooking does not train the family to ignore it.

The townhouses are still part of the co-op, so the same ownership questions apply. Some may share building systems, utility rooms or garage access with the tower, and the six-foot concrete walls NYC Urbanism describes wrap the townhouses as well as the towers. Before we work, we ask the managing agent whether any device in the townhouse reports to the building, and we follow the co-op's alteration rules for hours, insurance and drilling. Because the townhouses are low, they fall below the height where voice communication enters the code, but their residents still share some exits and grounds with hundreds of tower households, so we look at the route from the townhouse door to safety as well as at the rooms inside.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Lobby, Not at the Apartment Ceiling

These notes describe how our technicians approach a job in a tall building like the ones around Cadman Plaza. They describe our practice, not an event at any particular address.

Check in where the building watches itself. Before going up, the technician introduces himself at the desk or to the super, says which apartment or suite he is working in, and asks whether anyone needs to be told before tests sound. In an office tower, that person is often the Fire and Life Safety Director's office. A five-minute conversation in the lobby prevents a test alarm from becoming a building response.

Read the paperwork on the door. Co-ops and condos post rules for contractors: working hours, elevator padding, insurance, where debris goes. We follow them as written, and we keep a copy of the alteration agreement with the job.

Find the building's devices before choosing yours. Inside the apartment or suite, the first walk is to identify everything that belongs to the building: speakers, strobes, heat detectors, sprinkler heads. Those are mapped and left alone. Our devices go where they do not crowd or confuse them.

Test from the bed, door shut. Each alarm is tested from where someone actually sleeps. In a concrete tower, sound behaves differently from in a frame house, and a door that seals well keeps out smoke and noise alike.

Leave the lobby a note. On the way out, the desk hears that work is finished and no further tests will sound, and the shareholder or tenant gets a written list of every device and its date.

Compliance notes for offices, shops and restaurants near the plaza

  • Approval comes before installation. Under the Building Code's Section 907, fire alarm documents go to the FDNY for approval before installation, and the Fire Code's Section 907 governs the system afterward.
  • The national standard is not the whole rulebook. TFP1 notes that meeting NFPA 72 alone does not satisfy New York City's requirements; the city's codes add their own.
  • Panels trigger filings. ASM Integrators explains that replacing or relocating a fire alarm control panel requires new plans filed with the FDNY, and that alterations may require upgrading to the 2022 code and, in some cases, an auxiliary radio communication system (ARCS).
  • Like-for-like is a repair. Replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no design change is treated as a repair rather than a redesign.
  • Tall buildings announce. SPARC's summary of a DOB bulletin says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, which describes most office towers between the plaza and Borough Hall.
  • Outages have limits. The 2022 Fire Code addresses work that takes a system out of service for 8 hours or more in a 24-hour period or 30 hours or more in a month, which governs how fit-outs are scheduled in occupied buildings.
  • Landmarked exteriors need review. In the Brooklyn Heights Historic District and the Borough Hall Skyscraper District, the LPC reviews exterior changes visible from a public way, and DOB permits follow LPC approval.
Where the Rules Change

Sixteen Apartments, Three Stories, 125 Feet: The Code Thresholds Around Cadman Plaza

Most mistakes in fire alarm spending come from treating every building alike. Around Cadman Plaza, a single block can hold a two-level townhouse, a 32-story tower and a three-story building with a store downstairs, and they sit on different sides of several code lines. This section explains those lines as we read the public guidance. We are licensed installers, not code consultants or lawyers, and this is not legal advice; it is how we decide what path a job is on before pricing it.

The line between household alarms and a building system. A vendor guide from NY Engineers, which describes itself as a general guide and not a replacement for the code, says a residential occupancy needs a fire alarm system when dwelling units sit at least three stories above the lowest level of exit discharge, or when the building has at least 16 dwelling units. The three co-op towers and One Clinton are far beyond both numbers, so a building system is expected; our job in those buildings is the apartment side and, with the board, the building side. A three-story building on Henry Street with a few apartments over a shop sits much closer to the line, and its count of apartments and floors is the first thing we check. At the low end, the 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system for dwelling units in buildings of two stories or fewer when units are separated by one-hour fire partitions and each has a direct exit to a public way, court or yard. That exception depends on its conditions, and a townhouse that belongs to a larger complex is a case to examine, not assume.

What applies inside every apartment. Whatever the building has in its corridors, the FDNY's guidance for landlords says smoke alarms go outside each sleeping room, within 15 feet of the entrance to the room, and carbon monoxide alarms in the same locations. That is the baseline for the rental apartments in the towers and the units shareholders rent out, and it is the same layout we recommend to owners who live in their apartments. On our Brooklyn list, a basic residential alarm system starts at $600, a wireless system usually runs $600 to $1,200, and for a board or a shareholder landlord setting up several rental units, per-unit setup runs $400 to $800.

When a repair becomes a filing. In a building that already has a system, the dividing line is design. Along the corridor. Up from a lower floor. The five-alarm fire at Montague and Henry began on the second floor and spread upward, emptying the building and its neighbors on both sides. Across a roof. The Park Plaza Diner fire was on the roof of a one-story building, and smoke was seen coming from the roof of the Brooklyn War Memorial in May 2023. In from the street. The August 31, 2026 transformer fire blew a manhole cover and pushed carbon monoxide into nearby homes, with power problems on top. Living high in the towers

Our tower has sprinklers and a building fire alarm. Does our apartment still need its own smoke and carbon monoxide alarms?

Yes. A sprinkler head responds to heat from a fire that is already burning, and a building system's corridor detection watches the shared spaces outside your door. Neither is designed to wake you in your bedroom at the first trace of smoke from your own kitchen or living room. FDNY guidance for residential buildings places smoke alarms outside each sleeping room, within 15 feet of the room's entrance, and carbon monoxide alarms in the same locations, whatever else the building has. In the poured-concrete towers off Cadman Plaza West we usually meet that with sealed long-life alarms that interconnect wirelessly, so a fire at one end of the apartment sounds at the other without new cable across a concrete ceiling. If some device in your apartment is already wired to the building, such as a heat detector or a speaker, we leave it in place and work around it, because it belongs to the corporation's system, not to the apartment.

During the fire at 75 Henry Street, the doorman warned nearby floors over the intercom. Can the alarms inside our apartment tell the lobby anything?

Household smoke and CO alarms inside an apartment are normally standalone: they wake the people in the apartment and nobody else. That is by design, because a building cannot respond to every burnt-toast alarm in hundreds of units. The Eagle's report on the March 2022 fire shows how a staffed building fills the gap: the doorman alerted tenants near the affected apartment over the building intercom. Connecting an apartment's devices to the building's panel would be a change to the building's designed system, which only the board and its design professional can decide, and which may need filing. What a household can do is simpler. Make sure your own alarms are loud enough to wake everyone, know how the building announces an emergency, keep the lobby number in your phone, and call 911 yourself if you discover a fire. If you want your apartment's alarms to reach someone when you are away, partner monitoring of compatible residential equipment is an option, but it is separate from the building's system.

Why would the building's alarm sound on our floor and a few others but not across the whole tower?

In tall buildings, the fire alarm is often designed to signal and announce to the floors most affected rather than send the entire building into the stairs at once. SPARC's summary of a DOB bulletin says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, and the residential towers around Cadman Plaza, at 27 to 33 stories, are well past that height. Exactly how a particular tower is set up, which floors receive which signals and what the announcements say, depends on its design and its age, and the managing agent or the building's fire alarm contractor can tell you how yours works. What matters for a household is to listen to announcements when they come, follow the building's posted fire safety information, and never assume that silence on your floor means there is nothing to know. If smoke or fire is in your own apartment, get out and close the door behind you, whatever the building is or is not announcing.

The apartment next to ours has been gutted for months and is stacked with materials. What can we reasonably ask the co-op to do?

You can ask the managing agent what fire protection is in place in that apartment while it is a construction site, and you can make sure your own side is ready. The fourth-floor fire at 75 Henry Street in March 2022 started in an apartment that had been under renovation for six months and held construction materials; the cause was not reported, and we draw no connection beyond that. During long renovations, household alarms are often taken down and nothing replaces them until the work is done, while lumber, packaging and solvents sit in rooms nobody sleeps in. A board can require temporary detection in a unit under renovation as part of its alteration agreement, and can require that building devices in the unit stay uncovered and working. On your side, check that your alarms are working and interconnected, especially in rooms that share a wall with the renovation, and keep your entrance door closing on its own. If you smell smoke from next door, call 911 and the lobby; do not wait for an alarm.

The FDNY puts smoke alarms within 15 feet of each sleeping room. Our bedrooms sit at the end of a long hall. How many alarms does that mean?

Usually more than people expect. The FDNY's guidance says landlords must install smoke alarms outside each sleeping room, within 15 feet of the room's entrance, and carbon monoxide alarms in the same locations. If two bedrooms open off the same short stretch of hall, one combined position may cover both doors. If the hall is long, or the bedrooms are at opposite ends of the apartment, each needs its own. Many of the larger apartments in the co-op towers have a living area, kitchen and dining space at one end and bedrooms down a corridor at the other, so a typical plan has an alarm outside the bedroom group, another near the living area, and interconnection so a fire in the living room is heard behind the bedroom doors. We measure on site, draw the plan, and show you the result before drilling. Owners who live in their apartments are not bound by landlord rules in the same way, but the same layout protects them.

Offices, restaurants and the people who run them

Staff in our Montague Street office smelled smoke before the building alarm sounded. Does that mean the detection was too slow?

Not necessarily, and you cannot tell from the order alone. During the July 28, 2025 fire at 185 Montague Street, a medical-office tenant told the Eagle, "We smelled it and then the alarm went off," and nothing in that report says the system failed. People often smell smoke before any detector responds, because smoke drifts through doorways and ducts to where people are standing before it reaches a detector in enough concentration to trigger it, and because office buildings place detection according to their design and code requirements, not in every room. What you can usefully check is whether your suite's layout still matches what the building's detection was designed for. If your office has added walls, closed rooms, a server closet or a pantry since the system was designed, tell the landlord or the Fire and Life Safety Director, because the building's design may need to account for them. Above all, staff should treat the smell of smoke as a reason to act, not to wait for an alarm.

Our building has a Fire and Life Safety Director. What fire safety work still belongs to our own office?

Boards, budgets and the code

A vendor says the system they are proposing meets NFPA 72. Is that enough in New York City?

No. NFPA 72 is the national fire alarm code, and New York City builds on it with its own Building Code and Fire Code. TFP1's summary of the framework says the Building Code's Section 907 requires owners to submit fire alarm documents to the FDNY for approval before installation, that the Fire Code's Section 907 governs the system once it is installed, and that meeting NFPA 72 alone does not satisfy the city's requirements. A proposal for a building system near Cadman Plaza should therefore say who the registered design professional is, what will be filed with the FDNY, and how the work will be inspected, not just which standard the equipment meets. For household alarms inside an apartment, the relevant guidance is the FDNY's on where smoke and CO alarms go. If a quote for building work does not mention filing at all, ask why before you sign it.

Our board heard that replacing the building panel could pull the whole system up to the 2022 code, or even require ARCS. What does that mean in practice?

It means a panel replacement in an older tower is a project, not a swap, and the board should budget and plan accordingly. ASM Integrators explains that replacing or relocating a fire alarm control panel requires new plans filed with the FDNY, that alterations may require upgrading to the 2022 code, and that in some cases an auxiliary radio communication system, the in-building radio coverage firefighters rely on in large structures, must be installed. In a poured-concrete tower finished in the late 1960s or early 1970s, the scope depends on what the design professional finds: the condition of existing cable, which devices can stay, and what the current code requires once the work is defined. Our part is to survey and trace what is there, give the designer accurate existing conditions, install to the approved drawings, and stage the work so most of the building stays protected. Get the scope first; the price follows it.

Our Mitchell-Lama co-op is converting to an HDFC. Who pays for fire alarm work, the corporation or the shareholder?

Your proprietary lease and house rules answer that, and we do not interpret leases, but there is a common pattern worth knowing. The building's system, meaning the corridor devices, the panel, the notification and the wiring between them, is normally the corporation's to own, maintain and pay for. Alarms inside the apartment are often the shareholder's under the lease, and where a shareholder rents the apartment out, the FDNY's guidance puts the duty to install smoke and CO alarms on the landlord. Engine 205 and Ladder 118 are a few blocks away on Middagh Street. Does a firehouse that close mean we need less warning?
+

No. A nearby firehouse shortens the drive once someone has called, but the clock only starts when a person notices the fire and dials 911. Household smoke and CO alarms inside an apartment normally alert only the people inside it, so they are what gets you out and what prompts the call. If an apartment or business sits empty for long stretches, partner monitoring at $19.99 a month is the way a signal reaches someone who can act.

In a new condo tower, are the smoke alarms inside my unit mine to replace, or the building's?
+

Your condominium declaration and bylaws decide, and the managing agent can confirm. As a common pattern, household smoke and CO alarms inside the unit are the owner's to maintain, while anything wired to the building's panel, such as a speaker, strobe or heat detector, belongs to the condominium and its fire alarm contractor. We replace the household alarms and leave every building device exactly as it is.

What should a household do when a carbon monoxide alarm sounds?
+

Get everyone out to fresh air, call 911 from outside, and tell the lobby or super. Do not silence the alarm and go back to bed, and do not wait to find the source yourself. After the August 31, 2026 transformer fire, the FDNY found dangerous carbon monoxide in homes near Middagh and Henry Streets, a reminder that CO can come from outside the building as well as from appliances inside it.

The transformer fire knocked out power nearby. Do hardwired smoke alarms keep working when the electricity goes?
+

Only if their backup batteries are present and good. A hardwired residential alarm runs on house power and falls back on its battery during an outage, so an alarm with a dead or removed battery goes silent exactly when the lights do. Sealed long-life alarms run on their built-in battery all the time. We check backups on every visit and note the date on each device.

The panel in our co-op lobby shows trouble and supervisory lights. What is the difference?
+

An alarm indication means the system believes it has detected a fire. A supervisory indication usually means a monitored condition needs attention, such as a sprinkler valve or a related device that is not in its normal state. A trouble indication means the system has found a fault in itself, such as a wiring problem, a power issue or a weak standby battery. Trouble and supervisory signals should be reported to the managing agent and the building's fire alarm contractor promptly, not left lit.

For work inside one apartment, will you need the super to open the building's panel room?
+

Usually not. Household smoke and CO alarms inside an apartment are standalone and are installed and tested entirely inside the unit. We tell the doorman or super when tests will sound. Only if a device in the apartment belongs to the building's system does the managing agent, and where there is one the building's fire alarm contractor, need to be involved, and we leave that device alone until they are.
