The Neighborhood

Why Brownsville Properties Need Alarm Installation

Brownsville sits in eastern Brooklyn between Ocean Hill and Bedford–Stuyvesant to the north, East New York to the east, Canarsie to the south and East Flatbush and Crown Heights to the west. Wikipedia describes its usual limits as East New York Avenue, Van Sinderen Avenue, the Long Island Rail Road’s Bay Ridge Branch with Linden Boulevard, and Ralph Avenue with East 98th Street. Inside those lines are a little over a square mile, the 11212 ZIP code for most addresses, Brooklyn Community District 16, which it shares with Ocean Hill, the 3 train running through it and the L along its eastern edge.

The neighborhood was laid out for working people from the start. In 1858 William Suydam divided the land into 262 lots of small worker housing; after his money ran out, the property was auctioned in 1866 to Charles S. Brown, who gave the place its name and sold lots to Jewish families leaving the Lower East Side. By 1900, Wikipedia records, most residents lived in two-story wooden houses built for two families each, and factories, workshops and stores stood beside them. Pitkin Avenue became the shopping street: in 1942 it held 372 stores, eight of them banks and 43 selling menswear. Fortunoff began on Livonia Avenue under the elevated New Lots line.

From the 1950s the city replaced blocks of old tenements with public housing. Today NYCHA owns more homes in Brownsville than in any other neighborhood, roughly ten thousand apartments in 18 developments and more than a hundred buildings, about a third of the housing stock, according to Wikipedia’s account of the Authority’s figures. They range from the three-story buildings of Howard Avenue and the four-story rehabilitated walk-ups on Tapscott Street to the sixteen-story towers of the Tilden Houses, the 22-story Hughes Apartments and the 24-story tower at Glenmore Plaza. Several developments, including Seth Low and Howard Avenue, have moved to private management under the federal RAD program, which NYCHA runs as PACT.

Around the developments is a second Brownsville of small houses. South of Pitkin Avenue there are rows of semi-detached multi-unit houses, and across the western half stand the Nehemiah rowhouses built by East Brooklyn Congregations, which Wikipedia describes as attached homes with gardens, driveways and finished basements. Toward Ocean Hill the stock turns older again, with limestone and brownstone townhouses among the tenements. These are the houses where an owner often lives on one floor and rents the others.

And there is a third Brownsville going up now. The city’s Brownsville Plan, published by the Department of Housing Preservation and Development after a community planning process, commits to more than 2,500 new affordable homes and more than a billion dollars of investment. The NYU Furman Center counts 5,559 homes added to its Brownsville area between 2010 and 2025, and 4,142 of them were income-restricted. New York YIMBY reported in September 2025 that the Brownsville Arts Center and Apartments broke ground at 366 Rockaway Avenue: nine stories, 283 affordable rentals and an arts center, with completion expected by the end of 2027. In October 2026 Catholic Charities opened the Gail P. Duke Senior Residence, 141 homes for older New Yorkers with supportive services. On the Van Dyke Houses campus, Van Dyke III added 180 affordable apartments in a twelve-story building.

Put together, those numbers describe a neighborhood of tenants. The Furman Center’s figures give a homeownership rate of 15.8% in 2024 against 32.7% citywide, and public housing makes up 21.3% of the rental homes in its Brownsville area. Furman’s area is wider than the census neighborhood (it counts about 111,000 people in 2024, where the 2010 census found 58,300 inside the narrower lines), but the direction is plain: in Brownsville the person who wants an alarm is usually not the person who owns the walls.

That changes what a good alarm looks like here. An owner can drill, run cable through a basement and leave a panel behind for the next buyer. A tenant needs the opposite: equipment that asks nothing of the building, stays within what the lease or the NYCHA Resident Handbook allows, and belongs to the household so it moves when they do. A Brownsville family that moves from a walk-up on Sutter Avenue to a new building on Livonia Avenue should be able to take its alarm the way it takes its television. That is the system this page is built around, with the owner’s and shopkeeper’s versions alongside.

We are not selling fear, and this page quotes no crime figures. Brownsville has had more than its share of people writing about it from the outside; the residents we work for want what anyone wants from an alarm: to know the door they locked is still shut, to hear about a problem while they are at work, to let a teenager in without handing over the master code, to protect a shop’s stock overnight. Those are ordinary needs, and they deserve equipment that fits an ordinary renter’s budget and lease.

The Renter’s Alarm

An Alarm That Moves Out When You Do: What Brownsville Tenants Can Install

Before choosing a single sensor, a tenant needs to know what they are allowed to put up. In Brownsville that question has three different answers depending on who holds the lease, and getting it wrong is the most common way a renter’s alarm turns into a dispute. Here is what the written rules say, and how we build inside them.

If NYCHA is your landlord

The NYCHA Resident Handbook (2024 edition) is direct about changes to an apartment: residents “are not allowed to alter their apartments or add any fixtures or fittings in their apartments without prior written consent from their property management office,” and they are responsible for any damage that follows. The same handbook makes one specific allowance for security equipment. A video camera or video doorbell “may be attached to your apartment door as long as it is wireless, affixed only with adhesive (and no holes or alterations are made to attach it),” and is used only for personal safety and security.

Alarm sensors are not named anywhere in the handbook. We read that carefully rather than generously. A wireless door contact held on with adhesive is built the same way as the doorbell camera the handbook permits: no holes, no wiring, nothing altered. But the handbook does not say so, and the consent rule covers “fixtures or fittings” in general. So for every NYCHA apartment we do two things. We build the system to the doorbell standard, with every part wireless, every part on adhesive or standing on a shelf, nothing attached to NYCHA’s locks, and nothing on the hallway side of the door. And we give you a short written request to send to your property management office before installation day, listing each part, how it attaches and how it comes off. Keep the answer with your lease.

The handbook also sets limits we design around. NYCHA fits the bottom lock on every apartment door and does not allow tenants to replace it, though a tenant may add a second lock and then becomes responsible for it. Window guards, where installed, stay as they are: the Attorney General’s tenants’ guide notes that a tenant must not take down, alter or remove any part of a guard. And NYCHA staff sometimes need to enter for repairs or safety checks. A good apartment alarm accommodates all three: it watches the door without touching either lock, covers guarded windows with a motion detector instead of a contact, and can be put in a maintenance mode with a temporary code for a scheduled visit.

If a private landlord holds the lease

Private leases vary, and many say nothing about alarms one way or the other. New York law gives tenants in multiple dwellings one clear right in this area: under Multiple Dwelling Law §51-c, as the Attorney General’s Residential Tenants’ Rights Guide explains, a tenant may install and maintain a lock of their own on the apartment entrance door in addition to the landlord’s, providing a duplicate key on request, and a lease clause charging extra for it is void. An alarm is not a lock, so §51-c does not decide whether you may put one up; the lease and your landlord do.

In practice, landlords rarely object to an adhesive wireless system when they are told about it first. We suggest a short email with the parts list and a photo of a sample sensor, sent before the install, and a copy of the reply saved with the lease. That one email answers the question that matters at the end of the tenancy, which is whether the equipment was allowed.

Moving out with your deposit intact

New York’s General Obligations Law §7-108 requires a landlord, within fourteen days after a tenant moves out, to give an itemized statement of any part of the security deposit kept back and to return the rest; nothing may be kept for ordinary wear and tear, and a landlord who misses the fourteen-day statement loses the right to keep any of it. For a renter with an alarm, that makes two habits worth the trouble: use mounting tape rated for painted surfaces, and remove it properly, warm and slow, not ripped. Then photograph every spot where a sensor was, with the date showing, before handing back the keys.

Why the equipment should belong to you

An alarm sold with a long monitoring agreement is often tied to the address: the company may own or finance the panel, the contract can run for years, and moving early means a fee or a transfer process. That model suits an owner who stays put. It suits a Brownsville renter badly, because tenants move: to a bigger apartment when a child arrives, into a new lottery building, closer to work. We sell the equipment outright, monitoring is optional from $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and the panel, sensors and keypad are yours. When you move, you take them, and a reinstall in the new apartment is a short visit rather than a new system.

What a renter kit contains, and what it never does

  • Contains: a wireless panel with a cellular communicator and a backup battery, which stands on a shelf or attaches with removable strips; contacts for the entry door and any reachable window without a guard; a pet-tolerant motion detector in a corner; a keypad or key fobs; an interior siren that plugs into an outlet; the app on as many phones as you choose.
  • Never: drills into walls, frames or doors; touches the landlord’s or NYCHA’s locks; mounts anything on the hallway side of your door; connects to the building’s intercom, buzzer, lobby doors or fire alarm; runs a wire through the wall.
  • Leaves behind at move-out: nothing, if the tape comes off as designed. We include spare adhesive pads for the reinstall.

Brownsville has an unusual range of buildings for one square mile: postwar towers, three-story public housing, rehabilitated tenements, semi-detached multi-family houses, Nehemiah rowhouses, older limestone and brownstone townhouses and brand-new affordable mid-rises. Two- and three-family houses

The semi-detached multi-unit houses south of Pitkin Avenue and on the side streets, where an owner may live on one floor and rent the others. Shared front door and stairs, separate apartment doors, a basement entrance and a rear yard: the classic case for one panel with two areas.

Nehemiah rowhouses

Attached homes with gardens, driveways and finished basements, most of them in the western half of the neighborhood. Newer frames and standard door sizes usually make contacts easy; the finished basement and its side door decide whether we wire or go wireless downstairs.

Older walk-ups and townhouses

Tenement buildings and the limestone and brownstone townhouses toward Ocean Hill. Thick masonry can weaken radio between rooms, so wireless devices are signal-tested where they will live, and owners can choose wired runs on the lower floors.

New affordable buildings

The Brownsville Plan sites, Van Dyke III, the Gail P. Duke Senior Residence and, when it opens, 366 Rockaway Avenue. New doors, new paint and a fresh lease: exactly when tenants most want nothing drilled. Many new buildings have their own lobby security; an apartment kit stays inside the apartment.

How the method changes from building to building

Signal before sticking. In the towers the question is whether the panel can reach the cellular network from the twelfth floor of a concrete building, and whether each sensor can reach the panel through interior walls. We test both with the panel held where it will sit, and move it before mounting anything. A panel that works on the kitchen counter and fails behind the bedroom door is not a working alarm.

Paint first, tape second. Adhesive holds to the paint, and the paint holds to the wall. On fresh paint in a new building we wait until it has cured, or we use a lighter pad and accept that a sensor may need re-sticking once. On many coats of old paint, we test the pad on an inconspicuous spot first. Every mounting spot is cleaned with alcohol before the pad goes on.

Windows with guards. A window guard is a safety device the landlord installs and the tenant must not alter. We never mount anything to it. If the window can still open behind the guard, a contact can sometimes go on the sash and frame; otherwise the room’s motion detector or a glass-break detector covers it.

Owners’ houses. In a two-family or a Nehemiah house, an owner can choose wired contacts on the basement and first-floor openings, run along the basement ceiling, with wireless devices upstairs. Wired devices never need batteries; wireless devices never need a hole. Most owners mix them.

Shared doors. Where two households share a front door, both areas include it, and the panel arms the shared door only when both households have armed. That prevents one family locking the other out of its own hallway.

Rules a Brownsville shop alarm has to meet

  • The siren must stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code §24-238 says an audible burglar alarm on a building must be capable of terminating, and must terminate, automatically within fifteen minutes of activation. We set every siren to cut off inside that limit; a shop under apartments should never ring all night.
  • The installer must be licensed. The New York Department of State licenses anyone who installs, services or maintains a security or fire alarm system to detect intrusion, break-in, movement, sound or fire; the licence runs for two years. Ask any installer for theirs before work starts; AESS works under New York State license #12000287431.
  • A burglar alarm is not a fire alarm. Some commercial buildings need a code-required fire alarm system with its own filings. That is a separate system and a separate job; a burglar alarm can add monitored smoke and heat detectors but does not replace it.
  • The facade belongs to the lease. Outside sirens, signs and anything on the gate or sign band need the landlord’s agreement, and a storefront alarm works perfectly well with everything inside.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Brownsville: Questions Answered

These are the questions Brownsville tenants, owners and shopkeepers bring to us, grouped by who is asking. Each is answered in full here. The FAQ lower down deals with separate practical matters such as payment, scheduling and batteries.

For NYCHA residents

The NYCHA handbook says no fixtures without written consent. What should our letter to the property management office actually say?

Keep it short and specific, because the person reading it has to decide quickly. Give your name, development, building and apartment number. Say you want to install a wireless, battery-backed burglar alarm for your own apartment. List the parts: a door contact on the inside of the apartment door, one or two window contacts, a motion detector, a keypad, a small panel that sits on a shelf, and a plug-in siren. Say how they attach, which is with adhesive pads and no holes, screws or wiring, and that nothing will touch the NYCHA-installed lock, the hallway side of the door, window guards, smoke or carbon monoxide detectors, or any building system. Say that everything comes off at move-out without damage, and ask for a written reply. We draft this for you with your parts list filled in; you sign and send it, and we schedule the installation once you have the answer.

The handbook allows an adhesive doorbell camera on the apartment door. Does that cover a stick-on door sensor too, or is that a separate request?

We treat it as a separate request. The handbook’s wording names “a video camera or video doorbell,” and an alarm contact is neither, even though it attaches the same way and is far smaller. Reading the allowance as if it covered alarms might be reasonable, but it is not what the handbook says, and an apartment alarm also includes parts inside the home, such as the motion detector and panel, that the door-camera sentence does not touch at all. Asking in writing costs one letter and a short wait; skipping the question can cost an argument at the end of a tenancy. If you already have a doorbell camera on the door, mention it in the letter, since it shows the property office the kind of adhesive mounting you mean.

NYCHA staff sometimes need to get into the apartment for repairs. How does the alarm deal with a scheduled visit?

The handbook expects a resident or someone aged eighteen or over to be home for scheduled repairs, so most visits happen with you there and the alarm simply disarmed. For the times you cannot be, the panel offers two tools. A temporary user code can be created in the app for one day and deleted afterward, so you never share your own code. And a “maintenance” schedule can keep the alarm disarmed during a set window while still sending you an alert each time the door opens. If an emergency entry happens while the system is armed, the siren will sound and you will be alerted; if you pay for monitoring, the central station will call you before anything else happens, so put the property office’s number in your notes so you can explain the situation quickly.

Our windows have child-safety guards. Can the alarm still protect them?

Yes, without touching the guard. A window guard is a safety device that must not be altered, so we never mount a sensor on it or remove a screw from it. If the sash can still be raised behind the guard, a small contact can go on the sash and frame on the room side. If not, the room is covered from the inside: a motion detector sees anyone who gets in, and a glass-break detector on the ceiling listens for the sound of breaking glass across the whole room. On upper floors of the towers, where the windows are well out of reach, we usually leave windows to the motion detector and spend the budget on the door.

For tenants of private landlords

Will the mounting tape pull paint off and cost us part of our security deposit when we leave?

Not if it is chosen and removed properly. We use mounting pads rated for painted surfaces, clean each spot with alcohol so the pad grips evenly, and avoid fresh paint until it has cured. At move-out the trick is heat and patience: warm the pad with a hair dryer for half a minute, then roll it off slowly or stretch the release tab rather than pulling the sensor straight away from the wall. Wipe the spot and photograph it with the date showing. Under General Obligations Law §7-108, a landlord must itemize anything kept from the deposit within fourteen days of move-out and may not charge for ordinary wear and tear; your dated photos are your record that the walls were left as you found them.

When the lease ends, what does moving the alarm to the next apartment involve, and what does it cost?

You take the panel, keypad, sensors and siren down yourself on moving day, following the sheet we leave with you, or we do it for you. In the new apartment, a technician puts everything back up on fresh pads, retests the panel’s cellular signal from its new spot, changes the codes, updates the address on any monitoring account, and adds sensors if the new place has more doors or reachable windows. Because the equipment is already yours, you pay only for that labor and any extra parts. On our Brooklyn list, labor to set up equipment a customer already owns is $200 to $400 depending on how many devices there are, which is the line that normally applies to a reinstall.

The front-door buzzer and the lobby belong to our landlord. Can our apartment alarm connect to them at all?

No, and it should not try. The building entrance, buzzer panel, lobby cameras and any fire alarm belong to the owner, and wiring a tenant’s system into them would be an alteration of the landlord’s equipment. Your alarm begins at your apartment door. What it can do is sit happily alongside them: if your buzzer handset is beside the door, the keypad goes on the other side of the frame, and if the building has a video intercom, your phone can carry both apps. If the building entrance itself is the problem, a broken door or a dead buzzer, that is a repair request to the landlord, and an owner who wants it fixed can call us about it separately.

For owners of Brownsville houses

Our house is one of the newer Nehemiah rowhouses. Is it easier or harder to alarm than the old frame houses on the side streets?

Usually easier. A newer attached house typically has square frames, standard door sizes and plain drywall, so contacts sit flush and any cable can be fished through a hollow wall without patching plaster. The finished basement decides the method downstairs: if the ceiling is drywall we use wireless devices there, and if part of it is open, around the boiler or the electrical panel, the basement windows and side door can be wired. An older frame two-family is more work: frames are out of square, there may be several layers of old paint on the trim, walls can hide old wiring, and the basement is often unfinished, which actually makes wiring the lower openings simple. Either way the price follows the number of openings, not the age of the house.

The tenant upstairs in our two-family changes every couple of years. How do we hand the alarm over cleanly each time?

Give the rental unit its own area on the panel from the start, and treat codes like keys. When a tenant leaves, delete their user code and their app login on the day the keys come back, check the event log for the rental area, and test the unit’s contacts during the turnover inspection. When the new tenant moves in, create their code and invite their phone to the app with access to their area only, so they can arm and disarm their home and see its history but never the owner’s. If the shared front door is on both areas, explain how it arms. We can do the changeover remotely for most panels, and it takes a few minutes.

For shops and small offices

Will a home alarm system do for a small store, or does a bodega need a commercial panel?

A very small shop can sometimes use a residential panel, but most stores outgrow it quickly. A commercial panel handles more zones, more user codes (one per employee, so you can see who opened and closed), separate areas for the sales floor and back room, schedules that arm automatically after closing, and opening and closing reports sent to your phone. Insurers sometimes ask for central-station monitoring and a certificate on a business policy, which a commercial setup is designed to give. Our Brooklyn list prices a small-business alarm from $1,500, usually $1,500 to $3,000, with eight to twelve door and window sensors in the commercial package.

Cash and stock sit in our back office. Can that one room stay armed while the store is open?

Yes. The back office becomes its own area with its own contact on the office door and, if it has one, a motion detector inside. Staff arm and disarm the sales floor at opening and closing as usual, but the office stays armed through the day and only the people who need it have a code that opens it. When the office door opens without that code, the panel alerts you or the monitoring station even while customers are in the store. A rear door that should never open during business hours can be set as a 24-hour zone in the same way, so it reports any time it opens.

For families

Our kids get home from school before we finish work. Can we know they arrived safely without giving them the master code?

Yes. Each child gets their own user code or a key fob, which can disarm the alarm but cannot change settings or add users. When that code is used, the app sends you a message naming who came in and when, so the alert itself tells you they are home. You can limit a child’s code to school-day afternoons, and if the door opens and nobody disarms within the entry delay, you hear about that too. If a code is shared with a friend, delete it and issue a new one in a minute from the app.

My mother lives with us and uses a walker. Can the alarm include a help button for her?

Yes. A help button worn on a lanyard or wrist, or one placed by the bed and the bathroom, pairs with the same panel. Pressing it can sound a chime in the home, send an alert to every phone on the account, and, if you pay for monitoring, signal the central station, which follows the instructions you give it for a medical call. We set the button so it works whether the alarm is armed or not, and we test it from the farthest corner of the apartment. A help button is not a medical device or a substitute for calling 911, and we say so plainly when we set it up.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Brownsville

We could not load Google’s AI Overview for Brownsville alarm searches from our build environment, so nothing below is a quotation from it. These are generalizations that keep turning up in quick online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Brownsville apartment, house or shop actually involves.

“Renters can’t really have an alarm system.”

This one is usually implied rather than said: alarm advice is written for homeowners, and renters are told to buy a camera and hope. In a neighborhood where most households rent, that leaves most people out.

A wireless system with a cellular panel and adhesive sensors is a real alarm. It detects a door opening, sounds a siren, alerts phones and can call a monitoring station, all without a hole in the wall.

The real limit for renters is permission, not technology. That is why our renter installs start with a written request to the landlord or NYCHA property office, not with a drill.

“Any adhesive sensor is fine anywhere.”

Consumer guides present stick-on sensors as consequence-free. In NYCHA housing, the Resident Handbook requires prior written consent for fixtures or fittings, and the only security devices it expressly allows on adhesive are a wireless camera or video doorbell on the apartment door.

An adhesive alarm contact is probably within the spirit of that allowance, but it is not in the text. A tenant who relies on a quick online answer and skips the letter is taking a risk the online answer will not share.

Ask first, in writing, and keep the reply. It costs nothing, and it protects the tenancy.

“Wireless means Wi-Fi.”

Many cheaper systems do depend on the home router, and quick answers often treat wireless and Wi-Fi as the same thing.

The wireless that matters in a Brownsville apartment is the radio between sensors and panel, and the cellular link from the panel to the outside world. A panel with its own cellular communicator keeps reporting when the internet bill lapses, the router is unplugged or the building’s service goes down.

If a kit can only report over Wi-Fi, we tell you before you buy anything else for it.

“A professional alarm costs thousands.”

National guides often quote whole-house figures and leave renters assuming a professional install is out of reach.

On our Brooklyn list, which Brownsville pays at the base rate, a basic installed system starts at $600, and a wireless system with four to six sensors, a motion detector, panel and keypad runs $600 to $1,200 all-in. Setting up a kit you already own is $200 to $400.

Larger figures exist, but they belong to houses and shops: complete home protection from $2,500, and a small-business alarm from $1,500.

“You’ll need to sign a monitoring contract.”

Quick answers tend to assume monitoring and equipment come bundled on a multi-year agreement, because many national packages are sold that way.

For a renter that is the wrong shape of deal. We sell the equipment outright, and monitoring is optional from $19.99 a month with no long-term contract. Many households self-monitor through the app and pay nothing monthly.

Owning the panel is also what makes the alarm portable. Nobody has to transfer a contract when you move across the neighborhood.

“Every window needs a sensor.”

Generic layouts put a contact on every window. In a tower apartment above the lower floors, most windows cannot be reached from outside, and many have guards that must not be altered.

One motion detector in the right corner covers the room behind those windows, and the money saved goes to the door, which is how almost everyone, invited or not, enters an apartment.

Ground-floor apartments and windows on fire escapes are different, and we cover those individually.

“Put a siren outside so the whole block hears it.”

Advice written for detached suburban houses favors an outdoor bell. In an apartment building it is not yours to mount, and on a shop it usually needs the landlord’s agreement.

The city also limits how long a building’s burglar alarm may sound: Administrative Code §24-238 requires it to cut off automatically within fifteen minutes. A siren that keeps going disturbs neighbors and achieves nothing more.

An interior siren drives an intruder out and wakes the household; the phone alert and, if you choose, a monitoring call bring help.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Peel Test Comes First

This describes how we work in rented apartments generally, not any particular customer’s home. Before a single sensor goes up in a rental, the technician does a peel test. One mounting pad goes on a hidden spot, inside a closet door frame or behind where the keypad will sit, and stays there while the panel’s signal is checked around the apartment. At the end of the visit, before anything else is mounted for good, that pad comes off with warmth and a slow roll. If the paint comes with it, we know before it matters, and we change the plan: a lighter pad, a shelf-standing panel, a sensor on the door rather than the frame.

The second habit is the photo set. Every spot where a device goes up is photographed before and after, and the pictures go to the customer with the hand-over sheet. When a family moves out two years later, the before pictures show the wall as it was on the day we arrived, which is exactly the comparison a deposit dispute turns on. It takes five minutes during the install and saves arguments that can take weeks.

The third is to leave the building alone. We do not touch the door lock, the peephole, the window guards, the smoke detector the landlord installed, or anything on the hallway side of the door. When a resident asks whether the alarm could also ring the super or connect to the intercom, the answer is no, and we explain why: the moment a tenant’s equipment reaches into the building’s, the tenant has made an alteration the lease probably forbids.

Every figure, date, rule and place on this page was checked against the sources below in October 2026. The prices come from the AESS Brooklyn price list, and the descriptions of how we survey, mount, program and remove equipment are our working practice, not claims about any particular home or business.

  • NYCHA, Public Housing Resident Handbook (2024) — no alterations, fixtures or fittings without prior written consent from the property management office; wireless video camera or video doorbell allowed on the apartment door if affixed only with adhesive; bottom lock not to be replaced, an additional lock allowed; an adult present for scheduled repairs
  • New York Attorney General, Residential Tenants’ Rights Guide — tenants’ right to add their own lock under Multiple Dwelling Law §51-c; window guards not to be removed or altered by the tenant
  • New York General Obligations Law §7-108 and the NYC Rent Guidelines Board security deposit FAQ — itemized statement and return of the deposit within fourteen days of move-out; no deduction for ordinary wear and tear
  • NYU Furman Center, Brownsville neighborhood profile (updated May 6, 2026) — homeownership 15.8% in 2024 versus 32.7% citywide; public housing 21.3% of rental units in 2025; 5,559 homes added 2010–2025, 4,142 income-restricted
  • Wikipedia, “Brownsville, Brooklyn” — boundaries, area, 2010 population, ZIP codes and community district; 1858 lots and the 1866 sale to Charles S. Brown; Pitkin Avenue’s 372 stores in 1942; NYCHA developments and RAD/PACT conversions; Nehemiah and older housing; Betsy Head Park, Zion Triangle, Loews Pitkin, Stone Avenue Library, Brownsville Recreation Center; subway and bus routes; bodega-to-supermarket ratio from the city health profile
  • NYC HPD, Brownsville and city announcement of July 26, 2018 — the Brownsville Plan’s 2,500-plus affordable homes; Livonia Avenue sites; Osborn Plaza on the Belmont Avenue corridor
  • New York YIMBY (September 12, 2025) — 366 Rockaway Avenue: nine stories, 283 affordable rentals, an arts center, completion expected by December 2027
  • Catholic Charities Brooklyn and Queens (October 9, 2026) — Gail P. Duke Senior Residence, 141 homes with supportive services · Trinity Financial, Van Dyke III — twelve-story affordable building on the Van Dyke Houses campus
  • NYC Small Business Services, BID directory: Pitkin Avenue — established October 1993; 32 block faces; 212 ground-floor retail businesses · Institute for Sustainable Infrastructure, Osborn Street Plaza — $2.3 million plaza south of Belmont Avenue
  • NYC Administrative Code §24-238 — a building’s audible burglar alarm must stop automatically within fifteen minutes · New York Department of State, Security or Fire Alarm Installer — who must be licensed; two-year term

Two cautions. The Furman Center’s Brownsville area is larger than the neighborhood as Wikipedia defines it (Furman counts about 111,000 people in 2024; the 2010 census counted 58,300 inside the narrower lines), so its percentages describe the wider district. And nothing on this page is legal advice: the handbook, your lease and your landlord’s written answer decide what you may install, and a tenant with a dispute should contact a tenant advocate or the Attorney General’s office.

Some sources were out of reach. Our build environment could not open Google’s People Also Ask, related searches or AI Overview, Answer the Public, Bing or DuckDuckGo suggestions, or Yelp reviews.
