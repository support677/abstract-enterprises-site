Why Prospect Lefferts Gardens Properties Need Security Camera Installation

In Prospect Lefferts Gardens, the moments people most want on video tend to happen behind a door that is already closed. The neighborhood sits east of Prospect Park, between Empire Boulevard and Clarkson Avenue, running from Ocean Avenue across to New York Avenue, in Community District 9 and ZIP code 11225. Its blocks mix the single-family brick and stone houses of Lefferts Manor, attached row houses on the surrounding streets, prewar elevator buildings along Ocean Avenue and Flatbush Avenue, six-story buildings on the side streets, and a handful of towers built since 2008. Many people here come home through a shared street door and lobby rather than their own front door, and that changes where a camera does its best work.

The record from the last decade keeps pointing inward. In August 2024, ABC7 New York reported that a man followed a 25-year-old woman into the lobby of her building near Ocean and Parkside Avenues around 11 p.m., stood at the elevator while she walked to the mailboxes, then grabbed her around the neck before she kicked free and he walked out. Houses and walk-ups have a different weak side: the back. Inside the Building

From the Street Door to the Bike Room: Where Cameras Belong in a Prospect Lefferts Gardens Apartment Building

Most camera quotes start outside, with a bullet camera over the entrance aimed at the sidewalk. In a Prospect Lefferts Gardens apartment building, that camera records a lot of people walking past and very little of what tenants actually ask about. The 2024 lobby attack and the 2020 bike room thefts both happened after the person was already through the street door. Below is how we break a building into the places a stranger has to pass, and what each camera has to show there.

The vestibule: the gap between two doors

Many prewar buildings on Ocean Avenue, Flatbush Avenue and the side streets have a small vestibule between the street door and an inner lobby door, with the intercom panel on the wall. That space is where tailgating happens: someone waits for a resident to buzz through and follows them in. A camera mounted inside the vestibule, high on the wall opposite the inner door and aimed back toward the street door, shows the face of each person as they step in and turn toward the intercom. Because the vestibule is small and evenly lit, that camera can be set for face detail rather than a wide view, and it can be paired with the intercom so the person answering upstairs sees the same face.

The inner door and the elevator wait

In the 2024 case near Ocean and Parkside, the man pressed the elevator button and waited while the woman went to the mailboxes. A lobby camera set back from the inner door, covering the door, the elevator call button and the path between them, records that kind of loitering from the moment someone comes in. We aim it so a person standing at the elevator is framed from the front rather than from directly above, because a ceiling view of the top of a hat tells detectives very little. In larger lobbies we add a second camera at the far end looking back toward the entrance, so nobody can stand in a spot the first camera misses.

The mailbox wall and the package spot

Mailboxes and the corner where couriers leave packages are the lobby's busiest spot and its most common complaint. A camera aimed along the mailbox wall from one end, rather than straight at it, shows who opens which bank of boxes without recording anyone's mail. For packages, the camera should cover the floor or shelf where they sit and the route to the door, so a clip shows the box being picked up and carried out, not just someone walking through. Newer buildings in the neighborhood list package rooms; 185 Ocean Avenue, an eight-story condo from 2008, advertises one, and The Parkline at 626 Flatbush Avenue lists a package room with its doorman. A package room door gets its own camera at face height.

The bike room, the laundry and the basement doors

The Hawthorne Street thief kept coming back to the bike rooms, and residents' new locks did not stop him. A camera cannot stop a lock pick either, but it can make the second and third visit pointless. Inside the bike room, one camera aimed at the door from the back wall shows the face of anyone who opens it, and a second aimed along the racks shows what is taken. On the outside of the bike room door, a camera in the basement corridor shows how the person reached it: from the lobby stairs, the boiler room, the rear yard door or a cellar entrance from the sidewalk. We set those cameras to send an alert to the super or the managing agent when motion is detected in the bike room at hours nobody normally goes down there.

Stairwells, the roof door and the rear yard

In a six-story walk-up or elevator building, the stairs connect every floor to the roof and the cellar. A camera at the top landing covering the roof door, and one at the cellar landing, record anyone using the building as a route rather than a home. The rear yard of an apartment building, often a narrow concrete strip with a fence and the fire escapes above it, gets a camera on the back wall looking down the yard, set to show anyone who comes over the fence or starts up a fire escape. Inside hallways, we keep each camera on the corridor and the stair door, and never aimed into an apartment when its door opens.

Where the recorder goes, given what happened on Flatbush Avenue

When the 2016 burglars at 723 Flatbush Avenue took the store's surveillance hard drive, they also took the evidence. The same risk applies to a building recorder sitting on a shelf in an unlocked basement room. We put the recorder in a locked cabinet or a closet the public cannot reach, away from the doors the cameras watch, with battery backup, and we can set it to copy event clips off-site or to a second device, so losing the box does not mean losing the footage.

Covenant houses of Lefferts Manor: deep lots and a cellar route

James Lefferts divided his family's land into 600 lots in 1893 and wrote deed restrictions requiring a single-family brick or stone house of at least two stories, a covenant the Lefferts Manor Association, founded in 1919, still enforces. For a camera plan, that means one household, solid masonry walls, a cellar under the whole house, and a rear yard behind it that the street cannot see. The cellar is the cable highway. We drill each outside camera's hole from the inside where possible, through plain brick at a mortar joint, so the cable comes straight out behind the camera base and nothing runs across the face of the house. Interior runs climb through closets or alongside existing pipes to reach upper-floor rear windows. The rear yard camera, mounted high on the back wall, looks out across the yard to the back fence. Because the whole Manor sits inside the 1979 historic district, the front of these houses follows the Commission's rules, covered in the next section.

Row houses beyond the Manor: two or three households, one stoop

Off the Manor blocks, many attached houses have been divided into two or three apartments, often with the owner on the lower floors. The front door and stoop are shared, the yard may belong to the garden apartment or the owner, and a basement entrance under the stoop is common. Cameras on shared doors are the owner's responsibility and the owner's decision, but each household should know what is recorded and who can see it. We often set the recorder up with separate viewing accounts, so the owner sees everything, a tenant sees the shared front door and nothing else, and nobody's own entrance is aimed at by a camera they cannot see. Shared walls on both sides mean the exposure is front and back only, which keeps the camera count low.

Prewar elevator apartments: thick walls and long runs

A building like 115 Ocean Avenue, six stories and 90 units built in 1925, has plaster on masonry, a cellar that runs the length of the building, and a lobby that may still have its original marble or terrazzo. That stone is the reason we avoid drilling lobby walls where an alternative exists. Cable usually rises from the cellar through a pipe chase, an old dumbwaiter shaft or a service closet, then crosses the lobby ceiling. Where it truly cannot be hidden, we use painted metal raceway along a cornice line rather than cutting into original finishes. Runs to the top-floor landing and roof door can exceed what a single cable segment should carry for a power-over-ethernet camera, so long risers sometimes need a small switch partway up, housed in a locked box. We label every run at both ends, because the next super will need the map.

Six-story side-street buildings: fire escapes, bike rooms and a rear yard

The Hawthorne Street buildings in the 2020 thefts were six stories, and the side streets between Flatbush and Nostrand Avenues are full of buildings that size. They share a layout that shapes the camera plan: a street door and small lobby, stairs that run from cellar to roof, apartments with rear windows over a narrow yard, fire escapes on the back or front, and a basement with a boiler room, storage, laundry and often a bike room. That is a lot of doors for one super to watch. A typical plan covers the vestibule, the lobby, the cellar corridor, the bike room, the roof door and the rear yard, which is six to eight cameras before a single hallway is added. We size the recorder and switch for hallways later, so a board or owner can phase the work.

Towers and newer condos: work with the systems already there

The newer buildings arrived with their own security. The Parkline lists a doorman, concierge, package room, bike room and garage; Hello Lenox lists private keyed elevator access to each apartment; 185 Ocean Avenue lists a package room but no doorman. In these buildings, the developer usually installed cameras at the main doors, and a board or management company runs them. Our work is usually filling gaps the original system left, such as a garage ramp, a bike room, a roof terrace door or a package room, and connecting new cameras to the existing recorder where the brand allows. Unit owners who want a camera at their own door need the board's approval first, and a camera inside a private unit is the owner's alone.

Chester Court and Ocean on the Park: small districts with their own designations

Chester Court, designated in 2014, is a cul-de-sac of 18 Tudor Revival townhouses off Flatbush Avenue near Rutland Road. Ocean on the Park, designated in 2009, is a row of 12 townhouses facing the park. Both are landmarked separately from the 1979 district, and both have fronts where a visible camera or a wire would stand out. Half-timbering, stucco and leaded windows on Chester Court mean the mounting surface matters as much as the camera: we look for plain masonry or wood trim, never a decorative surface. Because the cul-de-sac has one way in from Flatbush Avenue, a single well-placed camera can show every arrival, which is a better plan than several cameras on each front.

Historic District

Cameras in the Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District: What the Commission's Rules Mean on the Street Front

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District on October 9, 1979, covering 63.54 acres that take in the Lefferts Manor blocks. The Lefferts Manor Association's own summary is direct: the designation "applies to the exterior of the buildings," and "any proposed alterations to the outside of homes must receive approval from the Commission before work begins." Security cameras on the outside of a district house fall under that. The good news is that the Commission's Permit Guidebook treats cameras as routine work its staff can approve when the installation meets a short list of criteria, laid out in chapter 11, on health, safety and utility equipment.

The count: two cameras for every 25 feet of frontage

The guidebook says cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and sets a number. On small residential or commercial buildings, no more than two security cameras can go on each 25 feet of street frontage; large residential, commercial and institutional buildings can have up to three. Most Manor houses are narrow, so in practice that means two cameras on the front at most, and often one is enough: a small camera at the door and another covering the stoop and sidewalk, or one camera that does both. That limit is one more reason we spend the camera budget on the back of the house, where the 2014 burglaries came in.

Where a camera can sit, and what it can be fastened to

The guidebook's general rule is that equipment should not go "on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists," and when it must, it should be discreet and keep clear of significant architectural features. It also says equipment "must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." On a Manor house that usually rules out carved stone, lintels and ornamental ironwork, and points us to plain brick at a joint, the underside of a stoop, or wood trim inside a doorway. Doorbells and light fixtures have their own rule: they go within or next to the door opening, with no more than two light fixtures and one intercom panel per door.

Hiding the wire

The strictest part of the guidance concerns conduit. It "must be concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding behind the installation." Where that is truly impossible, staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match the background and fixed into mortar joints. That suits how we wire a masonry house anyway: drill straight through the wall behind the camera base from the cellar or a closet, so the cable never shows on the outside. On a district house, we make that the plan from the start rather than a fix after the fact.

Which cameras look right, and what about the back

The guidebook shows dome-type cameras finished to match the surrounding materials, and asks that equipment be as small as possible and painted to blend in. On brick or brownstone, that points to a small dome or turret in a color close to the masonry or trim, rather than a large bullet. For the rear of the house, the yards chapter of the guidebook sends cameras and light fixtures back to the same chapter 11 rules, and the Commission often treats work that cannot be seen from a public street differently from work that can. We tell owners plainly which of their planned cameras sit on a street-facing wall, help prepare the photos and product sheets for the Commission's application, and do not mount anything on a district front before the permit is in hand.

Field Notes: Following a Resident's Route Home, From the Sidewalk to the Fourth Floor

This describes how we approach a building here, not one particular job. On a Prospect Lefferts Gardens apartment building, we start the walk-through at the curb and ask the super or a tenant to come home with us, the way they do every night. We stand where they stand when they reach for their keys, note where the light falls, and watch which way the street door swings. Then we go through the vestibule, wait at the intercom panel, pass the inner door and stop at the mailboxes. At each stop we ask the same question: if someone were standing behind you here, which camera would show his face? Then we take the stairs or the elevator up a few floors, and back down to the cellar, the bike room and the rear yard door.

Two things come out of that walk almost every time. The old camera over the street door, if there is one, points at the sidewalk and shows the backs of residents' heads as they walk in. And the cellar, where bikes, storage and laundry are, has either no camera or one that was never set for low light. The fix is rarely more cameras outside. It is moving the face camera to the inside of the vestibule, adding one at the mailboxes and one in the cellar, and setting each recorder channel so the super can find last night's motion events in a minute.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Prospect Lefferts Gardens: Questions Answered

In the 2024 case near Ocean and Parkside Avenues, the man waited at the elevator before he grabbed the woman. Where should our lobby camera be so it shows the face of someone standing there?

Across the lobby from the elevator, at a little above head height, aimed at the elevator doors and the call button. Someone waiting for an elevator faces the doors, so a camera behind him shows only his back, and a camera directly overhead shows the top of his head. Mounted on the far wall, or beside the mailboxes looking back toward the elevator, the camera catches him face on while he waits and again when he turns. In the 2024 case, the building's cameras did record the approach and the struggle, which is what gave police a clip to release; the aim is to make sure your building's camera would show a usable face as well.

Our building on Hawthorne Street lost bikes even after the lock was changed. Can a camera in the bike room wake the super at 3 a.m.?

It can send an alert to the super's phone, the managing agent's phone, or both, whenever it sees a person in the bike room during hours you choose. We use person detection rather than plain motion, so a light flicking on or a cat does not set it off, and we keep the alert hours to the overnight window when nobody normally goes down. In the 2020 thefts, the man came back four times in 48 hours; an alert on the first return is the point where someone can call 911 while he is still in the building, rather than finding the empty rack in the morning.

The 2014 burglaries came in from fire escapes, mostly in daytime. How does a rear camera watch the fire escape without seeing into the tenants' windows?

It watches the bottom of the fire escape and the yard, not the windows. A camera on the rear wall at the second-floor level, aimed down and along the yard, shows anyone who climbs the fence, pulls down the drop ladder or starts up the first flight. That is where every fire escape burglary has to begin. The recorder's privacy masks then black out each window that falls inside the frame, so the camera records the iron and the yard but never a room. For daylight hours, when most of those 2014 break-ins happened, the camera sends an alert only for a person on the lower flight, not for every pigeon on the rail.

Our restaurant on Flatbush Avenue has a back window over the yard next to the kitchen door. Can one camera cover both?

Often, yes, if the window and the door are on the same wall and close together. A camera mounted inside the kitchen, high in the corner opposite both, shows a face coming through either one. If they are on different walls or far apart, two cameras are better than one wide view, because a wide lens that takes in both makes each face too small to identify. Outside, a camera on the yard shows how the person got there. The 2016 Flatbush Avenue burglaries came through back doors and a back window, so this is usually the first pair of cameras we would put in a restaurant here.

Our Lefferts Manor house has a stone stoop and an original door surround. Can a video doorbell go on the frame, and does it need a Landmarks permit?

A doorbell on the outside of a district house is an exterior change, so it goes through the Commission, though staff can approve it when it meets the guidebook's rules. Those rules say doorbells, light fixtures and intercom panels go within or next to the door opening, and that equipment must be fastened at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through a decorative surface. In practice, the doorbell goes on plain wood inside the door opening rather than on the carved surround, wired from inside. We prepare the photos and product sheet for the application and mount it once the permit is issued.

I rent on Ocean Avenue and the landlord will not add cameras to our floor. Can I put one on my own apartment door?

Ask the landlord in writing first. Brick Underground's answer to this question, citing attorney Jeffrey Reich, was that a renter cannot install equipment without the landlord's permission, and that with permission a common-area camera has to be set up so it cannot film inside any apartment. A camera inside your own apartment, looking at the back of your own door, is a different matter and leaves the hallway alone. If the landlord agrees to a door camera, we mount it to cover your doorway only, use a peephole-style or small door camera that leaves no damage, and remove it when you move.

Our co-op board wants cameras in every hallway. How do we keep a camera from seeing into an apartment when its door opens?

By mounting each camera at the end of the corridor aimed down its length, not across from a door, and by masking any doorway that falls in the frame. From the end of a hallway, a camera sees people walking between the elevator, the stairs and the doors, but an open door shows only its edge, not the room behind it. Where a corridor bends or a door sits right in the line of view, the recorder blacks out that part of the image permanently. We give the board a frame-by-frame screenshot of every hallway camera before the system goes live, so shareholders can see exactly what is recorded.

We own a three-family row house off Lincoln Road, and the upstairs tenant wants to see who rings the front door. Can she see just that one camera?

Yes. The recorder can have a separate login for each household, and each login can be limited to certain cameras. The upstairs tenant gets the front door and stoop camera on her phone and nothing else. The owner keeps full access to every camera, including the rear yard and the basement. That way nobody can watch the yard of the garden apartment or the owner's own entrance, and the tenant still sees her deliveries and visitors. If she moves out, her login is deleted in a minute.

Cars on our block near Parkside and Nostrand get broken into. Can a camera on our house read plates across the street at night?

Sometimes, but not with the same camera that watches your stoop. Reading a plate at night across a street takes a narrow lens zoomed onto one stretch of curb, set to a fast shutter, and positioned so headlights do not blind it. That camera shows little else, so it is an addition, not a replacement. For most houses, a better aim is a camera that clearly shows the cars directly in front of your house and the people walking up to them; the face of someone trying door handles is usually more useful to police than a plate on a car that may itself be stolen.

Our building's old cameras run on coax that went in years ago. Do we have to tear out all the old cable to get newer cameras?

Not always. If the old coax is in good shape and runs to the right places, high-definition analog cameras and a matching recorder can reuse it, which saves the cost and disruption of a new pull. If you want network cameras with power over one cable, the coax can sometimes carry the signal with adapters at each end, though new cable is usually the more reliable choice. We test each old run first, and the quote shows both options side by side, so a board can weigh the price against the picture.

The Chester Court cul-de-sac has one way in from Flatbush Avenue. Could the homeowners share one camera at the entrance?

They could, if they agree in writing who owns it, who pays for it and who can see the footage. One camera at the mouth of the court, on a house whose owner is willing to host it and inside the Commission's rules, shows every person and car that enters. The recording sits on that owner's recorder, with viewing logins for each participating house. It is the same idea as a block association's shared list of cameras, made more formal. Each house still covers its own door and back separately.

Detectives from the 71st Precinct asked for our lobby footage. What is the fastest way to give them a clip without handing over the whole recorder?

Export only the time window they ask for, to a USB stick or a download link, in the recorder's own format and a common video format such as MP4. Write down the camera name, the date and the start and end times, and note whether the recorder's clock is correct. We set every system's clock to a time server so that the time on the clip matches real time, and we practice an export with you at handover so this takes minutes. You do not need to give anyone the recorder itself unless a court order requires it.

We run a day care on a Rogers Avenue block with a late-night business nearby. How long should the entrance camera keep recordings?

Long enough to answer a question that comes up a week or two later. For a day care, we usually suggest at least a month, because a parent's concern or an incident report often surfaces after the fact. On a standard four-camera system, a month of storage is $180 on our price list, against two weeks included. The camera covers the entrance and the sidewalk in front of it, framed so the neighbor's door is outside the view or masked, and access to the recordings is limited to the director and whoever she names.

Recordings from a camera you own are yours. Detectives from the precinct

After an incident on the block, detectives from the 71st Precinct usually ask in person, and in 2014 the precinct's community policing unit asked residents to share videos and photos of anything unusual during the fire escape burglaries. With a recorder we install, you export the clip yourself, you decide what to include, and nothing goes out automatically.

Landlords, managing agents and tenants

In a rental building, the landlord owns the lobby, hallway and cellar cameras and decides who sees them, usually the managing agent and the super. Tenants generally do not get live access to building cameras, but when something happens, a written request to management is the usual route to a clip. We set up separate logins so the super can export without full administrator rights, and we give the owner a short written procedure for handling tenant requests.

Co-op and condo boards

In the neighborhood's co-ops and condos, common-area cameras are a board decision, and so is the policy on who may review footage. We recommend that the board put that policy in writing before the system goes live: who holds the administrator login, who can export, how long footage is kept, and how a shareholder asks for a clip. A board that changes over every year or two needs that written down more than anyone.

The neighbors behind you

Rear yards here back onto one another, and a camera on the back wall can easily see into two or three other yards. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives the owner or tenant of a home the right to sue someone who installs a camera on neighboring property to record activity in their backyard without written consent, with intent to harass, annoy, alarm or threaten. We frame every rear camera on your own yard, fence and windows, mask anything beyond the property line, and show you the masked view before we leave.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Prospect Lefferts Gardens

The claims below turn up again and again in AI-written answers, cost guides and directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Thumbtack when people ask about cameras for a Brooklyn building, house or store. Where a figure comes from a named source, we say so. Each is followed by what actually holds in Prospect Lefferts Gardens.

"Start with a camera over the front entrance, pointed at the street."

For a detached suburban house with a driveway, that is a sensible first camera. For a Prospect Lefferts Gardens apartment building, it is usually the least useful one. A camera above the street door looks down at the tops of heads and the backs of residents walking in, and it shows the sidewalk far more than the doorway.

The incidents on record here happened past that door: at the elevator in the 2024 lobby attack, in the bike rooms on Hawthorne Street in 2020, on the fire escapes in 2014. None of them would have been solved by a better view of the sidewalk.

Our first cameras in a building are inside the vestibule, at the inner door and elevator, and at the mailbox wall. The outside camera comes after, framed on the doorway rather than the street.

"Camera installation in Brooklyn costs $680 to $2,145."

That range is real: HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page lists an average of $1,416 and a typical range of $680 to $2,145 for camera installation and repair, and Thumbtack's Brooklyn page says anywhere from $100 for a single camera line to $1,000 to $2,200 for an entire system. Those are reasonable figures for a house.

They say little about a six-story building that needs a vestibule, a lobby, a cellar, a bike room, a roof door and a rear yard covered, with long risers through prewar masonry. That job is a commercial-scale system even though it is a residential building, and it is priced like one.

Our four-camera packages start at $850 on the Brooklyn rate, with 4K packages from $1,292, and building and store systems usually run $1,500 to $5,000 or more. The written quote shows each line, so you can see what the building itself adds.

"Wireless cameras are the easy choice for apartments and old houses."

Easy to buy, yes. Easy to keep working in a prewar building, rarely. Thick masonry walls, plaster on metal lath, and floors of concrete and tile stop Wi-Fi signals well before they reach a cellar or a rear yard. A Brooklyn brownstone owner on the Brownstoner forum put it simply: "With these old brick buildings, even the best wireless systems are spotty."

Another poster in the same thread made the second point: a so-called wireless camera still needs a cable for power, so the hard part of the job does not go away.

We run cable to every camera that matters, so power and picture arrive over one wire. Battery cameras still have a place, such as a renter's door with no outlet, but not as the backbone of a building system.

"You cannot put cameras on a house in a historic district."

You can. The Commission's guidebook lets its staff approve security cameras that meet a short set of rules, and the Prospect Lefferts Gardens Historic District, Chester Court and Ocean on the Park all fall under the same guidebook.

What the rules do require is restraint: no more than two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on a small building, equipment fastened at mortar joints or plain surfaces rather than decorative ones, and conduit hidden behind the installation. The Lefferts Manor Association reminds owners that exterior changes need the Commission's approval before work begins.

So the right answer is not "no cameras" but "fewer, smaller cameras on the front, wired from inside, with the permit first," and as many cameras on the back as the house needs.

"A camera will stop package theft."

A visible camera may make some thieves think twice. It did not stop the man on Hawthorne Street in 2020, who kept returning even after residents had footage of him at work and had changed the locks. One resident described him as "very cavalier coming at random hours and not someone afraid of being detected."

What a camera does well is record the face, the time and the route, and send an alert while someone is still inside. That is what lets a super or a neighbor call it in during the second visit instead of the fifth.

The rest of the answer is physical: a locked package room or lockers, a self-closing street door that latches, and a bike room door that cannot be slipped. We point those out when we see them, because cameras work best alongside them.

"Cloud storage is safer than a recorder in the building."

Cloud storage protects against one real risk: someone taking the recorder, as the 2016 burglars did with the hard drive at the Flatbush Avenue pharmacy. It also comes with a monthly fee per camera, and it depends on the building's upload speed, which in many older buildings is the weakest link.

A recorder in the building keeps continuous footage from every camera, at full quality, with no subscription, and it keeps recording when the internet goes down.

The answer for most Prospect Lefferts Gardens buildings and stores is both: a recorder locked away from the doors it watches, with battery backup, plus event clips copied off-site. That covers theft of the box without paying to stream every camera all day.

"Crime is high in Brooklyn, so you need cameras everywhere."

The numbers for this precinct point the other way. Through November 1, 2026, the 71st Precinct recorded 64 burglaries against 71 the year before, robberies fell from 114 to 93, and felony assaults fell from 356 to 304. Burglary for all of 2025 was down 82.8 percent from 2001.

Some categories are up. Grand larceny rose 14.6 percent for the year, and car thefts jumped in the most recent four weeks. Those are specific risks with specific camera answers, not a reason to blanket a building.

We would rather put six cameras where they answer real questions than sixteen where they record hallways nobody will ever review. The plan follows the doors, the cellar, the back and the counter, not the fear.
