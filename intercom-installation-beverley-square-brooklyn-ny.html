Intercom installation in Beverley Square is mostly a job about big houses and long distances inside them. The Neighborhood

Why Beverley Square Properties Need Intercom Installation

Beverley Square is really two small neighborhoods. According to Wikipedia's history of the Beverley Squares, both run from Beverley Road in the north to Cortelyou Road in the south, and the open cut of the BMT Brighton Line, between Marlborough Road and East 16th Street, divides them. Beverley Square East runs from the cut to East 19th Street; Beverley Square West runs from the cut to Argyle Road. Ackerson began the East side in 1898 with large houses along East 19th Street and bought Catherine Lott's farm for the West side in 1901. When the West side was designated a city historic district on November 25, 2025, the City Planning Commission's report counted 118 free-standing houses on lots about 50 feet wide, 114 of them built by 1906, most with full-width front porches, angled bays and complicated rooflines. The houses were built for one prosperous household each, with servants' stairs, back kitchens and bedrooms spread over two upper floors.

That plan is exactly why intercoms matter here. A house of this size and age was designed for a world where someone was always near the front door. Today the person nearest the door may be two floors up on a video call, in the back kitchen with a mixer running, or down in the cellar with the laundry. A single bell in the front hall, which is what many of these houses still have, does not carry through plaster walls, closed doors and three stories of stairs. The 1-800-Doorbell guidance on wireless chimes puts the loss at about 20 percent of the signal for each wall a signal passes through, and more for masonry, so a plug-in chime on the third floor is often unreliable too. A real intercom solves a different problem from a doorbell: it puts a station where each person spends time, lets them see and talk to the visitor from there, and lets the people inside call one another without shouting up the stairwell.

The second reason is that the houses are being used differently. The zoning across the district, R3X according to the City Planning Commission, allows one- or two-family detached houses, and many owners have turned the upper floors or the garden level into a second home for a relative or a tenant. Two households behind one porch need two buttons, two sets of indoor stations and a clear rule about which household can open which door. At the north and south edges of the neighborhood, along Beverley Road and near Cortelyou Road, there are walk-ups and apartment buildings as well, including a six-floor walk-up at 1915 Beverley Road that a former tenant says was completed in 2022. In buildings like that, state law and the city's housing code put a working intercom on the owner's list of obligations, and tenants' reviews show what happens when it fails.

Security pressure comes from the same direction. In 2018, Bklyner reported that residents of one building near Cortelyou and Stratford Roads said more than 100 packages had gone missing over six months, and a resident, Eddie Shiomi, told the outlet, "There's just no accountability within the whole delivery pipeline." The building's answer was a camera in the mail area. An intercom plan addresses the earlier step: who gets through the door in the first place, who let them in, and where the packages are left once they are inside.

Six Beverley Square facts that change how an intercom is planned

  • The houses stand alone. Free-standing houses on wide lots mean four exterior walls, side yards and often a side driveway, so a visitor may reach the porch, the side door, the driveway gate or a garage at the back. The Brownstoner write-up of 340 East 18th Street, a Colonial Revival house dating to about 1901 or 1902, describes a front porch, a rear yard, a driveway along the side and seven bedrooms on the upper two floors.
  • The porches put distance between the sidewalk and the door. Most West-side houses have full-width front porches, so the door station sits under a roof, well back from the street, and the front walk and steps come first. That helps the station last and changes what a camera sees.
  • The West side is now a historic district. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Beverley Square West Historic District, LP-2692, on November 25, 2025, covering the houses on Marlborough, Rugby and Argyle Roads between Beverley and Cortelyou Roads. Its permit rules for intercom panels now apply to those facades.
  • The East side is not part of that district. The houses from the cut to East 19th Street, including those that Lewis H. Pounds and Delbert H. Decker built after the first East-side sales were disappointing, sit outside the 2025 designation, so a panel there does not go through the LPC.
  • Two households are allowed, three are not. The R3X zoning described in the City Planning Commission report allows single- or two-family detached houses, which means many houses here have, or will have, exactly two households sharing one front door.
  • The apartment buildings sit at the edges. Walk-ups and apartment buildings line parts of Beverley Road and the streets near Cortelyou Road. A building with eight or more apartments that went up after 1968 must have an intercom under New York Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, and that includes recent construction.

Repair Calls in Beverley Square

Need Repair in Beverley Square? A Third Floor That Can't Hear the Porch, an Old Master Station That Hums, or a Building Panel That Keeps Dying

Repair calls from Beverley Square tend to follow three patterns. The first is the house where the porch station still works at the door but the stations upstairs have gone quiet one at a time. In a house this age that usually means a run that was spliced in the cellar decades ago, a connection loosened when a wall was opened, or a station that someone replaced with a model the old system never supported. Brick Underground's NYC intercom repair guide makes the same point about apartment systems, quoting Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith: "It's all in the wire connections." The fault is far more often at a splice, a terminal or the power supply than somewhere in the middle of a wall, so the first visit is spent tracing, not tearing out.

The second pattern is the old room-to-room system that came with the house: a master station in the kitchen or front hall, speakers in several rooms, sometimes a radio built in, often installed in the 1960s or 1970s and now humming, crackling or dead. Some of its wire can serve a new system if it tests clean; the master station itself almost never can. We tell you which runs are worth keeping and which rooms need new cable, and we price both options so you can choose.

The third is the apartment building on Beverley Road or near Cortelyou Road whose panel works for a week, then fails again. One former tenant of 1915 Beverley Road wrote on openigloo that "the intercom consistently breaks down" in a building completed only a few years ago. In newer buildings, repeated failure is usually a power, network or installation problem rather than worn-out hardware, and it is worth finding the cause before anyone buys a replacement panel. Owners should also know the clock: ButterflyMX's summary of HPD guidance gives a general correction period of 30 days for a Class B violation and 24 hours for most Class C violations, and a 2022 Bronx Housing Part decision it cites recorded a Class B violation for an "inoperable bell and return-buzzer system."

A repair visit is also where we find out whether repair still makes sense. If the stations are discontinued, the wire is failing in several places and the household wants video or phone answering anyway, the honest answer may be to replace the system and keep only the cable that tests well. If the problem is one bad connection, we fix it and leave the rest alone. Either way, you get the finding in writing before any further work is booked.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in Beverley Square

Every Beverley Square job starts with three questions: how many floors people live on, how many households share the front door, and how many ways a visitor can approach the house. A one-family Victorian with a side driveway and a converted garage needs a different system from a two-family with a shared porch, and both need something different from a twenty-apartment walk-up. These are the services we plan and install here.

Porch station answered on every floor

A weatherproof audio or video station at the front door, with an indoor station on each floor people use, so a visitor can be seen and answered from the kitchen, a bedroom or an attic office instead of only the front hall.

Room-to-room talk-back

Indoor stations that call one another as well as the door, so a parent in the kitchen can reach a teenager on the third floor, or someone in the cellar can call upstairs, without shouting through the stairwell.

Two buttons for two households

A two-button porch panel, separate indoor stations for each household and release rights set so each household opens only the doors it should, for houses shared by an owner and a relative or tenant.

Side doors and driveway gates

A second station at a side door, a driveway gate or the back of the lot, ringing the same rooms as the porch and telling the household which entrance is calling.

Backyard studios and garages

A station in a converted garage, studio or workshop at the end of the driveway, connected by an existing conduit, a new buried cable or a wireless link, depending on what the yard allows.

Phone, computer and app answering

Calls from the porch forwarded to phones, tablets or a computer, for people who work from home or travel, alongside wall stations for anyone who does not want to answer the door on a phone.

Apartment building panels

Lobby or street panels with a button or directory entry for every apartment, apartment stations, a release on the self-locking door and a power supply with backup, for walk-ups and apartment buildings on Beverley Road and near Cortelyou Road.

Codes, fobs and access records

Entry codes, key fobs or app credentials for residents and staff, set up with the consent and privacy steps the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law requires in multiple dwellings, and a record of door releases the owner can read.

Takeovers and upgrades of old systems

Testing and reuse of old intercom or bell wire where it passes, removal of dead master stations, and replacement of discontinued equipment, with a written note of what was kept and why.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Beverley Square's Architecture

Six kinds of property account for almost every intercom request we expect from Beverley Square. The City Planning Commission's report describes the West-side houses as late Queen Anne, early Colonial Revival and Shingle style, and the LPC's 2025 announcement names architects including John J. Petit, John B. Slee and Benjamin Driesler, and the East side adds the simpler houses of the Pounds and Decker years. The apartment buildings and shops on the boundary streets are a different world again. The construction of each one decides where cable can travel, where stations can hang and what the front door will accept.

Queen Anne and Shingle houses

Asymmetrical houses with towers, angled bays, wraparound or full-width porches and three floors of rooms, often with a back stair that gives cable a second route between floors.

Colonial Revival houses

Symmetrical houses with a centered front door and hall, a straighter plan and stacked closets that make a clean vertical path from cellar to attic easier to find.

Houses shared by two households

One-families divided between an owner and a relative or tenant, often with the second household reached by the original stair or a side door, and each needing its own button and stations.

Houses with garages and studios

Lots with a side driveway, a garage or carriage house at the back, and outbuildings now used as studios, offices or workshops that also need to hear the door.

Walk-ups on the boundary streets

Masonry apartment buildings on Beverley Road and near Cortelyou Road, including recent construction, with a street door, a vestibule and a stair serving every apartment.

Shops with rooms above

Commercial buildings on Cortelyou Road with stores at street level and offices, practices or apartments upstairs, often reached by a narrow residential door beside the shopfront.

Queen Anne and Shingle houses: many rooms, many routes

The houses with the most character are also the ones with the most places for a station and the most ways to reach them. A Queen Anne house typically has a front hall that opens to a parlor and dining room, a kitchen at the back, a main stair and often a narrower back stair, bedrooms on the second floor and more rooms under the roof. For an intercom, that plan is good news. The back stair, the kitchen pantry and the closets stacked above it usually line up closely enough for a technician to fish cable from the cellar to the attic without opening a finished wall, and the cellar ceiling gives a clear path across the house to the porch.

The harder part is deciding where the stations go. People in these houses do not live in one room, so the station plan follows the household's day: a station in the kitchen, because that is where most deliveries are answered; one on the second floor near the main bedroom; one on the top floor if a teenager, a home office or a guest suite lives there; and sometimes one in the cellar if the laundry or a workshop is down there. We map that on a simple floor sketch at the walkthrough, and the quote lists stations by room, so you can add or drop one before anything is ordered.

Colonial Revival houses: a centered door and a straight stack

Colonial Revival houses, like the one Brownstoner described at 340 East 18th Street, tend to be easier to route. The front door is centered, the hall runs straight back, and closets and bathrooms often stack directly above one another, so a vertical path appears quickly. The front door itself is usually a wide wood door in a symmetrical surround, sometimes with sidelights, and the station has to sit beside it without breaking that symmetry. A slim station on the plain wall or porch post next to the door, painted or finished to match, usually sits better than a larger panel. These houses often have seven or more bedrooms spread over two upper floors, so they also tend to need more indoor stations than visitors expect.

Houses shared by two households: one porch, two front doors behind it

When a one-family is divided, the upstairs household is usually reached either by the original main stair behind a new door in the front hall, or by a separate side or rear entrance. Both arrangements work with an intercom, but they work differently. If both households use the front door, the porch panel needs two clearly labeled buttons, each ringing only its own household's stations, and the release rights need thought: often both households can open the front door, but only the upstairs household can open the inner door to its stair. If the upstairs household uses a side door, each door gets its own station, and visitors need to be told which one to use. Either way, each household's stations, codes and phone accounts stay separate, so neither household sees or hears the other's callers.

Houses with garages and studios: the back of the lot

The wide lots here leave room behind the house, and many owners use it. A garage at the end of a side driveway may now be a studio, an office or a gym, and people working there miss the door just as surely as someone on the third floor. The question is how to get a signal across the yard. If there is already conduit from the house to the garage, the cable goes through it. If there is not, the options are a shallow buried cable, an aerial run where it is allowed and sensible, or a wireless bridge that links the outbuilding to the house network. We compare them at the walkthrough, because the cheapest one depends on the yard, the paving and what else might go in the trench, such as power or camera cable.

Walk-ups on the boundary streets: shared doors, shared duties

The apartment buildings on Beverley Road and near Cortelyou Road follow the city's rules for multiple dwellings, and the bigger ones carry the intercom requirement of Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a. For an installer, the work is the familiar one of a walk-up: a panel at the street or vestibule door, a station in every apartment, a release on the self-locking door and a riser of cable climbing the building. The difference in the newer buildings is that the system is often networked, with a panel that talks to apartments over a building network and sometimes to tenants' phones. Those systems fail in their own ways, and the review left by a former tenant of 1915 Beverley Road, describing an intercom that "consistently breaks down" and an entry "code has never been changed, despite many tenants asking," shows how quickly residents notice.

Shops with rooms above: keep the two doors apart

Cortelyou Road's commercial buildings usually put a store at street level and offices, practices or apartments above, with a narrow door beside the shopfront for the upper floors. The intercom for those upper floors needs its own panel, its own power supply and its own cable path, independent of the store, so that a shop renovation or a change of tenant downstairs never takes the apartments' door off line. If the store also wants to answer its own back door or delivery door, that is a separate system, or at least a separate set of stations with no ability to release the residential door.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Beverley Square House From the Front Walk to the Attic

Start on the sidewalk, not the porch. On these lots the visitor's first decision is made at the front walk, the gate or the driveway. The technician walks it the way a courier would, at dusk if possible, and notes where the house number is visible, which steps a stranger would choose and whether a side entrance looks like the front.

Ask where everyone actually is at four in the afternoon. The station plan is built around the household's routine, not the floor plan. The kitchen, the home office, the teenager's room and the cellar laundry are the usual answers, and each one becomes a line on the quote.

Look at the lock before the wall. Many original front doors still carry their old mortise locks. Whether a release can work with that lock, or whether the lock has to change, decides more about the job than the choice of station.

Find the stacked closets. A pantry, a back stair or a column of closets running from the cellar to the attic is the route that keeps plaster intact. The technician looks for it on every floor before suggesting any surface raceway.

Test the old wire, then decide. Old bell, phone and intercom wire in these houses can be usable or useless. Each run is tested from end to end before anyone promises to reuse it.

Check the district map. A house on Marlborough, Rugby or Argyle Road between Beverley and Cortelyou Roads is inside the Beverley Square West Historic District, and the porch station follows the LPC's rules. A house on East 16th to East 19th Street is not.

Switch off the power and try the door. With the intercom and lock power off, the front door is opened by hand from inside and with the key from outside. If either fails, the hardware is wrong, and it is fixed before the job is signed off.

Three Floors, One Door

A Whole-House Intercom for a Beverley Square Victorian: Stations, Talk-Back and Who Hears What

The single most common intercom request we expect from a one-family house here is not really about the door. It is about the house. A family spread across a parlor floor, two upper floors and a cellar wants three things: to know someone is at the door wherever they are, to see and talk to that person without going downstairs, and to reach one another inside the house. A doorbell with an extra chime does the first. A video doorbell on a phone does part of the second. Only an intercom with indoor stations does all three, and the design decisions are more interesting than most people expect.

How many stations, and where

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost guide says installation takes about one to two hours per station, which is a useful reminder that every station is a real piece of work: a cable route, a box or bracket, a mount and a test. That is why we do not assume one per room. In most houses here the useful set is a kitchen station, a station on each bedroom floor placed in the hall or the main bedroom, and stations in any room where someone works for hours at a time. A cellar station is worth it if the laundry, a gym or a workshop is down there. A finished attic gets one if it is lived in. Bathrooms, closets and rarely used parlors usually do not. The result is often three to five indoor stations in a one-family house, more if a second household or an outbuilding is involved.

Talk-back between rooms

Most modern door systems can also call between indoor stations, but they do it in different ways. Some let any station call any other by name; some only offer an all-stations call; some reach the phone app but not the wall stations. For a family, the practical questions are simple. Can the kitchen call the third floor without the whole house hearing? Can a child's room be left out of the door chime at night but still be reached from the kitchen? Can a station be set so it can answer the door but not release it, for a younger child or a visitor staying in the guest room? We set those rules at programming, write them down and walk the family through them before we leave.

Video, audio or both

Video at the porch is the usual choice now, and in these houses it works well because the door sits under a porch roof, sheltered from rain and direct overhead light. Video inside is a matter of taste. A screen in the kitchen and on the main bedroom floor is common; an audio-only station on the cellar stairs or in a workshop is often enough and costs less. Mixing them is normal, as long as all the stations belong to the same system. Phone answering can be added on top, so someone in the backyard or away from home can still answer, but we always keep at least one wall station per floor that works without the home internet, because Wi-Fi drops and phones get left in other rooms.

Keeping the plaster intact

Original plaster, wood trim and built-ins are a large part of what owners value in these houses, so the cable plan aims to leave no visible trace. The usual route runs along the cellar ceiling to a point below a stacked closet, pantry or back stair, then up inside that cavity to each floor, with the station on the nearest suitable wall. Where a wall must be opened, the opening is kept small and placed where the station or a plate will cover it. Surface raceway is a last resort, offered only when the household accepts it, and painted to match when it is used.

Two Households, One Porch

One Panel Per Door Opening: Sharing a Porch Intercom Between Two Households in the Beverley Square West Historic District

The LPC's Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety fixtures sets out the rules its staff use to approve intercom panels on designated buildings. Panels, like light fixtures and doorbells, "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," or on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway. The guidebook allows a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel per door opening, asks for a design, size and finish compatible with the building, and requires the panel to be fastened at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork or concrete paving rather than through cast iron or decorative surfaces. Conduit should be concealed behind the installation through plain masonry or cladding, or kept to about ten inches of visible conduit painted to match.

For a two-family house that shares its front door, the important phrase is "one intercom panel per door opening." It means the familiar solution of two separate doorbells, one for each household, is not available for that door. Instead, both households share one panel with two buttons. That is easy to arrange technically, because almost every multi-household panel can carry two or more buttons, each ringing its own household's stations. It does mean the panel has to be chosen with care, because it is the only fixture allowed and it has to do the whole job: two clearly labeled buttons, a camera if either household wants video, and a keypad or reader if either household wants one, all in a housing small and quiet enough to sit beside a 1900s door.

If the second household has its own entrance, at the side or the back, that is a separate door opening, and it can have its own panel under the same rules. Many owners find that cleaner: the upstairs household's visitors go to the side door, the porch panel serves the owner's family, and nobody is confused. The trade-off is a second cable run and a second station outside, and a visitor who has to be told which door to use. We describe both arrangements at the walkthrough, with what each would cost, and let the owners decide.

The Law at the Lobby Door

Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road Walk-Ups: What New York Law Asks of an Apartment Building's Intercom

Most of Beverley Square is houses, and for a one- or two-family house an intercom is a choice. The apartment buildings at the neighborhood's edges are a different matter, and owners, boards and tenants there should know where the line falls.

Buildings with eight or more apartments, built or converted after 1968. New York Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 to have self-closing, self-locking doors at street-level entrances, and, where the building has eight or more apartments, an intercom at the self-locking lobby door that lets residents talk to visitors and release the lock from their apartments. Recent construction is included. A six-floor walk-up with three apartments on each floor, which is how a former tenant described 1915 Beverley Road, would have well over eight.

Older buildings with eight or more apartments. Section 50-a reaches buildings erected or converted before 1968 too, but only if tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing. Once they do, the owner must install the doors and the intercom on the law's schedule, with rules about recovering the cost, especially in rent-regulated buildings.

Approval and upkeep. The same section says the equipment must be approved by the responsible department and installed and maintained as the agencies prescribe. Failing to install or maintain it, and willfully damaging, jamming or tampering with it without justification, are misdemeanors under the law.

Habitability. Habitat magazine's 2024 article "A Working Intercom Isn't an Amenity. It's Required by Law" reports that courts have treated intercoms as an essential service under the warranty of habitability. Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group put it plainly for a co-op whose intercom had failed: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The article describes a 311 complaint or an HP proceeding in Housing Court as the next steps if a board or owner does not respond.

When it breaks. NYC311 lists broken doorbells, buzzers and intercoms as reportable conditions. ButterflyMX's summary of HPD guidance gives general correction periods of 90 days for Class A violations, 30 days for Class B and 24 hours for most Class C, subject to the terms of the violation itself.

What this means for a quote. For an apartment building, our written quote states what the system does at the lobby door, which is two-way voice and release from every apartment at a minimum, and anything beyond that, such as video, phone answering or fobs, is listed separately. That way an owner, a board and the tenants can all see that the legal baseline is covered before extras are discussed. This is general information about the rules, not legal advice; a building with a dispute should talk to its own lawyer.

Codes, Fobs and Phones

Entry Codes That Never Change and Calls That Can't Reach a Phone: Fixing the Software Side of a Newer Beverley Square Building

The tenant reviews of 1915 Beverley Road are worth reading for anyone who owns or manages a newer building here, because almost none of the complaints are about the hardware itself. A former tenant wrote that "the intercom consistently breaks down," that "there is no way to transfer the intercom calls to your phone," and that the entry "code has never been changed, despite many tenants asking both landlords to change it." The same tenant described locks that were not changed after tenants moved out, a front door "jimmied" with a screwdriver, and "packages that get stolen from this building." Every one of those is a decision about how the system is set up and run, not about which brand is on the wall.

Codes. A shared building code that never changes is known, sooner or later, to every former tenant, every former contractor and every courier who was once told it. Most current panels support individual codes per apartment or per person, which can be removed when someone leaves, plus temporary codes for cleaners or contractors. We set the building up that way where the panel allows, show the owner or manager how to add and delete codes, and recommend a written routine: remove a household's codes and fobs on move-out, change any shared code on a schedule, and keep a record of who has what.

Phones. Many networked panels can forward a call to a resident's phone when nobody answers the apartment station, or ring the phone and the station together. Whether that works in a given building depends on the panel, the building network and the service plan the owner signed up for. StreetEasy's Talk thread on replacing an old buzzer with video is a good counterweight: a 12-unit building there ruled out a phone-only system because some residents did not have smartphones, and one resident called it "illogical to rummage for your phone to answer the doorbell." The answer for most buildings is both: a station in every apartment, and phone answering for those who want it.

Privacy. When a multiple dwelling uses a smart access system, such as key fobs, RFID cards, mobile apps or biometrics, HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, says owners must get tenants' express consent, provide a data retention and privacy policy, destroy authentication data within 90 days and may not require smart access to enter a tenant's own apartment. We set up fobs and app accounts with those steps in mind, and we hand the owner the settings that control what is stored and for how long.

Reliability. When a newer system "consistently breaks down," the cause is usually one of a short list: a power supply too small for the panel and the strike together, no battery backup, a building network shared with tenants' internet and rebooted by whoever handles it, firmware never updated, or a cable termination done in a hurry at the end of construction. We test each one in that order before recommending any new hardware, and we leave the owner a written list of what was found.

Couriers and Switch-Overs

The Bklyner report from 2018, about a building near Cortelyou and Stratford Roads whose residents counted more than 100 missing packages in six months, ended with a camera in the mail area. Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Beverley Square: Questions Answered

These are the situations Beverley Square owners, families, tenants and managers bring to an intercom, grouped by the kind of property. Each answer is short on purpose; the walkthrough fills in the details for your address.

Porches, original doors and big houses

Our porch was enclosed with windows years ago, so there are now two doors: the new porch door and the original front door. Which one gets the outdoor station and the release?

Usually the outer porch door, because that is where a visitor first stops and where a courier can be let in to leave a package out of the weather. The station goes beside the porch door, the release goes on that door, and the original front door keeps its own lock and key. If the enclosed porch is not secure, for example if its windows are easy to open, some families prefer the station outside the porch and the release on the original door instead. We look at both doors before suggesting one.

Our front door still has its original mortise lock and key. Can an intercom open it, or does the lock have to go?

It depends on the lock and the frame. Many old mortise locks have a latch that an electric strike in the frame can release, and the original lock stays in place, keys and all. Some have a latch shape or a frame too thin or too decorative for a strike, and then the choices are a replacement mortise lock with an electric function that fits the same pocket in the door, or leaving the front door manual and putting the release on another door. We take the lock's measurements at the walkthrough and show you the option that changes the least.

Our porch wraps around a corner of the house, and guests come up whichever set of steps is closer. Do we need two outdoor stations?

Usually one is enough, placed beside the front door where both sets of steps lead. Visitors who come up the side steps still walk along the porch to the door. A second station only makes sense if the side steps lead to a different door that the family actually answers. A small sign or a lit house number at the top of the main steps does more to guide guests than a second station would.

Trees and hedges hide the house numbers on our block, and visitors and drivers walk up to the wrong porch at night. Can the intercom help?

It can help at the house. Some door stations have a lit name or number plate, and a station can switch on a porch light when the button is pressed, if the light is wired for it. The bigger fix is a lit number near the front walk or gate, where a driver looks first. We can mount a small illuminated number on the same power supply as the intercom; in the historic district, a new fixture on a facade or front wall follows the LPC's rules, so we keep it modest and check the location with the owner first.

Working and living across three floors

I work in a third-floor office on video calls all day. Can a visitor at the porch reach my computer or headset instead of a wall station?

Often yes. Many networked intercoms can ring a desktop or phone app, and some use the SIP standard so a call can reach a softphone on a computer and a headset. That lets you answer the porch without leaving the desk or the call. We usually still put a small station in the office, because computers sleep, apps update and headsets get taken off, and a station on the wall works regardless.

Can the porch station ring through the smart speakers we already have in several rooms?

Some door stations can send an announcement to smart speakers, usually through the speaker maker's app and a linked account, and some smart displays can show the porch camera. It is a useful extra, but it depends on the specific station and speaker, and it relies on the home internet. We treat it as an add-on to a proper intercom, not a replacement for stations, and we check compatibility for your exact devices before promising it.

Our driveway leads to a garage at the back of the lot that is now a studio. Can the studio get a station without digging a trench across the yard?

Often. If the garage already has power from the house, there may be a conduit with room for a low-voltage cable. If not, a wireless bridge between the house and the studio can carry a networked station's signal, provided both ends have power and a clear line of sight. A buried cable is the most reliable choice, and if a trench is planned for anything else, such as power or camera cable, the intercom cable should go in at the same time. We compare the options with prices.

Second households and later additions

We are adding a side entrance to our house in the historic district for an upstairs household. Can that door have its own intercom panel?

Yes, under the LPC guidebook's rules, because the limit is one intercom panel per door opening, and the new side door is a separate opening. The side panel follows the same mounting and finish rules as the front, and the new entrance itself will be part of the Landmarks approval for the alteration. The upstairs household's visitors then use the side panel, and the front panel stays with the owner's family. We plan both on one system so stations inside can still call each other if the households want that.

We may finish our attic in a few years. Can a station be added then without replacing the porch panel?

Yes, if the system is chosen with room to grow. Most current systems accept extra indoor stations up to a stated limit, and the easiest way to make that future job cheap is to pull a spare cable to the attic now while the technician is already working on that route. We note the spare in the wiring record, so whoever adds the station later knows it is there.

Apartment buildings on Beverley Road and near Cortelyou Road

The entry code in our newer walk-up on Beverley Road has not changed since the building opened. Who should change it, and how often?

The owner or managing agent controls the code, because they hold the panel's administrator account. A shared code should change whenever a tenant moves out or a contractor finishes, and on a regular schedule in between. A better setup gives each apartment its own code or fob, which can be removed on move-out without affecting anyone else. If your building's panel supports that, the change is a programming job, not new hardware. Tenants who cannot get a response can put the request in writing to the owner and keep a copy.

Our building went up only a few years ago, and the intercom keeps failing. What goes wrong in a new building's system so soon?

Usually the setup rather than the parts. Common causes are a power supply too small for the panel and the door strike together, no battery backup, a panel sharing a network with tenants' internet that gets rebooted or reconfigured, firmware that was never updated, and connections made in a rush at the end of construction. A technician should check each of those before anyone replaces the panel. The owner's obligation to keep the intercom working applies to new buildings as much as old ones.

Packages keep disappearing from our lobby. Can the intercom tell our manager when the door was released and by which apartment?

Many networked panels keep a log of calls and releases, with the time and the apartment or code used. That lets a manager match a missing package to a delivery window and see whether someone buzzed in a stranger. The log only helps if the system is set up to keep it and someone checks it, and in a multiple dwelling any records tied to fobs or apps fall under the Tenant Data Privacy Law's retention rules. We set the log up, show the manager how to read it and note what is stored.

Our landlord says the keypad on our door is enough and the talk-back intercom doesn't need fixing. Is that right?

For a building with eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968, the Multiple Dwelling Law requires an intercom that lets residents talk to visitors and release the lobby door from their apartments. A keypad lets residents in; it does not let a visitor reach an apartment or let a tenant open the door for a guest. Tenants can raise it in writing with the owner, and if that fails, NYC311 takes reports of broken intercoms. This is general information, not legal advice.

FAQ

Beverley Square Intercom Installation FAQ

Our house is in Beverley Square East, between the cut and East 19th Street. Do the Landmarks rules for porch intercoms apply to us?
+

Not under the 2025 designation. The Beverley Square West Historic District covers Marlborough, Rugby and Argyle Roads between Beverley and Cortelyou Roads, so houses on East 16th through East 19th Street are outside it. You can choose and place the porch station without an LPC permit, although we still recommend a slim station within the door opening so the house keeps its look. The price and the process are otherwise the same on both sides of the cut.

Does the installer need to be inside every room that gets a station, and for how long?
+

Yes, each room with a station needs a visit, usually twice: once while the cable is fished in, and once to mount, program and test the station. HomeAdvisor estimates about one to two hours of work per station, and that matches what a typical room takes when the cable path is clear. Rooms without stations are usually only entered if the cable passes through a closet there. We give you a room-by-room schedule with the quote.

Can the cable to the porch station come up through the porch floor instead of through the front wall of the house?
+

Often, and on a full-width porch it is usually the neatest route. The cable runs along the cellar ceiling to the front foundation, out under the porch, and up through the porch floor or a post beside the door, so nothing is drilled through the original facade. It depends on whether there is access under the porch and how the porch is built, which we check at the walkthrough. In the historic district, the LPC prefers concealed conduit, which this route provides.

Can the porch station be finished to match the paint on our porch and door trim?
+

Usually. Many porch stations come in several finishes, such as black, bronze, silver or white, and some housings can be painted to match. We pick a finish that sits quietly against your door surround, and in the historic district the LPC asks for a finish compatible with the building, so we include the finish in the photos and cut sheets for your filing. We never paint over a station's camera, speaker or button surfaces.

We rent part of our two-family to a tenant and gave them a keypad code. Does the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law apply to our house?
+

HPD's page on the law describes it as applying to multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as fobs, cards, apps or biometrics. A two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, so the law's specific duties are not usually triggered there. It is still good practice to give your tenant a personal code, change it when they leave and keep any app records to a minimum. This is general information, not legal advice.

If we renovate or enclose our porch later, can the station be moved?
+

Yes. A station is held by a bracket or a small box and connected by one cable, so moving it a few feet is a short job if the cable has slack. We leave a service loop at the porch for exactly that reason and note it in the wiring record. If the move is to a different door, a new cable run may be needed. In the historic district, a new location on the facade would need the owner to check with the LPC again.

Can the intercom unlock the side yard gate between our driveway and the back garden?
+

Yes, if the gate has a latch that can take an electric release and a way to bring a cable to it. A gate station can ring the house and let you open the gate for a gardener, a guest or a delivery to the back door. The cable usually follows the side of the house or a buried path from the cellar. The gate must still open from the yard side by hand, and we fit a closer or spring so it relatches.

Our porch station sits under the porch roof. Does it still need an outdoor-rated unit?
+

Yes. A porch roof keeps off direct rain, but wind-driven rain, snow, humidity and winter cold still reach the door. We use stations rated by the maker for outdoor use, and the porch roof simply helps them last longer and keeps the camera lens cleaner. Indoor stations are never used outside, even on a covered porch.

Your Brooklyn price list mentions brownstones and townhouses. Which line applies to a free-standing Victorian?
+

The one- or two-family house line, which covers a video system for a single- or two-family home at $600 to $1,800 on our Brooklyn list. A free-standing house is priced the same way as a row house; what moves the number is how many stations, how long the cable routes are and what the front door needs. A walk-up falls under the separate building line.

Can a station go at the top of the cellar stairs for whoever is doing laundry or working down there?
+

Yes, and it is one of the most popular extras in these houses. A small audio station at the top or bottom of the cellar stairs lets someone in the laundry, a gym or a workshop hear and answer the porch and call the kitchen. Because the cable starts in the cellar anyway, it is often one of the shortest and cheapest runs on the job.

Our house still has an old servants' bell board or speaking tube in the kitchen. Does a new intercom mean taking it out?
+

No. A new intercom does not need anything from an old bell board or speaking tube, and we leave original fixtures like that exactly where they are unless you ask us to remove them. The new kitchen station goes on a nearby wall, and its cable takes its own route. If old wires from the board run through the cellar, we tag them as abandoned in the wiring record so nobody mistakes them for the new system.
