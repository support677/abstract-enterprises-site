The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Properties Need Cat6 Repair Before Replacement

Ask anyone who has tried to bury a cable in Putnam County how it went, and you will hear about rock. The county sits on the Hudson Highlands, which the New York State Museum’s bedrock map of Putnam and Westchester describes as a Precambrian complex of gneisses, migmatite, marble, granite and granodiorite. Soil over it is often thin. When NYSEG hardened two circuits in the Town of Carmel in 2021, WAMC reported that the crews needed rock drilling just to set the new poles. A homeowner who wants a fresh Cat6 run from the house to a detached garage, a studio or a dock runs into the same ground, and that changes the arithmetic of every network cable fault on the property: the cable costs little, the route costs a great deal.

Water is the second constraint. Putnam is threaded with the reservoirs and controlled lakes of New York City’s Croton system — West Branch, Boyds Corner, Middle Branch, Croton Falls, East Branch, Bog Brook and Diverting are all named in the state’s watershed rules, and the West Branch Reservoir alone straddles the Towns of Kent and Carmel and holds about 8 billion gallons, according to the city’s Department of Environmental Protection. Around them sit private lakes such as Lake Mahopac, Lake Carmel and Lake Oscawana, ringed by houses. Town wetland ordinances regulate the ground near all of that water. The Town of Carmel’s permit application, for instance, says the town protects wetlands of one-eighth of an acre and larger plus a 100-foot buffer around each, and that construction or other activity inside that buffer needs a wetland permit. A replacement trench to a boathouse is exactly the kind of activity that question is written for.

Then there are the walls. Putnam’s houses are mostly post-war but not new: Stacker’s 2025 ranking of New York counties by housing age, drawn from Census data, puts the county’s median year built at 1968, with 17.9 percent of homes — 6,857 of them — built in 1939 or earlier. Many of those older homes are lake cottages that were winterized and finished long after the original wiring went in, or nineteenth-century buildings in Cold Spring, Nelsonville and Garrison where plaster, trim and exterior appearance matter. In the Village of Cold Spring, the Historic District Review Board reviews exterior changes visible from the street, and its design standards list utilities among the exterior features it looks at. A cable fix there has to be planned around what the street can see.

Weather finishes the picture. Putnam is wooded, hilly and served largely by overhead lines, and its storms are remembered by road name. On May 15, 2018, a line of thunderstorms with reports of tornado-like winds in Carmel left 28,459 NYSEG customers and 4,698 Central Hudson customers without power in the county, Patch reported that night. In July 2023 more than eight inches of rain tore into bridges, dams, roads and at least 130 homes in Philipstown and western Putnam, according to The Highlands Current. And on July 29, 2026, the Town of Putnam Valley declared a state of emergency over flash flooding and road and bridge closures, restricting travel on Cimarron, Horton Hollow, Canopus Hollow and Tinker Hill roads among others. Fallen limbs pull down cable strung between buildings; water finds the box where a run enters a foundation; a power return knocks a marginal PoE link over the edge.

Put rock, water rules, finished walls and weather together and you get a county where the cheapest Cat6 repair is the one that keeps the existing cable. That is the thesis of this page. A good technician in Putnam does not guess at a failing link and pull a new one; he measures it, finds the exact metre that failed, repairs the termination or the short damaged length, and proves the whole channel still meets Category 6. Only when the test says the run is damaged along its length, too long, wet through, the wrong cable for where it lies or simply not genuine Cat6 does the conversation move to replacement — and then the question becomes which route avoids the rock, the buffer and the historic façade.

What We Repair

Cat6 Repair Services in Putnam County

These are the repairs Putnam homeowners, small offices, shops and lake-property owners ask us for most. Each ends the same way: the link is tested against the limit for its category, the saved result is yours, and you are told in plain language what failed and why.

Fault Location by Distance

Time-domain readings on a certification-grade tester show how far along the run a short, open, split pair or impedance bump sits, so the wall, ceiling or ground is opened at one point only — the core of repair-first work on finished and rocky properties.

Jack and Keystone Re-Termination

Dead and intermittent wall jacks cut back to clean cable and remade on matched Cat6 or Cat6A hardware, pair twist carried to the contacts, faceplates matched where the old style is still made.

In-Line Repair of a Damaged Length

Where a screw, nail, staple or crush has damaged one accessible spot, the bad section is cut out and joined with a category-rated junction in a box that stays accessible, then the whole link is certified — or the test tells us a full replacement is needed.

Torn and Sagging Aerial Runs

Cable strung between a house and a garage or barn and pulled down by a limb: replaced on a proper support wire or moved to a protected route, with drip loops and sealed entries at both buildings.

PoE Camera and Access-Point Links

Cameras that will not power, reboot under infrared at night or drop video; ceiling access points that fall back to wireless backhaul. Cable resistance, terminations and outdoor plugs separated from switch and power-budget problems.

Water and Corrosion Damage

Runs that sat in a flooded basement, a wet foundation box or a leaking soffit tested, corroded plugs and jacks replaced, and the damaged length judged by measurement rather than by appearance.

Patch Panel and Switch-End Repair

Loose or half-seated punch-downs, wrong-standard panel ports and tired patch cords in a basement corner, utility closet or office wall cabinet re-terminated and labelled.

Replacement Runs When Repair Fails

When testing proves a run cannot be saved, a replacement on the least disruptive route — existing conduit, the original path, surface raceway or a new pull — priced from our published per-drop figure.

Certification After Every Repair

Wire map, length, insertion loss, crosstalk and return loss against the Category 6 or 6A limit, saved with the date, so the next time something changes you have a baseline to compare against.

How the repair is reached in each

Lake cottages. The first job is mapping how the house grew. A jack in what is now a living room may be fed by cable that runs through a former exterior wall, under a porch that was enclosed later, or through a crawlspace that floods in spring. We test from the jack, read the distance to the fault and compare it with where the additions are; nine times out of ten that tells us which part of the house to look in. Cable that has spent years in a damp crawlspace or under a porch deck is often sound inside a chalky, stiff jacket; the test, not the look, decides whether it stays.

Post-war houses. Basements and attics make these the easiest Putnam houses to repair. Most faults are at terminations made with the wrong tool, staples driven through the jacket, or cable crushed when a basement was finished and a soffit built around it. We reach the damage from the unfinished side wherever possible, and when the run is boxed into a finished soffit we open a small access panel rather than a long slot.

Village buildings. Plaster, wood trim and party walls mean the original cable route was improvised: along baseboards, behind door casings, through closets and up old chases. Repairs here favour the existing route and the existing openings. Where a new path is unavoidable we use closets, cellars and attics first and keep anything that would show from the street off the façade, because in the Cold Spring Historic District that is what the Review Board looks at.

Estates and outbuildings. Runs between buildings are where Putnam’s rock, trees and water converge. We test from the main house, find out whether the fault is at an end, at an entry or out in the ground or the air, and only then decide whether the fix is a new termination, a new entry, a repaired length or a different kind of link altogether.

Main streets and plazas. Tenant cabling in older commercial buildings is layered: abandoned runs from previous occupants, extensions with couplers, and panels nobody has labelled. We identify which cable is live before cutting anything, repair the failing link, and remove abandoned lengths that are accessible.

Rules that can shape a Putnam Cat6 repair

Most Cat6 repairs touch none of the following. Re-terminating a jack, replacing a cord or repairing a length inside a wall is ordinary maintenance. The rules start to matter when a repair turns into a new route outdoors or a visible change to a building, and we flag that before it happens rather than after.

Cold Spring Historic District Review Board

The village’s design standards say the Board reviews any change to exterior architectural features visible from the street, listing utilities among them, and exempt regular maintenance that does not change appearance and work not visible from the street. We keep repair work inside or out of view, and tell you before anything would need a Certificate of Appropriateness.

Town wetland buffers

The Town of Carmel regulates a 100-foot buffer around wetlands of an eighth of an acre or more, including lakes, ponds and streams, and requires a wetland permit for construction or other activity inside it. The Town of Kent’s building-permit guidance flags projects within 100 feet of wetlands, ponds, streams and watercourses. A trench to a dock or boathouse is the case to check.

NYC watershed regulations

State rules for the city’s watershed prohibit new impervious surfaces within 100 feet of a watercourse or wetland and 300 feet of a reservoir, reservoir stem or controlled lake, and require stormwater plans for larger land disturbance. A cable trench is not an impervious surface, but a repair that grows into new paving or a structure near the water is a different conversation.

Call before you dig

Any excavation for a replacement run goes through the 811 utility-locate process first, with the notice period the system requires. In Highlands ground that is shallow over rock, knowing where the electric, water and septic lines run is not optional.

Entry protection for runs between buildings

The National Electrical Code requires a listed primary protector as close as practicable to the point where a communications cable exposed to lightning enters a building. Where a repaired run leaves one structure for another, we look at how it enters each.

Abandoned cable

The Code also requires accessible portions of abandoned communications cable to be removed. When a repair replaces a length or a run, we take out the old one wherever it can be reached, unless you want it tagged for future use.

These summaries are written for property owners and are not legal advice. Your town building department, the village, or NYC DEP gives the binding answer for a specific address.

Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in Putnam County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Putnam owners actually put to us about failing Cat6, grouped by what is going on around the cable. Each answer is the short version of what we would tell you on the phone.

Rock, water and the ground between buildings

If trenching a new Cat6 run across our Putnam Valley lot means cutting through ledge, what are the alternatives to digging?

There are usually four, and we test the old run before choosing between them. First, the existing run may not need replacing at all: if the tester puts the fault at the garage end, at the entry into the house or at one bruised spot near the surface, we repair that and certify the link. Second, if the old cable lies in a pipe, a new cable can often be pulled through the same pipe using the old one as the pull line, which avoids opening the ground except at the ends. Third, a run can follow the surface — along a foundation, a retaining wall or a fence line — in outdoor-rated conduit fixed above grade, which is often the practical answer where soil over rock is only inches deep. Fourth, where the two buildings are far apart or on separate electric services, a fiber link or a point-to-point wireless bridge can replace the copper entirely; we will say plainly when that is the better route and refer the fiber side to our installation team.

What we avoid is the reflex of trenching a new copper run beside a failed one without knowing why the first one failed. In ledge country the second trench costs more than the first, and if the cause was a bad entry or water in the far box, the new cable fails the same way.

Does replacing a Cat6 run near Lake Mahopac or another lake in the Town of Carmel need a wetland permit?

A repair that stays at the terminations or inside the buildings does not disturb the ground and is not what the wetland ordinance is aimed at. A new trench is different. The Town of Carmel’s wetland permit application says the town protects wetlands of an eighth of an acre and larger and regulates a 100-foot buffer around each, asks whether any construction or disturbance will happen within 100 feet of a wetland “including lakes, ponds, streams”, and requires a permit if it will. A run from a house to a dock or boathouse on a lakefront lot will often fall inside that distance.

So on a lakefront property we first try to keep the fix out of the ground: repair at the ends, re-use an existing pipe, or run along a structure. If a new trench inside the buffer is still the right answer, we tell you before any work is scheduled so the permit question can be settled with the town, and we plan the route to disturb as little as possible.

We are close to the West Branch Reservoir in Kent — do the city’s watershed rules limit how a damaged Cat6 run can be replaced?

Mostly through the town, rather than directly. The New York City watershed regulations target things like new impervious surfaces — prohibited within 100 feet of a watercourse or wetland and 300 feet of a reservoir, reservoir stem or controlled lake — and stormwater plans for larger clearing and grading. A narrow cable trench that is backfilled is not an impervious surface. But the Town of Kent’s building-permit guidance separately flags projects within 100 feet of wetlands, ponds, streams and watercourses and larger soil disturbance, so a replacement trench near water is something to confirm with the town building department first.

In practice, most failed runs near the reservoirs are repaired without new excavation at all. Where excavation is unavoidable, we keep it short, hand-dug where we can, and away from the water side of the property.

Trees, wind and the power that comes back

A falling limb tore down the Cat6 we had strung between the house and the garage — should the aerial run be repaired or put underground?

It depends on how it was hung and what the ground looks like. Cable strung on its own between two buildings — no support wire, no drip loops, the jacket carrying its own weight — was always going to fail; splicing it back together and re-hanging it the same way only resets the clock. If an overhead route is still the sensible one, the repair is a proper span: a steel support wire tensioned between solid points on each building, outdoor-rated cable lashed or clipped to it, drip loops before each entry, and sealed, protected entries at both ends. That keeps the weight off the copper and lets the span move in wind without pulling on the terminations.

If the span crosses under trees that drop limbs every winter, or the garage has power of its own, underground or a different kind of link may be worth the extra cost. On rocky ground a surface route along a fence or wall is sometimes the middle option. We test the remaining cable at both buildings first: a run that was torn down often has a damaged entry or a cracked plug at the far end as well.

Our standby generator takes over during Putnam outages — why do the PoE cameras drop when it does, and is the Cat6 to blame?

Usually the drop at the moment of transfer is not the cable. When power switches from the utility to the generator there is a brief interruption, and a PoE switch without battery backup restarts — taking every camera it powers with it. The cameras then take a minute or two to boot and reconnect, which looks like a network failure. A small battery backup on the switch and recorder normally removes that symptom completely.

The cable becomes the suspect when only some cameras fail to come back, or come back and then keep rebooting. Those are often the longest runs or the ones with a poor termination: they were working with very little margin, and the inrush of every camera starting at once is enough to tip them over. We test those links under load, re-terminate or repair what is marginal, and tell you whether the PoE budget of the switch is part of the problem.

Lake houses, renovations and finished walls

Our cottage on Lake Carmel was winterized with spray-foam insulation, and the Cat6 inside the walls has failed — can it still be repaired?

Often, yes — at the ends. Spray foam locks cable in place, so it cannot be pulled out and a new one cannot be pulled in along the same path. But most failures in foamed walls are not in the middle of the wall; they are at the jacks, where the cable was cut short, kinked or stressed when the foam was trimmed flush, or at the point where the cable leaves the foamed bay into an attic or basement. The tester tells us how far from the jack the fault sits. If it is within the first few inches, we cut back and re-terminate; if it is at the attic or basement end, we repair it there.

When the damage is genuinely mid-wall in a foamed bay, the honest options are to abandon that run and bring a new one to the room by another path — a closet, an interior wall without foam, a baseboard raceway — or to open the wall at one point and make an in-line repair inside an accessible box. We price both before anything is cut.

A drywall screw or trim nail went through a Cat6 run during our renovation — how do you find the damaged spot without opening every wall?

By distance. A screw through a cable usually shorts or opens one or two conductors, and a certification tester shows how many feet from the jack that fault lies. We measure from both ends where we can, which narrows the location further, and compare it with where the new drywall, trim or cabinets went. That usually points to one stud bay. A small inspection opening at that spot confirms it.

The fix is then either an in-line repair at that point, made with a category-rated junction in a box that stays accessible behind a blank plate, or a new run if the damaged spot cannot be reached and left serviceable. Either way the whole link is certified afterwards. If the renovation contractor is responsible, the saved test result and photos of the damage are the clearest record you can give them.

Where the Cat6 passes from our attached garage into the house in Mahopac, the hole was never sealed — does the repair have to fix that?

It should. The wall between an attached garage and the living space is there to keep fire and car fumes out of the house, and a cable hole left open, or stuffed with fibreglass, defeats it. Garage penetrations are also where Cat6 gets damaged: the edge of a hole cut in drywall or block chafes the jacket every time the garage door shakes the wall, and the cable is often pulled tight across the opening.

When we repair a run that crosses that wall, we test it, repair or replace the damaged length, sleeve the penetration so the cable is not resting on a raw edge, and seal it with a fire-rated sealant suited to the wall. It adds minutes to the visit and removes a fault that would otherwise come back.

Since an EV charger went into our Patterson garage, the Cat6 link out there keeps dropping — can the new circuit cause that?

Rarely by itself. Twisted-pair cable is good at rejecting interference, and a properly installed charger circuit running alongside a Cat6 run for a short distance does not normally break a link. What we find far more often is that the installation disturbed the cable: it was moved to make room for a new conduit, crushed under a clamp, stapled through, or cut and rejoined behind the charger.

We test the run and read where the fault sits. If it is at the spot where the charger work happened, we repair that length and re-route the Cat6 so it keeps its distance from the new power wiring. If the link only fails while the car is charging and the cable tests clean, we look at shared grounding and the equipment at each end, and tell you whether your electrician needs to look at the charger side.

Village buildings and Putnam businesses

Does a Cat6 repair on a Main Street building in Cold Spring need approval from the Historic District Review Board?

Usually not, if nothing changes on the outside. The village’s design standards say the Board reviews changes to exterior architectural features visible from the street — including utilities — and that regular maintenance which does not alter appearance, and work not visible from the street, are not subject to review. Re-terminating jacks, repairing cable inside the building and replacing a run through cellars and interior walls fall on that side of the line.

The Cat6 to the kitchen printer and card terminal in our Cold Spring restaurant keeps failing — what about a kitchen kills network cable?

Heat, grease, water and people. Cable behind a line runs near hot equipment; cords to printers hang where they are splashed, wiped with degreaser and caught by carts; jacks are mounted low on walls that are hosed down. What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in Putnam County

Search summaries and generic how-to pages answer Cat6 questions as if every house sat on easy soil in a suburb with buried utilities. We could not view Google’s AI Overview from our build environment, so the claims below are ones commonly published in cost guides, forums and how-to articles, each corrected with what we see on Putnam properties.

“If a Cat6 run fails, just pull a new one”

Pulling new cable is the default advice because, in an open office ceiling, it is quick. Online cost guides price Cat6 by the drop, and the implication is that a failed drop is simply replaced with another. Fixr’s January 2025 guide, for example, puts a new Cat6 drop at $125 to $250 including materials and labour across the United States.

In Putnam the price of a new drop is dominated by the route. A run that has to cross Highlands bedrock, pass inside a lake buffer, go behind spray foam or avoid a historic façade can cost several times the cable. Our own Putnam installation rate for a Cat6 drop to 100 feet is $321, and that covers an ordinary interior route, not a trench through ledge.

So the first step is not a new pull; it is a measurement. If the tester says the fault is at a termination or one accessible spot, repairing it keeps the existing route, which is the most expensive part of the link and the part you already own.

“Ethernet cable cannot be spliced”

Forum answers often say that a damaged Ethernet cable must always be replaced end to end. As a rule for hidden, taped or twisted joins, that is right: a joint buried in a wall with no access and no test is a future fault.

But category-rated junction hardware exists, and an in-line repair made with it, in a box that stays accessible, can carry a link that certifies to Category 6. Whether it does is not a matter of opinion; the tester decides. What does not work is a join made with couplers, electrical tape or crimp connectors meant for power.

On a Putnam property where the alternative is opening a finished wall end to end or trenching through rock, a properly made and certified in-line repair is often the sensible engineering answer. Where it would not pass, or could not stay accessible, we say so and replace.

“Outdoor Cat6 just needs an outdoor-rated jacket”

Many how-to pages treat outdoor Cat6 as a jacket problem: buy outdoor cable, run it, done. The jacket matters, but most outdoor failures we repair are at the ends and in between: a plug with no seal, an entry with no drip loop, a span hung with no support wire, a run tied to a tree that has grown and moved.

In a wooded county the overhead span between a house and a garage takes ice, wind and falling limbs every winter. A cable carrying its own weight stretches and eventually tears at its weakest termination, whatever the jacket is rated for.

So an outdoor repair here includes the support, the entries and the protection, not only the cable. That is also where the National Electrical Code’s requirement for a protector near the entry of a lightning-exposed run comes in.

“A cheap continuity tester tells you if the cable is fine”

Inexpensive testers that blink each of the eight wires are widely recommended for checking a run. They confirm that wires are connected end to end in the right order, and that is useful.

They do not measure length, loss, crosstalk or return loss, and they do not show where along the run a problem sits. A run with a split pair, water in the jacket or a crushed section can pass a blink test and still fail to hold a gigabit link. A screw that has nicked a conductor without shorting it can pass too.

For repair-first work, the distance reading is the whole point: it is what lets us open one spot in a finished wall instead of guessing. That needs a certification-grade tester.

“Any electrician or handyman can fix network cable”

Homeowner forums often suggest calling whoever is already working on the house. For changing a faceplate or a cord, that can be fine.

Repairing a Cat6 link so that it still meets its category is different work: terminating with the twist kept to the contacts, matching parts to the cable, testing against the standard and interpreting the result. Electricians are the right trade for power, generators, panels and grounding, and on some Putnam repairs we want one alongside us. Most do not carry a certifier for data cable.

The question to ask any bidder is simple: which tester will you use, will I get a saved result for each repaired link, and what is your New York State low-voltage licence number. Ours is #12000287431.

“Wi-Fi mesh makes Cat6 repair unnecessary”

A common answer to a dead wall jack is to skip the cable and add a mesh node. In a small open house that can be reasonable.

Putnam houses are often long, multi-level, built into slopes and spread across additions, with stone, foil-faced insulation or a garage between the router and the room that needs the connection. Mesh nodes relaying wirelessly through that lose much of their throughput; the same systems are designed to use wired backhaul where it exists. Cameras, access points and home offices that depend on stable links still need the cable.

So mesh is a reason to repair Cat6, not a replacement for it: the wired backhaul is what makes the mesh work.

“Cable faults after a storm are always lightning”

After a storm, published advice tends to go straight to surge damage. Surges do happen, and Putnam recorded 75,093 lightning pulses in 2020 according to Earth Networks’ state report.

But in this county wind and trees cause as much damage as lightning. On May 15, 2018, the outages that hit 28,459 NYSEG customers in Putnam came with reports of tornado-like winds in Carmel. Limbs pull down aerial cable, flex entries and crack plugs; flooding like Putnam Valley’s in July 2026 puts water into foundation entries and basement panels.

The repair depends on which happened, and the test pattern usually shows it: surge tends to take out ports and devices at both ends of an exposed run, while mechanical and water damage shows up as a fault at a particular distance. We test before assuming either.

Lake and Second Homes

Opening a Putnam Lake House: A Cat6 Checklist

Owners who use a Putnam lake house part of the year, or rent it out, find their Cat6 problems the day they arrive. A short routine at opening time catches most of them while there is still time to book a repair before the first weekend.

  1. Power up in order. Provider equipment first, then the router, then the switch, then access points and cameras. Wait for each to settle. Many “dead” links on opening day are equipment that started in the wrong order.
  2. Walk every jack. Plug a laptop into each wall jack you rely on and note which ones link, at what speed, and which do not. A link at 100 Mbps instead of 1 Gbps usually means a damaged pair.
  3. Look at the outdoor ends. Check cameras, the dock or boathouse link and anything on the garage. Cracked plugs, sagging cable and water stains under boxes are worth a photo.
  4. Check the basement or crawlspace. Look for water marks near the panel and where cables enter the foundation. Do not unplug or handle anything that is wet.
  5. Write down what failed. A list of jacks and devices, with photos, lets us bring the right parts and often finish in one visit.
  6. Ask about a closing test. If a link failed last season and was patched, a certification test at closing time gives you a baseline to compare against when you reopen.

If you are not at the house, a caretaker or neighbour can do most of this. We can work with them on site, and send you the results.

Before You Call

Five Checks a Putnam Owner Can Make in Ten Minutes

None of these needs tools, and each one either solves the problem or makes the repair visit shorter.

  • Swap the patch cord at both ends. Cords get crushed under desks and chairs and pulled by vacuum cleaners. A known-good cord rules them out in a minute.
  • Try another device on the same jack. If a laptop links where the camera or printer would not, the device or its settings may be the problem, not the cable.
  • Move the cord to a different switch port. If the link comes back, the switch port is suspect; if not, the cable or jack is more likely.
  • Note whether one thing failed or everything did. Everything at once points to power, the router or the provider; one jack or one camera points to that run.
  • Think about what changed. A storm, a renovation, a provider visit, a new appliance plugged into the same outlet, a cleaner moving furniture. The last change before a failure is the best clue we get.

What not to do: do not open a panel or a jack that has been wet, do not join a damaged cable with tape or electrical connectors, and do not re-hang a torn overhead span without a support wire. Each of those creates a harder repair later.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Repairing Instead of Replacing

How we approach a failed run on a rocky, wooded Putnam lot — general practice, not one particular call

We start with the end we can stand next to. Most failed links on a Putnam property have at least one end in a basement, a closet or a garage, and that is where we connect the tester. Before anything else we read the distance to the fault and compare it with the length of the run. If the fault is within a metre of either end, the ground, the wall and the lake buffer never come into it: we cut back, re-terminate and certify.

If the fault is out on the run, we ask what is at that distance. A foundation wall. A tree the span passes under. The corner where a porch was enclosed. The place where the renovation crew hung new cabinets. A measured distance plus a walk around the property usually points to one spot, and we open that spot and nothing else.

Only when the test shows damage spread along the cable, or the cable is simply wrong for where it lies, do we talk about replacement — and then we start with the route that is already there: the old pipe, the old path, the surface of a wall. A new trench through Highlands rock is the last option, not the first. Before we leave, every repaired link is certified and the result goes on file, so that the next time a storm comes through you have a number to compare against.

Can you repair Cat6 that runs down to a dock or boathouse on Lake Mahopac?

Our builder used shielded Cat6 — does that change how a repair is done?
+

Yes. Shielded cable has to be repaired with shielded jacks and plugs, and the shield has to be carried through every termination and connected at the panel end as the system was designed; mixing unshielded parts into a shielded link, or leaving a shield unconnected, can make performance worse rather than better. We confirm what kind of cable and hardware is installed before we start, bring matching shielded parts, and certify the repaired link the same way.
