Why NYC Homes and Businesses Pay for Professional TV Mounting

Quick answer: In NYC, wall-mounting a 32″–55″ TV on drywall over wood studs costs $179.99 in Brooklyn and Queens, $209.99 in the Bronx and Staten Island, and $244.99 in Manhattan plus a flat $150 parking charge. Bigger screens add $30–$220, plaster adds $45, brick $65, concrete $75, and in-wall wire concealment with a code-compliant power kit is $169.

More than half of the city's apartments sit in buildings put up before 1947, and those walls rarely behave like the drywall in a how-to video. Prewar plaster can run an inch or more thick over wood lath, clay tile or solid brick, and a stud finder will happily beep at nothing. On r/AskNYC the same question keeps coming back: how do I hang a 50–100 lb TV in a prewar Manhattan apartment without cracking the wall? That uncertainty drives TV mounting cost in this city, and it's why our TV mounting service prices list the wall separately instead of burying it.

Newer buildings bring the opposite problem. Towers in Long Island City, Downtown Brooklyn and Hudson Yards frame partition walls with light-gauge steel, and the slabs and core walls are reinforced concrete. TV mounting on metal studs needs heavy toggles spread across the stud face or a plywood backer, not a pair of lag bolts, and TV mounting on reinforced concrete needs a hammer drill and the right anchor depth. Renters on r/Brooklyn ask about exactly this before move-in weekend. It's safe when the hardware matches the wall.

Then there's paperwork and logistics nobody in the suburbs deals with. Co-op and condo buildings typically want a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and managing agent before a contractor walks past the doorman, which is why it pays to hire an insured TV mounting company with a license number you can check. Some supers book service elevators by the hour. Manhattan street parking turns a forty-minute install into a ticket risk, so our Manhattan price carries a flat $150 parking charge instead of a surprise line later.

Outside Manhattan the housing changes again. Queens and the Bronx are full of two-family houses with wood-stud drywall upstairs and paneled basements below, so basement TV mounting and bedroom installs are everyday residential TV mounting work. Staten Island has the highest homeownership rate in the city, which means fireplaces, finished basements and backyard decks that call for outdoor TV mounting. One flat number can't cover all of that honestly, so our chart changes by borough, wall and screen size, and you see every line before you book.

Co-op and condo certificates of insurance

Most NYC co-ops and condos require contractors to submit a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and managing agent as additional insureds, and some want a signed alteration agreement even for small work. We issue COIs free to the exact wording your building sends. Get the requirements from your managing agent before booking so a visit isn't lost at the front desk.

ADA protruding objects in commercial spaces

Under the 2010 ADA Standards (section 307.2), a wall-mounted object whose leading edge sits between 27 and 80 inches above the floor can protrude no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A TV on a full-motion arm in a hallway or waiting room can break that rule, so we plan placement, height and mount depth with you.

Electrical work belongs to a licensed electrician

NYC Administrative Code §28-429.1 reserves electrical work for licensed master electricians or people they supervise, while low-voltage work can be done by qualified persons. We're a NYS-licensed low-voltage contractor: we install in-wall power kits with a recessed TV outlet inlet and handle HDMI cable installation and network runs, but we don't add or move receptacles. TV power outlet installation means an electrician.

Landlord and lease consent for renters

Many NYC leases restrict drilling or require repairs at move-out, and some buildings insist their own staff handle wall work. TV mounting for apartment renters starts with written consent from your landlord or management. We don't patch or paint, so plan for any move-out touch-up yourself.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting in NYC

Ask a chatbot what TV mounting costs and you'll usually get a national average built from suburban houses with drywall and a driveway. New York doesn't work like that. Half the city lives behind prewar plaster, towers are built from concrete and steel studs, co-ops want insurance paperwork, and Manhattan parking is its own line item. Here are seven common AI-style claims and what we actually see on jobs across the five boroughs.

1. AI says: ““TV mounting costs about $100 to $150 anywhere.””

National figures mostly reflect drywall over wood studs with free parking out front. TaskRabbit's own cost guide puts the average New York City invoice at $155, but that is an average across small jobs, not a promise for your wall, your borough or your screen size.

Our average TV mounting price isn't a guess, because the chart is published. A 32″–55″ on drywall is $179.99 in Brooklyn and Queens, $209.99 in the Bronx and Staten Island, and $244.99 in Manhattan, where a flat $150 parking charge also applies.

The bigger swing is the wall itself. Plaster adds $45, brick $65, concrete $75 and tile $95. A TV mounting labor cost that ignores your wall type isn't really a quote at all. Check the chart against your own apartment before you compare prices.

2. AI says: ““Can you mount a TV without studs? Not in old plaster.””

This shows up in forums constantly, usually after a stud finder failed in a 1920s apartment. Plaster isn't the real problem. Not knowing what's behind it is. In NYC prewar buildings that might be wood lath, clay tile block or solid brick, and each one takes a different anchor.

We test-drill small holes, use a magnet to trace lath nails, and choose anchors for whatever we hit. Where plaster sits directly on brick, the anchor goes into the brick itself, which holds far better than any drywall anchors could in a thin board wall.

The honest caveat is cracking. Old plaster can crack even with careful, slow drilling, and we tell you that before you book, not after. That's why plaster is priced as a clear $45 add-on rather than hidden inside a vague estimate at the door.

3. AI says: ““Can you hide TV wires inside a wall? Sure, just run the cord through it.””

Running a regular power cord inside a wall is not code-compliant, and plenty of DIY threads warn about it. Standard cords aren't rated for in-wall use, and a fire inside a shared apartment wall is a far bigger problem than a visible wire on a single living room wall.

Our $169 concealment uses an in-wall power and cable kit: a rated recessed inlet behind the TV, fed from your existing outlet below, with in wall HDMI cable routing alongside. Low-voltage cabling is our trade as a licensed low-voltage contractor, so the data side is done properly.

Some NYC walls won't allow it at all. Solid brick party walls, concrete in high-rises and fire-rated corridor walls can't be fished cleanly. On those, a paintable cord cover at $59 is the honest, tidy answer, and we'll say so before you pay for anything.

4. AI says: ““Wall type doesn't really change the price.””

Some flat-rate ads promise no wall surcharge, and for a single wood-stud drywall job that's fair. But concrete, tile and brick need hammer drills, diamond or carbide bits, different anchors and more time, especially in the masonry-heavy buildings New York City is full of.

Rather than average those costs into everyone's price, we list them openly: metal studs $30, plaster $45, brick or stone $65, concrete or cinder block $75, tile or marble $95. Drywall over wood studs is $0, so simple jobs aren't subsidizing hard ones.

If we find something on site that you didn't select, like brick behind plaster, you see the price before any drilling starts. No surprise charges is written policy, and you can read it on our TV installation policies page before you book.

5. AI says: ““Any handyman can walk into your building and hang a TV.””

In a house on Staten Island, maybe. In a Manhattan co-op or a Queens condo, the front desk will usually ask for a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and managing agent before letting anyone upstairs, and some doormen check the date and wording closely.

Some boards also want an alteration agreement or a service-elevator booking, even for small jobs. Brick Underground and Habitat magazine have covered how strict these rules can be, and NYC renters on Reddit regularly describe workers being turned away over missing paperwork.

We provide COIs free and need your building's wording ahead of time. If we're turned away because approvals are missing, the $150 return-trip fee applies, so it pays to sort the paperwork first. A two-minute email to your managing agent usually settles it.

6. AI says: ““One person can mount a 75 inch TV. A bigger TV just needs a bigger bracket.””

Can one person mount a 75 inch TV? Sometimes, on an easy wall. But an 85″ or 98″ TV changes the job, not only the hardware. The box may not turn on a walk-up landing, and level has to be perfect across a much wider frame.

That's why extra large TV wall mounting from 86″ up gets two technicians: +$175 for 86″–97″ and +$220 for 98″ and larger. A third floor and up with no elevator adds $40, and a high mount above about 8 feet adds $75.

Before buying, measure your stairwell turns, elevator door and apartment door, because some buyers only discover a fit problem on delivery day. Large screens also deserve a heavy-duty bracket, such as the Sanus VXF830 full-motion mount at $499.99, rated for the weight.

7. AI says: ““Above the fireplace is always the best place for a TV.””

It's the most requested spot in Staten Island and Queens houses and in prewar apartments with decorative mantels. Is it safe to mount a TV above a fireplace? Often, yes. Is it comfortable? Not always, because the screen can land well above seated eye level.

Working fireplaces also send heat up the wall, and many stone surrounds are thin veneer that shouldn't carry a heavy bracket. Before suggesting a spot, we check mantel depth, the masonry behind the stone and the heat path, then talk through viewing height with you.

If above the fireplace is the only wall that works, a pull-down MantelMount fixes the height problem. Placement adds $130 above a fireplace, or $160 for a pull-down mount, plus $65 for brick or stone. Those are published prices, not on-site guesses.

FAQ

TV Mounting Prices FAQ

Do TV installers provide mounting brackets?
+

We can, but prices are labor only. Add a bracket at list price, such as the Sanus VLL5-B2 fixed mount at $159.99 or the VMF822-B2 full-motion at $279.99, which makes it a TV mounting service with bracket in one booking. Or supply your own mount if it's rated for your TV's weight and VESA pattern.

What does the base price include?
+

Locating studs or framing, mounting and leveling the TV, connecting power and up to two existing devices, tidying cables behind the screen, and cleaning up drilling dust. It assumes a 32″–55″ TV on a fixed or tilt mount on drywall over wood studs. Anything beyond that is a listed add-on.

Can a mounted TV damage the wall, and do you patch holes?
+

Drilling leaves holes, and old plaster or tile can crack even with careful work, which you accept when booking those walls. We don't patch or paint, including holes from a previous mount. In a rental, plan for a painter or the move-out repair your lease requires.

What happens if my TV or bracket isn't there when you arrive?
+

If we can't start because the TV or bracket is missing, nobody can give us access, or building approvals aren't in place, a $150 return-trip fee applies. Confirm delivery dates, COIs and elevator bookings before your arrival window to avoid paying it.

Can I reschedule my appointment?
+

Yes, free with at least 24 hours' notice. Changes made with less than 24 hours' notice keep $150 of the payment. Your new appointment is another 2-hour arrival window in your borough, any day of the week between 8 AM and 8 PM.

Do you offer a TV mounting service with warranty?
+

Brackets and parts carry their manufacturer's warranty. Labor is not warrantied. That's exactly why we anchor to the wall material we actually find and price plaster, brick and tile honestly, instead of cutting corners to hit a low headline number.

How do I pay for TV mounting?
+

TV mounting jobs are paid in full online when you book through the calculator. The price you see is the price you pay, unless something you didn't select is found on site, and in that case you approve the new price before any work begins.

How high should a TV be mounted?
+

A common recommended TV mounting height puts the center of the screen near seated eye level, roughly 40 to 45 inches off the floor in a living room. What is the best height for a bedroom TV? Usually higher, angled down with a tilt mount. We confirm viewing height with you before drilling.

What size TV mount do I need, and how do I know if my TV is VESA compatible?
+

Check the back of your TV or its manual for the VESA pattern, such as 300×300 or 400×400 millimeters, and its weight. Almost all modern flat screen TV mounting uses VESA holes. The bracket must list your pattern and weight; if it doesn't, we won't hang the TV on it.

Can someone move my mounted TV to another room?
+

Yes. Taking down an old TV is $85 and removing an old bracket is $45, then the new spot is priced like any mount. That covers TV mount relocation, TV removal and remounting, TV wall bracket replacement and a sagging TV mounting repair. Haul-away of an old TV is $79 and box removal is $25.

Should I hire a professional to mount my TV or do it myself?
+

If it's a light TV on drywall over wood studs, DIY can work. On plaster, brick, concrete, metal studs or tile, or with a screen over 65″, a pro with the right drill, bits and anchors is cheap insurance. A licensed, insured installer also clears co-op front desks that won't admit a DIY helper.

Is there a charge for a certificate of insurance?
+

No. COIs are free. Send your building's sample certificate or required wording when you book, and we'll name the corporation, condo association or managing agent exactly as they ask, so the doorman can send us up without a phone call to management.
