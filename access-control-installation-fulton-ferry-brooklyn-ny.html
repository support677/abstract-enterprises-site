Fulton Ferry is the small wedge of nineteenth-century Brooklyn at the foot of Old Fulton Street, squeezed between the Brooklyn Bridge, the East River and the park entrance at Pier 1. Its handful of old commercial buildings were turned into apartments decades ago and now sit on one of the busiest walking routes in the borough: visitors coming down from the bridge, ferry riders heading for the landing, and long weekend lines for pizza and ice cream that stretch along the sidewalk past residential front doors. Entry control here is less about big lobbies and more about a dozen or so households, or a few dozen, keeping one old street door working, locked and quietly anonymous while thousands of strangers walk past it. We install fob, card, phone and keypad entry, video call stations and the locks behind them for Fulton Ferry co-ops, condos, restaurants and offices, planned around the crowds, the landmark rules and the boards that run these buildings themselves.

The Neighborhood

Why Fulton Ferry Properties Need Access Control

The place is older than the bridge above it. Brooklyn Bridge Park describes the Fulton Ferry Landing pier at the foot of Old Fulton Street as the site of the first ferry service between Brooklyn and Manhattan, in 1642. The Fulton Ferry Company began regular service in 1814, the same year the old Ferry Road was renamed Fulton Street after Robert Fulton, and by 1872 there were about 1,200 crossings a day. Hotels, taverns, a newspaper, banks and insurance offices grew up around the landing because everyone travelling between the two cities passed through it. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s designation report calls Fulton Street the district’s “main street” and explains what happened next: the Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883, traffic moved up onto the span, and the ferry district slowly drained. The ferry stopped in 1924.

What survived is a short run of brick and cast-iron commercial buildings. The National Register listing for the Fulton Ferry District, made in 1974, counts 15 contributing buildings built between 1830 and 1895, mostly two to four stories, plus one eight-story building, the Eagle Warehouse of 1893. The city designated its own Fulton Ferry Historic District on June 28, 1977. Its report lists, among others, a commercial row of stores with lodgings above built between 1836 and 1839, the Franklin House hotel and dining saloon at the Water Street corner, the 1834 office of the Long Island Insurance Company on Front Street — which the Historic Districts Council calls possibly the oldest surviving office building in the city — the cast-iron Long Island Safe Deposit Company of 1868–69, and the Brooklyn City Railroad Company’s office of 1860–61 with cast-iron piers at street level.

Those buildings became homes in the 1970s and 1980s. The same LPC report notes that the first sign of new life on the street was the conversion of the old railroad company building into apartments; real-estate records put that conversion in the mid-1970s, with about ten loft apartments, two to a floor, under an offering plan dated December 1975. The Eagle Warehouse, built for the storage of household furniture and silverware with vaults in its cellar, was converted to a cooperative of roughly 84 apartments in 1980, early in the wave that later reached the factory lofts up the hill. We name these buildings only to show what kind of building Fulton Ferry has; they are not AESS clients, and nothing on this page describes work done in them.

The result is a neighborhood of small, owner-run residential buildings that happens to sit at a major public entrance. In 2018 the Brooklyn Eagle listed only four ways into Brooklyn Bridge Park — Atlantic Avenue, Joralemon Street, the Squibb Bridge and Old Fulton Street — and the park reported that nearly 5 million visitors came during its 2023 season. The park is open from 6 a.m. to 1 a.m. every day. NYC Ferry boats call at the landing on the East River and South Brooklyn routes. Grimaldi’s, on the Front Street corner, does not take reservations, and Wikipedia notes that its lines for tables often extend far up Old Fulton Street. Ice cream, a lobster shack, a concert barge, a famous restaurant on the water and a hotel at Pier 1 all add to the foot traffic.

For a building with eleven or eighty-four apartments, that traffic changes what a front door has to do. The door is seen, photographed, leaned on and tried by more strangers in a summer weekend than a side-street door sees in a year. A line of visitors may stand directly in front of it. People looking for a restroom, a phone charger or directions press buttons on the call panel. Couriers wait there because it is the only doorway out of the sun. And on a handful of days a year — the Fourth of July above all — the police close the surrounding streets and residents have to prove they live on their own block. An entry system for Fulton Ferry has to let residents in quickly through all of that, lock behind them every time, and give strangers nothing useful to work with.

Crime on these blocks follows the same pattern as anywhere crowded: opportunistic, quick and often not through the front door at all. A Brooklyn Paper 84th Precinct report from December 2019 described a burglary of an Old Fulton Street apartment between Water and Front Streets at 8 a.m., in which the burglar climbed in through a bedroom window and took bags, shoes and jackets worth more than $12,000. That is worth remembering throughout this page. A reader on the lobby door controls the lobby door. It does nothing for a ground-floor window, a fire escape or a door that does not close. We plan for what the system can actually cover and say plainly what it cannot.

Fulton Ferry at a glance: the facts behind this page

  • Two historic designations: the National Register’s Fulton Ferry District, listed June 28, 1974 (16 acres, 15 contributing buildings from 1830 to 1895, bisected overhead by the Brooklyn Bridge), and the city’s Fulton Ferry Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on June 28, 1977.
  • Old streets under new names: the LPC report records that the old Ferry Road became Fulton Street in 1814 and was known within the district as Cadman Plaza West when the report was written; today the addresses read Old Fulton Street, and City Council legislation (Int 0956-2026) co-names its corner with Front Street “Patsy and Carol Grimaldi Way”.
  • A park gateway: Old Fulton Street is one of four entrances to Brooklyn Bridge Park named by the Brooklyn Eagle in 2018; the park is 85 acres along 1.3 miles of waterfront and is open 6 a.m. to 1 a.m.
  • Crowds measured in millions: Brooklyn Bridge Park reported nearly 5 million visitors in its 2023 season.
  • A working landing: Fulton Ferry has had ferry service to Manhattan again since 2006, and NYC Ferry’s East River and South Brooklyn routes have served it since 2017.
  • Local administration: ZIP code 11201, Brooklyn Community District 2, and the 84th Precinct at 301 Gold Street.

Most Fulton Ferry buildings that add a reader also end up replacing the old buzzer panel and adding a camera that looks at the inside of the entrance, and we can do all three in the same visit if the board wants it.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Fulton Ferry’s Architecture

Fulton Ferry has fewer building types than most Brooklyn neighborhoods, but each one sets its own limits on where a reader goes, how a lock is fitted and how cable gets from the door to a controller.

1830s commercial rows

Brick buildings of three or four stories built as stores with lodgings upstairs, now apartments above restaurants or shops. Expect one narrow residential door beside a storefront, a stair straight up from it, little or no lobby, and walls of old brick and plaster that do not welcome new cable.

Cast-iron fronts

Bank and office buildings of the 1860s with facades made partly or entirely of cast iron. Iron is decorative, brittle and protected; we mount equipment inside the door opening, on the door frame or in the vestibule rather than drilling the facade.

Warehouse co-ops

Large masonry warehouses converted to cooperatives around 1980, with deep walls, heavy arched entrances, freight-era stairs and big shared lobbies. These are the buildings most likely to need two doors controlled, a package arrangement and several administrators.

Converted office and loft co-ops

Mid-nineteenth-century office buildings turned into a few loft apartments per floor in the 1970s. Small boards, no staff, one street door, an intercom that is often as old as the conversion, and residents who know each other by name.

Riverfront warehouses

Brick storage buildings on the water side of Water Street, now mostly cultural, retail and event uses. Access work here is commercial: staff and performer entrances, loading doors, office suites and rooms that open only on event nights.

New buildings at Pier 1

Twenty-first-century condominiums and a hotel on Furman Street by the park, with staffed lobbies, amenity floors and garages. Here the questions are about sharing amenities between residents and hotel guests and keeping the two sets of credentials apart.

How the old materials change the work

Brick bearing walls. In the older rows the walls carry the building, and they are often more than a foot thick. A cable from a reader on the street side to a controller inside goes through the wall once, in a sleeve, at a mortar joint, and is sealed against water on the outside. We avoid running cable across the face of the facade, and we never cut into brick to bury it.

Cast iron. Iron columns and panels are hollow in places, solid in others and easily cracked by a hammer drill. Where a building has an iron front, our default is to keep every fastener off the iron. A reader can sit on the wooden or steel door frame, inside the reveal of the opening, or just inside the door; cable comes through the frame or the floor rather than the column.

Heavy timber doors and arched openings. The original entrances in the warehouse buildings are often pairs of tall wooden doors set in round arches. They swing heavily, they move with the seasons, and the frames are not square. Before any electric lock goes on, the doors have to close and latch reliably on their own, so many of our Fulton Ferry quotes include rehanging, a new closer or hinge work before the electronics.

Plaster, tin and landmarked interiors. Inside, old buildings have plaster walls, pressed-metal ceilings and wood trim that owners want kept. We look first for existing routes — the old intercom cable, a dumbwaiter shaft, a pipe chase, the space behind a baseboard — and use surface raceway only where there is no other way, painted to match.

Doors on a Visitor Route

One Saturday at a Fulton Ferry Front Door

The easiest way to explain how we design for this neighborhood is to follow an ordinary residential entrance through an ordinary summer Saturday. This is a composite drawn from how the blocks around the landing work, not a record of any particular building.

Seven in the morning. The street is quiet. A resident leaves for the first ferry; a dog walker comes in; the restaurant downstairs takes a produce delivery at its own door. This is the only time of day the entrance behaves like any other Brooklyn front door, and it is when we prefer to work.

Late morning. Tour groups start coming down from the bridge toward the park. People stop on the step to look at a map, and someone photographs the old door because it is beautiful. A reader mounted at eye level on the facade is right in the frame. A panel with forty name buttons tells everyone looking at it that apartments are upstairs, and which name lives in which one.

Lunch. The line for pizza runs along the sidewalk. Depending on how the restaurant manages it, people may be standing shoulder to shoulder against the building, including in front of the residential door. A resident coming home with groceries has to ask people to step aside, reach past them for the reader, push the door open against the crowd and then wait for the door to close before strangers drift into the vestibule. If the closer is weak, the door does not finish closing at all.

Afternoon. Couriers arrive for several apartments within the same hour. Visitors looking for restrooms press call buttons at random. Somebody leans against the door and it moves, so they try the handle. If the latch did not catch properly the last time someone came through, the door opens.

Evening. The crowd thins but does not leave. The park stays open until 1 a.m., the restaurants are full, and on warm nights people sit on any step they can find. A late call on the panel is more likely to be a stranger than a guest.

Once or twice a year. A major event closes the surrounding streets entirely. For the 2026 Fourth of July fireworks, Brooklyn Bridge Park was open only to holders of the city’s free lottery tickets, and Bushwick Daily, citing the Brooklyn Eagle, reported that NYPD checkpoints in Brooklyn Heights would start at 1 p.m., that residents would need ID or other documentation to reach their blocks, and that towing would start the night before. On those days deliveries, contractors and guests cannot reach the door the normal way at all.

Every part of a good Fulton Ferry system answers one of those moments. The lock has to re-latch every single time, so the door and closer get attention before the electronics. The reader has to be reachable from the doorway itself, not from the sidewalk where the line stands. The call panel should reveal as little as possible and respond differently at midnight than at noon. Residents need a credential that works one-handed and fast. And the board needs a simple way to let a guest or tradesperson in on a day when the street is closed. None of this is exotic equipment; it is ordinary access control set up for an unusual sidewalk.

Landmark District

Working Inside the Fulton Ferry Historic District

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fulton Ferry Historic District on June 28, 1977. Its boundary, as written in the designation report, runs from Cadman Plaza West and Doughty Street down to the river and along Water Street, and takes in the old commercial buildings on Old Fulton and Front Streets, Columbia Heights at the foot of the hill, and the warehouses on the water side of Water Street. If your building is inside that line, any change to its exterior needs LPC approval, and that includes a reader, a call station or a conduit on the outside of the building.

The good news is that ordinary entry equipment usually qualifies for a staff-level permit rather than a public hearing. The LPC’s Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety work allows staff to approve an intercom or doorbell placed within or next to the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the building; it limits each opening to one intercom and two light fixtures; it asks that equipment be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork rather than cast iron or decorative features; and it asks for conduit to be concealed, with only short painted runs left visible. The same chapter treats installations on the primary facade as a last resort and limits the number of exterior cameras on small buildings. Interior work, including everything behind the front door, does not need LPC approval at all.

For Fulton Ferry, that guidance points in a clear direction. Put the reader and the call station in the door opening or the vestibule, not on the face of the building. Choose slim units that fit inside a reveal. Keep every fastener off cast iron and carved stone. Bring the cable through the door frame or the floor rather than across the front. Then the LPC application is short, the equipment is protected from the crowd, and the building looks the way it did. We prepare the drawings and photographs the permit application needs and can submit it on the owner’s behalf; the LPC’s preservation staff can be reached at 212-669-7817 for questions about a specific building.

The National Register listing is a separate matter. Under federal law, the National Park Service says, listing places no restrictions on what a non-federal owner does with private property unless a project involves federal funding, licensing or permits. In practice the city designation is the one that governs a Fulton Ferry front door.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Fulton Ferry

Fulton Ferry’s businesses are mostly about food, drink and visitors, which means long hours, many staff on rotating shifts and deliveries at all times of day. Most of them share a building with residents, which shapes how their doors should be controlled.

Old Fulton Street: restaurants under apartments

The blocks of Old Fulton Street below the bridge are lined with pizzerias, cafes and shops on the ground floors of old buildings that have apartments above. Juliana’s occupies 19 Old Fulton Street, the original location where Patsy and Carol Grimaldi opened their pizzeria in 1990, and the Brooklyn Ice Cream Factory, which left the fireboat house on the pier in 2019, reopened in March 2021 at 14 Old Fulton Street, across the street from its old spot. These are named as landmarks of the street, not as clients. For a business like these, the doors that matter are the staff entrance, the cellar and stockroom, and the office where cash and records are kept, and each should open on its own schedule for its own staff without ever opening the residents’ stair.

Front Street and the Water Street corner

Grimaldi’s moved to 1 Front Street in 2011, into the block that also holds the cast-iron Long Island Safe Deposit Company building and the 1834 insurance office. The corner of Old Fulton and Water Streets was once the Franklin House hotel. Businesses on these corners have street doors that face crowds directly and back-of-house doors that open onto narrow side passages. We usually recommend a keypad or reader on the staff door with personal codes for each employee, so that the morning opener and the late closer can be told apart in the log and a departing employee’s code can be deleted the same day.

The landing and the river

At the foot of Old Fulton Street, the pier holds the old fireboat house, a lobster shack in the former smokestack building, the Bargemusic concert barge, and the River Café at 1 Water Street, which was heavily damaged in Hurricane Sandy in 2012 and reopened in February 2014. The pier itself is part of Brooklyn Bridge Park, so before we quote any work on a business that operates on park property, the operator needs to confirm with the park what it allows.

Water Street’s warehouses

The LPC report places the Tobacco Inspection Warehouse of the 1850s and the Empire Stores, built in two stages in 1870 and 1885, on the river side of Water Street inside the district. Large converted warehouses like these hold offices, shops and event spaces, and their access needs are commercial: separate tenants with their own administrators, performer or vendor entrances that open only for an event, and loading doors with tight schedules.

Furman Street by Pier 1

The newer buildings at Pier 1 combine condominiums with a hotel and share some amenities between them. Pierhouse, at 90 Furman Street, is a 109-unit condominium developed by Toll Brothers whose residents, according to its sales materials, have use of amenities at the adjoining 1 Hotel Brooklyn Bridge. Arrangements like that need careful separation between a hotel’s guest keys and residents’ credentials, which we discuss in the questions below.

Rules that apply to Fulton Ferry entry systems

  • Electrically locked doors on the way out. New York City Building Code §1010.1.9.8 (sensor release) and §1010.1.9.9 (door hardware release) set the conditions for locking an exit door electrically: release on loss of power, release by the fire alarm or sprinkler system where present, a manual “push to exit” device within reach, and listed hardware. A Fulton Ferry front door must always open from the inside without a credential.
  • Apartment building doors. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing and self-locking entrance doors on multiple dwellings and, under the conditions it sets, an intercom; Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043 covers locks and keys on apartment doors. Converting a buzzer to a card system does not remove those duties.
  • Smart access in residential buildings. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) governs owners of Class A multiple dwellings that use electronic or app-based entry: a written data policy, consent, limits on what is collected and how long authentication data is kept, and a ban on making a smart system the only way into a tenant’s own apartment. Co-op and condo boards should ask their counsel how it applies to their building.
  • Short-term rentals. Local Law 18 requires short-term rental hosts to register with the Mayor’s Office of Special Enforcement and bars booking platforms from processing transactions for unregistered rentals. OSE keeps a Prohibited Buildings List, and owners can notify OSE that rentals are prohibited in their building.
  • Landmarks. Exterior equipment in the Fulton Ferry Historic District needs LPC approval, usually at staff level under the Permit Guidebook guidance above.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Fulton Ferry: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come out of Fulton Ferry’s particular mix of old buildings, small boards and heavy foot traffic. Each is answered directly underneath.

Front Doors Beside the Park Entrance

On weekends the line for the pizzeria runs right past our front door. Where should the reader go so residents are not reaching through a queue to get home?

Inside the door opening, not on the facade beside it. When the reader sits on the wall next to the door, a resident has to stand where the line is standing, reach across whoever is there, and then step back to open the door. When it sits in the reveal of the doorway or on the door frame itself, the resident steps into the doorway, presents a fob or phone, and pushes the door in one movement, using the opening as a small private space. On most old Fulton Ferry entrances the reveal is deep enough for a slim reader, and the LPC prefers equipment there anyway.

The second part of the answer is speed. A reader that needs the fob held flat for a second, or a keypad that needs six digits typed, is slow with people watching and pressing close. Fobs, cards and phone credentials that read at a few centimetres in a fraction of a second keep the resident in the doorway for the shortest possible time. We set the lock to release on the read and re-lock as soon as the door position switch sees the door shut, not after a fixed timer.

Visitors press our apartment buttons asking for a restroom, directions or a phone charge. Can the outside panel be set up so it doesn’t invite that?

Yes. The old panels that show every resident’s name next to a button are the worst possible design for a street with this much foot traffic: they announce that the building is residential, they give every passer-by a list of names, and they make random pressing easy. Modern call stations can work quite differently. A single-button station can ring a phone list in order or a designated board member. A keypad station can call apartments by a code that the resident gives to guests, with no names displayed. A scrolling directory can show names only after a visitor starts typing, or show unit numbers instead.

Night settings help too. Brooklyn Bridge Park stays open until 1 a.m., and the later it gets, the less likely a call is to be a real guest. Most call stations can be scheduled so that after a set hour calls go only to residents who have opted in, or the station shows a message asking visitors to phone the person they are visiting. Residents expecting a late guest can still let them in from their phone.

Keep the building’s duties in mind while doing this. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom in some apartment buildings, and whatever replaces the old panel has to let genuine visitors and emergency services reach residents. Hiding names is fine; making the building unreachable is not. We set the panel up so that the people who should be able to call still can, and everybody else finds nothing worth pressing.

On the Fourth of July the police set checkpoints and residents had to show ID to reach their own blocks. What should a building set up before a closure day like that?

Plan the day like a short building shutdown. For the 2026 fireworks, the city gave out 100,000 free tickets for viewing areas at Brooklyn Bridge Park and the Seaport, the park was closed to anyone without a ticket, and reporting at the time described NYPD checkpoints in Brooklyn Heights from 1 p.m., ID or other documentation for residents, passes available from the local Council Member’s office, and towing starting the night before. Fulton Ferry sits right at the edge of that zone. Residents generally got home; the people who struggled were everybody else.

So set the system up for everybody else. A week ahead, the board should send residents the closure details and remind them that guests, cleaners, caregivers and dog walkers may not reach the door on time. Any regular service person who will still come should have a credential that works that day. Deliveries are better moved to the day before. If the building has a restaurant tenant, agree what its staff door does when the street is closed. On the day, a volunteer should check that the door-held alert is working, because crowds pressing against a building are exactly when a propped door goes unnoticed.

Check two things in advance: that every resident has a working fob or card in case crowded cell networks slow phone credentials, and that any temporary “residents only” setting on the call station works. The plan can be reused every year.

A burglar got into an Old Fulton Street apartment through a bedroom window. Would a reader on the front door have changed anything?

Not in that case, and it is important to be honest about why. The Brooklyn Paper’s report on the December 2019 burglary says the burglar entered the apartment, between Water and Front Streets, through a bedroom window at 8 a.m. and left with bags, shoes and jackets worth more than $12,000. A card reader on the building’s street door controls who passes through that door. It has no effect on a window, a fire escape, a roof hatch or a rear yard.

What it can do is close off the other common routes: the propped street door, the copied key in the hands of a former tenant or contractor, the inner door left unlocked because the outer one is “good enough”. For windows, the right tools are different. Window locks or limiters on ground-floor and fire-escape windows, alarm contacts and glass-break sensors connected to a monitored alarm, and lighting on any rear or side area that a person could reach from the street. In some buildings a camera inside the entrance and one at the rear complete the picture.

Old Buildings and Small Co-ops

Our building’s front is cast iron. Where can a reader or call station be mounted without drilling into the ironwork?

There are almost always three better places than the iron. The first is the door frame: on most Fulton Ferry entrances the residential door hangs in a wooden or steel frame set inside the iron opening, and a slim reader fixes to that frame with ordinary screws. The second is the reveal, the inside face of the opening between the facade and the door, where a reader or a narrow call station can sit flush and out of the weather. The third is just inside the door, which works well for keypads and for readers that can read through glass.

The LPC’s Permit Guidebook supports this. Its guidance on intercoms and doorbells asks for equipment to be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork rather than cast iron or decorative elements, and to sit within or next to the door opening. Following that guidance usually means a staff-level permit instead of a hearing.

Our entrance is a pair of tall doors and only one leaf ever opens. How is a pair like that wired and locked?

The usual answer is to treat the pair as one active door and one fixed door. The inactive leaf is held shut top and bottom with flush bolts or surface bolts, so it acts as part of the wall. The active leaf gets the electric lock, the closer and the reader. On a pair of wooden doors that is most often an electric strike in the frame on the latch side, or, where the doors meet in the middle without a frame, an electrified lock or exit device mounted on the active leaf with power brought across through a concealed hinge or power transfer.

The meeting edge needs attention. On old doors there is usually an astragal, a strip covering the gap between the leaves, and the latch has to engage into the fixed leaf or into a strike plate on it. If the fixed leaf moves at all, the active leaf will not lock reliably, so we tighten or replace the bolts first. Tall doors also need a closer that is strong enough to pull them shut from wide open in a crosswind without slamming, which often means a heavier closer than the one in place.

Our entry hall is only a few feet deep and has one door to the street. Is a second, inner locked door worth adding, and is there room for one?

On a block like this, usually yes, if the space allows it. The value of an inner door is simple: if the street door is propped, held, or opened by someone following a resident, there is still a locked door between the sidewalk and the stairs. It turns a shallow hallway into a small vestibule where couriers can leave parcels and visitors can wait out of the crowd while someone comes down.

The question is space. The inner door needs room to swing without hitting the street door, and both doors still need to meet the exit requirements. In a narrow hall, a door that swings in the direction of exit travel, a sliding glass panel or a lightweight steel door in a slim frame may fit where a heavy wooden door would not. If the hall has a stair rising straight from it, the inner door can sometimes go at the foot of the stair instead. We measure and sketch the options during the survey, including whether the building’s fire separation or sprinkler layout limits where a door can go.

We are a self-managed co-op with no super. When the door won’t lock at 11 p.m., who is supposed to notice, and who do they call?

Decide that before installation, because in a building without staff nobody notices by default. We recommend naming two board members or volunteers as the people who receive alerts, so that one is always available. The system sends them a message if the street door is held open for more than a set time, is forced open, or loses power or its connection. The message says which door and what happened.

The next step is a written one-page procedure kept in the building and on each volunteer’s phone: first check the door in person or ask a neighbor to; if the closer has failed or the door is jammed, try the simple fixes; if the electronics have failed, call our repair line; if the door cannot be secured, a resident stays with it or it is locked mechanically with the key until we arrive. Our number goes on that page, along with the controller’s location and the location of the key.

Small co-ops also benefit from a short annual check that we can schedule: closer adjustment, a power-failure test, a fire alarm release test where the building has a fire alarm, a look at the battery backup, and a review of who still holds credentials. It is not a contract you have to sign; it is a visit you can book when the board decides it is time.

A shareholder has been renting their apartment to tourists by the night. Can the entry system help the board enforce a no-short-term-rental rule?

It can support enforcement but not replace it. Local Law 18 requires short-term rental hosts to register with the Mayor’s Office of Special Enforcement and stops booking platforms from processing transactions for unregistered rentals. OSE keeps a Prohibited Buildings List of buildings where short-term rentals are barred by law or by the building’s leases or occupancy agreements, and owners can notify OSE that their building is one of them. That legal step is the board’s, usually with its counsel, and it is the strongest tool available.

What the entry system adds is control over credentials. Each fob or phone credential is issued to a named person, so the board knows how many each apartment holds and can refuse to issue extras without a reason. Guest access can be time-limited and tied to the resident who requested it. If the proprietary lease or house rules prohibit transient use, the board has a clear record of what was issued to whom.

There are limits. A system should not be used as a surveillance tool against residents, and if your building is a Class A multiple dwelling, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law restricts what data can be collected and how it is used and kept. Patterns in entry logs are not proof of anything, and boards should get legal advice before acting on them. We set up the credential rules; the board and its lawyer decide how to use them.

The Waterfront Side of the Neighborhood

Our condominium shares amenities with the hotel next door. Should residents’ fobs and the hotel’s guest keys ever be on one system?

Usually not on one database, but they have to meet at the shared doors. Hotels issue guest keys from their own property-management systems, and those keys change daily; residential buildings issue long-term credentials to named owners and their households. Mixing the two in one list makes both harder to manage and raises privacy questions for the residents. The cleaner design keeps two systems, each with its own administrator, and lets them both operate the doors that are genuinely shared.

In practice, that means readers on the shared amenity doors that accept both credential types, or a pair of readers, one for each system, wired to the same lock. The residents’ lobby, stairs, elevators and garage stay on the residential system alone. The agreement between the condominium and the hotel decides which doors are shared and at what hours; the access system enforces what the agreement says.

Wind off the East River catches our street door so it either slams or hangs open. Does that matter for an electric lock?

It matters a great deal. An electric lock only secures a door that is closed, and a door that the wind holds open a few inches is unlocked no matter how good the reader is. A door that slams wears out the strike, loosens the frame and eventually stops latching altogether. Doors facing the river, or down a street that funnels wind from it, are exactly where we see this.

The fix is mechanical before it is electronic. A properly sized closer with adjustable closing and latching speeds pulls the door through the last few inches against the wind without slamming. Some closers have a backcheck that stops the door being blown back against the wall. A door position switch then tells the system whether the door is truly closed, and the held-open alert goes out if it is not. On the latch side, a strike suited to the door weight and fitted square to the latch keeps the door from bouncing off.

Equipment

Types of Access Control We Install in Fulton Ferry

The hardware below is what a Fulton Ferry building is most likely to need. We are not tied to one manufacturer; we choose by door, by building size and by who will run the system afterwards.

  • Slim mullion readers. Narrow readers designed for door frames and the reveals of old openings. They read encrypted fobs and cards and, on many models, phone credentials, and they are the default choice for a doorway where the facade is off limits.
  • Keypad and reader combinations. Units that take a fob, a card or a code, useful where some residents or staff prefer a code and others prefer a fob. Codes are personal, never shared across the building.
  • Video call stations. Door stations with a camera, a speaker and either a single call button, a keypad directory or a touchscreen, calling residents’ phones or indoor monitors. Many include a reader in the same housing, so one opening holds one device.
  • Electric strikes. Fitted in the frame on the latch side of a wooden or steel door, releasing the latch when a valid credential is read. They suit most Fulton Ferry residential doors once the door latches properly by itself.
  • Electrified locks and exit devices. Used where a strike does not suit the door, such as the meeting edge of a pair of doors or a glass storefront door, with power carried into the door through a concealed transfer.
  • Magnetic locks, only where they fit the code. Maglocks have a place on some interior doors, but on an exit door they bring the sensor-release and push-to-exit requirements of Building Code §1010.1.9.8. On most street doors here we prefer a strike or lockset that leaves the mechanical exit untouched.
  • Door closers and position switches. The unglamorous parts that make everything else work: a closer that latches the door every time, and a switch that tells the system when it has not.
  • Controllers with local memory and battery backup. The controller decides who opens what, keeps working if the internet drops, and holds its log until it can upload. A backup battery keeps the lock behaving correctly through a short power cut.
  • Cloud management software. For volunteer boards, a web and phone app that lets an authorized person add or remove a resident in a minute, issue a guest pass, see an alert and export a log, without a computer on site.
  • Cameras at the controlled doors. Inside-facing cameras at the entrance and at restaurant or service doors, tied to door events so that a forced-door alert comes with the clip of what happened.
AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Fulton Ferry

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for this search from our build environment, so we did not quote it. The statements below are generic claims that turn up repeatedly in AI answers and national cost guides when people ask about entry systems for small buildings. Each is followed by what we see on Fulton Ferry’s blocks.

“A video doorbell is an access control system.”

Consumer doorbells from Ring and similar brands are often suggested as a cheap way to secure an entrance. A doorbell shows you who is there and lets you talk to them. It does not lock the door, does not issue a separate credential to each resident, and does not keep a record of who opened the door with what.

For a single-family house that may be enough. For a Fulton Ferry co-op with ten or eighty households sharing one street door, it leaves the real problem untouched: keys that have been copied over decades, a door that stays unlocked if it does not latch, and no way to cancel a former resident’s access.

The two can work together. Many buildings keep a video call station for visitors and add a reader and electric lock for residents. The station answers the question “who is at the door”; the access system answers “who is allowed through it.”

“Historic buildings can’t have electronic entry.”

This one appears in answers about landmarked properties, and it is wrong for New York City. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regularly approves intercoms, doorbells and similar equipment at staff level when it is placed in or next to the door opening, kept small, attached at mortar joints or plain material and fed by concealed cable.

What the LPC does care about is how the work looks and what it damages. A reader bolted into a cast-iron pilaster, a conduit run across a brick front, or a large panel on the facade of a building in the Fulton Ferry Historic District is a problem. The same reader on the door frame inside the opening is usually not.

So the accurate version is: historic buildings can have electronic entry, provided the equipment is placed with the building in mind and the permit is obtained. Interior work behind the front door needs no LPC approval at all.

“Angi and HomeAdvisor say a keypad lock costs a few hundred dollars.”

National cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish ranges that are accurate for what they measure, which is usually a homeowner’s smart lock or keypad deadbolt on a private door. Those prices do not describe a controlled entrance to a multiple dwelling.

A building entrance needs a reader, a controller with its own power and battery, an electric lock suited to the door, a door position switch, cable through old masonry, an exit arrangement that meets the Building Code, and setup for many users. Our published Brooklyn figure for that single controlled door is $1,500 to $2,500, including ten programmed fobs.

“A keypad with one building code is fine for a small building.”

Shared codes are cheap and convenient, and AI answers often suggest them for buildings under a dozen units. On a street with Fulton Ferry’s foot traffic, a shared code is seen, passed on and written down within weeks, and there is no way to tell who used it.

Changing a shared code means telling every household at once, and someone is always locked out. Former residents, cleaners and contractors who knew the old code keep knowing it until the change happens.

A keypad is a perfectly good device if every person has their own code that can be deleted on its own. Most modern keypads and combination units support that at no extra hardware cost. The weakness is the shared code, not the keypad.

“Access control stops break-ins.”

Access control stops unauthorized use of the doors it controls. The burglary of an Old Fulton Street apartment reported in December 2019 happened through a bedroom window, which no door reader would have prevented.

Promising otherwise sells the wrong equipment. A building worried about break-ins needs a look at every opening: the street door and inner door, yes, but also ground-floor windows, fire escapes, roof access, rear yards and any cellar entrance from the sidewalk.

What access control does well is remove the easy routes through the doors, record their use and alert someone when a door is propped or forced. Paired with alarm sensors on the other openings, it becomes part of a real plan instead of a reassuring device on the front.

“ADT or SimpliSafe will handle it.”

National alarm brands such as ADT and SimpliSafe are often recommended in answers about building security. They are alarm and monitoring services first. Their equipment is designed to detect intrusion into a home or business and call for help, not to issue and cancel credentials for dozens of residents on a shared street door.

A Fulton Ferry co-op that wants monitored alarms on its common areas can certainly have them, and we can connect a door-forced event to an alarm panel. But the entrance itself needs commercial access-control hardware installed by a licensed low-voltage contractor and set up for a board to run.

“Phone-only entry is the modern standard.”

Phone credentials are convenient and we install them often. But AI answers that recommend dropping fobs entirely skip over two things that matter here.

First, the law: the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law limits how residential owners using smart access collect and use data, and it bars owners from making a smart system the only way into a tenant’s own apartment. Second, the street: on the busiest days in Fulton Ferry, when crowds overload local cell coverage, a phone that cannot reach the cloud may be slow to open a door that depends on it.

The sensible standard is choice. Residents who want to use a phone can; everyone has a fob or card that works without a data connection; and the door opens from the inside, always, with nothing at all.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Open the Door and Let It Go

This describes how we begin every survey of an old entrance; it is a method, not a story about a particular building. Before we look at a reader, a panel or a cable route, we open the street door to its widest point and let go. Then we do it again from half open, and again from a crack. We count how long it takes to close, listen for whether the latch clicks, and push on the door to see if it actually locked.

On buildings that are more than a century old, the result is often surprising to the owners. The door that “always locks” turns out to stop an inch short when it is opened gently, or to bounce off the strike when the wind is behind it. Nobody notices on a quiet street, because the door is rarely opened gently by a stranger. On a street like Old Fulton Street, it matters every hour.

So our written plans for buildings like these nearly always start with a line for the door itself: closer, hinges, alignment, strike, sometimes a threshold. It is the least exciting line on the quote and the one we will not leave out, because an electronic lock on a door that does not close is just a more expensive way of leaving it open.

FAQ

Fulton Ferry Access Control FAQ

Our building is on the National Register of Historic Places as well as in the city's historic district. Does the federal listing add rules for a private owner?
+

Generally no. The National Park Service says that under federal law, listing in the National Register places no restrictions on what a non-federal owner does with their property unless the project involves federal funding, licensing or permits. The rules that apply to a Fulton Ferry front door come from the city's designation of the Fulton Ferry Historic District in 1977, administered by the Landmarks Preservation Commission, which reviews exterior work such as readers, call stations and conduit.

How high should the reader be mounted so a resident who uses a wheelchair can reach it?
+

We work to the reach ranges in the 2010 ADA Standards, which set an unobstructed forward reach between 15 and 48 inches above the floor or ground, and the same range for an unobstructed side reach. In practice we mount readers and call-station buttons near the middle of that range and check the approach: a step, a sloped sidewalk or a door that swings toward the reader can make a correct height hard to use. If a resident has particular needs, we plan the position with them on site.

When is the best time of year and week to schedule installation in Fulton Ferry?
+

Weekday mornings outside the summer peak are easiest, because the sidewalks are quieter and the door can be out of normal use for a few hours without a crowd outside. We avoid weekends from late spring to early fall, and we never schedule around a known closure day such as the Fourth of July. That said, a failed door is repaired whenever it fails; scheduling preferences apply to planned installations, not repairs.

Will an electric strike click or hum loudly enough to bother the apartment beside the entrance?
+

A strike makes a short click when it releases and another when the latch engages, and some cheaper strikes buzz while powered. In a ground-floor apartment next to the entrance that can be noticeable at night. We choose strikes that are rated for quiet operation and wire them so they are powered only for the moment the door is released, rather than held open electrically. Mounting the power supply away from the shared wall also helps.

Can each shareholder see which fobs are registered to their apartment?
+

Most cloud access platforms can give each household a resident account that shows its own credentials and lets it request changes, without showing anyone else's. Where the platform does not offer that, the board can send each apartment a short list once a year. Either way it is a useful check: residents often discover a fob they gave a former cleaner or relative and never got back, and that fob can then be cancelled.

If our self-managed co-op later hires a managing agent, can the agent take over the system without reinstalling anything?
+

Yes, if the system was set up with that in mind. We make the co-op itself the owner of the software account, not a board member personally and not us, and we keep the administrator logins in the board's records. A managing agent can then be added as an administrator with the permissions the board chooses, and a volunteer can be removed, without touching the hardware. The handover sheet we leave lists every device and account so nothing has to be rediscovered.

A resident dropped their fob somewhere between the ferry and home. How quickly can it be shut off, and by whom?
+

Within a minute, by any administrator with the app on their phone. The lost fob is cancelled on its own, every other credential keeps working, and a replacement can be issued at the next convenient time. Because each fob belongs to a named person, nobody else has to change anything. If the building runs on a shared code or old keys instead, a lost credential means changing it for everyone, which is one of the strongest arguments for personal credentials.

Where the Fulton Ferry Facts Come From

We read the documents listed here in October 2026 while preparing this page, and the history, street details, code requirements and reporting above are drawn from them. Prices are not on this list because they are our own: the published Brooklyn rate card, which applies unchanged in Fulton Ferry.

  • NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Fulton Ferry Historic District designation report (June 28, 1977): boundaries, the renaming of the old Ferry Road in 1814, the 1835 street widenings, the commercial row of 1836–39, Franklin House, the Long Island Insurance Company (1834), the Long Island Safe Deposit Company (1868–69, William Mundell), the Brooklyn City Railroad Company building (1860–61), the Eagle Warehouse (1893–94, Frank Freeman), the Marine Fire Boat Station (1926), the Tobacco Inspection Warehouse and the Empire Stores, and the first residential conversion
  • Historic Districts Council, Fulton Ferry entry (designation date; the 1834 Long Island Insurance Company building as possibly the oldest surviving office building in the city)
  • Wikipedia, “Fulton Ferry, Brooklyn” (National Register listing June 28, 1974; 16 acres; 15 contributing buildings 1830–1895; ferry history from 1642 and 1814; 1,200 daily crossings by 1872; ferry ended 1924; service resumed 2006; NYC Ferry routes since 2017; Community District 2)
  • Brooklyn Bridge Park, Fulton Ferry Landing page (1642 ferry site, pier reopened 1997, Whitman railings, Bargemusic, the Smokestack Building; park hours 6 a.m. to 1 a.m.) and 2023 season wrap press release (nearly 5 million visitors)
  • Brooklyn Eagle, “More cops, cameras should control unruly crowds in Brooklyn Bridge Park, president says” (April 20, 2018): the park’s four access points
  • Brooklyn Paper, 84th Precinct report (December 18, 2019): the Old Fulton Street apartment burglary through a bedroom window
  • Office of the Mayor, announcement of 100,000 free tickets for the 2026 Macy’s Fourth of July fireworks (June 26, 2026); Bushwick Daily, “NYPD Checkpoints Hit Brooklyn Heights for July 4th Fireworks” (July 1, 2026, citing the Brooklyn Eagle); a public post from Assemblymember Jo Anne Simon’s office on the park closure and entrances (search excerpt)
  • The Roebling Team building notes for 8 Old Fulton Street (conversion by architect David Morton, about ten loft apartments, offering plan December 15, 1975) and 28 Old Fulton Street (acquired September 1979, converted November 1980); StreetEasy and CityRealty building pages (cooperative conversion in 1980, about 84 units)
  • Wikipedia, “Grimaldi’s Pizzeria” (opened 1990 at 19 Old Fulton Street; no reservations and long lines; move to 1 Front Street after a 2011 landlord dispute); Juliana’s Pizza, About page (19 Old Fulton Street, opened 2012); NYC Council legislation Int 0956-2026 (Patsy and Carol Grimaldi Way)
  • Wikipedia, “Brooklyn Ice Cream Factory” (left the fireboat house June 2019, reopened at 14 Old Fulton Street March 2021); Wikipedia, “The River Café (Brooklyn)” and Eater NY (Sandy damage, reopened February 2014)
  • Pierhouse sales site and Let It Grow project page (90 Furman Street, Toll Brothers, 109 condominiums, amenities shared with 1 Hotel Brooklyn Bridge); Marvel Architects project page
  • NYC Office of Special Enforcement, short-term rental registration law (Local Law 18; platform verification; Prohibited Buildings List; enforcement phase from September 5, 2023)
  • National Park Service, National Register of Historic Places FAQs (no federal restrictions on non-federal owners without federal funding, licensing or permits)
  • 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, section 308 (reach ranges)
  • New York City Building Code §1010.1.9.8 and §1010.1.9.9; Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a; Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043; NYC HPD, Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021); NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission, Permit Guidebook, Chapter 11 (intercoms, doorbells, conduit and cameras)
