The Neighborhood

Why Dumbo Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Dumbo was a factory town before it was a neighborhood. Robert Gair, whose company invented the mass-produced folded cardboard box, built so much of it that the area was known as Gairsville, and Brownstoner's history notes that by 1920 Gair owned more buildings here than any other manufacturer. Around his plants stood the warehouses and refineries of companies like Arbuckle Brothers, who refined and packaged sugar and coffee. When manufacturing left, artists moved into the empty floors, and by the late 1970s they had given the place its acronym. In 1981 David Walentas and his company, Two Trees Management, bought the Gair complex and several other factories from Harry Helmsley, and over the following decades the floors that once held box-folding machines filled with offices, studios and apartments. Today the Gair building is home to Etsy, and Dumbo is described as the center of the Brooklyn Tech Triangle. That history explains almost every cabling decision we make here. These buildings were designed around line shafts, belts, freight elevators and rail sidings. Nobody planned a riser for data, a ceiling cavity for cable or a dry room for a rack, so every one of those has to be found, made or built.

The second thing that shapes the work is protection. Brownstoner describes the Dumbo Historic District as about 91 buildings, most of them built between 1880 and 1920, bounded by John Street to the north, York Street to the south, Main Street to the west and Bridge Street to the east. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it in 2007 and the City Council adopted the designation the following year. The designation reached beyond the buildings: the Commission called the original streets and sidewalks important to the character of the district. The Brooklyn Rail recalled that in the late 1980s utility companies began digging up those cobblestones to lay gas, electric, sewer and television lines, and that the patched streets became a long fight. The fight ended with a $108 million New York City Department of Design and Construction project that restored the Belgian block and rebuilt flood infrastructure underneath, and which won the 2026 Lucy G. Moses Preservation Project Award. For a building owner, that means a new carrier entry or a new piece of conduit on a street-facing wall is not a casual decision in Dumbo. For a tenant, it means almost all of your cabling will happen indoors, inside the existing envelope, and that is exactly where we keep it.

Inside, the materials are tougher than most offices in the borough. Gair called himself the father of concrete construction, and his buildings are among the earliest large reinforced-concrete factories in the country; the Clocktower at 1 Main Street, finished in 1914, was described as the tallest reinforced-concrete building in the world when it was built, and listings for its condos describe concrete ceilings and mushroom-capped columns. Other buildings are brick outside and heavy timber inside, built in what the Landmarks report calls slow-burning mill construction. Some, like 68 Jay Street from 1915, are a steel frame with concrete floors. And some interiors are stranger still: the design firm Format describes a landmarked Dumbo office renovation with exposed brick columns, concrete floors and original brick barrel vaults, built out for a tech company. A barrel vault has no space above it at all. When the ceiling is the structure, a cable has two choices: travel on the surface, neatly, or travel through a hole somebody drills. Both are legitimate. Both need planning that a drop-ceiling office never asks for.

The people in those buildings need the network to work hard. The DUMBO Improvement District says more than 500 tech, design, architecture and other owner-operated businesses are here, and its data page counts about six million square feet of commercial space, 15 office buildings, more than 1,000 creative and tech companies and 11,266 employees in 2022. The Dumbo Heights campus up the hill, the former Watchtower buildings at 81 Prospect, 117 Adams, 77 Sands, 77 Prospect and 175 Pearl, holds large office floors, and 45 Main Street advertises tech conference rooms. Getting fast service to the building is rarely the problem. One provider tracker shows Verizon Business fiber reaching about 90.7 percent of Dumbo addresses, with OCG at 48.1 percent and Zayo at 6.8 percent, and Brooklyn Fiber sells dedicated internet here at up to 10 gigabits in each direction. The weak link is usually the last few hundred feet: the run from the cellar where the carrier stops, up through floors that were poured a century ago, across a ceiling with nowhere to hide, to a desk. Ten gigabits arriving in the basement means very little if it reaches the fourth floor on a patch cord someone threw through a stairwell.

Then there is the river. During Hurricane Sandy in 2012, a StreetEasy discussion reported that One Brooklyn Bridge Park's main electrical switchgear was submerged in the surge and its fire protection panel knocked out. Gothamist reported that the basement under Jane's Carousel flooded and the electronics that run it were damaged. The Real Deal reported more than four feet of water on a John Street site, and noted that the plan proposed for it put the electrical system on a top floor. Cellars are where carriers bring service into old buildings, and in plenty of Dumbo lofts they are where the network room ended up too, because that is where the space was. A switch, a firewall and a battery backup sitting a few feet above a cellar floor near Water Street are a decision worth revisiting, and we raise it on every survey near the waterfront.

Finally, Dumbo is a residential neighborhood now, with its own expectations. Loft conversions such as the Clocktower, where Two Trees created 124 condominiums, sit beside newer buildings: the 33-story Olympia with its 76 condos, the 42-unit One John Street inside Brooklyn Bridge Park, the 17-story mixed-use 60 Water Street and the full-block Front & York at 85 Jay Street. Residents in those buildings want wired backhaul for access points, a rack in a closet and outlets behind a desk, and their boards want insurance paperwork and working hours respected. The streets bring their own schedule as well. Brooklyn Bridge Parents reported Washington Street residents complaining about tourist crowds, trash, vendors and security in the run-up to the 2026 World Cup, and NY1 reported years ago on film shoots that took over blocks for days. A cabling crew with a van full of cable boxes plans around all of it.

Six Dumbo facts we check before we size a single cable run

  • The landmark district covers about 91 buildings. Most date from 1880 to 1920, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission treats visible conduit and rooftop equipment as exterior work that needs its own approval, so we keep pathways indoors wherever the building allows.
  • The floors are early reinforced concrete. Gair's factories pioneered the method, and the Clocktower at 1 Main Street was described as the tallest concrete building in the world in 1914. Every new vertical path means locating, approving and coring a slab, or reusing an old opening.
  • Some ceilings are not ceilings. Landmarked offices with brick barrel vaults, condo units with exposed concrete and mushroom columns, and timber-beam warehouses leave no cavity. Cable runs exposed, so it has to be run like a finish, not hidden like a secret.

Same-Day Repair in Dumbo

Need Cabling Repair in Dumbo? Ports Gone Dark After a Renovation, a Run Cut by Another Trade, or a Closet Nobody Can Read

Most urgent cabling calls from loft offices come in three kinds. The first is the renovation casualty. Somebody refinished a floor, moved a partition, or ran new electrical conduit across an exposed ceiling, and a bundle of data cable got crushed under a strap, nicked by a saw or pulled tight enough to stretch the pairs inside. The ports at the far end go dead or drop to a slower speed, and the desks that rely on them start complaining before anyone connects the two events. The second is the slow decline: a closet that grew one patch cord at a time over several tenants, where jacks were re-punched by whoever was handy, labels faded or never existed, and a switch was added on a shelf with its own tangle of cords. Things work until one day they do not, and nobody can say which cable leads where. The third is water or heat. In a cellar near the river or a closet with no ventilation, connectors corrode, battery backups fail and switches restart for no visible reason.

The fix starts with finding out what is actually there. A technician traces the affected runs with a tone generator and probe, tests each one, and tells you which ones can be re-terminated at the jack or the panel and which ones are damaged somewhere along their length and have to be replaced. In a building with exposed ceilings, a damaged section is often easy to see once someone looks for it. In a building where cable was fished through old walls, it may be quicker to pull a new run than to search for the break, and we say which we recommend and why. Call (347) 934-8335 or send photos of the closet and a list of the dead ports on WhatsApp. If the cause is a cable that another trade damaged, we document what we find with photos and test results, which is usually what a landlord or general contractor needs to settle who pays. If the real problem is the internet service or a device rather than the cabling, we tell you that plainly instead of selling new cable.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling for Dumbo's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every cabling call between the bridges. They differ in what the floors and ceilings are made of, how cable can travel between levels, and who has to approve the route. Those three questions decide the plan long before anyone talks about cable categories.

The poured-concrete box factory

Gair's early reinforced-concrete buildings on Washington, Main and Jay Streets: deep slabs, big columns, tall windows and ceilings that are the floor above.

The river warehouse with timber posts

Brick warehouses like the Empire Stores, built between 1869 and 1885, and 45 Water Street, with heavy wood columns and beams in slow-burning mill construction.

The steel-framed loft with concrete decks

Buildings such as 68 Jay Street from 1915, with a steel frame, brick walls and reinforced-concrete floors that often carry later electrical conduit across their ceilings.

The office campus on the hill

The Dumbo Heights buildings on Prospect, Sands, Adams and Pearl Streets, converted into large office floors with shared risers and building management.

The loft condo

Factory floors converted into apartments, like the Clocktower's 124 condos, where the owner controls the unit and the board controls the slab, the shafts and the hours.

The glass-and-concrete tower

Newer residential buildings such as Olympia, One John Street, 60 Water Street and Front & York, with modern risers, fire-rated corridors and a management office.

The poured-concrete box factory: every route through a floor is a decision

In the Gair buildings the floor of one story is the ceiling of the next, and it is solid. There is no cavity to fish a cable through and no stud bay to hide it in. That shapes three things. First, horizontal cable on a floor travels either along the ceiling of that floor, supported from the concrete, or along the walls and columns at desk level in surface raceway. In a drop-ceiling office you would never see the cable; here, the job is to make it look intentional, running parallel to beams, turning at column lines and keeping bundles tight. Second, vertical cable between floors needs a path through the slab. The best paths are the ones already there: old pipe chases, the shafts beside freight elevators, abandoned openings left by chutes or lifts that were sealed during conversion, and the risers that the building's own electrical and telecom work already use. Where none of those reach the right spot, a new core is drilled, which is the point where the landlord, and in many buildings an engineer, gets involved, because a hole in an early concrete slab has to avoid reinforcing steel and has to be firestopped afterward.

Third, the concrete itself can surprise. Century-old concrete varies from bay to bay, and anchors for hangers or tray supports are set with the right drill, the right anchor and enough distance from edges and cracks. We scan before coring and before setting anchors near columns, and where the ceiling has been painted or sealed as a design feature, we agree with you on the anchor spacing and pathway color before drilling, because every fastener in an exposed concrete ceiling is a fastener someone will look at every day. In practice the answer in these buildings is often a single, well-placed tray or a row of hangers down the middle of the floor, with short drops to the desks along columns, so the ceiling carries one clean line instead of dozens of separate cables.

The river warehouse with timber posts: respect the wood, use the brick wisely

The warehouses closest to the water, the ones the Landmarks report describes as slow-burning mill construction, carry their floors on heavy timber beams and posts set inside thick brick walls. Those beams are one of the reasons tenants rent these spaces, and they are also the obvious place to run cable, because a beam is a straight, strong line across a room. The method matters. We prefer clamp-type supports and straps that grip a beam or hang from it rather than many screws driven into old timber, and where a fixing has to go into wood, it goes in once, in a planned position, with a pilot hole. On brick walls, light pathways such as surface raceway are commonly fixed into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, a long-standing preservation habit, because a mortar joint can be repointed later while a drilled brick face stays drilled.

Vertical paths in these buildings are often easier than in the concrete factories, because the floors are wood plank on timber, but they come with their own rule: an opening through a floor between occupancies has to be sealed afterward so it does not become a path for fire or smoke. A neat sleeve, firestopped and labeled, is part of the job, not an extra. Warehouses near the river also tend to have their carrier entries and older equipment at or below street level, which brings the flood question straight back into the plan; we come back to it in the questions further down.

The steel-framed loft with concrete decks: a crowded ceiling

Buildings with a steel frame and concrete floors, like 68 Jay Street, often look tidy until you study the ceiling. Decades of tenants have run sprinkler pipes, electrical conduit, ductwork and their own data cable across it, sometimes in neat lines and sometimes not. New cabling here has to find its own space and stay out of everybody else's: clear of sprinkler heads, off the outside of electrical conduit, away from light fixtures and ballasts, and never strapped to a sprinkler pipe or hung from someone else's conduit, which codes and good practice both forbid. The steel itself is a gift, because beam clamps and threaded rod give strong, reversible supports without drilling the concrete at all.

In these buildings, old abandoned cable is often the first thing we deal with. Previous tenants left bundles of dead cable on the ceiling, and the electrical code expects accessible abandoned communications cable to be removed rather than left in place, in part because it adds fuel and clutter above an occupied floor. Removing it before new cable goes in makes the new pathway easier to support, easier to inspect and easier for the next contractor to understand. We include the cleanup in the written scope so the landlord can see it.

The office campus on the hill: shared risers and a building manager

The Dumbo Heights buildings were converted into large commercial floors, and their structure behaves much more like a modern office building: shared telecom risers, building-controlled closets on each floor, and a management team that keeps track of who runs what through those shafts. A tenant's job there usually stays inside the suite, from the tenant's own rack to the desks, plus a run or a pair of fibers from the building's riser closet to the suite. The building will usually say which closet and which riser space a tenant may use, which pathway is allowed in the corridor ceiling, and whether cable in shared spaces must be a particular type or color. We ask for those rules in writing and design to them.

Large floors bring a different issue: distance. The standard copper channel for Ethernet is limited to 100 meters including the patch cords, so on a big floor with one closet at one end, the far desks can sit close to the limit once the cable climbs to the ceiling, crosses the floor and drops to the outlet. A second closet on the floor, linked back to the first with fiber, is often the cleaner answer than a long run that barely passes. We measure the actual routes during the survey rather than assuming the straight-line distance on a floor plan.

The loft condo: the unit is yours, the slab is the building's

Converted condo buildings in Dumbo mix factory structure with residential rules. Inside a unit, an owner can usually add network outlets, a small rack in a closet and cable for ceiling access points, provided the work stays inside the unit and respects the house rules for hours, noise and contractor paperwork. The concrete ceilings and mushroom-capped columns described in the Clocktower listings mean that, again, there is no hidden route, so the cable follows the architecture: along the edge where a wall meets the ceiling, beside a column, inside a soffit or millwork, or in a slim painted raceway. Many owners choose to route cable inside built-in cabinetry, which makes it disappear completely.

The moment work touches the building's own structure or shared spaces, such as a hole through a slab to the unit below or a run into a corridor, it becomes the board's decision, and usually the managing agent's paperwork. Condo and co-op buildings commonly ask for a certificate of insurance naming the board or management company as an additional insured. We send that before the visit and keep the scope inside the unit unless the board approves more.

The glass-and-concrete tower: modern risers, fire-rated walls

The newer towers near the waterfront and at the edges of the neighborhood were built with telecom in mind, so they usually have risers, closets and a provider already wired to each apartment or office. The cabling questions there are smaller but strict. Corridor walls and walls between units are typically fire-rated, so any penetration needs the right sleeve and firestop, and management offices often want to know exactly where a hole will be before approving it. Garages, amenity rooms and lobbies are building space, not tenant space. And towers near the water have their own history with Sandy, which in some buildings moved critical equipment higher up after 2012.

For residents, the most common job is a home network that does not depend on a single router in the corner: wired outlets in a home office, cable to one or two ceiling access points, and a small cabinet or closet where the provider's equipment, a switch and a battery backup can live together. For commercial tenants on lower floors, it is a small office or shop network built to the building's rules. Either way, we work through the management office and leave the paperwork the building asks for.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Hour in a Dumbo Loft With No Ceiling to Hide In

These notes describe how our technicians approach buildings like Dumbo's. They are our working practice, not a story about any particular address or customer.

Start where the service enters. Before looking at the office floor, the technician asks to see where the internet provider's cable comes into the building. In most loft buildings that is the cellar or a ground-floor utility room. We note how high the equipment sits above the floor, whether there is a drain or a history of water, and what path already climbs from there: a riser, a shaft, a pipe chase or a line of conduit.

Look up, then look along. On the office floor, the first look is straight up. Bare concrete, painted concrete, brick vault, timber, or a drop ceiling added later each mean a different pathway. The second look is along the ceiling: where the beams run, where the sprinklers are, what other trades have already hung up there, and where a single clean line could carry most of the cable.

Find the old holes. The technician walks every floor along the same vertical line, looking for openings that already pass through the slab: old chases, sleeves from former tenants, shafts beside freight elevators. An existing, approved opening that can be cleaned up and firestopped is better than a new core in almost every way.

Count the far desk. On a big floor, the technician measures the route to the desk farthest from the closet, up to the ceiling, along the pathway, down the column and to the outlet. If it gets close to the copper limit, a second closet goes into the plan before anyone falls in love with a single room.

Ask who signs. Before leaving, the technician asks one question: who approves holes, hours and the freight elevator here? In Dumbo that answer is often a building manager working for a landlord with many buildings, and getting their rules on paper first saves more time than any tool we carry.

Pathways

Concrete, Vaults, Timber and Brick: How Cable Actually Travels Through a Dumbo Building

A cabling job is mostly a pathway job. The cable is the cheapest part and the easiest to pull once the route is right. In a Dumbo loft the route is the whole challenge, so here is how we choose it, material by material, in plain terms.

Across a bare concrete ceiling. The two good options are a basket tray, which is an open wire tray that carries many cables in one neat channel, and individual hangers spaced every few feet, which carry smaller bundles. Both are anchored into the slab. Tray suits a central "spine" down an open floor where many desks branch off; hangers suit lighter runs along a wall or a corridor. Either can be supplied in black, white or a painted finish to match the ceiling. We do not staple cable to concrete, and we do not let cable lie loose on top of light fixtures, pipes or ducts. Cable that hangs in a straight line, with every bend gentle and every drop at a column, looks like part of the design, and it is easy to add to later.

Under a brick barrel vault. A vault leaves no surface flat enough for a tray and no space above it. The usual answer is to follow the springing line, the low edge where the vault meets its supporting beam or wall, in a slim raceway or conduit painted to match, and to drop to desks along columns. Where the vaults are a protected feature of a landmarked interior or simply the reason the tenant chose the space, we agree with you on every fixing position before drilling and keep the count low. Sometimes the best route is not the ceiling at all but the floor: a raised floor in part of the space, floor boxes where a slab can accept them, or furniture with built-in cable channels.

Through a concrete slab. Vertical paths come first from what exists. If no usable opening exists, a new core is drilled in a position agreed with the building, after scanning for reinforcing steel and anything embedded in the slab. The opening gets a sleeve sized for today's cable plus room for growth, and once the cable is in, it is firestopped with a listed system so the floor keeps its fire separation. Coring is loud and wet for a short time, so in an occupied building we schedule it with management, protect the area below, and clean as we go.

Along timber. In the mill-construction warehouses, beams are a natural route. Beam clamps, straps and saddles hold cable or tray without many fasteners in the wood. Where a fixing must go into timber, it is placed once, in a planned position, and kept well away from checks and splits in old beams. We never notch or bore a structural beam for cable.

On brick walls. Drops from the ceiling to a desk on an exterior or party wall usually run in a surface raceway, fixed into mortar joints where possible so the brick face is not drilled. Where a wall will be visible in photographs, which in Dumbo is more often than you would think, painted conduit or a raceway in a color matched to the mortar can almost vanish.

Above a drop ceiling. Where a conversion added a suspended ceiling, the space above it is used for the long runs, supported from the structure with hooks or tray, never from the ceiling grid or its wires. If the space above that ceiling is used for air return, the cable has to be rated for it, and we check before ordering.

Keeping data away from everything else

Old buildings collect services. A single loft ceiling may carry sprinkler mains, electrical conduit from three different decades, steam or hot-water pipes and refrigeration lines from a former tenant's kitchen. Data cable keeps its distance from power where it runs in parallel, crosses power at right angles where it must cross, stays well clear of hot pipes and never uses another trade's pipe or conduit as a support. In buildings near the bridge, where many offices invested in heavy soundproof windows, we also make sure that no new hole through a wall or window frame undoes that work; cable comes in from inside the building, not through the envelope.

What a finished pathway should look like

You should be able to stand in the middle of the floor and follow every pathway with your eyes: a clear line from the closet, consistent supports, labeled bundles, gentle bends and drops that land exactly where the outlet is. Nothing should be draped over a beam, cable-tied to a sprinkler pipe or left coiled in a corner. In a drop-ceiling office, that standard is hidden. In a Dumbo loft, everyone sees it, which is exactly why we hold it.

Owners, Tenants and Boards

Landlords, Condo Boards and Lease Clauses: Who Signs Off on Cable in a Dumbo Building

Very few Dumbo tenants own the building their network runs through, and even owners in a condo do not own the slab. Before any cable is ordered, we work out who has a say. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice; it is what the job requires in practice.

The landlord's building staff. In a commercial loft, the owner's building manager usually controls the freight elevator, the hours contractors may work, which riser and closet spaces a tenant may use, and whether new cores are allowed. Some owners in Dumbo hold many buildings, so their contractor rules may already be written down and applied the same way across several addresses. We ask for them at the start and plan around them. Expect a request for a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent, and sometimes a list of the crew who will be on site.

The lease. Commercial leases often say who owns cabling installed by a tenant, whether it must be removed at the end of the term, and what condition the space must be returned in. Some landlords want tenants to leave good, documented cabling in place because it helps them lease the floor again; others want it gone. Read the alteration and surrender clauses before the job, because they affect whether you spend a little more on documentation and tidy pathways or plan for removal later. Either way, a clean, labeled system with a port map is easier to hand over or take out.

The condo or co-op board. For residential lofts, the board and managing agent typically set contractor hours, insurance requirements and limits on work in common areas. Work inside a unit is usually straightforward. Work that crosses into the building's structure or shared spaces, a new hole between floors or a run into a corridor, needs approval first. We send the certificate of insurance and a short description of the work in advance, and we keep to whatever the board approves.

The landmark rules. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's jurisdiction is the exterior of the district's buildings and the streets themselves. Anything visible from the street, including exposed conduit, rooftop equipment and exterior HVAC units, needs its own approval, and the Commission's rules for exterior conduit ask that penetrations through a facade be as small as possible and that conduit be painted to match the surface beneath. The Commission also offers a faster review for certain work on facades that are not visible from the street, which its rules describe as a permit in 10 working days. For a cabling job, the practical lesson is to keep everything inside if you can. When a carrier entry, an exterior conduit or a rooftop antenna really is needed, the building owner applies, and we supply drawings and product details.

The internet provider. A carrier brings its service to the building's demarcation point, usually in a cellar or entry room, and installs its own equipment there. Getting from that point to your suite is often your responsibility, or the landlord's, not the carrier's. That run, sometimes called a demarc extension, is one of the most common Dumbo cabling tasks, and it touches the building's shared spaces, which brings the building staff back into the conversation.

Compliance notes for Dumbo businesses and buildings

  • The permit rule changed. Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A hiring guide gives the effective date as December 21, 2025, and says the work must still be done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. One vendor still writes that most commercial low-voltage work in the city needs permits, so where a building or project raises the question, we confirm it with the Department of Buildings rather than guess.
  • Fire alarm wiring is a separate category. Data cabling and fire alarm wiring are different trades with different licensing and permits. We do not mix fire alarm circuits into a data pathway, and fire alarm work is handled under its own rules.
  • Fire separations stay intact. Every penetration through a fire-rated floor or wall is sleeved and sealed with a listed firestop system once cable is in place, so the separation the building relies on is not quietly undone.
  • Cable has to match the space. Cable in vertical shafts must be rated for risers, and cable in air-handling spaces must be rated for those spaces. We check each space before ordering instead of buying one type for everything.
  • Old cable comes out. The electrical code expects accessible abandoned communications cable to be removed. On floors with decades of leftover cable we include removal in the scope.
  • Security devices need a licensed installer. New York General Business Law §69-m requires anyone in the business of installing security or fire alarm systems to be licensed. When our cable serves cameras, door readers or intercoms, the devices are handled under our license, NYS license #12000287431.
  • Exterior work goes through Landmarks. Visible conduit, rooftop equipment and new facade penetrations in the historic district need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval, applied for by the owner, before anything is installed outside.
Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Dumbo: Questions Answered

These are the situations Dumbo tenants, owners and boards describe when they call: ceilings with nowhere to hide, slabs that need holes, service that stops in the cellar, closets nobody can read and quotes that disagree. Each one is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Routes through old structure

Our new office has original brick barrel vaults and no dropped ceiling anywhere. Where does the cable actually go?

It goes along the edges of the architecture rather than across the middle of it. Under a barrel vault, the cleanest route is usually the low line where each vault meets its beam or wall, in a slim raceway or small conduit painted to match, with drops to the desks running down columns or along walls. Where the room has a raised floor area, built-in furniture or millwork, those can carry a lot of the cable out of sight. In a few spaces the floor is the better route than the ceiling, using floor boxes or furniture with cable channels. We mark the route on photos of your space before any fixing goes in, so you approve what the vault will look like when we are done, and we keep the number of fixings in the brick as low as the support needs allow.

The cable on our concrete ceiling will be on show. Can it be painted or finished so it blends in?

Yes, and in Dumbo it often should be. Basket tray, hangers, conduit and raceway are all available in black or white and can be painted to match a ceiling, a column or a brick wall, and the cable itself comes in jacket colors that disappear against a dark or light ceiling. What matters more than color is geometry: one straight spine down the floor, supports at even spacing, drops placed at columns and bundles kept tight. We do not recommend painting the cable jackets themselves, because paint can hide the markings that show what the cable is rated for, which inspectors and future contractors need to read. Paint the pathway, choose the jacket color, and the result looks deliberate.

We need cable between floors and there is no shaft where we want it. Who has to approve a new hole through a century-old concrete slab?

The building owner, nearly always through its building manager, and in many buildings an engineer the owner trusts. The slab belongs to the building, and an opening through it has to avoid reinforcing steel, keep its fire separation and land in a spot that does not interfere with another tenant below. Our part is to look first for an existing opening, an old chase, a sleeve or a shaft beside the freight elevator, because reusing an approved opening avoids the question. If a new core is needed, we provide a marked-up plan with the location and size, scan the slab before drilling, schedule the coring with management, protect the space below and firestop the opening afterward. Most owners approve a small, well-documented core quickly; what they refuse is a hole they were not told about.

Service, rooms and the river

The internet provider's fiber stops in the cellar of our loft building. Whose job is it to bring that service up to our fourth-floor suite?

Usually not the provider's. Carriers bring service to the building's demarcation point, which in Dumbo's older buildings is typically a cellar or ground-floor utility room, and install their equipment there. The run from that point to your suite is normally the tenant's or the landlord's responsibility, depending on the lease and the building. That run should be planned like any backbone: a path the building approves, cable rated for the riser it travels in, firestopping at each floor, labels at both ends and a test record. For high-speed service, the run is often fiber, or a pair of fibers plus a copper cable for anything that needs it. Ask your landlord who owns that path before ordering service, because the answer decides who pays for it.

Our network closet sits in a Water Street cellar that took water during Sandy. What is involved in moving it upstairs?

Three things: a new room, new paths and a careful cutover. The new room needs space for the rack, ventilation, a dedicated power circuit installed by an electrician, and a door that locks. The paths are new or extended backbones from the carrier's entry, which usually stays in the cellar because the carrier puts it there, up to the new room, and new runs from the new room to wherever the desks are. The cutover is planned so the old closet keeps working until the new one is built and tested, then services move across in a set order. Reports from 2012 describe switchgear and electronics in waterfront Dumbo basements being damaged by the surge, which is why we suggest this on surveys near the river. Not every move is worth it; if the cellar has been dry for years and the equipment sits well above the floor, a better battery backup and a shelf higher up may be enough. We give you both options in writing.

New leases and inherited messes

We are signing a lease on a raw loft floor. What should the cabling scope cover before the fit-out starts?

It should cover the things that are hard to add later. First, the network room: where it goes, how big it is, who installs its power and cooling, and whether it sits above any flood concern. Second, the backbone: the path from the building's demarcation point to that room, approved by the landlord before the lease is signed if possible. Third, the pathways: where tray or hangers will run, where columns carry drops, and where floor boxes or furniture take cable instead. Fourth, outlet counts planned for the likely next layout, including access points, printers, conference rooms and any door readers or cameras. Fifth, coordination with the general contractor so walls, millwork and ceilings do not close before cable goes in. A cabling contractor involved at layout stage usually saves more than any discount on the cable itself.

Our inherited closet is a nest of patch cords and nobody knows which cable goes where. Can it be rebuilt without taking the office offline for a day?

Usually, yes. The rebuild is split into stages. First, every run is traced and labeled while the network keeps working, using a tone generator and probe at the panel and the outlets. Second, a port map is produced so everyone can see what goes where. Third, the new cable management, patch panels or switches are prepared beside the old ones, often in the same rack. Fourth, connections are moved across in small groups at a time agreed with you, often early morning or evening, with each group tested before the next. Short interruptions to individual desks are hard to avoid entirely, but a whole office down for a day is a sign of poor planning. At the end you have a dressed rack, a port map and labels that match.

Our conference room will host video calls every day. What cabling should be at the table and behind the screen?

At the table, a floor box or a table connection with wired network for the presenter, power from an electrician, and a path for a video cable or a wireless presentation device. Behind the screen, a network outlet for the display or its streaming device, one for any room controller, and a path for the camera and microphones, which are often network-powered now. Near the room, ideally in the floor closet rather than the room itself, sits the switch that powers those devices. In Dumbo's loft offices, conference rooms are often glass boxes inside an open floor, so the cable may need to come down a single mullion or a column. If the screen goes on a masonry wall, the mount comes from our TV list with the $65 brick or stone add; if the wall is a framed partition, the $169 in-wall kit puts power and cable behind it legally.

Rules, licenses and quotes

Our building wants a new carrier conduit on the outside wall and a small antenna on the roof. How do we find out whether Landmarks approval is needed?

Start from the assumption that it is, because the building is very likely inside the Dumbo Historic District, which the Commission designated in 2007. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says anything visible from the street, including visible conduit and rooftop equipment, needs its own approval, and its rules for exterior conduit ask for small facade penetrations and conduit painted to match the surface. The Commission's website has a map that shows whether an address is in a district, and the building owner is the one who files. Work on facades not visible from the street may qualify for a faster review that the rules describe as 10 working days. Our role is to suggest the least visible route, often through a rear wall or an existing opening, and to provide drawings and product sheets for the application. We do not install anything outside until the permit is in hand.

Two cabling quotes for the same Dumbo loft came back very far apart. Why would that happen?

Usually because the two contractors priced different jobs. Look for these differences: one quote may assume cable can lie above a ceiling that does not exist, while the other includes tray or hangers across exposed concrete; one may include cores and firestopping, the other may assume a shaft is available; one may include certification testing of every link and a port map, the other only a basic continuity check; one may include removal of abandoned cable and the other not; one may assume after-hours work for disruptive tasks and the other daytime only. Distance matters too, because big floors and long routes through old buildings use more cable and labor. Ask both contractors for a written scope with outlet counts, cable type, pathway type, penetrations, testing and documentation, then compare line by line. Ours is written that way so you can.

Since the code change at the end of 2025, who is actually allowed to run data cable in a Dumbo office?

Local Law 128 of 2024 added a rule that an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts used for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A hiring guide describing the change says it took effect on December 21, 2025, and that the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. So a permit may not be needed for an ordinary data job, but that does not mean anyone at all should do it. Your landlord will still want insurance, your building may want proof of competence, and any part of the job that touches security devices falls under the state licensing law for security installers. "Low-voltage cabling in New York City always needs a permit"

Some vendor pages still say most commercial low-voltage cabling in the city requires permits, and an AI answer built from those pages may repeat it as settled fact.

Local Law 128 of 2024 added a rule that an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, and a hiring guide gives December 21, 2025 as the effective date, with the work done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. In Dumbo the bigger approvals are often not the Department of Buildings at all but the landlord's building staff, a condo board and, for anything outside, the Landmarks Preservation Commission. We confirm the permit question for each project and plan for the approvals that actually apply.

"If fiber reaches the building, the network is taken care of"

Generic answers about business internet focus on which providers serve an address, as though choosing the right carrier settles everything.

"All the cable can be hidden inside the walls and ceiling"

"The basement is the natural place for the network room"

Generic advice often suggests putting the main equipment room in the basement because it is out of the way, cool and close to where services enter.

"A landmark district only cares about windows and storefronts"

The Landmarks Preservation Commission says anything visible from the street needs its own approval, including visible conduit and rooftop equipment, and its rules for exterior conduit ask for small penetrations and conduit painted to match. FAQ

Dumbo Structured Cabling FAQ

What paperwork will our managing agent or condo board want before cabling work starts?
+

Usually a certificate of insurance naming the owner, the managing agent or the board as additional insured, a short description of the work, the crew's names and the dates. Some buildings also want the work kept to set hours and the freight elevator booked in advance. We send the certificate before the visit at no charge.

When our lease ends, do we have to pull out the cable we paid for?
+

It depends on the surrender clause in your lease. Some landlords want documented cabling left in place to help lease the floor again, and others require removal. The electrical code also expects accessible abandoned cable to be taken out, so check the lease and ask the landlord in writing well before you move.

Can the noisy parts, like coring and the final switchover, happen at night or on a weekend?
+

Yes, if your building allows contractors at those times. We schedule coring, wall openings and the cutover from old equipment to new for early mornings, evenings or weekends where the building rules permit, and keep the quiet work for normal hours.

We rent desks on a coworking floor. Can we add our own network outlets?
+

Only with the operator's approval, because the operator controls the floor, the closet and the network. Many operators provide wired ports on request or allow work in private offices under their rules. Ask them first, and we can work to whatever they allow.

What should we receive at the end of the job?
+

The certification results for every link, a port map that matches the labels on every outlet and panel port, a marked-up floor plan showing the pathways and penetrations, and photos of anything that will be hidden later. Keep them with your lease documents and give copies to your IT provider.

How do you work around film shoots, crowds and closures on the photo blocks near Washington Street?
+

We check for posted parking restrictions before the visit, ask the building where deliveries can stop, and avoid unloading at the busiest hours when visitors fill the blocks. NY1 has reported film shoots taking over Dumbo streets for days, so if signs go up near your building, tell us and we will adjust the date.

How do you get cable and equipment up when the freight elevator has to be booked?
+

We book the window through building staff, bring everything for that stage in one trip, and stage it on your floor so the elevator is free again quickly. Racks and long tray sections are planned around the elevator's size, which we ask about during the survey.

Does our new rack need its own electrical circuit, and do you install it?
+

A rack with switches, a firewall and a battery backup usually should have a dedicated circuit. That is line-voltage electrical work done by a licensed electrician, so we specify the load and the outlet type and coordinate with your electrician or the building's.

Does being inside a landmarked building limit what you can drill indoors?
+

The landmark designation covers exteriors and the streets, so ordinary interior cabling does not need Landmarks approval. Your landlord or board may still protect features like vaults, timber and exposed brick, so we agree every fixing position in those areas before drilling.

Our IT is handled by an outside company. Can you build to their design?
+

Yes. We review their outlet plan and equipment list, point out anything the building makes difficult, and install to their labeling scheme so their records and ours match. They receive the test results and port map at handover.

Do you also mount and connect the switches and firewall, or only run the cable?
+

Both. We mount and patch switches, firewalls and battery backups in the rack and connect them to the cabling. If your IT provider configures the equipment, we hand it over physically ready, labeled and patched for them to set up.

Is there an option for a small job, like connecting one display to the network?
+

Yes. A setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour on our Brooklyn list, then billed in 30-minute blocks. It suits small tasks such as connecting a display or streaming device, while anything involving new cable runs is quoted as a cabling job.

Before we move out, can you identify which cables in the ceiling are ours?
+

Yes. We trace each run with a tone generator and probe from your patch panel and outlets, label what belongs to you, and mark what belongs to the building or other tenants. That record helps settle what must be removed or left behind under your lease.
