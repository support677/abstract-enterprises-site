The Neighborhood

Why Long Island City Homes Need Sonos Installation

Long Island City has added homes faster than almost anywhere in the five boroughs, and nearly all of them are small, new and shared between work and rest. The State Comptroller's September 2025 economic snapshot of the area counted 26,177 housing units in Long Island City and Hunters Point at the end of 2024, an increase of 308.1 percent since 2010, with roughly three in four occupied homes rented rather than owned. Queens West alone holds 4,757 units in eleven buildings; Hunters Point South added 5,255 between 2013 and 2024. That is not a neighborhood of family houses with a basement and an attic. It is a neighborhood of studios, alcove studios and one- and two-bedroom apartments, most of them stacked thirty, forty and sixty floors above the rail yards and the river.

Small apartments change what a Sonos system is for. In a four-bedroom house the question is which rooms get music. In a Hunters Point studio there is only one room, and it keeps changing what it is. In the morning it is a kitchen with the news on. From nine until six, for a large share of residents, it is an office with a laptop on a desk that faces the same wall as the television. In the evening it is a dining room, at weekends a party, at ten a cinema, and at midnight a bedroom, sometimes for one person while the other is still awake at the other end. A speaker that sounds right for a film at ten can be in exactly the wrong place for a video call at two in the afternoon, and a Sub that makes the film exciting will thump through the mattress three feet away.

The buildings add their own conditions. Towers in Court Square, Queens Plaza and along Center Boulevard are reinforced concrete: the ceiling over your bed is usually the underside of the floor above, with no void to hide a speaker in, and some slabs are post-tensioned with steel tendons under enormous tension. Exterior walls are often glass from knee height to ceiling. Internet arrives through whatever the developer chose, frequently a metal panel in a closet. Management companies and condo boards want insurance certificates, elevator bookings and a say in anything that is drilled. A resident on the neighborhood forum, asked about quiet buildings, put the trade-off plainly: luxury high-rises are generally better than older buildings "simply because they need to build it with concrete" — a fact that also decides where a Sonos cable can and cannot go.

Older Long Island City is still here, and it asks different questions. The Hunters Point Historic District on 45th Avenue keeps a row of 1870s and 1880s houses whose facades the Landmarks Preservation Commission has reviewed since 1968. Dutch Kills, between Queens Plaza and the Sunnyside Yard, mixes walk-ups, hotels and industrial buildings, some of them now lofts, such as the Arris Lofts in a former printing plant. Queensbridge Houses, opened in 1939 with 3,142 apartments, is still the largest public housing development in North America. And in November 2025 the City Council approved the OneLIC plan for 54 largely industrial blocks and about 14,700 more homes, so the new-apartment pattern will continue for years.

This page is therefore built around one idea: in Long Island City, Sonos is planned by the hour rather than by the room. We start by asking what the space has to do at each part of the day, then decide where each speaker sits and how it is controlled so that the switch from desk to sofa to bed is one tap, not a rearrangement. Only after that do we deal with the concrete, the glass, the closet panel and the building's paperwork, which is where a licensed installer earns the fee.

The Long Island City Approach

One Room, Four Jobs: Planning Sonos by the Hour

When a Long Island City client sends us a floor plan, the first thing we ask is not "which rooms?" but "what happens here, and when?" A studio has one main space, a one-bedroom has two, and both are used for more things than any house room ever is. Writing the day down, hour by hour, turns a vague wish for "good sound" into decisions we can make: which speaker is the anchor, which one moves, which one stays quiet, and what has to happen with one tap when the room changes job. The table below is the version we sketch most often. Yours will differ, and that difference is the point of the walkthrough.

Time of dayWhat the room is doingWhat Sonos should do
7 AM–9 AMKitchen and getting readyAn alarm or a favorite station on the speaker nearest the kitchen counter; nothing playing near the bed if one person sleeps later
9 AM–6 PMHome officeQuiet background music on one speaker behind or beside the desk, never between the laptop microphone and the speaker during calls
6 PM–9 PMDining room, friends overWhole apartment grouped at moderate volume, with the soundbar joining in so the music comes from the long wall rather than one corner
9 PM–11 PMCinemaSoundbar, Sub and any rear speakers as a home theater; the desk speaker either becomes a surround or stays out of it
After 11 PMBedroom for one, study for the otherNight Sound on the soundbar, the Sub's level trimmed, a sleep timer on the bed-side speaker, headphones for whoever is still watching

Every row in that table is something the Sonos app can already do. Sonos describes Night Sound as reducing "the intensity of loud sounds while increasing the level of quieter sounds", which is exactly what an open-plan apartment needs at eleven at night. Sleep timers and alarms are standard features in the app, and Speech Enhancement lifts dialogue on the home theater products. What the app cannot do is put the speakers in the right places. That is a physical decision, made once, and in a small space the margin between right and wrong is often a foot or two.

The anchor and the floater

In most studios and one-bedrooms we recommend one fixed anchor and one or two speakers that can move. The anchor is the TV wall: a Beam, Ray or Arc Ultra under the screen, wall-mounted or on the console, with a Sub or Sub Mini nearby if the household wants one. It is wired into place, tuned once with Trueplay and left alone. The floater is an Era 100 or a portable Roam or Move that can sit on the desk during the day, on the kitchen counter in the evening and on the bedside shelf at night. Sonos lets products be grouped and ungrouped in seconds, so the floater joins the soundbar for a party and leaves it for the late shift without anyone touching a cable.

Getting the anchor right first saves money. A soundbar that is in the wrong place forces every other speaker to compensate; a soundbar that is centred, at ear height for the sofa, with its upward drivers clear of shelves, makes a single Era 100 sound like a much bigger system when they are grouped. In Long Island City the wrong place is usually the result of the floor plan: the only long wall is the one shared with the corridor, the window takes the other, and the TV ends up on a narrow pier between them. We look for the wall that serves both the sofa and the desk before suggesting anything is drilled.

Where the bed is, the bass is a problem

The most common mistake we see in Long Island City studios is a Sub placed where it sounds best for the sofa, which in a single room is often a few feet from the foot of the bed. Low frequencies travel through concrete and furniture, and the person who wants to sleep feels every explosion. The answer is rarely to remove the Sub. It is to put it where its energy points away from the sleeping corner, lift it off the slab on a firm stand rather than a soft pad, and trim its level in the app for late listening. Sonos's own guidance is that the Sub works against a wall, in a corner or behind furniture, with its front opening clear; we use those options to keep it away from the bed and from the wall shared with a neighbor.

Work hours change the plan

The desk is the newest room in most Long Island City apartments, and it changes Sonos plans more than anything else. A speaker on the desk sounds lovely for music while you work, but a laptop sending its sound to a Sonos speaker during a video call is a different matter. Sonos's AirPlay article says that system audio sent from a Mac over the older AirPlay route may come with "a delay or audio interruptions", and that Sonos speakers cannot play AirPlay audio while the source device is on a phone call. For calls, the dependable choices are the laptop's own speakers, a headset, or a speaker connected to the computer directly. We set the desk up so the music speaker stays a music speaker, sitting behind the screen rather than facing the microphone, and the call audio never has to take a detour across the network.

The last tap of the night

At the end of the evening the room has to calm down quickly. We name the rooms in the app the way you speak about your apartment — "Sofa", "Desk", "Bed" rather than "Living Room 2" — and set the sleep timer and alarm on the bedside product. For a couple on different schedules we show how Sonos headphones can take the TV sound through TV Audio Swap on supported soundbars, so the film can carry on in silence while the other person falls asleep a few feet away. None of this costs anything extra in equipment. It is the difference between a system that was set up and one that was planned.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Long Island City's Architecture

Long Island City's housing falls into six broad families, and each one decides something different about a Sonos job: what can be drilled, where cable can travel, who must agree, and how far the signal has to reach. Most of our work is in the first two; the others turn up every week.

Rental towers since the 2000s

The glass-and-concrete buildings along Center Boulevard, Jackson Avenue, Queens Plaza and the blocks around Court Square. Studios to three-bedrooms, often managed by the developer. Ceilings are slab, walls between apartments are concrete or rated steel-stud assemblies, and most leases require written consent before anything is fixed. Plans here lean on freestanding and console placement, with a wall bracket only where management agrees.

Condominium towers

Skyline Tower's 802 units in Court Square are the largest example; smaller condos line the side streets of Hunters Point. Owners can usually do more than renters, but the condo's alteration rules still govern drilling, working hours and anything touching the slab or the corridor wall. Boards often ask for a product sheet and an insurance certificate before the day.

Converted industrial lofts

Factory and printing buildings turned into homes and studios, like the Arris Lofts in Dutch Kills. Tall ceilings, exposed concrete or timber, sprinkler pipes and conduit running in the open, big steel windows. Speakers can often hang or sit where they couldn't in a tower, and exposed cable in conduit is part of the look rather than a compromise.

Hunters Point rowhouses

The 1870s and 1880s houses on 45th Avenue and their neighbours: brick or brownstone fronts, wood joists, plaster on the older walls and, inside the historic district, facades reviewed by the Landmarks Commission. Many are divided into two or three apartments. Inside work follows the ordinary rules; anything on the front belongs in a separate conversation.

Dutch Kills walk-ups and small buildings

Three- to six-storey brick buildings between Queens Plaza and the rail yards, some over shops, some beside hotels. Walls are brick and plaster, apartments are railroad or compact layouts, and the elevated trains along Queens Plaza and 31st Street are close by. Sonos plans here deal with plaster, train noise and walk-up stairs.

Queensbridge and Ravenswood

NYCHA's six-storey brick buildings north of the Queensboro Bridge. House rules from the housing authority come first, so we keep these jobs to products that plug in and sit on furniture, set up properly on the resident's own network, and avoid any drilling the rules don't clearly allow.

Inside the Tower

The Concrete Box: Signal, Cable and Drilling in a New Long Island City Apartment

Once the daily plan is clear, a new tower apartment sets the technical limits. They are not difficult, but each one quietly decides what is possible, and most surprises on install day come from one of these five.

1. The slab overhead

In a typical concrete tower the ceiling of your apartment is the bottom of the next floor's slab, painted or skimmed. There is no cavity for an in-ceiling speaker, and in some buildings the slab is post-tensioned: steel tendons run through the concrete under very high tension. Concrete-scanning firms are blunt about the hazard. Penhall's guidance notes that a single half-inch strand carries roughly 30,000 pounds, and that cutting into one without scanning first is dangerous; the remedy it describes is a ground-penetrating radar scan to mark the tendons before anything is drilled. For a Sonos job that means we do not drill the slab on the strength of a guess. If the building's manual or the managing agent says the slabs are post-tensioned, overhead fixings are off the table unless the building arranges and approves a scan; we plan around the slab instead.

2. Soffits and bulkheads

Where a tower does have space overhead, it is usually in a dropped soffit along the hallway, over the kitchen or around the window line, built in drywall on steel framing to hide ducts and sprinkler pipes. Those soffits are the only places in many apartments where an in-ceiling speaker or a concealed cable can go, and they are full of things that matter more than music. We open nothing until we know what is inside, and we do not move or obstruct sprinkler heads, smoke detectors or duct access panels.

3. The closet panel

Many newer apartments bring every cable — internet, TV, sometimes phone — into a recessed metal panel in a coat or utility closet, and the provider's router ends up inside it. A metal box behind a closet door is a poor place to broadcast Wi-Fi. Sonos's interference guidance rates metal as the worst obstruction it lists, "Very High", ahead of plaster and concrete. Where the panel has a spare cable to a room, we move the router out to that room and leave only a modem or fiber terminal in the closet. Where it doesn't, we wire one Sonos product or an access point near the panel and let the rest of the system work from there.

4. The glass wall

Floor-to-ceiling glass is beautiful and acoustically lively. Sound reflects off it, a soundbar's side-firing drivers bounce off it unevenly, and a speaker on a window ledge picks up heat and the slight hum of the convector or through-wall unit below. We keep the anchor on a solid wall when one exists, use Trueplay after the curtains or shades are in place, and treat the window line as somewhere for a movable speaker on a nice evening rather than a permanent home for anything.

5. The heating and cooling unit

Through-wall heating and cooling units, common in Long Island City rentals, blow air up the glass and make a steady noise when they run. A speaker placed in their airflow gathers dust and sounds different every time the fan starts. We keep speakers out of that stream and run Trueplay with the unit off, because Sonos's tuning instructions ask for a quiet room while the tuning sweep runs.

Rules and conditions that apply to business sound

  • The 42 dBA ceiling. According to the city's noise code fact sheet, music played by a commercial establishment "shall not exceed 42 dBA as measured in any dwelling unit receiving the noise". In mixed buildings along Vernon Boulevard and at the base of the towers, that figure decides speaker type, position and the volume limit we set.
  • Return-air ceilings. In office floors where the space above a hung ceiling carries return air, cable run there must be rated for that use. We check before pulling, not after.
  • Sprinklers and detectors. No speaker or cable is fixed to sprinkler pipes or hung from them, and nothing obstructs a sprinkler head or a smoke detector.
  • Music for customers. Consumer streaming plans are sold for listening at home. A shop, cafe or studio playing to the public should sign up for a business music service, and we connect Sonos to whichever one the owner picks.
  • Landlord and tenant. In multi-tenant commercial buildings, anything fixed to the structure needs the landlord's agreement, and we bring an insurance certificate naming whoever the lease requires.
Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Long Island City: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island City residents and business owners put to us that the rest of our Sonos pages don't already answer. They are grouped by what is really being asked: how one room does several jobs, what a brand-new concrete building allows, how the older lofts and rowhouses differ, and what businesses need.

One room doing several jobs

In a studio where the bed sits a few feet from the TV, where can a Sub go so the bass doesn't come up through the mattress?

Put it on the far side of the seating from the bed, not between them, and get it off the bare slab. A Sub 4 or Sub Mini against the wall behind or beside the sofa still fills the seats, and its energy points away from the sleeping corner. A firm, heavy stand or a solid piece of furniture underneath is better than a soft foam pad, which can let it rock. After that, two settings do the rest: Trueplay with the Sub in its final position, and a lower Sub level in the app that you keep for late films. If the only workable spot is near the bed, a Sub Mini is easier to tame than a Sub 4, and in some studios the right answer is no Sub at all, with an Arc Ultra's own bass doing the work.

One of us sleeps in the alcove while the other is up late at the desk — how can the kitchen end have music without reaching the bed?

Separate the two ends physically and in the app. A small speaker on the kitchen counter or desk, facing away from the alcove and kept at low volume, carries surprisingly little across an open room, especially if a bookcase or curtain sits between. Group nothing with the bedside speaker after a set hour, put a sleep timer on it, and leave the soundbar out of late-night music altogether, because its width spreads sound across the whole space. For anything louder than background music, the person who is awake should use headphones; Sonos headphones can take over a supported soundbar's TV sound so the picture stays on and the room stays quiet.

Can the Era 100 on my desk be the speaker for video calls on my laptop?

It can play music while you work, but it is not the right speaker for calls. Sonos's own AirPlay article warns that sending a Mac's system sound over the older AirPlay route can bring "a delay or audio interruptions", and that Sonos products cannot play AirPlay audio while the source device is on a phone call. A delay of even a fraction of a second makes conversation awkward. Keep calls on the laptop's speakers, a headset or a wired desk speaker, and let the Era 100 handle music before and after. If you want the computer to play through it reliably for music, a cable through Sonos's Line-In Adapter into the Era 100's USB-C port is steadier than wireless.

Our flex bedroom was made with a pressurized temporary wall — can a speaker or soundbar be mounted on it?

We don't fix anything to it. A pressurized wall is held in place by pressure against the floor and ceiling rather than being anchored, and it usually belongs to the landlord or a wall company, not to you. A bracket and the weight of a soundbar or speaker can loosen it. Brick Underground's guide to these walls also notes that the Buildings Department does not treat them differently from ordinary walls for permit purposes, which is one more reason not to add to them. Put the speaker on a stand, a shelf or the dresser in the flex room, and keep the TV and soundbar on one of the apartment's permanent walls.

My roommate games and streams all evening on the same router — will that make the Sonos stutter?

Usually not because of bandwidth. Sonos needs very little of your internet speed to stream music. What does cause trouble is a crowded Wi-Fi channel and a router struggling with many devices at once, and in a tower dozens of neighboring networks compete for the same airwaves. Sonos's interference article suggests restarting the router so it picks a new channel, and lists cordless phones, baby monitors, other access points and microwaves among the usual culprits. The most reliable fix in a busy apartment is to connect the gaming computer or console to the router by cable, and if the router is close to the TV, to wire the soundbar too, which takes both off the crowded wireless.

A brand-new concrete building

The building manual says our floor slabs are post-tensioned — can anything be drilled into the ceiling or floor?

Not by us without the building's approval and a scan. Post-tensioned slabs carry steel strands under great tension, and concrete-scanning specialists warn that cutting one can release that load violently; the standard precaution is a ground-penetrating radar scan to mark them before any drilling. That is the building's call and usually the building's contractor. For Sonos, the practical answer is that you rarely need to drill the slab: soundbars go under the TV on a wall or console, Era speakers on stands or shelves, and any cable runs along walls or inside soffits. If you do want something overhead, ask the managing agent first, and we will work to whatever the scan and the building allow.

The internet arrives in a metal panel inside our coat closet — is that why the Wi-Fi is weak, and can the router come out?

Very likely, and yes in most cases. A router sealed in a metal enclosure behind a closet door loses much of its signal before it reaches the room; Sonos's interference guidance rates metal as the worst obstruction on its list. Look inside the panel for a second cable labelled for a room, often the living room or bedroom. If there is one, we move the router to that room and keep only the modem or fiber box in the closet. If there isn't, we connect one Sonos product or a small access point near the closet by cable and let the system build its own network from there. Either way, the panel itself is not ours to modify if it belongs to the building.

Our building just received its temporary certificate of occupancy and punch-list crews are still on our floor — should the Sonos wait?

Wait for the parts that depend on finished walls, but not for everything. In a newly opened building, painters, electricians and the developer's own trades may still be fixing items on your list, and a soundbar fixed today may come off when a wall is repainted next month. We suggest setting up the freestanding speakers and the network straight away, so you have music from the first week, and booking the wall-mounted soundbar and any concealed cable once the punch list for your apartment is signed off. If the developer's crew needs to reach a wall where a bracket already sits, we can take it down and refit it.

The through-wall heating and cooling unit under our window is loud — where should the speakers sit, and does it matter for tuning?

Keep speakers out of its airflow and switch it off while tuning. Those units push air up the window and across the ledge, so a speaker placed there collects dust and sounds different every time the fan cycles. A shelf or stand a few feet along the wall is better. Trueplay asks for a quiet room while it plays its test tones, so we turn the unit off for the three minutes or so each room takes, then turn it back on. If the unit is loud enough to mask quiet passages in films, Speech Enhancement or Night Sound on the soundbar will help more than turning everything up.

Can the sponsor add speaker wiring to our unit before we close on a new-construction condo?

Sometimes, if you ask early enough and the sponsor agrees. Some developers allow buyers to request changes before the walls in their unit are finished; others refuse anything that isn't on the offering plan. The useful requests are modest: a network cable from the closet panel to the TV wall, a recessed box behind the planned TV position, and blocking in the wall for a bracket. We can mark those on your floor plan for you to send to the sponsor. Anything agreed is the sponsor's work under their contract; we then install and set up the Sonos once you own the apartment. This is general guidance, not legal advice about your purchase agreement.

Lofts, rowhouses and older buildings

Our converted-factory loft has exposed conduit and sprinkler pipes across a concrete ceiling — can speaker cable run in the open too?

Yes, and in a loft it often looks better than hiding it. We run it in its own conduit, matched to what is already on the ceiling, fixed to the concrete rather than to the sprinkler pipes or anyone else's conduit, with neat bends and straps at even spacing. Speakers can then hang from the ceiling on pendant mounts or sit on brackets on columns, which suits tall loft rooms better than anything flush. Before we drill a converted building's concrete, we ask the owner or board what is known about the slab, for the same reason as in a new tower. Inside the conduit, keep speaker cable separate from the building's electrical wiring.

Our 45th Avenue rowhouse is split into apartments — can the owner's floor have ceiling speakers without disturbing the tenants below?

Usually, because the ceiling of the owner's floor is the floor of the apartment above or the roof, not the tenants' space. In these wood-framed houses the joists run between the brick party walls, and speakers go between them. The work is reached from the owner's own rooms, cable travels through the owner's closets and joist bays, and the Amp sits on the owner's floor. Where a ceiling speaker would sit over a tenant's bedroom — for example on a garden-floor ceiling — we suggest wall-facing products instead, because a speaker in a shared floor sends some sound both ways. Anything that would touch the front facade inside the historic district is a separate question for the Landmarks Commission.

Our front room near Queensboro Plaza looks onto the elevated tracks — should the speakers face the window or away from it?

Away from it, and toward where you sit. A speaker aimed at the listener from a few feet away needs less volume to stay clear over a passing train than one bouncing its sound off the glass. In a narrow front room near the el we usually put the soundbar or a stereo pair on the wall opposite the window, run Trueplay between trains, and set the room to a sensible maximum so nobody turns it up for a train and forgets to turn it down afterwards. Heavy curtains on the window side help both the sound and the noise.

Businesses

Our taproom has a concrete floor, brick walls and a steel roof deck — how do we keep the music from turning to mush in all that echo?

Use more speakers at lower volume rather than a few loud ones, and point them at the people, not the walls. In a hard industrial room, sound reflects off every surface, and a pair of big speakers turned up to fill it creates a wash where speech and music blur. Several smaller speakers spread along the seating, each covering a short distance, keep the music clear at a level where customers can still talk. Soft things help too — banquettes, wood tables, acoustic panels in the ceiling bays — and we can suggest where a few panels would do the most good. We tune each zone after the room is open and full, because a crowd changes the sound more than any setting.

Wi-Fi bands and the newest routers

Sonos's system requirements say every Sonos product works on the 2.4 GHz band and that routers offering only 5 GHz are not supported; its list of supported Wi-Fi modes covers 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz and says nothing about 6 GHz. The routers Long Island City residents get from new providers or buy themselves increasingly combine 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz under one name, and some let the 2.4 GHz band be switched off. We check that it is on, that Sonos and the phone running the app share one network, and that no guest network or captive log-in page is involved, because Sonos does not support those.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Long Island City

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our research environment, so the claims below are the ones that circulate in AI answers, retail videos and cost guides generally. Each is tested against what a compact Long Island City apartment actually involves.

"A small apartment doesn't need any planning — just plug the speakers in."

It is true that Sonos products plug into power and join an app, and in a studio nothing needs to be cut. It is not true that a small space is simple. The smaller the room, the more jobs it does, and the closer every speaker sits to the desk, the sofa and the bed at once.

The common failures in Long Island City studios are placement failures: a Sub at the foot of the bed, a soundbar on a pier so narrow that its side drivers fire into the window frame, an Era speaker facing the laptop microphone. None of them shows up on the day the box is opened. They show up the first time someone tries to sleep while someone else is watching a film.

That is why our Long Island City visits start with a written day: what the room does at each hour, and who is in it. Once the plan exists, plugging in really is the easy part.

"Concrete buildings make Wi-Fi speakers useless."

Concrete does weaken Wi-Fi. Sonos's own interference table rates plaster and concrete as "High" obstruction. But inside a single apartment the router and the speakers usually share the same concrete box, and the walls between rooms are often steel-stud partitions, which pass signal far better than the slab.

What actually defeats Sonos in towers is less dramatic: a router shut in a metal closet panel, a mesh system that wasn't set up the way Sonos requires, and dozens of neighbors' networks on the same channel. Sonos lists metal as a "Very High" obstruction, worse than concrete.

The fix is ordinary network work: move the router out of the panel, wire one Sonos product if the layout allows, restart the router so it finds a quieter channel, and confirm the 2.4 GHz band is on. We rarely need more than that inside a Long Island City apartment.

"You can always put ceiling speakers in for an invisible look."

Ask an AI assistant for a tidy Sonos setup and it tends to reach for speakers flush in the ceiling. Even Sonos treats those as a job that usually needs a professional, and it is describing houses whose ceilings are hollow between joists.

In many Long Island City towers the ceiling is a concrete slab with nowhere to put a speaker, and some slabs are post-tensioned, where drilling without a scan is dangerous. The places that can take ceiling speakers are the dropped soffits, the hung ceilings in renovated condos and offices, and the wood-joisted rowhouses of Hunters Point.

So the honest answer for a typical tower apartment is that the clean look comes from a well-placed soundbar and small speakers on shelves or brackets, not from cutting the ceiling. Where a ceiling can take speakers, we price them per zone after seeing it.

"Any speaker can be your computer speaker over AirPlay."

With so many Long Island City residents working from home, this one matters. Sonos does support AirPlay on most of its current products, and for music from a phone or a Mac it works well.

But Sonos's own support page warns that streaming a Mac's system audio over the older AirPlay route can bring delay or interruptions, and that Sonos speakers can't play AirPlay audio while the source device is on a phone call. For video calls, where the voice has to match the lips and a delay derails conversation, that is a real limitation.

We set desk speakers up for music and leave calls on the laptop, a headset or a wired speaker. If you want the computer connected for music without the wireless delay, the Line-In Adapter and a cable are the dependable route.

"Plug in an extender and the dropouts stop."

Generic Wi-Fi advice says to add an extender wherever the signal is weak. Sonos's system requirements list wireless range extenders and powerline Ethernet among the things it does not support, and say that in a mesh network with a wired Sonos product, that product must connect to the primary mesh unit.

In Long Island City apartments we often find a plug-in extender bought to "help" the bedroom speaker, which is exactly what makes it disappear from the app. Removing it, and placing a properly configured mesh unit or access point instead, usually ends the problem.

Mesh is not wrong; it just has to be built the way Sonos expects. We check the configuration rather than adding another box.

"Professional Sonos installation costs hundreds of dollars a room."

National cost guides talk in room-by-room figures for whole-home audio; one we read put additional rooms at $500 to $1,000 each. Those figures describe cutting ceilings and pulling cable through houses, which is not what most Long Island City apartments need.

Our Queens list prices the actual tasks. Setting up a whole Sonos system is $125, a soundbar on a console is $79 and on the wall $129. A studio with a soundbar on its console, a Sub and an Era speaker on the desk can be set up for the $125 line plus $79. Ceiling zones in lofts and rowhouses sit within the published $800 to $2,500 per zone range, priced after a walkthrough.

The difference is that small homes pay for decisions and setup more than for construction, and our prices reflect that.

"Renters can't have a proper Sonos system."

This one appears in forum answers and some AI summaries: if you can't drill, you can't have a good system. In a neighborhood where about three in four homes are rented, it would be bad news if it were true.

It isn't. A soundbar on a console, a Sub Mini against a wall, Era speakers on stands or shelves and a portable for the balcony make a complete system without a single hole. Where a landlord does allow a bracket, the holes are small and easy to fill when you leave.

What renters do need is a plan that moves. We write down which products are where and how the system is set up, so the next apartment, upstairs or across the river, takes an hour rather than a weekend.

Habits our installers keep on apartment calls around western Queens. Read them as working practice, not as accounts of any one customer or address.

Ask where everyone sleeps before asking where the TV goes. In a studio the bed decides more about the Sub than the sofa does, and it is far easier to move a speaker on the plan than after it is on the wall.

FAQ

Long Island City Sonos Installation FAQ

Our TV faces the bed rather than a sofa — does a soundbar still work from that angle?
+

Yes, as long as the bar is centred on the screen and the bed is roughly in front of it. A soundbar aims its sound forward and to the sides, so it serves whoever is in front of the TV, sitting or lying down. Run Trueplay from the bed if that is where you watch. Keep the bar's top clear if it has upward-firing drivers, and if the bed sits much lower than a sofa would, a slight downward tilt of the TV helps the picture more than anything changes the sound.

Our new router puts 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz under one network name — will Sonos connect to it?
+

Usually, provided the 2.4 GHz band stays switched on. Sonos's requirements say all its products support 2.4 GHz and that routers offering only 5 GHz aren't supported, and its list of supported Wi-Fi modes covers 2.4 and 5 GHz only, so the 6 GHz band does nothing for Sonos. Problems start when a router's setup turns the 2.4 GHz band off, puts Sonos on a guest network, or separates the phone running the app from the speakers; we check those three things at every visit.

Can the Sonos visit be booked for the same day the movers come?
+

We'd rather come a day or two later. On moving day the service elevator is usually reserved for the movers, the furniture isn't where it will end up, and the internet often isn't live yet. Sonos placement depends on all three. Book us once the sofa, bed and desk are in place and the provider has connected the service; if you want music on the first night, a portable speaker set up on your own phone will tide you over.

In an apartment under 600 square feet, is a wired Sonos connection ever worth it?
+

Sometimes. In a small apartment with a router in the open, Wi-Fi alone is normally fine. A cable earns its place when the router is shut inside a metal closet panel, when dozens of neighbors' networks crowd the airwaves and the music stutters every evening, or when the soundbar sits right beside the router anyway and a short cable costs nothing extra. The $99 cable run on the Queens list covers up to 25 feet, which reaches most rooms in an apartment this size.

Will our music carry into the corridor through the apartment door?
+

Some will, because the gap around an apartment door is usually the weakest point in an otherwise solid tower wall. Keep speakers and the Sub away from the entry wall, aim them into the room rather than toward the door, and use the volume limit in the Sonos app for late evenings. A door sweep or seal, fitted with the building's agreement, helps more than any speaker setting. We can stand in the corridor while you play music, so you hear what the neighbors would.
