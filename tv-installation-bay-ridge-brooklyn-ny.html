The Neighborhood

Why Bay Ridge Properties Need TV Installation

Bay Ridge is one of the few corners of Brooklyn where three very different generations of housing sit within a short walk of each other, and each one hides a different wall behind the spot where a TV wants to go. The oldest layer that most people picture is the row of bay-fronted limestone houses. The best known of them, on Bay Ridge Parkway between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, became the neighborhood's first historic district in June 2019, when the Landmarks Preservation Commission voted unanimously to designate the Bay Ridge Parkway Doctors' Row Historic District. Brownstoner's report on the vote describes 54 row houses built between 1906 and 1913 in the Renaissance Revival style, and quotes the commission's chair, Sarah Carroll, saying the block "really does stand out in the neighborhood because of the quality of architecture." Similar houses of the same years, in limestone and in brick, line the side streets between Third and Fifth Avenues and the blocks off Ridge Boulevard. Inside, almost all of them still have plaster walls, and many keep the parlor details that make an owner nervous about drilling at all: pier mirrors between the front windows, picture rails with plaster ornament, pocket doors and original woodwork.

The second layer runs along the water. According to a history of Shore Road published by the local site Hey Ridge, about 60 percent of the road's 2.1 miles is lined with apartment buildings, nearly all of them built in or after the late 1920s, once the subway had reached Bay Ridge and older mansions were being pulled down for more affordable housing. Many of those buildings are co-ops now. Corcoran's building records show what they tend to look like: 8701 Shore Road is a co-op from 1932 with an elevator, and 9281 Shore Road is a six-story, 107-unit co-op from 1940 with elevators, a live-in superintendent and rentable storage in the basement. The same generation of six-story elevator buildings runs inland along Ridge Boulevard and the streets around Third Avenue. For a TV installer, these buildings mean plaster partitions inside, masonry around the outside and around the shafts, a superintendent who wants to know who is coming, and very often a board or managing agent with rules about contractors.

The third layer is the one most visitors never see: the detached and semi-detached houses on the blocks above Shore Road. The same Hey Ridge history explains why twelve blocks between 75th and 87th Streets still have freestanding houses rather than apartment buildings. The old Crescent Hill development carried a 1908 restriction, running 21 years, that barred buildings designed for more than one family and set lots of at least 60 by 100 feet; residents fought apartment zoning there for decades, and much of the area is still zoned R2, which allows only single-family detached houses. Those houses run from the early twentieth century to the late postwar years, and their living rooms can have anything from plaster on wood lath to modern drywall on studs, sometimes in the same room after an extension. Fill in the gaps with brick two-families on the side streets and apartments over the stores on Third Avenue, Fifth Avenue and 86th Street, and you get a neighborhood where a stud finder that worked perfectly at one address is close to useless at the next.

The people in those buildings shape the job too. The NYU Furman Center's 2024 figures for Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights, the community district that takes in both neighborhoods, put the homeownership rate at 41.0 percent, which means most households rent, from a landlord in a two-family, a management company in a prewar building or a co-op shareholder subletting an apartment. The same figures show 20.2 percent of residents aged 65 or older, up from 13.1 percent in 2010, and 38.2 percent born outside the United States. In practice that translates into three questions we ask on nearly every Bay Ridge call. Who has to agree to the holes? Who will actually use the TV, and from which chair? And which apps, channels or devices does the household need set up, including services from abroad that a cable box will not show? Getting those answers on the phone is what keeps a visit short, quiet and finished in one trip.

Then there is Fort Hamilton, the Army post at the southern end of the neighborhood, established in 1831. Installation guidance for the post says on-post family housing is "extremely limited" and that "most personnel live off-post throughout the greater New York metro area," with Bay Ridge right outside the gate. Families renting here on orders often know they will move within a few years, and for them the right TV setup is the one that leaves the least behind for the landlord, which we cover further down.

Same-Day Repair in Bay Ridge

Need Repair in Bay Ridge? A TV Sagging Off Old Plaster, a Bracket Loose in a Mortar Joint, or a Wall Full of Old Holes

The urgent TV calls from Bay Ridge come in three familiar shapes, and the neighborhood's walls explain each one. The first is a screen on a prewar plaster wall that has started to tilt forward or drop on one side. Plaster from the 1910s and 1920s is hard on the surface and soft underneath, so a plastic plug or a small drywall toggle feels tight on the day and then slowly crushes the material around it. Full-motion arms make it worse, because every time someone pulls the screen out to face the dining table, the top fixings take a lever load. The second is a bracket on a brick party wall in a row house or two-family that was screwed into the mortar between bricks instead of the brick itself. Mortar in a century-old wall can be soft enough to scratch with a key, and an anchor in it works loose with the seasons. The third is the wall left behind by a previous tenant or owner: an empty bracket, a cluster of holes, and sometimes a TV power cord that was pushed into one hole and pulled out of another.

If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back up. If two people can lift it safely, take it down and unplug it; if not, keep everyone clear of it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV goes back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, that part is priced as a new mount on that wall type, so a 55-inch TV rehung on plaster would be $179.99 plus $45, with the $85 take-down added. If we find the trouble is the wall itself, a crack running through the plaster, a brick that moves, or lath that has pulled away from the framing, we say so plainly and stop, so you can bring in the landlord, the super or a plasterer before anything goes back on that wall. And if we find a power cord fished through the wall, it comes out: the National Electrical Code does not allow it, and we replace it with an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) or a cord cover on the surface ($59).

TV Installation for Bay Ridge's Architecture

Seven kinds of building account for nearly every Bay Ridge TV call. They differ in three ways that decide the plan long before anyone talks about TV brands: what the wall behind the screen is made of, how the screen gets from the street to the room, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled.

The limestone row house

Two- and three-story houses from the first years of the twentieth century, with bowed or angled fronts, plaster inside, brick party walls on both sides and a parlor full of original detail.

The Doctors' Row house

One of the 54 landmarked houses on Bay Ridge Parkway between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, built 1906 to 1913. Same walls as other row houses, with the facade under the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

The Shore Road co-op

Elevator buildings from the late 1920s onward, like 8701 Shore Road (1932) and 9281 Shore Road (1940, six stories, 107 units), with a board, a superintendent and windows on the Narrows.

The Ridge Boulevard prewar rental

Five- and six-story apartment buildings inland from the water, with plaster partitions, masonry around the outside and a management company that sets the rules.

The brick two-family

Two stacked apartments on the side streets, often with the owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, a shared stair and a basement below.

The detached house on the Crescent Hill blocks

Freestanding houses between 75th and 87th Streets near Shore Road, with wide living rooms, fireplaces, and extensions whose walls do not match the original house.

The apartment over an avenue store

Walk-up flats over shops on Third Avenue, Fifth Avenue and 86th Street, with long narrow rooms, a street-facing wall and a stair that decides how big a TV box can go up.

The limestone row house: a parlor full of detail and two brick walls you share

In a Bay Ridge row house the TV almost always ends up in one of three places: the long side wall of the front parlor, the chimney breast if the room has one, or the back parlor or dining room behind it. The long side wall is usually the party wall you share with the house next door, and in houses of this age that is solid brick under the plaster. A Brownstoner forum thread on mounting a TV in a brownstone made the same point years ago: posters noted that party walls in these houses often have no studs at all, just plaster over brick, while the interior walls are usually plaster over wood lath. Both are workable, but they take completely different fixings. On brick the anchor has to go through the plaster and into the brick itself, with a hammer drill, at a depth matched to the anchor, and it has to land in the face of a brick rather than the softer mortar line. On lath and plaster, the fixing has to find the framing behind the plaster or use an anchor rated for a hollow wall, because the plaster alone will not hold a TV for long.

The other thing a row house parlor brings is detail that nobody wants damaged. Brownstoner's report on the house for sale at 6732 Ridge Boulevard, a brick single-family from between 1906 and 1915, lists a pier mirror in the parlor, a picture rail with plaster ornament, pocket doors and unpainted woodwork, and plenty of houses on the side streets have the same. We work around all of it. Picture rails and plaster ornament are not load-bearing and are never used to carry a screen. Woodwork stays untouched; if the bracket would sit across a chair rail or a casing, we move the bracket or choose a different wall. And because the front of a Bay Ridge row house is often a bow or an angled bay, the flat wall space in the front room is smaller than it looks, so we measure the screen's width against the flat section before you decide.

Getting the TV in is usually easy, since the parlor is one flight up the stoop or at grade, but the stoop and the front door decide the largest box that can come through without being turned on its side. If the TV is in the store, ask for delivery in the original carton, and leave it sealed until we arrive. On the wall, our Brooklyn list puts a 55-inch screen on a brick party wall at $179.99 plus $65, before the bracket. The same screen on a lath-and-plaster interior wall is $179.99 plus $45. The difference is not the age of the house; it is the tools, the anchors and the time each wall takes.

The Doctors' Row house: what the landmark designation does and does not touch

Owners on the landmarked block of Bay Ridge Parkway often assume that the designation reaches inside the house. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's own guidance says otherwise in most cases. Its page on permits and alterations says that "most exterior changes to the front and rear facades of buildings in historic districts require review by LPC," and that an LPC permit for interior work is needed only when the work requires a permit from the Buildings Department, when work on the interior affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark. Hanging a TV on an inside wall, running its cables down to a console and putting a soundbar under it do not normally fall into any of those three cases. If you are unsure, the commission's preservation staff will tell you whether a particular job needs a permit, and we are happy to wait for that answer before booking.

Where the landmark rules do matter is at the front and rear of the house. A cable or antenna line run up the limestone facade, a box screwed to the front of the building, or a new opening drilled through the front wall is exterior work, and that is where we stop and tell you to talk to the commission first. Inside, the job is the same as any other limestone row house of those years: plaster on the interior partitions, brick at the party walls, and a parlor with original detail that we keep clear of. Several houses on the block were built with ground-floor rooms used as medical offices, which is how the block got its name, and some still have a practice downstairs and a household upstairs. That combination sometimes means a waiting-room screen and a living-room TV in the same house, priced separately but done in one visit.

The Shore Road co-op: easy carry, a superintendent, and a board with paperwork

The apartment buildings that line most of Shore Road make the carry simple. There is an elevator, a lobby and usually a superintendent, which is exactly why there is also paperwork. Brick Underground describes an alteration agreement as the document that protects a building's shared systems and structure from work in one apartment, covering things like risers, building-wide electrical and plumbing, insurance requirements and the days and hours work is allowed, and quotes architect Alex Shkreli saying the rules are "about protecting the building from changes that could harm it." A TV on a wall is usually treated as minor work, but an in-wall power kit that opens a wall can look different to a managing agent, so we ask before we book which of the two your building allows, and we send whatever insurance certificate the office asks for ahead of the visit.

Inside, the walls of a 1930s or 1940s co-op are mixed. Partitions between rooms in buildings of that era were often built of hollow clay tile or gypsum block with plaster over them; outer walls and the walls around elevator and stair shafts are masonry. Hollow tile is the one that catches people out. It sounds and drills like solid masonry for the first quarter inch, then the bit breaks through into a void, and a standard plug simply spins. The fix is an anchor designed for hollow masonry, drilled slowly so the tile face does not shatter, with as many of the bracket's fixing points used as the plate allows. We price that wall by what it is once the pilot hole shows us: plaster over framing at $45, plaster over brick or masonry at the $65 brick rate.

Two Shore Road details come up again and again. The first is the view. Many living rooms face the Narrows and the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, which is why people moved in, and the TV ends up on the wall opposite the windows or beside them. Western light off the water in the late afternoon can wash out a screen, so we tape the outline and look at it at the time of day you usually watch. The second is the superintendent's knowledge. In older buildings the super often knows which wall has a riser pipe in it, which one backs onto the stair, and which neighbor complained about the last renovation. We ask, and we listen.

The Ridge Boulevard prewar rental: a landlord's wall and a tenant's TV

Inland from the water, the same generation of five- and six-story elevator buildings runs along Ridge Boulevard and the side streets toward Third Avenue, many of them rentals run by a management company. The walls are the same mix as the Shore Road co-ops, plaster on tile or block partitions inside and masonry outside, but the person who decides is different: it is the landlord, through a lease. Before booking, read the part of your lease about alterations or attaching things to walls. If it is silent, or allows fixtures with consent, ask the management office in writing and keep the reply. If it is strict, the TV on its stand at $119.99 gives you the same tidy result with nothing to patch at move-out.

Rentals here also tend to have the outlet low on the baseboard or on the wrong wall, and the cable jack wherever the provider left it. A paintable cord cover ($59) runs the cables neatly from the screen down to the outlet, can be painted to match, and leaves only a few small screw holes when it comes off. It never carries the TV's own power cord inside the wall.

The brick two-family: an owner downstairs and a stair that sets the size limit

Bay Ridge's two-family houses are mostly brick, with two stacked apartments, a shared entrance and stair, and a basement under both. The living room in each unit usually runs across the front of the house. Its longest wall is often the party wall shared with the next house, which is solid masonry and an excellent place for a heavy screen, and the other long wall is frequently the one with the stair or the hallway behind it, which is framed and plastered. A TV on the first takes the brick add-on; a TV on the second takes the plaster add-on and needs the fixings to find the framing. We tell you which is which from the pilot hole before the bracket goes up.

In a two-family, the landlord is often the neighbor. If you rent the upper unit and the owner lives below, a short message before the visit saying which wall, roughly how many holes and where the wires will go saves an awkward conversation when the drill starts. If you own the house and are setting up the rental unit, mounting the TV yourself on a wall you chose, with the wires in a cover or an in-wall kit, means future tenants use one bracket rather than drilling their own. Either way the job is priced the same: by the wall, the add-ons and the cable plan.

The detached house on the Crescent Hill blocks: wide rooms, fireplaces and extensions

The freestanding houses near Shore Road between 75th and 87th Streets were built under a restriction that set large lots and single-family use, and the living rooms reflect it: wide, often with a fireplace on the end wall and windows on two or three sides. The fireplace wall is the obvious place for a TV and often the worst one for watching, because the screen ends up high and the neck pays for it. Brownstoner forum posters made the same point about brownstones, one calling a TV over a mantel "awfully high" and another noting that heat from a working fireplace "won't be good for the tv." On our list, above-fireplace mounting is a $130 add-on on top of the wall rate, and we only recommend it after checking the mantel depth, the heat at screen height with the fireplace in use, and where you actually sit.

Many of these houses have been extended over the decades, with a family room, a sunroom or a finished basement added in a different era from the original house. The extension's walls are usually drywall on studs, sometimes on metal studs, and the original house's walls are plaster. That is good news for hiding wires, because a framed drywall wall takes an in-wall power and cable kit easily, and it often makes the family room the better TV room. We look at both rooms with you and give you both prices before you decide.

The apartment over an avenue store: a narrow stair, a street wall and a store below

The flats above the shops on Third Avenue, Fifth Avenue and 86th Street are walk-ups, usually two or three floors over the store, and the stair is the first problem. A 65-inch TV in its carton is long and awkward, and in a narrow stair with a turn at the landing it may have to go up on its edge, which is fine for a TV still in its original packing and risky for one that has been unboxed. Inside, the living room often faces the avenue, and the front wall is masonry with windows, while the side walls are party walls or framed partitions. Street noise and light come with the front wall, so the TV usually lands on a side wall, angled slightly away from the windows.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Ten Minutes Inside a Bay Ridge Parlor

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like Bay Ridge's. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address or customer.

Find the party walls first. In a row house or two-family, the technician works out which walls are shared with the next house before touching a tool. Those are almost always brick, and they decide the anchor, the drill and the noise. Interior walls with the stair or hall behind them are usually framed.

Look for what runs inside. Steam risers in the corners, gas to the kitchen, surface conduit from an electrical upgrade, and in co-ops the riser walls the superintendent has warned about. A metal box on the surface of the wall usually means wiring added later in conduit, and conduit runs somewhere we must not drill.

Knock, then scan, then trust neither. A knock at screen height tells a lot: a hollow drum for drywall, a dull hard knock for plaster on masonry. A scanner confirms framing, but on plaster over metal lath it reads metal everywhere, and on dense plaster it reads nothing. That is why the next step exists.

One pilot hole where the plate will hide it. White powder and then wood means lath and framing. White powder and then red grit means brick. A sudden drop means hollow tile or a void. That single hole sets the anchor and the add-on, and if it changes the price from what we quoted, you hear it before any more drilling.

Tape, then sit down. Painter's tape marks the screen's outline. You sit where you watch, at the time of day you watch, so the bay-window glare in a row house or the late sun off the Narrows in a Shore Road apartment is seen on the wall, not discovered after the bracket is up.

Drill once, and say so first. In a co-op or a two-family, the neighbors hear masonry drilling. The technician warns the household, does all the drilling in one short burst inside the building's working hours, and keeps everything else quiet.

Wall by Wall

Lath, Tile, Brick or Limestone Chimney: What Holds a TV in a Bay Ridge Wall

Our Brooklyn base price, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. Very few of the walls in a Bay Ridge row house or prewar apartment are drywall over wood studs, which is why we explain the wall add-ons before anything else. Each one exists because that wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time. None of them exists because a house is old or a block is expensive.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). This is the classic interior wall of a Bay Ridge house from before the Second World War: thin wooden strips nailed across the studs, covered with two or three coats of plaster that can add up to close to an inch. Plaster is strong when pushed and weak when pulled. A screw into the lath alone, or a plug in the plaster alone, can feel solid and still fail over months, especially under a full-motion arm. The fixing that lasts goes through plaster and lath into the stud behind, or uses a toggle rated for the load where the bracket cannot reach a stud. Studs in these houses are rarely on modern spacing, so we find each one rather than measuring from a corner.

Plaster on hollow clay tile or gypsum block (priced by what is behind it). Common in the partitions of Shore Road and Ridge Boulevard apartment buildings from the late 1920s and 1930s. Tile sounds solid and is not; a normal plug spins in it and a sleeve anchor can break through the thin face. Anchors made for hollow masonry, placed slowly, are the answer, with the bracket's full set of fixing points used. Once the pilot hole shows tile or block, we price the wall at the brick and stone rate, because it needs masonry tools and anchors.

Plaster on brick (+$65). The party walls of row houses and two-families, and the outer walls of most apartment buildings. Brick holds a TV very well when the anchor is in the brick, not the mortar joint. We drill with a hammer drill, choose sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and keep each hole clear of the brick's edges. On older, softer brick we spread the load across more fixings at lower load each.

Stone and the chimney breast (+$65, plus +$130 above a fireplace). A chimney breast in a parlor or a Crescent Hill living room is masonry even when the fireplace is purely decorative, and limestone or brick facing around a fireplace is drilled with the same care as a party wall. Mounting above the firebox adds $130 on our list because the screen sits higher, the mantel may stick out, the heat has to be checked, and the cables need a plan that does not go up the inside of a chimney.

Drywall on studs (base price). Found in renovated apartments, finished basements and the extensions on detached houses. If the studs are wood, it is the base price. If a renovated apartment turns out to have light-gauge steel studs, the bracket needs fasteners made for steel spread across two studs, and we tell you from the pilot hole what that wall needs before going further.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The request that follows "put it on the wall" is almost always "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a common wrong way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord through a hole behind the screen and pull it out of another hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow it. Section 400.12, as summarized by the trade magazine EC&M, says flexible cords "are not permitted to be run through holes in walls, structural ceilings, suspended or dropped ceilings, or floors" and "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." A cord inside a wall can be nicked by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected.

The right way on a framed wall is an in-wall power and cable kit. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives you a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. On our Brooklyn list it is $169 including the kit. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits lath-and-plaster partitions and drywall, and it does not suit brick, hollow tile or stone. On those walls, the honest choices are a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly on the surface and can be painted to match, or placing the TV directly above a console so the cables drop straight behind the furniture. In a co-op, the in-wall kit is also the option most likely to need the managing agent's agreement first, so we raise it before the visit, not on the day.

In a rental, the difference matters at move-out. An in-wall kit leaves two neat rectangular openings to patch; a cord cover leaves a handful of small screw holes; a bracket leaves four to six holes about the width of a pencil. All of them can be filled and painted, but a landlord may want to agree to the bigger ones first, which brings us to leases, boards and moving.

Compliance notes for Bay Ridge businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, wall-mounted objects with their leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may not protrude more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a waiting room or corridor can break that rule when it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls or ceilings. NEC 400.12 bars flexible cords from being run through holes in walls, ceilings or floors, or concealed by them. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through a rated in-wall power kit or a surface cover.
  • Exits, signs and life-safety devices stay clear. We do not mount screens where they block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and cables never cross a path people use to leave.
  • Landmarked fronts stay untouched. On Doctors' Row, exterior work on the front and rear facades needs Landmarks Preservation Commission review; a screen inside the window is interior work, but anything fixed to the facade is not.
  • Insurance paperwork before the visit. Commercial landlords and managed buildings often require a certificate of insurance naming the owner and agent. We send it ahead of time.
  • Hardware rated above the load. Every bracket we install is rated for more than the screen's weight, and masonry anchors are rated for the material they are in. A bar TV that gets leaned on, bumped or pulled needs that margin more than one in a living room.
Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Bay Ridge: Questions Answered

These are the situations Bay Ridge households and businesses describe when they call: curved front rooms, co-op paperwork, military moves, chimney breasts, a back parlor that Wi-Fi cannot reach, and a bar racing the Friday crowd. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Row houses and the parlor floor

The front room of our limestone row house is a rounded bay with three tall windows. Is there anywhere flat enough for a 65-inch screen in that room?

Usually not in the bay itself. A bowed front has no flat run wide enough for a large screen, and the windows take most of what there is. The usual answer is the long side wall of the same room, which in a row house is the party wall: solid brick under the plaster, very strong, and facing across the room rather than into the light. We measure the flat width of that wall between the bay and the doorway, check it against the screen's width plus a few inches each side, and tape it out. If the wall is shorter than it looks, the back parlor is often the better TV room.

Our back parlor sits behind two brick walls and the router is in the front room. Will a smart TV there stream properly?

Sometimes yes, often weakly. Brick and old plaster on metal lath both absorb Wi-Fi, and a TV two walls from the router may buffer at night. Before mounting, we check the signal at the TV wall with your own network. If there is an existing network jack nearby, we test it and use it. If there is not, a setup-only visit ($195 for the first hour) can cover moving the streaming device, adding a mesh unit you supply, or setting the TV to the stronger band, and we tell you honestly if the room needs a cable run that is a separate job.

The chimney breast in our parlor was bricked up decades ago. Does a sealed fireplace still count as mounting above a fireplace?

It counts as masonry, and the price depends on where the screen goes. A sealed chimney breast is still brick or stone behind the plaster, so the wall takes the $65 brick and stone add-on. If the screen hangs above the old mantel or firebox opening, the $130 above-fireplace add-on also applies, because the height, the mantel depth and the cable route are the same problems as a working fireplace. If the breast is flat to the floor with no mantel, it is priced as a brick wall only. Heat is no longer a concern once a flue is sealed, but we still check the breast for soft spots and old patches before drilling.

Co-ops and apartment buildings near the water

Our Shore Road co-op wants a contractor's insurance certificate and limits work to weekday business hours. How long will the noisy part of the visit actually take?

The loud part is short. On plaster over hollow tile or brick, a bracket takes four to six holes with a hammer drill, which is a few minutes of noise in one or two bursts. Add a few more if a soundbar bracket goes up. Everything else, the scanning, taping, hanging, cable work and setup, is quiet. We schedule the visit inside the building's hours, send the certificate in the names the managing agent gives us before the day, and tell the superintendent when we arrive so the elevator and lobby are not a surprise.

Our living room faces the Narrows and the only long wall gets the late-afternoon sun. Should the screen go on that wall anyway?

Often it should, with care. A wall that faces the windows can take a screen if the glare is managed. We tape the outline and look at it at the hour you usually watch; if the reflection is bad, a slight tilt, a little more height, or moving the screen a foot away from the window line often fixes it. If the only long wall is the one with the windows in it, a screen between windows is possible but will fight the light, and the side wall, even a shorter one, may be the better place. We would rather show you on the wall than guess.

The superintendent says the wall we want hides the building's riser. Can the TV go a few feet over, or is the whole wall off limits?

Usually a few feet over is fine, and the super's warning is exactly the information we want. A riser pipe or conduit runs vertically in a chase, typically near a corner or beside a kitchen or bathroom. We scan to confirm where it is, keep every fixing well clear of it, and if the screen needs to be centered on the room, we use a bracket with a wide plate or a slight offset so the anchors land in the safe part of the wall. If the riser runs through the middle, we will suggest another wall.
