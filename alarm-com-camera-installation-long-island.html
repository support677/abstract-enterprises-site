The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Alarm.com Camera Installation

Long Island is not one place for a camera installer. It is two counties with different houses, different police departments and different permit rules, and an Alarm.com system has to fit all three. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau County, of which 364,529 are one-unit detached houses, and 579,790 units in Suffolk County, of which 463,095 are detached. That is roughly three in every four homes in Nassau and four in every five in Suffolk standing on their own lot. The same survey gives a median year built of 1955 for Nassau and 1970 for Suffolk. Fifteen years sounds small. On a ladder it is the difference between a low, wide ranch with a shallow attic and no basement, and a two-story colonial with a full basement, a garage under a bedroom and a roof line that puts the soffits twenty feet up. The state's own county profiles add the political map that sits over the houses: Nassau has two cities, three towns, 64 incorporated villages and more than 60 unincorporated hamlets in 453 square miles, while Suffolk is divided into 10 towns across 2,373 square miles. Every camera we mount sits inside one of those municipalities and one of those police jurisdictions.

The Nassau half of that story begins in a potato field. Keri Murray Architecture's history of Levittown records 17,447 homes built there between 1947 and 1951, ranch houses on concrete slabs with an expandable attic, no garage and hot-water radiant heating pipes running through the slab itself. Encyclopedia.com notes that Levitt and Sons dropped the Cape Cod model in 1949 and moved to the ranch, and US History Scene observes that so much remodeling has happened since that very few original Capes survive. Before Levittown, according to History.com, William Levitt had already built 1,080 homes in Westbury and 2,250 in Roslyn. For an Alarm.com job, that heritage means three things. There is no basement to run cable through, so the attic is the highway. The slab carries heating pipes, so nothing is ever drilled downward into the floor. And after seventy-plus years of dormers, extensions and finished attics, no two houses on a block are wired the same way, so the route we used next door tells us nothing about yours.

Uniondale and East Garden City, the area many people mean when they say "the Nassau Hub," show the same pattern a few miles south. NeighborhoodScout reports that single-family detached homes make up 69.74 percent of Uniondale's housing and that 55.45 percent of its homes were built between the 1940s and the 1960s. Homes.com describes the area as split by the Hempstead Turnpike, with East Garden City to the north busy with commercial life and condos, townhouses and apartments, and the residential south made up mostly of Cape Cods and minimal traditional houses, often with detached garages and private driveways. A detached garage is one of the most common reasons an Alarm.com camera job takes longer than the owner expects. The camera on the garage needs power, and it needs a reliable connection back to the house, and the gap between the two is open ground or a driveway that was not built with a conduit under it.

Driveways are also where Long Island break-ins tend to start. When Nassau and Suffolk police, both district attorneys, the NYPD, State Police and the FBI announced a joint burglary and stolen-car task force in January 2024, ABC7 reported that police believed the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways and that break-ins while people were home had gone up. In November 2025 NBC New York reported a Jericho break-in in which security cameras showed how quickly a crew of four reached the second floor. In June 2026 Daily Voice described an East Hills case in which a garage door alarm leading into the main floor was triggered and a woman went to investigate and found intruders already in her kitchen. A month earlier the same outlet reported a Roslyn Heights homeowner who spotted would-be burglars on his surveillance system, called police, and saw arrests follow. LI Life and Politics, summarizing Nassau's figures for January through May, reported burglaries up 5 percent, from 234 in 2025 to 245 in 2026, even as overall property crime fell. Those stories are the clearest argument for what Alarm.com is built to do: tie a door sensor that trips to a camera that shows you what tripped it, on the same phone, at the same moment.

Suffolk's pattern in the news is more about the porch. In November 2025 CBS New York reported that the Suffolk District Attorney had charged 14 men in a porch theft ring that worked 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, between October 2023 and February 2025, allegedly hacking systems to learn when carriers were delivering phones and tablets, and taking more than 200 devices. CBS also reported a Patchogue package thief on Rider Avenue who waved at the camera. ABC7 covered a man in Central Islip caught on video using a knife to destroy a doorbell camera. Over the county line, News 12 Long Island reported porch thefts caught on Ring cameras on Walters Avenue in Wantagh and on Atlantic Avenue in Massapequa Park, and Garden City's police log for the week ending September 15, 2026, as published by Patch, recorded packages taken from a front porch and damage to a Flock camera on Clinton Road. A doorbell camera that a thief can reach with a knife, or that only catches the top of a hooded head, is not doing its job. Mounting height, angle and a sturdy fixing are part of an Alarm.com install, not an afterthought.

Then there is the island's newest housing, which is not houses at all. The Governor's office describes Station Yards, also called the Ronkonkoma Hub, as a transit-oriented development spanning 53 acres around the Ronkonkoma Long Island Rail Road station, now expected to cost about $1.2 billion. GreaterLongIsland reported that by March 2020 four vacant industrial buildings on the site had been converted into 489 luxury apartments. Trite CRE's February 2026 update describes a full build-out of up to 1,450 apartments, 195,000 square feet of retail and 360,000 square feet of office space stretching from Ronkonkoma Avenue east to Mill Road, and Long Island Wave's October 2026 report on the latest phase covers three new five-story buildings with 285 apartments and a 30,000-square-foot Northwell medical hub, after The Core at 3 Hawkins Avenue put 388 apartments over 70,000 square feet of retail. RentCafe lists self-service package lockers and a concierge at the Alston at Station Yards, 1000 Mill Road, and a renter review on ApartmentRatings mentions gated parking, common areas, an Amazon Hub and a pool. Renters in managed buildings like these usually cannot run cable through walls, and Alarm.com itself describes self-install as a good fit for renters, small spaces and people who prefer to set things up on their own. Part of our job on Long Island is telling a renter honestly when they do not need an installer at all, and when a single visit to mount and configure saves them a weekend.

Businesses make up the rest of the call list. Long Island's office market stood at 38.45 million square feet with an 11.5 percent total vacancy rate in CBRE's second-quarter 2026 figures, and its industrial vacancy at 7.0 percent. The Long Island Innovation Park at Hauppauge, as Certilman Balin described it in 2017, holds about 1,350 businesses and roughly 55,000 workers in eleven square miles, making it the largest industrial park in the Northeast by number of companies and employees. When a flex building or an office suite changes hands or tenants, the camera system often changes with it, and Alarm.com's commercial recorder, which handles up to 16 cameras at up to 4MP with built-in PoE and works with third-party ONVIF or VAPIX cameras, is one of the systems new tenants ask us to put in or take over.

Weather is the last local factor, and it matters more for a cloud-connected camera than for a standalone recorder in a closet. When Tropical Storm Isaias hit in August 2020, ABC7 reported that almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers were in the dark at the peak. In August 2024, the state Department of Financial Services recorded Suffolk County's first-ever flash flood emergency, the failure of the Stony Brook Mill Pond dam and more than 2,000 residents and business owners with flood damage. Superstorm Sandy left Long Beach with about 150 million dollars in damage, according to Wikipedia's account of the storm in New York. A camera that reports to the Alarm.com cloud needs power and an internet connection to keep sending clips. That is why every Long Island quote we write asks where the router lives, whether it is on battery backup, and whether a camera at the far end of the property should also keep a local copy of its video.

Two Counties, Two Rulebooks

Nassau or Suffolk: How the County Line Changes an Alarm.com Camera Job

An Alarm.com camera is a video device, but on most Long Island homes it lives on the same account and the same app as a burglar alarm, and burglar alarms are regulated county by county. Nassau and Suffolk built their rules for the same reason, too many false alarms tying up patrol cars, but they built them differently. The table sets out what the sources say for each county. It is a summary for planning, not legal advice, and the county police pages and codes are the final word.

Why the permit question belongs in a camera quote

Strictly speaking, a camera on its own is not a burglar alarm, and none of the county sources we read say that a camera-only Alarm.com account needs a police permit. But very few Long Island Alarm.com accounts are camera-only. Most carry door and window sensors, a motion detector or a garage sensor, and those are what the permit rules are about. When we add cameras to a monitored system, we ask whether the system is registered, because the worst time to discover that a Nassau system was never registered is the night a sensor trips and the police dispatcher sees "No Police Response" against the address. Registration is the owner's job, not ours, but it takes us one question to flag it, and we would rather ask than assume.

The Suffolk police district is not the whole county

The Suffolk County Code's wording ties its permit to the Suffolk County Police District, not to every address with a Suffolk ZIP code. Some parts of the county are policed by town or village departments, so the first thing to check is whether your address falls inside the district the code names. If it does, the permit applies before the alarm is operated. If it does not, ask the department that does police your address what it requires. Either way, the cameras go on the same as they would anywhere else; the paperwork is what changes.

How video changes the false-alarm math

Both counties' rules exist because of false alarms, and Suffolk's own figure of 97,000 in 2015 explains why fees start after the free allowance runs out. This is where cameras earn their keep beyond catching a thief. Connextivity, writing about Alarm.com commercial systems, describes video verification as linking camera footage to a triggered alarm so the scene can be checked before anyone is dispatched. On a home system, the same idea works on your phone: the garage sensor trips, a clip from the garage camera arrives with the alert, and you can see whether it is a person at the side door or the wind pushing a loose panel. A camera aimed at each alarmed entry is the simplest way we know to avoid paying for a false alarm you could have spotted yourself.

Camera registries are separate, and voluntary

Registering cameras with the police is a different thing from registering an alarm. The Suffolk police SHIELD program lets residents and businesses list where their cameras are, free, and says plainly that the department does not get access to those cameras; detectives may ask a participant to check video for a date and time, and retrieval is at the owner's convenience. SHIELD also runs an Emergency Remote Camera Access option that partners can opt into or out of at any time. Garden City's police run a similar voluntary registry and also say it does not give them access to privately owned systems. Whether you join is your choice. We mention it because owners often ask, after we have mounted the cameras, whether they should.

Login, Router, Attic: Six Things to Check at a Long Island Address Before We Arrive

Most of the time lost on an Alarm.com camera job is lost before a camera comes out of its box: nobody can find the account login, the video add-on was never ordered, the router sits in the far corner of the basement, or the attic hatch is behind a closet full of winter coats. Ten minutes with a phone and a flashlight prevents nearly all of it. Picture the camera views from inside the house

Before you count cameras, walk through the house and stand at each door you use, then look out. The front door, the side door into the kitchen or garage, the back slider and the driveway are the four views most Long Island owners end up wanting, and the news stories above show why: thieves come for the car in the driveway, the package on the step, and the door they think nobody is watching. Notice where the sun sets relative to each door, because a camera facing straight into a low western sun at five o'clock in December will struggle. Notice where a tree or a hedge blocks the view of the driveway. Send us a rough sketch, even on the back of an envelope, and we will turn it into camera positions.

Check what your neighbors' windows look like from your doors

On a Levittown or Hicksville block where houses sit close together, a camera aimed at your side yard often looks straight at the neighbor's kitchen window. Vertex Security's guidance on New York camera law recommends privacy masking on exterior cameras so you do not capture a neighbor's backyard in a way that runs into Civil Rights Law section 52-a, which bars video surveillance where someone has a reasonable expectation of privacy. We plan views and masks with that in mind, and it helps if you tell us in advance which neighboring windows face your property.

Alarm.com Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Slab Ranches and Expanded Capes

The Levittown model and its neighbors: concrete slab, radiant heating pipes in the floor, an attic that may now be a bedroom. Cable goes up and over, never down, and a finished attic means fishing behind knee walls.

Flex, Office and Retail Buildings

Block walls, drop ceilings, telecom closets and parking lots. PoE cameras on a commercial recorder, cable in J-hooks or conduit, and a landlord's approval before anything goes on the facade.

Working in a slab ranch

The Levittown ranch, as Keri Murray Architecture describes it, sits on a concrete slab with hot-water heating pipes running through it. That single fact shapes the whole job. We never drill down into the floor to pass a cable, because a bit that finds a heating pipe in a slab turns a camera job into a plumbing emergency. Instead, everything goes up. From the router, usually in a living room or a front bedroom in these houses, the cable climbs inside a wall cavity or a closet corner to the attic, runs across the ceiling joists, and drops into the soffit above each camera. Where the attic has been finished into a bedroom, as so many have since the 1950s, the route goes behind the knee walls, along the eaves, where there is usually just enough room to work with a fish tape and a light. We check the insulation before we crawl, because pushing through loose fill without care leaves gaps that cost the owner in winter.

Reaching the detached garage

The garage camera is where Long Island jobs most often change shape. A detached garage needs power for the camera and a connection back to the house. If the garage already has a power line and a conduit from the house, cable can usually share the path, as long as data and power are separated the way the code expects. If there is no path, there are three honest choices: trench a new conduit, which is a bigger job and sometimes worth it; mount the camera on the house facing the garage, which often gives a better view of the driveway anyway; or use a battery camera on the garage itself. We lay out those choices with the trade-offs on the first visit rather than picking for you.

Colonials, split-levels and high soffits

Suffolk's 1970s houses are kinder to cable than Nassau's slab ranches, because most have a basement or lower level where the router, a small switch and, for larger systems, a recorder can sit. The challenge is height. A two-story colonial puts its rear soffits high above a deck or a sloped yard, and a split-level puts some eaves low enough to reach from the ground and others well above the driveway. We mount the cameras that watch doors at a height that still sees faces, and the cameras that watch the property line high enough that nobody can reach them from the ground. Ladder work on those rear walls is planned, not improvised, and on a sloped yard it sometimes takes two people.

North Shore estates and outbuildings

The Gold Coast estates, from Old Westbury Gardens' 1906 Phipps house to the private properties around it, bring distance. A gatehouse at the end of a long drive, a pool house, a garage block or a barn may sit hundreds of feet from the main house. Standard copper Ethernet runs have length limits, and a wireless link across a lawn depends on line of sight that summer leaves can block. We look at what is already in the ground, whether there are conduits from earlier work, and whether a switch can live in the outbuilding with its own power. Where an outbuilding has no data path at all, battery cameras with local microSD recording are the fallback.

South Shore and waterfront houses

Houses near the water face salt air, wind-driven rain and, as Sandy and the 2024 flash flood showed, water where it is not supposed to be. Our own Long Island installer notes call for weather-rated housings on waterfront jobs. In practice that means sealed junction boxes behind every camera, drip loops on every cable entry so water runs off rather than into the wall, stainless fixings where the standard ones would rust, and the router, switch and recorder kept off the floor of any lower level that has taken water before.

The Mitchel Field officers' housing

Within East Garden City, the Mitchel Air Base and Flight Line was added to the National Register of Historic Places on May 4, 2018, covering roughly 108 acres around Charles Lindbergh Boulevard, Ellington Avenue and East and West Roads, and the American Airpower Heritage group's description says its northern boundary takes in two rows of officers' housing on Ellington northeast of Selfridge Avenue. What a business camera system has to do

A home camera mostly sends clips when something moves. A business camera system usually has to record all the time, because the question after an incident is not "did something move" but "what happened at the back door between two and four in the morning." That is the job Alarm.com's commercial recorder, the SVR, is built for. The Surety support forum explains that the SVR is an add-on to Alarm.com cloud video whose main benefit is around-the-clock recording, and that cloud video has to be on the account to use it. Alarm.com's business camera page says the commercial SVR handles up to 16 cameras at up to 4MP, with built-in PoE so each camera gets power and data on one cable, and works with third-party ONVIF or VAPIX cameras. For a flex unit in Hauppauge with a loading dock, a front office and a parking lot, that is usually enough recorder; for a larger building we plan more than one, or a different architecture altogether, and tell you before you book.

Compliance and practical limits for business cameras

  • The installer needs a state license. New York requires a license from the Department of State to install or maintain security systems, and a trade association course page notes that this includes cameras and CCTV, with unlicensed installers facing fines of up to ten thousand dollars. The Department of Labor's licensing summary lists the requirements: age 18, at least 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprints. Our work is done under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Third-party cameras need the right plan. The Surety forum notes that a commercial SVR can sit on a residential account, but third-party ONVIF and Axis camera support needs a commercial video plan. If a business already owns cameras it wants to keep, the plan has to match.
  • Audio is a legal decision. New York is a one-party consent state for recording conversations, as the Digital Media Law Project explains, so a camera microphone at a register that records two customers talking, with neither of them consenting, is a problem. We usually leave audio off on business cameras unless the owner has a clear reason and has checked it.
  • Private spaces are off limits. RecordingLaw notes that a camera recording inside a bathroom, changing room or similar space without consent is a class E felony under Penal Law section 250.45. We will not mount one there, and we check that a camera in a corridor cannot see into one.
  • Permits come from the town, village or city. Long Island has no single building department. Whether exterior work needs a permit depends on the municipality, so we check per address rather than promising none is needed.
  • The landlord owns the facade. In a leased building, cameras on the exterior, the roof or the parking lot poles need the landlord's or property manager's written approval, which we ask the tenant to get before we schedule.

Buying and accounts

Can a Long Island homeowner order Alarm.com cameras straight from Alarm.com?

No. Alarm.com's own answers page says it does not sell systems directly and that its services are sold through authorized security professionals who install, monitor and support the system. In practice, your cameras sit on an account held by a service provider, and that provider controls what your plan includes. If you already have an Alarm.com panel at home, you already have a provider, and new cameras go on the same account. If you do not, the provider is the first decision, and we explain on the phone how the account side will be handled for your property before we quote the installation.

If we cancel or pause our monitoring plan, do the cameras keep recording?

Not on their own. Alarm Grid's FAQ says all Alarm.com cameras require active service and that the monitoring company must add the video surveillance add-on to the account before the cameras will work; that page is several years old, but we have seen nothing from Alarm.com that changes the principle. The one partial exception in our research is local storage: Alarm.com's March 2026 release describes the ADC-V731B battery camera recording continuous video to microSD. If you are thinking of dropping your plan, talk to your provider first, because a camera system without an account is not a system you can rely on.

We just closed on a Nassau house and the previous owners left Alarm.com cameras. How do we take them over?

Alarm.com's help pages address exactly this: a new home that already has an Alarm.com system. The cameras stay tied to the previous owner's account until that account is closed or the equipment is released, so the first step is getting the old account ended and a new one opened in your name with a provider. Until then, the old owner may still see your driveway. Once the account side is settled, we trace every camera's cable, test each camera, reset the ones that need it, and add them to your account. We also check that nothing else on the network, such as an old recorder in a closet, is still sending video anywhere you do not expect.

Hardware and recording

Our house has wired cameras from an older system. Can they work with Alarm.com instead of being replaced?

Often, yes. Alarm.com's answers page lists upgrading an existing security system to Alarm.com using existing equipment as one of its standard questions, and its help pages say analog and existing cameras can be connected through an Alarm.com video server. Whether it is worth doing depends on the cameras: a decade-old analog camera with fogged lenses may cost more to keep than to replace. We test each one on site, tell you which are worth keeping, and use the existing cable where it is sound, which in a slab ranch can save a lot of attic work.

Cloud clips or a recorder in the house: which suits a Suffolk family home?

For most homes, cloud clips are enough. Cameras send short videos to Alarm.com's cloud when something triggers them, and you watch on your phone. A recorder, the SVR, adds continuous recording; the Surety forum describes around-the-clock recording as its main benefit and says the SVR works alongside cloud video rather than replacing it. A Suffolk colonial with a basement, a long driveway and a detached shed is a good candidate for continuous recording if you want to review what happened overnight even when nothing triggered an alert. A small ranch with four cameras on its doors usually is not. We explain the difference with your own layout in front of us.

Can a homeowner put the business recorder and PoE cameras on a residential account?

According to the Surety support forum, a commercial SVR can be used on a residential account, but support for third-party ONVIF and Axis cameras needs a commercial video plan. So the recorder itself is not off limits for a home, and its built-in PoE makes wiring cleaner, but if you want to mix in cameras from other makers, the plan has to be the commercial one. Your provider sets the plan, so we confirm it before we buy or mount anything.

What types of Alarm.com cameras are there, and which do Long Island houses actually use?

Alarm.com's help pages list video doorbells, indoor cameras, outdoor cameras and the video server for existing cameras, and its business page adds the commercial recorder. Its March 2026 release added a battery spotlight camera and AI video search. On Long Island houses we most often fit a doorbell at the front door, outdoor cameras on the soffits over the side door and the driveway, and sometimes an indoor camera facing the door from the garage. Renters lean toward indoor and battery cameras. Businesses lean toward PoE cameras on a recorder. The mix depends on the doors you use, not on a package.

Permits and the law

Does adding cameras to our Alarm.com system mean we need a Nassau County alarm permit?

The Nassau rule is about the alarm system, not the cameras. All Island Security's summary says residents served by the Nassau County Police Department must register their alarm system, and the NCPD form explains that unregistered systems are put in a "No Police Response" status. If your Alarm.com account includes door, window or motion sensors that report to a monitoring station, that system should be registered whether or not it has cameras. Our sources do not say that a cameras-only account needs a permit. If your village has its own police department rather than NCPD, ask that department what it requires.

Does whoever installs Alarm.com cameras on Long Island need a license?

Yes. The NYS Department of State licenses anyone who installs or maintains security or fire alarm systems, and the course page from the Nassau-based trade association makes clear that security systems include cameras, CCTV and access control. The Department of Labor's summary lists the requirements, and the fines for unlicensed work run up to ten thousand dollars. Our license number, NYS #12000287431, is on this page and on every quote; any installer you consider should be able to give you theirs just as easily.

Can our outdoor cameras record sound on the front walk?

They can, but the law matters more than the microphone. New York's eavesdropping law is a one-party consent rule, as the Digital Media Law Project explains, which means recording a conversation is lawful when at least one person in it consents. A Justia answer on home cameras puts the practical point simply: disable audio unless you know a consenting participant, including you, will be part of any recorded conversation. A camera on your front walk that records two strangers talking on the sidewalk has nobody consenting. We usually set outdoor cameras to video only and leave audio on the doorbell, where you are a party to the conversation.

Weather, power and planning

When a storm knocks out power on the island, what happens to Alarm.com cameras?

Wired cameras stop when the power or the internet stops, unless something keeps them running. Isaias left almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers without power at the peak, so this is not a theoretical question here. A battery backup on the router, the PoE switch and any recorder keeps a wired system alive through a short outage, as long as the internet service itself is still up. Battery cameras keep recording to their microSD card even when the house loses power. For longer outages, nothing short of a generator keeps a home system online, and we say so plainly rather than overselling the backup.

How many Alarm.com cameras does a typical Long Island house need?

Count the doors and the driveway, not the corners. A slab ranch with a front door, a side door and a driveway usually needs a doorbell camera and two outdoor cameras. A colonial with a back slider, a deck and a detached garage may need five or six. The burglary reports above all start at a door or a car, so cameras that clearly see those spots do more than cameras that cover every inch of lawn. We plan the views on the first visit and you choose how far to go; more cameras is not automatically better, especially on a cloud plan.

Our Station Yards lease bans drilling exterior walls. What can a renter put in?

Quite a lot, without touching the walls. Alarm.com describes self-install as a good fit for renters and small spaces, and providers that offer it ship systems pre-configured. Indoor cameras on a shelf facing the door, a battery camera inside a window, and a doorbell solution the building allows will cover most apartments. In a building with a concierge, package lockers and gated parking, as RentCafe and ApartmentRatings describe at the Alston, the common areas are the landlord's responsibility, not yours. If you want help setting up cameras so they work properly, we can do that in one visit, but we will not drill anything your lease forbids.

FAQ

Long Island Alarm.com Camera Installation FAQ

Can I add an Alarm.com camera to my account myself, without an installer?
+

Often, yes. Alarm.com's Video Installation Wizard in the app lets users add video devices to their own accounts, and some providers ship self-install systems already configured. That works well for indoor cameras and battery units. Outdoor wired cameras on a Long Island house still need cable through an attic or basement and sealed entries, which is where an installer earns the visit.

How much does Alarm.com camera installation cost on Long Island?
+

It depends on the county, the number of cameras, the cable routes and the recording you choose. Our Long Island pricing uses 1.15 times our Brooklyn base for Nassau and 1.20 times for Suffolk, and every line is published on our Long Island Alarm.com camera price page. The monthly service plan is separate and set by your Alarm.com provider.

Are cameras actually worth it for a Nassau County homeowner?
+

The local news suggests they are useful. A Roslyn Heights homeowner spotted would-be burglars on his surveillance system in May 2026 and called police, and cameras in a Jericho break-in showed how quickly a crew reached the second floor. Nassau burglaries rose 5 percent in early 2026. Cameras tied to door sensors show you what tripped an alert, which is the practical benefit.

Does it cost anything to list my cameras with the Suffolk police registry, and can they then watch my feed?
+

Registration with the Suffolk police SHIELD Camera Registry is free, and the department says it does not get access to registered cameras. Detectives may ask you to check your video for a particular date and time, and handing it over is at your convenience. A separate Emergency Remote Camera Access option exists, and partners can opt in or out at any time.

What happens in Suffolk once our alarm has had two false alarms in a year?
+

According to GreaterLongIsland's report on the county law, registered owners get two free false alarms a year, and after that each residential false alarm costs one hundred dollars. Cameras aimed at your alarmed doors help here, because a clip arriving with the alert lets you see whether anyone is actually there before the situation escalates.

What if one of our cameras ends up looking into the house next door?
+

We move it or mask it. New York Civil Rights Law section 52-a bars video surveillance where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy, and installer guidance recommends privacy masking on exterior cameras so a neighbor's backyard or window is blocked out of the image. On close-set Long Island blocks we plan masks before the camera goes live.

Can our cameras, alarm sensors and door locks all run in the same Alarm.com app?
+

That is the main reason people choose Alarm.com. Cameras, alarm sensors and other devices on the same account appear in one app, and a sensor trip can trigger a clip from the matching camera. Which devices your account supports depends on your provider and plan, so we confirm that before adding anything.

Do you work out on the North Fork and the South Fork, or only near the city?
+

We work across both counties, from the Queens line to the East End. Suffolk addresses are priced at the Suffolk rate wherever they are. For seasonal houses further east, tell us when the house is occupied and whether someone can let us in, and we will plan the visit around it.

Will my town or village need a building permit before cameras go on the house?
+

Long Island has no single building department, so it depends on where you live. Nassau alone has two cities, three towns and 64 villages, and Suffolk has ten towns plus its own villages. We check the rules for your address rather than promising that no permit is needed anywhere on the island.

We just had a new system installed in Nassau. What if it sends a false alarm in the first few weeks?
+

Nassau gives new systems some room. Kirschenbaum Esq. describes an exemption for the 90 days after installation, covering up to three false alarm notifications, as long as an alarm permit has been issued. That is another reason to register before or right after the system goes live, rather than waiting.

Is a battery camera a sensible choice for a shed or a fence line with no power?
+

Usually, yes. Alarm.com's ADC-V731B Battery Spotlight Camera, announced in March 2026, can record continuous video to a microSD card, so it keeps working where no cable can reach. Battery cameras need recharging or fresh batteries, and they depend on a good wireless signal back to the house, which we check where you want to mount it.

Can the monitoring station look at our camera video before it calls for police?
+

Alarm.com supports video verification, which links camera footage to a triggered alarm so the scene can be checked before dispatch, as Connextivity describes for commercial systems. Whether your monitoring station uses it, and how, depends on your provider and plan, so ask them. We make sure the cameras actually see the doors the alarm protects.
