The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Properties Need Google Nest Camera Installation

Brooklyn has been building houses for a long time, and almost every one of those decades left something on the front of a house that a camera now has to work around. The settlement the Dutch called Breuckelen was established in 1646. The City of Brooklyn absorbed Williamsburgh and the Town of Bushwick in 1854, and by 1860 it was the third-largest city in the country. It joined New York City on January 1, 1898, over the objections of many of its own residents. Through the late 1800s and early 1900s the borough grew largely by the block: long runs of brick and brownstone rowhouses, two to five stories tall, built for one or two families. Later, as Brownstoner and other local histories describe, owners divided many of those houses into smaller apartments, and the borough added luxury towers, public housing and small rental buildings around them. In 2020 Brooklyn had 2,736,074 residents. For a Nest installer that history is not background; it is the list of surfaces we will be drilling.

The Census numbers make the point plainly. In the 2023 five-year tables for Kings County, the median year a home was built is 1943, and 513,629 housing units date from 1939 or earlier. Two-unit buildings account for 175,805 units and buildings of three or four units for another 170,655, more than either detached or attached one-family houses. Roughly seven in ten occupied homes are rented. Put those together and a typical Brooklyn Nest call looks like this: a masonry building close to a century old, a front door that may be shared, a landlord or board with an opinion, and a household that wants to see who is on the stoop without starting a fight with the neighbors. A doorbell that clips onto a vinyl-sided ranch in ten minutes is a different job on a brick front with a brownstone face, where the stone is a veneer only a few inches thick over brick and the right fixing point is the mortar joint, not the carved stone.

The reason people call is usually on the evening news. CBS New York has aired package theft caught on camera on Eldert Street in Bushwick, where a thief walked off with roughly two thousand dollars' worth of clothing; a thief strolling up the steps of a home near Montgomery Street and New York Avenue in Crown Heights; a stroller taken from a stoop near Carlton and Willoughby Avenues in Fort Greene; a man apparently buzzed into a Lexington Avenue building in Bed-Stuy and Clinton Hill before taking a package; thieves cutting open boxes inside a Park Slope lobby; and, on Crescent Street between Atlantic Avenue and Fulton Street in Cypress Hills, a package thief who slashed a homeowner. News 12, citing the city's Department of Transportation, put the number of packages stolen in New York City at about 90,000 a day in December 2025. Brooklyn's stoops are a delivery shelf that faces the street, and a well-placed Nest doorbell or camera is how many households decide to keep an eye on them.

Then there is the paperwork side of a Brooklyn front. Brooklyn Heights became New York City's first historic district in 1965, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission followed with Park Slope, Cobble Hill, Fort Greene and many more; in June 2024 it added the Willoughby-Hart Historic District in Bedford-Stuyvesant, where every house went up within about twenty years starting in 1871. The Commission's rules list security cameras by name among the small equipment it reviews, and its Permit Guidebook shows dome cameras finished to blend with a facade as the kind of work staff can approve without a full hearing. That is good news, because it means a camera on a landmarked rowhouse is usually possible. It also means the order of work matters: check the building's status, pick a discreet spot, get the permit if one is needed, and only then drill. We do the first part on the phone before we quote.

The last Brooklyn wrinkle is the network. In a tall, narrow house the internet usually arrives in the cellar; one Brownstoner forum member describing a Fios install wrote that the fiber terminal "goes in the cellar," with network cable carried up to access points around the house. A Nest Cam on the back of the parlor floor or a doorbell at the top of a high stoop is then several floors and several brick walls from the router. Nest devices are Wi-Fi devices, so a weak signal shows up as a doorbell that lags, a camera that drops offline or a clip that arrives after the person has left. Part of every Brooklyn visit is measuring the signal at each spot before mounting anything, and when it is too weak, solving the network rather than blaming the camera.

In a brownstone that is almost always distance and masonry between the camera and a router in the cellar, sometimes made worse by a router that the internet provider installed in its default spot and nobody moved. Building Specialists

Google Nest Camera Installation for Brooklyn's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every Brooklyn Nest call. They differ in four ways that matter for a camera: what the front is made of, where the internet comes in, how far power has to travel and who controls the outside of the building.

The brownstone

A brick house with a brownstone face a few inches thick, a high stoop, a parlor floor and a garden level, often inside a historic district and often split into apartments.

The brick rowhouse

Two to five stories on a lot about twenty feet wide, brick front and back, router in the cellar, a long narrow yard behind and party walls on both sides.

The two- or three-family house

Stacked apartments with one shared front door or separate doors, an owner who often lives on one floor and tenants on the others, and one internet line or several.

The prewar walk-up

Masonry apartment buildings with a buzzer at the street, plaster inside, a landlord who controls the facade and the hallway, and tenants who control only their own door.

The detached house

Freestanding houses in Ditmas Park, Mill Basin and much of southern Brooklyn, with wood framing or brick, side driveways, rear garages and more room for cameras.

The co-op, condo or new tower

Board- or management-run buildings with house rules, insurance paperwork and limits on anything visible from outside, plus newer towers with building-wide Wi-Fi.

The brownstone: a stone face over brick, and a stoop that changes the camera angle

Brownstoner's architecture writers make a point that surprises many owners: a Brooklyn brownstone is, structurally, a brick house. The brownstone is a facing or veneer on the street front, typically four to six inches thick, carved into window hoods, door surrounds and cornices. That matters for a camera in two ways. The stone is soft and, after well over a century in New York weather, often fragile at the surface, so an anchor driven into the face of a carved surround can spall the stone around it. And the parts of the front that look most solid are often the most decorative, which is exactly what the Landmarks Commission asks installers to avoid. Its guidance for cameras is to attach at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, not through cast iron or decorative surfaces.

The stoop is the other half of a brownstone. The front door sits a full flight above the sidewalk, so a doorbell at the door looks down at the top of a visitor's head and may not see the bottom step, where packages often end up. Some owners solve that with a doorbell at the door plus a small camera tucked under the stoop or behind the areaway wall, both of which the Commission names as discreet locations for equipment on a primary facade. The garden-level door under the stoop, often the entrance to a separate apartment, gets its own device. We look at the stoop from the sidewalk and from the door before choosing, and we show you on your phone what each position actually sees before anything is fixed.

The brick rowhouse: twenty feet wide, router in the cellar, yard out back

The prototypical New York lot is twenty feet by one hundred, and most Brooklyn rowhouses sit on something close to it, with a house roughly forty to fifty feet deep and a yard behind. Inside, that shape is the problem for Wi-Fi. A router in the cellar at the front of the house has to push a signal up through floors and back through the long, narrow rooms to a camera on the rear wall, and one member of the r/HomeNetworking forum, describing a four-floor New York brownstone, put it well: the router was already on the least busy channels, and the problem was that it "doesn't reach far enough through the house." A Nest camera at the far end of that path will connect on a good day and drop on a bad one.

Our approach on rowhouses is to measure first and then decide. If the signal at the camera spot is strong, the camera goes up as is. If it is marginal, the cheapest fix is often moving the router out of a cellar corner to a central, higher spot, which also takes it out of harm's way in a flood. If it is weak, the durable fix is a wired access point on the floor nearest the camera, fed by a network cable run up through the house, and in many rowhouses there is already an old cable or an existing chase that can carry it. Mesh systems that relay wirelessly floor to floor can work, but every brick floor and wall they relay through costs signal. The back wall itself is brick, which holds a Nest mount very well once the anchors go into the right part of it.

The two- or three-family house: one front door, two households

Two-family houses are common across the city; the Comptroller's office counts roughly 225,000 two-family structures citywide, where the owner typically lives in one unit and rents the other. In Brooklyn that often means one front door, one stoop and one doorbell serving two households who do not want to see each other's every coming and going. A Nest doorbell is tied to one Google Home account, and whoever controls that account controls the history, the alerts and who else is invited. Before we mount anything we settle three things: whose account the shared front devices live in, who is invited to see them, and whether any device should be private to one household, such as a camera on the owner's own back door.

Networks matter here too. If the upstairs tenant has their own internet line, their devices belong on it, and the owner's devices on the owner's line. If the house shares one connection, we keep the devices of each household in their own Google Home homes so that alerts and history do not mix. The physical installation is the same as a rowhouse, usually a brick front and back, but the setup takes longer, and it is the part of the job where a tenant and an owner thank us most for asking the questions out loud.

The prewar walk-up: your door, their hallway, the landlord's facade

Prewar masonry walk-ups are where Nest choices are most limited by who owns what. The street door usually has a buzzer panel, not a doorbell, and it belongs to the building. The hallway outside your apartment is a common area. The facade belongs to the landlord. A tenant generally controls the inside of the apartment. Installers who work in Brooklyn describe the practical line the same way: a camera inside your unit or a peephole-style camera on your own door is generally within a tenant's reach, while anything aimed down a shared hall or at a neighbor's door, or anything that needs drilling outside, usually needs the landlord's written approval. We do not drill a facade or a common hallway without it.

Inside, walk-up walls are typically plaster over brick or lath, and the plaster add-on on our list is $45 when a mount goes into it. Indoor Nest Cams rarely need much fixing; many sit on a shelf. Where an indoor camera needs to be fixed high to see an entry hall or a back window, we use anchors that reach past the plaster to whatever is behind it, and we keep the power cord tidy along trim or under a paintable cord cover ($59) rather than chasing it into the wall.

The detached house: driveways, garages and more wall to work with

Not all of Brooklyn is attached. Ditmas Park is known for freestanding Victorian houses in styles uncommon elsewhere in the borough, and in southern Brooklyn neighborhoods like Mill Basin, housing is largely detached and semi-detached, with many properties having driveways, private outdoor space or waterfront access near Jamaica Bay. These houses behave more like suburban homes for a Nest installer: wood framing or brick, eaves and porch ceilings that shelter a camera from weather, side walls that can carry a driveway camera, and garages at the back of the lot that need their own coverage.

The challenge shifts from masonry to distance and weather. A detached garage at the end of a driveway can be beyond the house Wi-Fi, so we test signal there before promising a camera on it. Waterfront and southern Brooklyn homes sit in the parts of the borough that the city's flood vulnerability index flags as especially exposed, so we keep routers and power off the cellar floor and choose mounting spots under eaves where wind-driven rain does not hit the lens straight on. Porch ceilings in older wood houses are often the best doorbell-adjacent camera position in Brooklyn: sheltered, high enough to see faces and close to power.

The co-op, condo or new tower: rules first, then hardware

In board-run and managed buildings, the hardware is the easy part. Most Brooklyn co-op and condo proprietary leases and house rules require contractors to be licensed and insured and to provide a certificate of insurance naming the building, and many limit anything visible from the outside or in common corridors. A Nest Cam inside your apartment is usually the realistic choice, with a peephole-style camera or an interior view of your own entry rather than a doorbell in the hallway. We send the certificate before the visit, follow the building's working hours, and if the board's rules stop a device you wanted, we say so rather than looking for a way around them.

Newer towers along the waterfront and in Downtown Brooklyn bring a different issue: building-provided Wi-Fi or a crowded wireless environment with hundreds of networks nearby. Nest devices need to join a network you control, and some building-wide networks require a sign-in page that a camera cannot complete. We check which network the device will use before the visit, and if the building's network will not work, the answer is usually your own router in the apartment, which the camera can join directly.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks on a Brooklyn Front Before Unboxing a Nest Doorbell

These notes describe how our technicians work on Brooklyn houses. They are our standing practice, not a story about any particular address.

Stand on the sidewalk first. Before touching the door, the technician looks at the whole front from across the street: which parts are carved stone, which are plain brick, where the mortar joints run, whether there is a band course, an areaway wall or an under-stoop space that could hide a small camera, and whether the house has any existing wires, old bell buttons or conduit on its face. If the building is in a historic district, this is also the moment to compare the plan with the Commission's discreet-location guidance.

Check power at the door. Old bell wiring, if there is any, gets tested before a wired doorbell is chosen. Many Brooklyn houses have none at the front door at all, which steers us toward a battery doorbell or a different power path. We never open a wall to find out.

Walk the signal. With a phone or meter, the technician checks Wi-Fi at each planned camera spot and at the door, then walks back toward the router to see where the signal falls off. In a brownstone that fall-off is often at the parlor floor, and that one walk decides whether the job needs network work.

Pair in hand. Each Nest device is added to your Google Home app while it is still in the technician's hand, as Google recommends, so the QR code is easy to scan and updates finish before the device goes up a ladder.

Show the view. Before drilling, the technician holds the camera in place and shows you the live picture on your phone, so you can see whether it catches the bottom step, the gate and the face of someone at the door, and whether it also sees a neighbor's windows that should be masked out.

Landmarks and Facades

Landmarked Fronts, Mortar Joints and Cable: Putting a Nest Camera on a Historic Brooklyn House

No borough has more landmark questions per Nest call than Brooklyn, because so many of its historic districts are blocks of rowhouses whose owners want exactly the devices Nest makes: a doorbell and one or two cameras. The Commission's own materials are more practical than many owners expect. Section 2-20 of the LPC rules covers health, safety and utility equipment and names security cameras alongside fire alarm bells, sprinkler connections and lighting. The Permit Guidebook's chapter on that equipment shows dome cameras finished to match a facade and explains that when work meets the rules, Commission staff can approve it without a public hearing. The introduction to the guidebook says staff approval can come in as few as ten business days when an application meets the rules.

Where a camera can go. The guidebook's general criteria start from a simple idea: equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, and if it must, it should go somewhere discreet, such as beneath a band course, underneath a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence, or behind plantings. Cameras should be well spaced to avoid a cluttered look. For small residential and commercial buildings, the criteria allow no more than two cameras for each twenty-five feet of street frontage, and for larger buildings no more than three. A twenty-foot rowhouse front is therefore planned around two devices at most, and a doorbell usually counts as one of them.

How a camera is attached. The same criteria say equipment should be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and not through cast iron or decorative surfaces. That lines up with good practice for a Nest mount anyway. Google's own instructions for the Nest Cam Outdoor note that the box includes wall anchors for hard surfaces like brick or stone, and call for a 7/32-inch masonry bit and a hammer. On the Google Nest Community forum, one owner who mounted a Nest Cam on brick described using a plywood backer and drilling into the mortar to avoid making several holes in the brick itself. Mortar is easier to repoint than a brick or a carved stone is to replace, which is why we prefer it whenever the mount allows.

What happens to the cable. The criteria ask that conduit be concealed through plain masonry or cladding directly behind the installation where possible, and where it cannot be, that no more than about ten inches be visible, painted to match and secured into mortar joints. For a Nest device, that pushes us toward three choices: battery models where there is no hidden route for power, wired models fed from directly behind the mount, or cameras placed under the stoop or on the rear of the house where cable can run out of sight. Google suggests routing the wired outdoor camera's cable along a ledge or hidden area and painting it to blend in, which works well along a rear cornice. One Brooklyn installer describes a common brownstone pattern of never penetrating the front at all, running cable through the rear yard, stairwells or roof bulkheads, keeping rear cameras in the eaves and using a single discreet device, often a doorbell, at the stoop. That is close to how we plan most landmarked jobs.

Timing, honestly. Ten business days is the Commission's best case. An architecture firm that files with the LPC regularly writes that staff are often backlogged and that owners should expect around sixty days for a Certificate of No Effect, and another Brooklyn installer estimates two to six weeks for non-emergency exterior approvals. In practice, a family that wants a camera on a landmarked front before the holidays should start in early autumn, and put the indoor and rear cameras up first while the front waits.

Which Brooklyn districts. Installers who work across the borough list Brooklyn Heights, Park Slope, Fort Greene, Cobble Hill, Carroll Gardens, Boerum Hill, Crown Heights North, Stuyvesant Heights, Clinton Hill and Greenwood Heights among the historic districts where exterior hardware needs approval, and the Commission keeps adding to the map, as it did with Willoughby-Hart in 2024. Rather than relying on a list, we check the individual building: the city's Buildings Information Search shows a landmark status field, and an "L" there means the work needs LPC approval. You can also call 311 and ask for the Commission.

Renters, Owners and Boards

Whose Wall, Whose Account: Nest Cameras When a Brooklyn House Has More Than One Household

With roughly seven in ten Brooklyn homes rented, and so many buildings holding two or more households, the questions that decide a Nest install are often not technical. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. What follows is how we handle the common situations, drawn from the public guidance we cite and from doing this work.

Renters. A tenant can generally put a wireless camera inside the rented unit. Installers who work in Brooklyn add that a tenant can usually cover the unit's own entrance from outside, as long as the camera is not pointed at anyone else's door or unit, and that anything outdoors or anything that requires drilling needs written landlord approval. Indoor Nest Cams fit that line well, because most sit on a shelf or a small bracket. For outdoor devices, we ask you to get the landlord's yes in writing before we book; a short message naming the device, the spot and how it will be attached is usually enough.

Owners who rent out a floor. If you own a two-family and live downstairs, the cameras on the front door and the back yard affect your tenant too. Telling the tenant which areas the cameras see, keeping them off the tenant's private entrance and windows, and deciding together whether the tenant gets access to the front doorbell avoids most disputes. Where the tenant has their own door, under the stoop for example, their device can be theirs entirely, on their own account and their own network.

Co-ops and condos. Board-run buildings normally require a licensed, insured contractor and a certificate of insurance naming the building, and many restrict devices in hallways or on the outside. We send the certificate, follow building hours and keep the job inside your apartment unless the board approves more.

Audio. Nest cameras can record sound. Installers who work in Brooklyn note that New York follows one-party consent for recording conversations, and commonly advise turning audio recording off on cameras that face shared or public spaces. We decide audio settings with you, device by device, during setup.

Tenants and cameras in the news. Brooklyn residents have pushed back on cameras when they felt watched rather than protected. Tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville fought their landlord's plan for a facial-recognition entry system, and in August 2026 two NYCHA tenant associations representing residents of the Tilden Houses and Hughes Houses sued the city, accusing it of using a free internet program to give police access to public housing cameras. Neither case is about a household's own Nest doorbell, but they explain why your neighbors may ask what your camera sees, and why we aim and mask every device to your own space.

Compliance notes for Brooklyn homes and businesses

  • Landmarks. Cameras on designated buildings and in historic districts fall under LPC rules (Section 2-20). Check the building's status in the DOB Buildings Information Search or by calling 311 before anything goes on the front.
  • Low-voltage work and permits. Under NYC Admin Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024 and effective December 21, 2025, an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to run below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, provided a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person as defined in the city electrical code does the work. Our camera work falls under NYS license #12000287431.
  • Line voltage is different. A wired Nest Cam with Floodlight connects to 120-volt house wiring, and line-voltage electrical work in New York City needs a DOB permit and a licensed master or special electrician. We plan that part with a licensed electrician, not around one.
  • Entrance doors. Apartment buildings covered by the city's self-closing, self-locking door rules must keep those doors working, and HPD issued 3,151 self-closing door violations in one recent year of inspections. Nothing we mount may interfere with a door closer or lock.
  • Exits and fire equipment. We do not mount cameras or run cable where they would obstruct an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and cables stay off the floor in any path people use to leave.
Your Questions Answered

Google Nest Camera Installation in Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the questions Brooklyn owners, tenants and shopkeepers bring to a Nest job: landmark status, carved fronts, high stoops, shared houses, flooded cellars and whether Nest is even the right system. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Landmarks and the front of the house

How can we tell whether our Brooklyn building is landmarked before we pick a spot for the camera?

Look the address up in the Department of Buildings' Buildings Information Search, which has a landmark status field; according to an architecture firm that files with the Commission regularly, an "L" in that field means the work requires LPC approval. You can also call 311 and ask for the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Being on a pretty block is not proof either way: some striking streets are not designated, and some districts are newer than people think, such as Willoughby-Hart in Bed-Stuy, designated in June 2024. When you send us your address, we check the status before quoting, so the plan you get already accounts for it.

How many Nest devices can a landmarked rowhouse front have?

The Commission's Permit Guidebook allows no more than two cameras for every twenty-five feet of street frontage on small residential and commercial buildings, and no more than three on larger buildings, and asks that they be well spaced so the front does not look cluttered. A typical twenty-foot Brooklyn rowhouse is therefore planned around two devices at most on the front. A Nest doorbell at the door is one; a small camera under the stoop or behind the areaway wall is usually the second. Everything else goes on the rear of the house or inside, where the front-facade limits do not set the count.

How long should we expect to wait for Landmarks approval on a camera?

The Commission says staff can approve work that meets its rules in as few as ten business days. Real-world timing is often longer: an architecture firm that works with the LPC writes that staff are often backlogged and suggests expecting around sixty days for a Certificate of No Effect, and another Brooklyn installer estimates two to six weeks for non-emergency exterior work. Because of that, we usually suggest installing the rear and indoor cameras straight away and adding the front device once the approval arrives, so the house is not waiting on paperwork to be covered at all.

Do cameras inside our landmarked house, or on its back wall, also need the Commission's approval?

Interior cameras generally do not need LPC approval, though a co-op or condo may still have its own rules. The rear is less simple than many owners hope. The Commission's permits page says most exterior changes to both the front and rear facades of buildings in historic districts require review, but an architecture firm that files with the Commission notes that it is most concerned with what is visible from the street, and work that is not visible from public thoroughfares has an easier path. A small camera under a rear cornice is far easier to approve than one on the front, but on a designated building we still check before drilling rather than assuming the back is exempt.

Brownstone, brick and the mount itself

Will drilling into our brownstone front crack the stone?

It can if the drilling goes in the wrong place. Brownstone on a Brooklyn house is a facing a few inches thick over brick, and old carved surfaces are often soft or flaking, so an anchor in a window hood or door surround can break the stone around it. The Commission's criteria point to mortar joints, plain masonry, wood and metalwork as attachment points and away from decorative surfaces, and that is where we fix Nest mounts too. On a brick or stone wall, the brick or stone add-on on our list is $65. Where the only good camera position is on carved stone, we look for another spot, such as under the stoop, rather than risk the facade.

Why does your technician set the camera up on my phone before mounting it?

Because Google tells installers to. Its setup guidance says to connect the camera to the app before you install or mount it so you can still reach the QR code, which on some models is on the device itself and hard to scan once it is on a wall or under a stoop. Pairing in hand also lets firmware updates finish on the ground, lets us confirm the camera joins your network at full strength from a spot nearby, and lets you see the live view before we choose the final position. It adds a few minutes and saves a second trip up the ladder.

Can the camera or its cable be colored to blend into a brownstone or brick front?

The cable, yes. Google's guidance for the wired outdoor Nest Cam suggests running the cable along a ledge or hidden area and painting it to blend in with the house, and the Commission's criteria ask for any visible conduit to be painted to match. We generally do not paint the camera body itself, because paint can interfere with the lens, the sensors or the housing's seals. The better way to make a Nest device disappear on a Brooklyn front is placement: under the stoop, beneath a band course, behind the areaway wall, or tucked under a rear cornice where nobody on the sidewalk is looking.

Stoops, garden doors and the street door

Our front door is a full flight up a high stoop. How do we see someone taking a package from the bottom step?

A doorbell at the top of a stoop looks down at heads and can miss the lower steps, where couriers often leave boxes. One approach shared on a camera forum for a brownstone uses cameras on either side of the door about six feet up, angled at the stoop, with a third above the door pointing down the stairs. On a landmarked front the camera count limits that, so we usually pair the doorbell at the door with one small camera under the stoop or behind the areaway wall, which the Commission names as discreet spots, angled up the steps toward the sidewalk. Before drilling, we hold each device in place and check the view on your phone.

The garden apartment under our stoop has its own door. Can that door get its own Nest doorbell?

Usually, and it is often the most sheltered door on the house. Garden-level doors rarely have doorbell wiring, so a battery doorbell is the common choice; Google also offers an indoor power adapter for wired models where no bell wires exist. The space under a stoop is one of the discreet locations the Commission's guidance names, so on a landmarked house it is easier to approve than a device on the main facade, though it still counts toward the front's camera limit. If the garden unit is rented, the doorbell can live on the tenant's own Google account and network, so the owner upstairs does not see their visitors.

Package thieves in our building get buzzed in by someone else. What can a camera at the street door actually show?

CBS New York reported a case on Lexington Avenue where cameras caught a suspect who was apparently buzzed into the building before a package went missing, which is a common Brooklyn pattern. A camera inside the vestibule, facing the inner door, shows who came in and when, and a camera on the outside shows who pressed the buzzer. In a building you own, that can be a Nest Cam in the vestibule. In a building you rent, the street door and vestibule are the landlord's, so the camera there needs the landlord's approval; a tenant can still cover their own apartment door from inside. Either way, it is evidence after the fact, not a lock.

Shared houses, boards and southern Brooklyn

We own a two-family and rent the top floor. Should our tenant be able to see the front door camera?

That is your decision together, and it is worth making before the doorbell goes up. A Nest doorbell lives in one Google Home account, and the account owner decides who else is invited and sees the history. Many owners invite the tenant so they can answer their own deliveries, and keep the back-yard and owner-only cameras in a separate home that the tenant cannot see. If the tenant has a separate door, their device can be on their own account and network. Whatever you choose, tell the tenant what each camera covers, keep cameras off the tenant's private entrance and windows, and write it down.

Our co-op board wants a certificate of insurance naming the building before anything is mounted. Can you provide it?

Yes. Most Brooklyn co-op and condo proprietary leases require licensed, insured contractors and a certificate naming the building, and we send ours before the visit. Give us the exact names the managing agent wants on it, the building's working hours and any rules about hallway or exterior devices. If the board allows only devices inside your apartment, we plan around that, usually an indoor Nest Cam watching your own entry from inside, rather than putting anything in a common corridor. We will not install against a board's rules, because it is your lease, not ours, that pays for the mistake.

Our router sits on the cellar floor, and the cellar took water during Ida. Does that matter for the cameras?

It matters a lot. Every Nest camera depends on that router, so a router that floods takes the whole system down exactly when you would want it. During Hurricane Ida rain fell at a record 3.5 inches an hour, about double the sewer capacity of 1.75 inches an hour, and the city's health department describes unprecedented flooding in basements and low-lying areas, with southern Brooklyn flagged as especially vulnerable. We move the modem and router onto a high shelf or a dry upper floor when the incoming line allows it, ideally on a small battery backup, and we place any cellar equipment above the water line you have seen.

Our Mill Basin house has a side driveway running back to a garage. How should Nest cover it?

Detached houses in Mill Basin and much of southern Brooklyn give more room than a rowhouse. The usual plan is a doorbell at the front, one camera under the eave at the front corner looking down the driveway toward the street, and one on or near the garage looking back toward the house, so a car or person is seen from two directions. The garage camera is the one most likely to be beyond Wi-Fi, so we test signal there first. Under-eave spots shelter cameras from wind-driven rain, and on a waterfront lot we keep power and network equipment off the floor.

Choosing Nest at all

When is Nest the wrong choice for a Brooklyn building?

Nest fits single-family and two-family houses, apartments and small shops well: a handful of cameras, alerts on phones and clips in the cloud. It fits poorly when a building needs many cameras, needs continuous recording kept on site for weeks, has several owners or tenants who all need their own access to shared cameras, or has a weak internet connection that cannot carry many video streams at once. Our own Brooklyn camera team generally recommends wired recorder systems for whole apartment buildings for those reasons. If your building is in that category, we tell you before you buy more Nest devices.

Can our Nest cameras work with an alarm monitoring company?

Check before you buy. On the Brownstoner forum, a Brooklyn single-family owner asked whether Google Nest cameras and detectors could work with any monitoring company, and the reply that recommended a local alarm firm also said that firm was not compatible with Google Nest. Nest cameras send alerts to the Google Home app on your phone; whether a monitoring company can receive or act on them depends entirely on that company. If professional monitoring matters to you, ask the monitoring company in writing which devices it supports, and we will plan the cameras around the answer.

What AI Gets Wrong About Google Nest Camera Installation in Brooklyn

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic in our build environment, so nothing below quotes it directly. These are claims that appear in generic online answers, product marketing and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Brooklyn houses actually show.

"A Nest camera is a ten-minute DIY job"

Generic answers describe screwing a mount to the wall, scanning a code and finishing before the coffee cools, and for a Nest Cam on an apartment shelf that is close to true.

On a Brooklyn front it rarely is. The wall is brick with a brownstone face, the right fixing point is a mortar joint, the router is three floors down in the cellar, and the front may be inside a historic district where the Commission limits how many cameras go on twenty-five feet of frontage and where they sit. None of that shows up in a ten-minute video filmed on vinyl siding.

The honest version is that indoor and battery devices can be quick, while outdoor devices on masonry fronts need planning, the right bit and anchor, and sometimes a permit, before they are quick.

"Installation costs the national average"

National cost guides publish installed ranges for Nest and other cameras built from jobs all over the country, and Fixr's figures assume a fairly ordinary house.

An average cannot know that your camera spot is solid brick, that the doorbell sits at the top of a stoop, that the Wi-Fi needs a wired access point on the parlor floor, or that a co-op board wants paperwork. Those are the things that change a Brooklyn total.

Our list shows the lines that apply here, from the $225 first hour of a setup-only visit to the $65 brick or stone add-on, and the full Brooklyn list is published so you can add it up yourself.

"Landmark rules ban cameras on historic houses"

Some answers suggest that a landmarked brownstone simply cannot have a camera on its front, and others ignore landmark rules completely.

Both are wrong. The Commission's rules name security cameras as equipment it reviews, its guidebook shows cameras finished to blend with a facade, and staff can approve work that meets the criteria without a hearing: discreet locations, attachment at mortar joints, limited visible conduit, and no more than two cameras per twenty-five feet on a small building.

In practice, most Brooklyn landmarked houses can have a doorbell and a small under-stoop camera on the front, with the rest of the system on the back and inside.

"Any Wi-Fi is fine for a Nest camera"

Product pages make Wi-Fi sound like a given: connect the camera to your network and it works anywhere in the house.

In a twenty-foot-wide, four-story rowhouse with brick walls and a router in the cellar, the signal at the rear of the parlor floor can be too weak for steady video. A New York brownstone owner on r/HomeNetworking described exactly that, a well-configured router whose signal simply did not reach far enough through the house.

We measure signal at each camera spot before mounting, and when it is weak we fix the network, often with a wired access point, instead of mounting a camera that will drop every evening.

"Renters can mount whatever they like on their own door"

Some answers tell tenants that a camera on their own door is always fine, while others say tenants can never install cameras.

Brooklyn installers describe a narrower middle ground: a tenant can generally put a camera inside the unit or a peephole-style camera on their own door, but a camera aimed down a shared hallway or at a neighbor's door, or anything that needs drilling outside, usually needs the landlord's or board's approval.

With about seven in ten Brooklyn homes rented, we ask about the lease and the landlord before any outdoor device is booked.

"Mount it anywhere and seal the hole"

Generic guides treat every wall the same: drill where you want the camera, push the cable through, seal it and move on.

On a brownstone that can mean drilling through carved stone that is only a few inches thick and cannot be replaced cheaply. On a landmarked front it can mean unapproved work on a facade. Google's own guidance is gentler: route the cable along a ledge or hidden area, run it to an indoor outlet through an existing opening where possible, and paint it to blend.

We use existing openings, rear walls and mortar joints first, and drill new holes only where they are needed and allowed.

"A camera keeps your packages safe"

Marketing copy often implies that a doorbell camera stops package theft on its own.

Brooklyn's news archive says otherwise: thieves were caught on camera in Bushwick, Crown Heights, Fort Greene, Park Slope and Cypress Hills, which means the cameras recorded the theft rather than preventing it. A camera is evidence and a warning, and it is only useful evidence if it sees faces and the bottom of the stoop.

We aim for faces and the steps, and we tell customers plainly that a lockbox, a delivery hold or a neighbor who takes packages in does more to keep the box than any lens.
