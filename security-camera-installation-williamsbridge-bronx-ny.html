The Neighborhood

Why Williamsbridge Properties Need Security Cameras Built Around the Pad, the Stoop and the Back Window

Williamsbridge takes its name from John Williams, an 18th-century farmer whose land sat on the east bank of the Bronx River near what is now the corner of Gun Hill Road and White Plains Road, and who is credited with building the first bridge across the river there. It became an incorporated village on November 23, 1888, and stayed close to rural until the elevated line came up White Plains Road; the first stretch of that line reached East 219th Street in 1917, and through the 1920s brick row houses, two-family homes and small apartment buildings filled in around the stations. People still draw its edges differently. Bronx Community Board 12 describes it as running from East 222nd Street on the north to Boston Road on the east, East Gun Hill Road on the south and the Bronx River on the west; Wikipedia puts the southern line farther down at Adee Avenue and also folds in the old Olinville name for the blocks west of White Plains Road. The mail comes through three ZIP codes, 10466, 10467 and 10469, and the 47th Precinct, at 4111 Laconia Avenue, covers the area. For a camera job, the map matters less than the building, and in Williamsbridge the building is usually small.

NeighborhoodScout counts 47.3 percent of the area's real estate as small buildings of two, three or four units, a higher share than in almost every other neighborhood in the country. The Department of City Planning, in a City Planning Commission report, describes the blocks away from the main avenues as mostly detached two-story single-family homes and row houses, with some small three- to five-story apartment buildings. Wikipedia notes that few buildings here rise above 70 feet. NeighborhoodScout puts much of the housing between 1940 and 1969, with a share older than that; Homes.com gives a median build year of 1945 and points out that many homes in the northern part of the neighborhood were designed with a parking spot in front. Apartments.com adds that a small number of late-19th-century cottages still stand on White Plains Road. Put together, that means low eaves you can reach from a ladder, short cable runs, a basement or cellar under almost every house, and a front yard that is often a slab of concrete with a car on it.

It also means most of these buildings have an owner close by. CityNeighborhoods credits Williamsbridge's high rate of owner-occupancy and its active block associations with holding the housing stock together through the 1970s, when other parts of the Bronx were losing buildings to disinvestment and arson. Today the typical camera customer here lives in the building, often on one floor of a two-family with a tenant on the other, and wants to protect the car, the front door, the back of the house and the people renting from them without turning the house into a place where everyone feels watched. Homes.com notes that co-ops and multifamily homes make up much of the housing, and the neighborhood also includes NYCHA's Gun Hill Houses on Gun Hill Road, the Parkside Senior Center, and Sydney House on Tilden Street, a Habitat for Humanity project. Each of those has its own rules about who may put a camera where.

What has been said in public explains why so many calls start at the back window or the curb. In January 2026 News 12 aired NYPD video of two men forcing their way into a Holland Avenue home through a back window during a Sunday snowstorm; police said the safe they took held cash, jewelry, passports and business documents. Bronx Times reported that the Gun Hill Road Civic Group was founded in 2014 by about fifteen neighbors after a string of car break-ins in driveways. The NYPD has released surveillance footage from near East 213th Street and Holland Avenue in a robbery investigation, and ABC7 has aired video from East Gun Hill Road and from Fenton Avenue in other cases. News 12 reported that district leaders announced a one-million-dollar expansion of NYPD ARGUS cameras across the 47th Precinct, aimed at cutting the delays in getting footage. Council Member Kevin Riley has said that White Plains Road "for decades has been an eyesore for this community when it comes to public safety, sanitation, or even having access to resources for our small businesses over there." None of that tells us what a particular house needs. It does tell us that in Williamsbridge, the clip people end up wanting is usually from the back of the house, the car out front or the shop door, and usually from a camera they own.

What the record shows about Williamsbridge

  • Small buildings dominate. 47.3 percent of the real estate is two-, three- and four-unit buildings, according to NeighborhoodScout, and City Planning describes the side streets as mostly two-story detached houses and row houses.
  • Precinct-wide numbers, not block numbers. Wikipedia's summary of the 47th Precinct lists 300 burglaries, 758 grand larcenies and 461 grand larcenies of autos in 2022; the precinct also covers Wakefield, Edenwald, Baychester, Olinville and other areas, so these are not Williamsbridge-only figures.
  • More public cameras, aimed at public places. News 12 reported a one-million-dollar investment to expand NYPD ARGUS camera systems across the 47th Precinct, with the stated goal of reducing delays in obtaining footage.
  • A NYCHA development with its own camera history. Gun Hill Houses, built in 1951 with 19 buildings, was on NYCHA's 2015 list of developments getting cameras, and Council Member Riley later pushed capital funding for more cameras on its grounds after a bullet struck a daycare on White Plains Road in January 2022.
  • Many households without a car. City Planning's report on the Williams Bridge Metro-North station area found that 64 percent of housing units there have no vehicle and 69 percent of workers take transit or walk.
  • A corridor under study. The White Plains Road Neighborhood Plan covers the avenue from Adee Avenue to the Mount Vernon border, including parts of Gun Hill Road and East 233rd Street, and officials describe it as still dominated by one- and two-story commercial buildings. The 2011 Williamsbridge/Baychester Rezoning had earlier mapped R5A districts over pockets of one- and two-family detached homes west of White Plains Road.

The Front of the House

Parking Pads, Curbs and the Car That Sleeps Outside

On many Williamsbridge blocks the front of the house is not a lawn or a stoop with a garden. It is a concrete pad between the house and the sidewalk, sometimes behind a low iron fence and a gate, with one car nosed in toward the front wall. Homes.com describes that pattern in the northern part of the neighborhood, and anyone who has walked the blocks above Gun Hill Road has seen it. The Gun Hill Road Civic Group, by its own account in Bronx Times, began in 2014 after neighbors had their cars broken into in their own driveways. A camera plan for one of these houses starts with the car, because the car is the one valuable that spends every night a few steps from the sidewalk.

Why a pad is a harder view than a long driveway

A long driveway gives a camera distance: someone walks twenty or thirty feet toward the house and the camera sees their face for several seconds. A front pad gives almost none. The car sits right under the front windows, the sidewalk is a few feet beyond its bumper, and a person can step off the sidewalk, reach the driver's door and be gone in the time it takes a motion alert to reach a phone. That changes three things. The camera has to cover the sidewalk edge, where the person first appears, not just the car. It has to be set to record continuously, or at least to keep a few seconds before each motion event, so the approach is on the recording. And it has to be aimed so the person is seen from the front or the side, not from the top of their head.

Height, angle and the roof of the car

The most common mistake we see on these houses is a camera mounted as high as the ladder would reach, under the roof eave of a two-story house, pointing straight down at the pad. It records a car roof and the crown of a head. The view that identifies someone usually comes from lower and farther to the side: a camera at the first-floor ceiling line on the front wall, or under the porch roof, set off to one side of the pad and angled across it toward the sidewalk, so a person walking to the driver's door is seen in profile and then face-on as they turn. On houses where the pad is beside the stoop rather than in front of it, a camera on the stoop side wall looking back across the pad often does the job of two.

What the sidewalk does to alerts

The sidewalk in front of a Williamsbridge pad is busy. People walk to the 2 and 5 at Gun Hill Road, 219th Street and Burke Avenue, to the Metro-North station at Webster Avenue, to schools and to White Plains Road. A camera that alerts on every passerby gets muted within a week. We draw alert zones on the pad itself, the driver's and passenger's doors and the gate opening, and leave the sidewalk out of the alert area while still recording it. The phone buzzes when someone steps onto your concrete, not when someone walks past it.

Night on a pad

Many of these blocks have streetlights, porch lights and passing headlights all mixed together, which is harder for a camera than plain darkness. Headlights sweeping across the lens can make the picture flare and then go dark for a moment. A camera with good wide dynamic range handles that better, and so does a fixed light on the house that puts steady light on the pad. On a pad that faces a dark stretch of street, a dusk-to-dawn fixture over the front door often does more for the video than a more expensive camera. Infrared lights on the camera reach the car, but they also bounce off a windshield and a shiny hood, so the camera's own infrared is sometimes switched off in favor of a light on the wall.

The car at the curb

Not every house has a pad, and plenty of owners park on the street. A camera aimed along the curb in front of the house can show someone walking up to a car, trying the doors or breaking a window, and which way they went afterward. It will rarely read a plate on a car driving by at night unless it is chosen and aimed just for that, with a narrow view and fast shutter at a spot where cars slow down, such as near a corner or a stop sign. For most owners a clear view of their own car and the few feet of sidewalk beside it is worth more than a wide shot of the whole block.

The gate and the fence line

Where a low fence and gate close off the pad, the gate is where most visitors stop. A camera that covers the gate from the house side catches faces as people reach for the latch, which is also where delivery drivers stand. On houses where the mail slot or mailbox is beside the door, the same camera can usually take in both. We keep these views on your side of the fence and mask anything that looks into the house next door.

The Back of the House

Back Windows, Side Passages and What the Holland Avenue Break-In Shows

The burglary News 12 reported in January 2026 is a useful case because it happened where most house cameras are weakest. Police said two men forced their way into a Holland Avenue home through a back window during the Sunday snowstorm and left with a safe. NYPD released surveillance video afterward. The front of a Williamsbridge house faces the street, the neighbors and the sidewalk traffic; the back faces a small yard, a fence and other people's yards, and on a snowy night almost nobody is out there to notice anything.

Rear windows on a 1940s to 1960s house

Most of the houses here have their kitchen and a bedroom or two at the back, with windows at a height a person can reach from the yard, sometimes from a back porch roof or the top of a rear stair. Some have a basement window or two at ground level as well. A camera at the back has to see the windows themselves, not just the yard. On a house that is only twenty or twenty-five feet wide, one camera at a back corner of the house, under the eave or at the first-floor ceiling line, aimed across the rear wall, usually covers every rear window and the back door in one view. On a deeper or L-shaped house, a second camera at the other corner looks back the other way, so nobody can stand against the wall below the first camera and stay out of its picture.

The side passage

Detached and semi-detached houses usually have a narrow passage on one side leading from the front to the yard, often behind a gate. That passage is how people reach the back without being seen from the street, so it gets a camera of its own, aimed down its length from the back toward the front. A camera at the rear looking forward catches faces as people come in; a camera at the front looking back sees only backs. Because these passages are narrow and the neighbor's wall or windows are often a few feet away, we use a narrower lens and mask the neighbor's windows.

Weather

Snow, rain and fog are hard on outdoor cameras in different ways. Falling snow and rain show up as bright streaks when the camera's own infrared lights are on, and they can set off motion alerts all night. Snow on the ground reflects light and can wash out a night picture. Wind-driven snow sticks to a lens that faces into the weather. We mount rear cameras under an eave or a soffit where possible, angle them slightly down so water runs off the lens hood, and on views that face open yards we lean on a fixed light on the house instead of the camera's built-in infrared, which keeps the streaks down and the picture in color.

The yard and the people next door

Williamsbridge yards are small and close together. A camera high on the back wall covers your yard well and easily looks over the fence into two or three others. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. So the rear camera is aimed at your windows, your door and your yard, mounted lower where it still works, and masked over any neighboring yard that stays in the frame. We show you the masked view before we leave.

An inside camera as the second layer

An outdoor camera records someone at a window; it cannot stop them. Many owners add one small indoor camera aimed at the room a back window opens into, or at the stairs, so that if someone does get in, the recording shows where they went and what they touched. Indoor cameras in a home where a tenant or family member lives need the agreement of the people who use that space, and none of them should face a bedroom or bathroom. A camera is also not a substitute for a window lock, a gate or a window that closes properly, and we say so when we see a back window that does not latch.

Where the recorder lives

The recorder that stores all of this usually sits in the basement or a closet. If it sits on a shelf beside the basement door, anyone who gets in can see it. We place it out of sight, off the floor and on a battery unit, and set up a copy of key clips or alerts to the owner's phone, so the most important seconds leave the house even if the recorder does not.

Two Households, One Building

Two- and Three-Family Houses: The Owner, the Tenants and One Recorder

With almost half the neighborhood's real estate in two-, three- and four-unit buildings, the most common Williamsbridge camera job is a house where more than one household lives. Often the owner lives on the first or second floor and rents the other, or lives in one unit of a three-family and rents two. Sometimes the owner lives elsewhere and every unit is rented. In every version, the cameras serve more than one set of people, and the plan has to settle who sees what before the first hole is drilled.

One system, separate views

A single recorder in the basement can give each person their own view. The owner sees every camera. A tenant household sees the cameras that cover what they share and use: the front door, the stoop, the gate, the sidewalk and their own entrance, and, if they park on the pad, the pad camera. Tenants do not see cameras on the owner's private entrance or inside the owner's unit, and the owner agrees in advance not to aim anything into a tenant's windows or private space. Every adult gets their own login. A shared password passed around a house is the fastest way to lose track of who can see what, especially after a tenant leaves.

Tell tenants in writing

We give the owner a one-page list of every camera, where it points and who can see it, written so it can be handed to tenants or attached to a lease. In our experience that list prevents most of the friction that cameras cause in shared houses, because it answers the question people do not like to ask out loud: is that camera there to watch me? We also recommend leaving audio off on every camera that covers a shared door or entryway. Recording conversations a person is not part of can raise eavesdropping issues under New York law, and whether a camera passively picking up hallway sound crosses that line has never been settled cleanly, which is why most installers leave it off.

Separate entrances

Many of these houses have more than one door: a main door at the top of the stoop, a second door for the lower unit, a side door into the kitchen and sometimes an outside stair to an upper unit. Each door that a household uses as its own front door deserves a face-level view, because that is where visitors, deliveries and anyone with bad intentions will stand. Where two doors are close together, one camera placed between them and set back can cover both; where they are on different sides of the house, each gets its own.

The basement is the hub

On most of these houses the recorder, the switch that powers the cameras and the battery backup live in the basement, on a shelf or a small wall rack above any line water has reached before. From there, cable goes up through the basement ceiling to the front wall, through a sleeved hole in the foundation to the side passage and the back, and up through a closet or a chase to an upper-floor camera if one is needed. In a finished basement or one rented as an apartment, we route through utility areas and closets and keep the equipment in a space the owner controls.

When tenants change

Turnover is where camera systems in shared houses go wrong. A former tenant who still has the app can see the front of the house months after they leave. We show the owner how to remove a user in under a minute, and recommend doing it on move-out day along with collecting keys. New tenants get their own login and a copy of the camera list.

Power and internet

Wired cameras get their power through the same cable that carries their video, from a switch beside the recorder, so no camera needs its own outlet. The recorder records whether or not the internet is working; the internet only matters for watching from a phone. If tenants and the owner have separate internet accounts, the camera system usually goes on the owner's, and tenants watch through the app like anyone else.

Co-ops and Apartment Buildings

Small Co-ops, Walk-Ups and the Buildings Run by Boards and Agencies

Williamsbridge is not only houses. Listings show a number of co-op buildings, most of them low-rise walk-ups: NYBits lists Williamsbridge co-ops at 3550 Carlisle Place, 664 East 223rd Street, 710 East 230th Street and 839 East 217th Street, and Homes.com names the Surrey Coops at 814 and 836 Tilden Street. StreetEasy describes 3215 Olinville Avenue, known as Olinville Manor, as a six-story, 47-unit HDFC co-op built in 1963 with income guidelines, a part-time doorman, a common laundry and garage parking. City Planning describes small three- to five-story apartment buildings mixed in among the houses, and the corridors along Gun Hill Road and White Plains Road hold apartments over stores. In every one of these, the decision about common-area cameras belongs to a board or an owner, not to a resident.

The board decides, and wants paperwork

Co-op boards usually want three things before a camera project is approved: a plan showing where each camera goes and what it sees, a written policy on who may view and export video, and a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and its managing agent before anyone starts work. We prepare the camera plan and supply the insurance certificate. The viewing policy is the board's to write, but we can tell a board what other buildings typically put in one: who has live access, who can export clips, how long video is kept and how shareholders ask for a clip after an incident.

What a small building's core cameras cover

A walk-up co-op or rental building follows a familiar pattern: a street door, a vestibule, a lobby with mailboxes, one stair, a cellar door and often a rear yard where garbage cans sit and a back door opens. The core cameras watch the inside of the vestibule facing the street door, the mailboxes, the foot of the stair, the cellar door and the rear door. Stair cameras look along the stair rather than at any apartment door. A legal answer published on Justia sums up the line we follow: as long as a camera is not aimed into an apartment when its door is open, common-area video surveillance is legal.

No law requires cameras, but light matters

There is no general New York City law that requires an apartment building to have cameras; the rules that do exist are about entry doors, intercoms and lighting. That makes lighting the place to start in an older building. A camera pointed at a dark cellar landing or a rear yard with a dead bulb records nothing useful. When we walk a building, we note every fixture that needs fixing before a camera there is worth buying.

Buildings with staff part of the day

Where a building has a doorman or a super only part of the day, the cameras carry the rest of it. The lobby monitor at a part-time desk should show the street door and the vestibule, and the recorder should sit in a locked room, not under the desk. Outside the staffed hours, the board decides who, if anyone, gets alerts.

Renters in small buildings

A tenant in a rental building generally needs the landlord's permission to put any device in a common area, including the hallway outside their own door, and a doorbell camera on an apartment door is a gray area that depends on the lease. Inside the apartment, aimed at your own front door, is your business. We ask renters to get any landlord approval in writing before we quote anything on the hallway side.

Gun Hill Houses and other NYCHA property

Gun Hill Houses, NYCHA's 1951 development on Gun Hill Road with addresses including 3440 White Plains Road, 712 and 740 Gun Hill Road and 3435 Holland Avenue, has its own camera program: it was on NYCHA's 2015 list of developments receiving cameras, and more grounds cameras were funded after a bullet struck the development's daycare on White Plains Road in 2022. Cameras on NYCHA buildings and grounds are NYCHA's to install. Residents who want something for their own apartment should check their lease and talk to management first; at another Bronx NYCHA development, Parkside Houses, a resident was told she had breached her lease by installing cameras.

Footage That Counts

From Your Camera to the 47th Precinct: Making a Clip Someone Can Use

In nearly every Williamsbridge case we found in the news, a camera clip did the work afterward. NYPD released video from the Holland Avenue burglary, from the robbery near East 213th Street and Holland Avenue, and from incidents on East Gun Hill Road and Fenton Avenue. The new ARGUS cameras planned for the 47th Precinct are being added, in the words of News 12's report, to reduce the delays in obtaining footage. All of that points the same way: the camera is only half of the job. The other half is a recording that is still there when someone asks for it, with the right time on it, in a form that can be handed over.

The clock

A recorder whose clock has drifted ten minutes, or never switched to daylight saving time, produces clips that do not line up with a 911 call, a neighbor's video or a store's receipt. We set every recorder to take its time from the internet automatically and check that it shows the right time zone. It is a small setting that decides whether a clip can be matched with anything else.

How long video is kept

A recorder keeps video until its drive fills, then writes over the oldest. How many days that is depends on the number of cameras, their resolution and whether they record all the time or only on motion. We size the drive so a typical house keeps a few weeks, and we tell the owner the actual number after the system has run for a few days. A clip worth keeping should be saved the same day it is noticed, not left on the recorder.

Exporting the original

Police and insurers want the original file with its time stamp, not a phone recording of a screen. We show every household how to export a clip from the app or from the recorder to a thumb drive, and how to name it with the address, the camera and the date. Owning the camera means owning the footage. A landlord is not generally obligated to share building footage with a tenant, and a homeowner decides whether to share their own clips. The Holland Avenue burglars came in through a back window during a snowstorm. Will snow hide someone at our rear windows from the camera?

It can, if the camera is set up the usual way. It could, if the wrong person ever sees that camera's picture. If you want a camera inside, aim it at the room's doorway or the stairs so it records whoever goes in, not at the safe itself, keep that camera on the owner's login only, and use a strong, unique password with two-step sign-in on the camera account.

The Law in Plain Words

New York Rules Behind a Williamsbridge Camera Job

A camera job on a two-family, a co-op or a shop on White Plains Road runs into the same handful of New York rules. This is a plain summary of how we work within them, not legal advice; for a dispute, talk to a lawyer.

Private spaces stay off camera

Recording people without consent where they reasonably expect privacy, such as bedrooms, bathrooms and changing areas, is unlawful surveillance in New York. A camera may watch your stoop, your pad and your yard; it may not look into a neighbor's or a tenant's windows. Where a view takes in a window that is not yours, we black it out with a privacy mask in both the live view and the recording.

Neighbors' yards

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. On Williamsbridge's small, closely packed lots, this is the rule most likely to matter, so rear cameras are aimed at your house and yard and masked over everyone else's.

Sound

Recording a conversation you are not part of without consent can be eavesdropping, and whether a camera passively picking up hallway or doorway sound crosses that line has never been settled cleanly. Talking to a visitor through a doorbell is a conversation you are in. A camera quietly recording tenants talking in a shared hall is not. We leave continuous audio off on shared and outdoor cameras.

Who decides

In a co-op, the board controls the common areas. In a rental, the landlord controls the building, and tenants generally need permission for anything in a hallway, lobby or other shared space. In a two- or three-family, the owner decides on the building and should tell tenants in writing what each camera covers. On NYCHA property, NYCHA decides. There is no general New York City law that requires apartment buildings to have cameras.

Permits

Most residential camera installations do not need a permit. If a job adds a new electrical circuit, such as a dedicated circuit for a new building's head-end room, that part is electrical work and is handled as such. The cameras themselves are low-voltage.

Who may install

New York's General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. The Department of State's application adds that installing "passive," non-detecting security devices does not require licensure, and sources disagree on where ordinary camera systems fall. We do not need to settle that argument: AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and you should ask any installer for both.

Our house faces the elevated train, and the lit cars flash past all night. Will that keep setting off motion alerts?
+

It can, because a sudden change in light across the picture looks like motion to a basic camera. The fix is to aim the camera so the tracks take up as little of the frame as possible, draw the alert zone only over your door, steps and pad, and use the person or vehicle detection on the camera or recorder rather than simple motion. A steady light on your own front also helps, since the passing trains then change the picture less.
