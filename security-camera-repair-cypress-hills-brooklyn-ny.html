The Neighborhood

Why Cypress Hills Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Cypress Hills sits at the far eastern edge of Brooklyn, where the borough runs up against Queens and the cemeteries. To the north are Highland Park and the Cemetery Belt, to the south and west the blocks run down into East New York, and Fulton Street, Atlantic Avenue and Jamaica Avenue carry most of the traffic and most of the shops. The housing is a mix: wood-frame houses from around 1900, brick rows put up by a single builder, semi-detached houses, two- to four-family houses and apartment buildings, with the 1955 Cypress Hills Houses south of Sutter Avenue. That mix matters for a camera repair, because every one of those buildings puts its cameras, cable and recorder in a different place, and most were wired by whoever was available at the time.

What brings people to a camera repair here, more than anything, is something that already happened. In the early hours of May 26, 2026, News 12 Brooklyn reported, a man forced open a door at Chef Quang Guyanese Chinese Express near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street, hid his face with a T-shirt, spent about seven minutes inside and left with a safe that staff said held about $100,000 in personal valuables. The station published the business's surveillance video, and "another camera captured the person carrying the safe across a parking lot." Investigators tied the same man to a register break-in on Conduit Boulevard four nights later, where about $2,000 was taken after a window was broken. The restaurant told News 12 it was not the first time it had been targeted. A few months earlier, on February 10, a man pulled a gun in a smoke shop at 2945 Fulton Street just before midnight, took the register cash and ran into the Cleveland Street station at the end of the block; police released photos of him in a hooded jacket, mask and gloves. In both cases, the only description anyone had came from cameras.

The third reason is that shared cameras are often the ones that fail longest. At the City Council's public housing committee hearing on March 10, 2025, James Elseviera, the tenant association president of Cypress Hills Houses, told members that "all the cameras are spray painted and taped up," that "none of the cameras is working," and that it had been that way since about December 10. Cypress Hills Houses had been on the city's 2014 list of developments getting new cameras, and NYCHA's own fact sheet says its camera and access projects typically take about three years from planning to the end of construction. NYCHA's cameras are NYCHA's to fix, not ours, but the pattern is the same in private buildings: a camera that serves everyone is the one nobody notices has gone dark, until somebody needs it.

The commercial strips add their own pressure. When the city's Small Business Services department surveyed the corridors in this part of Brooklyn, a quarter of merchants named security as the improvement they wanted most, and people described the lighting under the elevated tracks on Fulton Street as poor. The Cypress Hills Fulton Business Improvement District, formed in 2024 to cover 26 blocks of Fulton Street from Van Siclen Avenue to Eldert Lane, listed "improved sanitation, street lighting, public safety, and public space programming" as its priorities in its city filing. Better lighting helps cameras, but a change in lighting also changes what a camera sees at night, and a picture that was set up for one kind of light can turn useless under another. Stores that add bullet-resistant glass or a buzz-in door after a robbery, as the Fulton Street smoke shop did, often find the camera behind the counter now looks at a reflection.

Last, the land itself. Cypress Hills is built on the ridge left by the last glacier, and Brownstoner's writers have described the steep streets near Sunnyside Avenue, where houses sit above the street at the top of long stairs. For cameras, the slope means long cable runs up and down stairs and across porches, cameras that look down from well above a visitor's head, and back yards that open onto the dark of the park and the cemeteries rather than onto another lit yard. None of that breaks a camera by itself, but it shapes which repairs come up and how they are done.

The wood-frame house: water finds the cable hole

The frame houses are some of the oldest buildings in Cypress Hills. Brownstoner's 2014 profile of 218 Arlington Avenue describes a Queen Anne house from about 1900 whose fish-scale shingles, visible in a 1941 tax photo, are now covered in vinyl siding, and that is typical. A camera on a house like this is fixed through the siding into whatever is behind it, and its cable usually goes into the wall through a hole drilled beside it. When the siding was put on over the original walls, the screws may be holding only the siding, and the hole may never have been sealed behind it.

The repairs that follow are predictable. A camera that sags or shifts in wind is held by too little. A camera that dies after every heavy rain has water running down the cable into its connection or into the wall. A camera that went dark after the siding or the gutters were redone had its cable pinched or cut by the crew. We find what the camera is actually fastened to, move the fixing into solid wood where it is not, put a drip loop in the cable below the entry so water drips off instead of running in, and seal the hole. If the connection inside the camera's base has already corroded, we redo it rather than hope it dries out.

The builder's brick row: one block, one set of mistakes

Brownstoner's 2012 profile of 149 to 181 Sunnyside Avenue describes about 90 houses that Richards Real Homes built there between 1900 and 1920, in red and buff brick with tile roofs, deep porches and bays. Rows like this were built from one plan, and their cameras tend to be put up the same way too, often by the same installer working down the block. When one house on a row calls with a fault, it is common to find the same weak point on its neighbors: the same unsealed connection under the porch roof, the same adapter plugged into the same porch outlet, the same recorder on the same cellar shelf.

Brick changes the repair in two ways. A camera on brick should be anchored into the mortar joints or into the brick with the right masonry anchors, not into a wooden porch post that moves with the seasons. And cable that enters through brick needs a sleeve and a seal, or it will chafe and let water in. On a brick row we also look at the porch roof itself, because many cameras are fixed to the underside of it, where water collects and where the porch light circuit sits within reach of a careless switch.

The house above the street: long stairs, long cable and a camera too high

On the steeper blocks of the ridge, houses sit well above the sidewalk at the top of long flights of steps. A camera mounted by the front door of a house like that is looking down a full story or more at the street, and the person ringing the bell from the bottom of the stairs is a small figure at the edge of the frame. Owners usually call because the camera is "blurry," but the camera is often fine; it is simply too far away and too wide to show a face.

There are three honest fixes, in order of cost. If the existing camera has a zoom lens, we narrow its view and refocus it on the steps. If it does not, we can move it lower or add a second camera at the gate or the bottom of the stairs on the same recorder. The cable for that second camera has to come down the steps or along the side of the house, and on a frame house that means a new protected run, so we price it before we pull anything. The same applies to the back of these houses, where the yard is often a story below the back door.

The two- to four-family house: one recorder, several households

Two- and three-family houses are everywhere in Cypress Hills, and most have one camera system that the owner put in and everyone in the house relies on. The recorder is usually in the cellar, sometimes inside the basement apartment, and the cameras cover the porch, the side driveway and the back yard. When the system stops working, the call often comes from a tenant, but the person who can approve the repair and who knows the password is the owner.

The repair in these houses usually begins with the recorder's position and power. A recorder in a damp cellar, on the floor, plugged into the same strip as a dehumidifier or a freezer, wears its drive and its power supply faster. We check whether it is actually saving, whether its clock is right, and whether the drive reports errors, then we test every camera. Where the recorder sits somewhere only one household can reach, we say so in writing, because it decides who can save a clip when something happens at 2 a.m.

The walk-up and apartment building: cameras added by several hands

Small apartment buildings along the side streets and the larger buildings closer to the parks tend to have cameras added in stages: one at the vestibule when the door was replaced, two in the stair hall after a burglary, one at the back after packages went missing. Each may have come from a different installer, and each may run to a different recorder or to none at all. Brick Underground has pointed out that New York City does not require buildings to have cameras, so when one dies, nobody is obliged to notice.

Our first job in a building like this is an inventory: every camera, every cable, every recorder, and who controls each one. Then we test from the recorder outward, label what we trace, and write down which cameras are working, which are dead and which are not connected to anything. That written list is often the most useful thing a landlord or managing agent gets from the visit, because it turns a vague complaint into a priced repair.

The store with apartments above: the counter, the door and the el

On Fulton Street, Atlantic Avenue and Jamaica Avenue, most shops are small, many stay open late, and the camera system is often a recorder under the counter with four to sixteen cameras. The J and Z run overhead on Fulton Street, and the Small Business Services survey noted the poor lighting under the tracks. Store cameras here fail in a few repeatable ways: the counter camera loses its view when glass or shelving changes, the door camera sees a dark silhouette against the bright street, and the recorder under the counter gets kicked, unplugged or overheated.

We plan store repairs around the hours the store is open, test what can be tested with the store running, and keep any work on the recorder to a few minutes when it has to be switched off. Every store repair ends with us checking the cameras that cover the register, the door and the way out, because those are the clips the 75th Precinct asks for first.

The lot, garage and auto shop: long runs and poles

The Conduit Boulevard break-in in May 2026 began with a broken window, and businesses with lots and yards along Conduit and Atlantic often have cameras at the far ends of their property, on poles, fences or the corners of sheds. Those cameras sit at the end of the longest cable runs, are the most exposed to weather and are the easiest to knock out of aim. We test the cable end to end, check that the camera at the far end is getting enough power over that distance, and re-aim it on the entrance and the line a vehicle or a person would take to leave.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Checking a Cypress Hills Store System the Day After a Break-In on the Block

When a store owner on Atlantic Avenue or Fulton Street calls because a business down the block was broken into, the visit follows a set order, and it is worth knowing before we arrive. We start with the recorder, not the cameras. We open playback for the previous night and the night before that, because a recorder that shows live pictures can still have stopped saving. We compare the recorder's clock with a phone, because a clip with the wrong time is hard to use. We read the drive's health page and how many days of video it is actually keeping.

Then we walk the store from the outside in, the way someone breaking in would. Which camera sees the door that would be forced? Which sees the window? Which sees the route out, to the sidewalk, the lot or the alley behind? In this spring's Atlantic Avenue case, the camera that showed the man carrying the safe across the parking lot was as important as the one inside. Most stores we check have the inside covered and the way out not covered at all.

Last, we look at the picture at night. A store camera that is perfect at noon can be useless at midnight under the el, so for a night complaint we either come back after dark or test with the store lights off. The owner gets a written list: what works, what does not, what it costs to fix, and which camera to fix first if the budget is tight. We do not take or keep any footage we see on the visit.

Fault by Fault

Power, Drives, Light or Aim: How Cypress Hills Camera Systems Break and What Each Repair Involves

Reolink's guide to video loss lists the usual suspects: power that is too weak or too far away, power spikes, weak Wi-Fi or a loose connector, night-vision faults, cameras the recorder cannot read, damaged cable, old hardware or buggy firmware, and two devices fighting over one network address. In Cypress Hills nearly every call fits into one of four groups.

Power: the box nobody looks at

Analog cameras usually draw power from one central box with a fuse or a breaker for each camera, and network cameras get theirs from a PoE switch. When several cameras go dark at once, the cameras are rarely the problem. A blown fuse in the power box, a switch that lost ports, or a battery backup that has worn out and cuts the power during every flicker will take out a row of cameras together. We test each output under load, replace what has failed, and check that whatever powers the system is not sharing a strip with a space heater or a freezer. Power supply repair is $150 to $400 on our Brooklyn list.

Drives: the part that wears out first

The hard drive inside the recorder spins around the clock, and it is the part that fails most often. On a long-running CCTVForum thread about how long recorders last, one installer wrote, "In my experience, I've seen a lot of DVRs last 5-7 years," and several others said the drive, not the recorder, is usually what dies, and that a recorder that will not start or freezes on startup often runs again with a new drive. We read the drive's health report on every visit, swap it when it is failing, and set the recording schedule again so the new drive actually fills. A drive swap runs $200 to $450 by capacity.

Light: the picture that changed when the lights did

A camera is set up for the light it sees. When a store adds bright lights, bullet-resistant glass or a new sign, when a block gets new street lights, or when a yard camera faces the open dark toward the park or the cemetery, the picture can go from good to useless without anything breaking. The fixes are usually settings, aim or the camera's position, and occasionally a different camera built for that light. We test at the time of day the complaint is about. Night vision repairs run $250 to $650; settings with no part fall within the service call.

Aim: cameras that see the wrong thing

A camera knocked by a ladder, turned by someone who did not want to be seen, or put up years ago for a door that is no longer used still works, it just watches the wrong place. In stores and houses here the most common gap is the way out: the parking lot, the alley, the side driveway or the gate at the bottom of the stairs. Re-aiming is part of the service call. Moving a camera to a new spot, with the cable extended, is priced in writing first.

Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street

Atlantic Avenue is a wide, fast road lined with restaurants, delis, gas stations, car businesses and buildings with parking lots behind or beside them. Jamaica Avenue along the cemetery side

The people who broke into stores on our stretch of Atlantic Avenue covered their faces. Is our camera still worth repairing if it may never show a face?

Yes. A face is the best thing a camera can catch, but it is not the only useful thing. In the May 2026 safe theft near Atlantic Avenue and Crescent Street, the man hid his face with a T-shirt, and investigators still released photos showing his cap, jacket, pants and what appeared to be a Citi Bike, taken from the business's cameras and a second camera across a parking lot. Clothing, build, the way someone walks, what they carry, the time they arrived and left and the direction they went are all things a working camera records. We set the repaired camera so its picture is sharp enough to show those details at the distance it is mounted, and we check it at night.

Our camera covers the back door, but anyone who broke in would leave across the lot behind the building. Can one camera cover both?

Sometimes. If the door and the lot are in the same direction from the camera, a wider view or a slightly different angle may cover both, at the cost of less detail on each. If they are not, the honest answer is a second camera on the same recorder, aimed down the lot toward the street. We look at both from where a person would actually walk, show you the picture each option gives on the screen before anything is fixed in place, and price the second camera in writing if you need one.

We put a bullet-resistant partition across our counter after a robbery. Now the camera behind it shows the store lights reflected in the glass instead of the customer. Can that be fixed?

Usually, without a new camera. The reflection comes from the camera looking through the glass at an angle while bright lights sit behind or beside it. Moving the camera so its lens is close to the glass and square to it, or moving it to the customer side of the partition where it is protected, removes most of the glare, and a small hood around the lens can stop the rest. If the camera has to stay where it is, its exposure can sometimes be set to favor the customer's face. We test the change with the store lights on as they normally are.

We added a buzz-in door to our store. Can the camera at that door come up on the counter screen by itself when someone rings?

Often, yes, depending on the recorder and the door system. Many recorders can switch a monitor to one camera full screen when an alarm input is triggered, and many door release and intercom systems have a contact that can trigger it. Where both are present, we wire the two together so the worker sees the person outside, full size, before pressing the release. Where the recorder cannot do it, we can set the door camera to stay in a large tile on the counter screen. We look at both systems before quoting.

New, brighter street lights went up on our block, and since then our storefront camera's night picture has dark bands rolling through it. Is the camera failing?

Probably not. Some LED lighting flickers faster than the eye can see, and a camera whose shutter is out of step with that flicker records bands or a picture that pulses. It is a setting problem in most cases: changing the camera's shutter speed or anti-flicker setting to match the power frequency usually clears it. If the camera has no such setting, the bands may also ease if its view is moved so the light is not in the frame. We test after dark so we see what you see.

I live in Cypress Hills Houses and the cameras in our building have been taped over for months. Can I hire you to fix them?

No, and nobody outside NYCHA's own contractors can. The cameras in NYCHA developments belong to NYCHA, and work on them has to go through the Authority. What helps is reporting the cameras through NYCHA's own repair channels with the building address and the camera locations, and raising them with your tenant association, as the Cypress Hills Houses association president did at the City Council in March 2025. If you own a private house or building near the development, we can repair your own cameras.

Our house sits a full story above the sidewalk. The front camera sees the street, but anyone at the bottom of the steps is tiny in the picture. Do we need a new camera?

Not always. If the camera has a zoom lens, we can narrow and refocus it on the steps and the gate, which often turns a tiny figure into a usable face. If the lens is fixed, the choices are moving the camera lower on the house or adding a second camera at the gate or the bottom of the stairs, wired back to the same recorder. On a frame house the cable for that second camera needs a protected run down the side of the house, so we price it in writing before we start.

Our back yard faces the cemetery side, and at night the yard camera shows almost nothing past the fence. Is its infrared broken?

Usually it is working as designed. A camera's built-in infrared only reaches a limited distance, and it lights whatever is near it most brightly. Facing open, dark ground with nothing to reflect the light back, the picture fades to black a short way past the fence, while the fence itself may glow white. We check the infrared works, then improve the picture by aiming the camera at the part of the yard people would actually cross, adjusting its exposure, or adding a light, which also tells anyone in the yard they are being seen.

In our playback a person is at the door in one frame and gone the next. Is the recorder dropping video?

Sometimes, but more often it is set that way. Many recorders are set to save only a few frames a second to stretch the drive, and at that rate a person walking quickly jumps across the picture. It can also happen when the recorder is overloaded or its drive is failing and it skips frames. We check the recording settings and the drive's health, raise the frame rate on the cameras that cover doors and paths, and tell you how many days the drive will hold at the new setting so you can choose.

A store down the block was broken into last night. Which of our cameras should we fix first?

The ones covering the ways in and out. Start with the camera on the door or window someone would force, then the one covering the route away from the building, whether that is the sidewalk, a lot or an alley, then the counter or register. A camera on a corner nobody uses can wait. Before any of that, make sure the recorder is actually saving and its clock is right, because a perfect camera on a recorder that stopped recording last week is worth nothing. We can check all of this on one service call and give you a written list in that order.

We rent the ground floor of a building on Jamaica Avenue, and the landlord's cameras cover our entrance. Can our store have its own recorder without disconnecting his?

Yes, with the landlord's agreement. We can put separate cameras on your store's own recorder, inside your space, so you control your footage and your password, and leave the building's cameras on the landlord's system untouched. Where a building camera already covers your door, it is often possible to share its picture with you without giving you control of the landlord's recorder, but that is the landlord's decision. We will need the landlord's permission before any cable runs through common areas or onto the facade.

Our church on Barbey Street was nominated to the State Register of Historic Places. Do the cameras on the building need approval before they are replaced?

Not because of the listing itself, as far as our sources go. Equipment

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the building, whoever put it there. These are the kinds of equipment we work on in Cypress Hills houses, apartment buildings, stores and lots, and the way each tends to fail here.

Analog and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras on round BNC plugs, from old analog to HD-TVI, HD-CVI and AHD, still common in two-families and small stores. They fail at the twist-on connector, the plug-in adapter and the central power box.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras that get power and data through one cable from a PoE switch, found in newer buildings and larger stores. Faults: a dead switch port, a loose RJ45 plug, an address clash with a new device or firmware out of step with the recorder.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that keeps the footage, in a cellar, a closet, a back room or under a counter. Faults: worn drives, clocks that drift, overheating in tight spaces and passwords nobody remembers.

Zoom and varifocal cameras

Cameras whose view can be narrowed and refocused, the first thing we try on houses set above the street and on lots where the subject is far away.

Dome, turret and bullet housings

Cracked domes, fogged windows, brackets pulling out of siding, and housings knocked out of aim by ladders, deliveries or someone who did not want to be filmed.

Wi-Fi and battery cameras

Consumer cameras on porches, yards and doors that drop off when the router changes, run flat in cold weather or stop saving when a cloud plan lapses.

Pole, fence and long-run cameras

Cameras at the far end of lots and yards on Conduit Boulevard and Atlantic Avenue, where long cable, weather and vehicles cause most faults.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes for analog cameras, plug-in adapters, PoE injectors and battery backups, tested under load and replaced when they can no longer carry the system.

Counter monitors and phone apps

The screen behind the register, the monitor in the hall and app access for owners, relatives, tenants and managers, set up again after an internet change or a change of owner.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Cypress Hills

These claims appear in AI-written answers and in national how-to and cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is paraphrased below and then checked against what repairs actually look like in Cypress Hills houses, stores and lots.

"Security cameras last five to ten years, so an older system should simply be replaced"

Lifespan articles tend to give a range of years and treat the end of it as the time to replace everything, cameras, recorder and cable together.

The parts of a system do not age together. The cable in the walls of a Cypress Hills house can outlast several cameras. The recorder's hard drive is usually the first thing to wear out, and installers on the CCTVForum thread about recorder lifespans described recorders that ran again for years once only the drive was replaced. A camera that has faced the weather on a frame house for a decade may well be done; the one inside the hall may not.

So we test part by part. If the recorder is sound and only the drive is worn, a $200 to $450 drive swap is the repair. If the cameras are fine but the recorder cannot run a modern app, a new recorder on the same cameras may be enough. We tell you where replacing makes more sense than repairing, and why.

"Video loss is almost always a bad cable"

Many troubleshooting answers jump straight to the cable when a camera shows "no signal" or "video loss," and suggest re-running it.

Reolink's own guide to video loss puts power at the top of its list, from loose connections and failing PoE switches to one supply asked to power too many cameras, followed by spikes, weak Wi-Fi, a loose BNC connector, night-vision faults and compatibility. On the systems we see in Cypress Hills, a failed power box, a dead switch port or a corroded plug at the camera end are far more common than a cable that has actually broken inside a wall.

Re-running cable through an occupied two-family or a store ceiling is the most expensive repair there is, so it should be the last conclusion, not the first. We test the cable with an analyzer before anyone suggests replacing it.

"Any new, higher-resolution camera will work with your existing recorder"

Product listings and quick answers often suggest swapping a dead camera for a sharper model, as if every camera spoke to every recorder.

They do not. Reolink notes that a recorder may not be able to decode a camera's stream, especially with mixed brands, or may not support a camera with a higher resolution than it was built for. An older analog recorder in a cellar may not read an HD-over-coax camera of a different standard at all, and a network recorder may reject a camera from another maker until its settings are matched.

Before you buy a replacement, send us the recorder's label and model. We tell you what will actually work with it, or whether the recorder is the part holding the system back.

"If the burglar covered his face, the footage is useless"

It is common to read that cameras only matter if they catch a clear face, and that masked intruders make footage worthless.

The May 2026 Atlantic Avenue case says otherwise. The man covered his face, but the business's cameras and a second camera across a parking lot gave investigators images of his clothing, what appeared to be a Citi Bike, how long he was inside and which way he went, and those images were released to the public. The Fulton Street robbery suspect in February was masked and gloved, and police still released his photos.

A repair should aim for detail at the distance that matters, a correct clock and coverage of the way out, not just a face at the door. That is how we set cameras up after a repair.

"You do not need a licensed company just to repair cameras"

Some answers say a license only matters for new installations, or only for alarms, and that anyone can service an existing camera system.

New York's rules say otherwise for businesses that do this work for others. The state requires a license to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and its regulations cover closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement, whether full-time or part-time. An owner who works on their own system, or has an employee do it on their own property, does not need the license.

If someone else is working on your cameras, ask for their license number. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and a certificate of insurance can be sent to a landlord, managing agent or church board before the visit.

"Older Brooklyn neighborhoods need Landmarks approval for any camera on the facade"

Because so much of brownstone Brooklyn is landmarked, generic answers often assume that any old building in the borough needs city approval before a camera is mounted or replaced.

That is true inside a designated historic district, but we found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district over Cypress Hills' residential blocks. The 1908 church at 105 Barbey Street was nominated to the State and National Registers in 2023, and Brownstoner noted that those listings, unlike city designation, do not bring regulatory oversight over private owners.

That does not mean anything goes. A co-op, a landlord or a church board may have its own rules, and we still mount cameras in existing holes or mortar joints rather than through decorative stone. But for most Cypress Hills houses, replacing a dead camera does not wait on a city permit.

"Public housing developments have working CCTV, so residents are already covered"

The testimony from Cypress Hills Houses in March 2025 described cameras painted and taped over for about three months. For residents of the development, the route is NYCHA's own repair process and the tenant association. For private owners on the blocks around it, it is a reminder not to assume a nearby camera has your door covered. Your own cameras, working and checked, are the only ones you control.

FAQ

Cypress Hills Security Camera Repair FAQ

Why won't a clip copied from our recorder play on an ordinary laptop?
+

Many recorders export video in their own file format, which plays only in the maker's player program. Most recorders can also export a common format such as MP4, or include the player on the USB drive. When we save a clip for you we export it both ways, so an investigator, an insurer or a relative can open it on any computer.

Can an indoor camera look out through a window at night?
+

Not well with its infrared on. The infrared light bounces off the glass straight back into the lens, so the camera sees a white glare instead of the street. With the infrared switched off and enough outside light, such as a porch light or a street light, a camera pressed close to the glass can see out. For a dark yard, a camera mounted outside is the better answer.

Is it a problem to mix older 1080p cameras with new 4K cameras on one recorder?
+

Only if the recorder cannot handle the newer camera. Many recorders accept cameras of different resolutions side by side, but an older recorder may not support a 4K camera at full quality, or at all. We check the recorder's model and limits before recommending a replacement, so the new camera is not wasted on a recorder that cannot use it.

Our recorder says 8 channels. Does that mean eight cameras is the most we can ever have?
+

On that recorder, yes, eight cameras is its limit. If you need more, the choices are a recorder with more channels, which can usually take over the existing cameras and cable, or in some network systems a second recorder viewed in the same app. We count what you have and what you want before suggesting either.

Are cameras with a built-in spotlight or siren a good replacement on a storefront?
+

They can be, especially where the street is dark at night. A camera that switches on a light when someone walks up records a color picture and lets the person know they are being filmed. The siren is more of a judgment call on a busy corridor, where it may go off for passers-by. We can set the light to come on without the siren, and only during the hours the store is closed.

Can a camera be mounted on a fence post or a tree in the back yard?
+

A solid fence post or a dedicated pole, yes. A tree, we avoid, because it moves in the wind, grows around the bracket and shakes the picture enough to set off motion alerts. Wherever the camera goes, its cable needs a protected run back to the house, which we price before we start.

Do you repair solar-powered cameras?
+

Yes, though the fix is often the panel rather than the camera. A solar camera that runs flat usually has a panel in shade, a dirty panel, a battery that no longer holds a charge, or a winter sun too low for where the panel was placed. We test the battery and the panel's output and move the panel if the spot is the problem.

Will the technician need to use my phone during the repair?
+

Only to set up or test the app on it, and only with you there. We bring our own equipment to test the cameras and the recorder. At the end of the visit we check live view and playback on the phone of each person who needs access, with that person entering their own password where possible.

What should we use to clean a dusty camera lens between visits?
+

A clean, dry microfiber cloth and a gentle wipe, nothing more. Avoid paper towels, household glass cleaner and rough cloths, which can scratch the dome or the lens cover. If the lens is out of reach, leave it for a visit, because a ladder on porch steps or a slope is how most falls happen.

What does PoE on a camera's label mean?
+

Power over Ethernet. The camera gets its power and sends its video through one network cable, from a PoE switch or a recorder with PoE ports. It means there is no separate power adapter at the camera, so when a PoE camera goes dark the switch or recorder port that powers it is one of the first things we test.

Can the recorder email us a picture when someone comes to the door?
+

Many can. Most recorders and apps can send a phone notification with a snapshot, and many can also email one when a camera detects a person. We set it on the cameras where it is useful, such as the front door, and keep it off the busy ones so you are not flooded with messages.

Can a camera be hidden so it does not look like a camera?
+

Small and discreet cameras exist, and on a storefront or a porch a less visible camera is sometimes harder to cover or turn. In most places, though, a visible camera also discourages people, and hidden cameras in private areas such as bathrooms or bedrooms are never acceptable. We will talk through what you want to protect and suggest the right mix.
