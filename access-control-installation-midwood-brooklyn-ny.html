The Neighborhood

Why Midwood Properties Need Access Control

Midwood is a neighborhood of houses first. When the Department of City Planning rezoned about 80 blocks of Community District 14 in 2006, bounded by Avenue H to the north, Ocean Avenue to the east, Avenue P and Kings Highway to the south and Coney Island Avenue to the west, it described the area as mostly two- and three-story detached and semidetached houses, with attached rows on some blocks. Bisnow's profile of the neighborhood put the rental picture plainly: "There are few rental buildings, with most rentals residing in multifamily homes." That shapes every access control job here. The typical Midwood door is not a lobby with a desk. It is the porch door of a freestanding house, the side door at the end of a driveway, the shared front door of a two-family, or the cellar entrance a tenant uses, and each one has been opened by more keys over the years than the owner could count.

Detached houses have a particular weakness: more doors than anyone watches. A semidetached house on a side street usually has a front door, a side or kitchen door off the driveway, a cellar door and, at the back of the lot, a garage. The family tends to come and go through the side door, which faces a neighbor's wall rather than the street, and that is exactly the door that gets left on the latch while someone carries in shopping. A system that gives each person their own code, relocks the side door by itself and tells the owner when a door was left open does more for a Midwood house than any extra deadbolt on the front.

The apartment buildings are fewer but larger. The 2006 rezoning found four- to eight-story buildings clustered along Avenue K, Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway and parts of Avenue N. CityRealty lists 1947 Ocean Avenue, between Avenues N and O, as a 1927 co-op of six floors and 77 apartments with an elevator, and 1801 Ocean Avenue as a seven-story co-op from 1962 with an elevator, a live-in superintendent, an intercom, "keyless entry," no doorman and an indoor garage with a waiting list. Brownstoner described 1710 Avenue H in June 2026 as a six-story, 37-unit elevator building from about 1937 with "an elaborate entrance with crenellated top and arched door." Newer buildings are on the same avenues: a 2020 condo of 16 apartments at 2025 Ocean Avenue advertises a "Virtual Doorman." Each of these buildings has a lobby, a cellar, a laundry and often a garage, and most have no one at a desk.

The calendar matters here more than in most of Brooklyn. Bisnow described Midwood as largely Orthodox Jewish, with Chinese, Turkish, Pakistani, Polish, Russian and Bangladeshi families as well, and noted that most shops on Avenues J and M close Friday evening and stay closed all day Saturday. For many households a fob, a keypad or a phone key cannot be used from Friday evening to Saturday night or on Yom Tov. Chabad.org's guidance is direct: "Doors that open with electronic key cards, fobs, or keypads may not be used on Shabbat." So a Midwood door that works only electronically is a door some of its own residents cannot open one day a week. Every system we install here keeps a mechanical key path, and we plan who holds those keys as carefully as who holds fobs.

Houses of worship and schools are part of almost every block. UJA-Federation of New York named Midwood, with Kensington, Williamsburg, Crown Heights, Borough Park and Flatbush, among the neighborhoods whose small synagogues could draw on a $250,000 security fund it created in 2022, with packages that "can include new doors, locks and video cameras." On Coney Island Avenue, News12 reported that on June 21, 2026, just before 11:30 p.m., a man entered the Makki Masjid Muslim Community Center at 1089 Coney Island Avenue and took a 69-year-old worshipper's cellphone and wallet from a changing room. A building that has to welcome people at prayer times still needs inner doors that stay shut, and that is a job for access control rather than for a lock on the front door alone.

The crime news, read carefully, argues for clear thinking rather than fear. News12 reported that on February 18, 2026, at about 1:35 a.m., "multiple people forced their way into" a residence near East 13th Street and Avenue P. No reader stops a group willing to force a door, and we do not pretend otherwise. What a system does is make the everyday cases safe: a side door never left on the latch, a front door answered by video before anyone opens it, and a phone alert the moment a door is forced.

A Keypad That Ignores Codes, a Lobby Reader Gone Quiet or a Shop Door That Stays Open: Repair in Midwood

Not every Midwood call is a new system. If the keypad on a house's side door has stopped taking codes, the reader in an Ocean Avenue lobby no longer answers its controller, a shop's back door on Avenue M unlocks for a fob and never locks again, or the laundry room door in a co-op cellar stands open every morning, ring (347) 934-8335 and tell us what the door is doing, or send a short video of it opening and closing. A visit is booked within two hours of the call. In houses the cause is often the door itself, such as a door that has dropped on its hinges so the latch misses the strike; in apartment buildings it is more often a tired backup battery, a controller that lost its network, or an administrator account that belonged to a board member who sold years ago. We work on systems other companies installed as well as our own, and we tell you plainly when a part is not worth repairing.

The building decides the hardware. These are the six kinds of property we meet most often in Midwood, from the freestanding houses on the numbered East streets to the prewar apartment houses on the avenues, with what each one usually needs at the door.

Detached houses with a porch and a side driveway

Two- and three-story freestanding houses, many with a front porch, a side door off a driveway and a garage behind. The porch door gets the visitor station; the side door, which the family actually uses, gets a reader or keypad and a strike that relocks itself; the cellar door gets a contact so the owner knows when it opens.

Semidetached brick houses

Pairs of houses that share one wall and often a driveway. A controller on the owner's side of the basement, cable that stays on the owner's side of the party wall, and nothing mounted on the shared driveway without both owners' agreement.

Two- and three-family houses

The most common rental building in the neighborhood, with one street door for the owner's family and one or two tenant households, and sometimes a separate cellar or garden entrance. Personal codes and fobs for each household, end dates for tenants, and an owner account that keeps control of the whole list.

Houses in Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park

Freestanding suburban houses from 1890 to 1930 in the landmarked district at the north of the neighborhood. Small devices placed within or beside the door opening, finishes that suit the house, and manufacturer cut sheets ready for the Landmarks permit.

Prewar elevator buildings on Ocean Avenue, Avenue H and Avenue K

Six-story co-ops and rentals from the 1920s and 1930s, often with elaborate stone entrances and original iron-and-glass doors. Electrified hardware chosen for the doors as they are, cable routed through the vestibule and cellar rather than through the stonework, and a lobby panel sized to the directory.

Postwar co-ops and new condos

Buildings like the 1962 co-op on Ocean Avenue with a garage and a live-in super, and newer condos with remote attendant services. Networked readers on every common door, garage control, and administrator accounts held by the board rather than by a vendor.

What the materials mean for the install

Midwood's freestanding houses are mostly wood framed behind their brick, stucco, shingle or siding fronts, and the side door off the driveway almost always sits in a wood jamb with a hollow wall beside it. That makes the cable route easy to find: down through the wall cavity into the basement, or under the porch floor to a basement window well. It also makes the jamb the part to check first. A century of locks, hinges and repainting can leave the strike side of a jamb soft or split, and a strike screwed into soft wood will rattle loose within a season. Where the wood is tired, we splice in a hardwood section or fit a strike with a longer reinforcing plate before any electronics go on.

The prewar apartment houses are the opposite case. Their entrances are stone, brick and iron, and their doors are often a heavy pair of iron-and-glass leaves in a masonry opening. Nothing gets drilled into carved stone. Cable comes up from the cellar through the vestibule floor or inside an existing conduit, power reaches the moving leaf through an electrified hinge or a protected door loop, and devices are mounted on the door frame, the vestibule wall or a small post rather than on the facade.

The Side Door Off the Driveway: Where Midwood Houses Are Really Entered

Ask a Midwood family which door they use and the answer is rarely the one with the porch and the house number. In a freestanding or semidetached house with a driveway down one side, the car stops beside the kitchen or side door, the shopping comes in that way, the children come home from school that way, and the porch door is kept for guests and the mail. That is a sensible routine and a weak one at the same time, because the side door faces a neighbor's wall or a fence, is out of sight from the street and is the door most likely to be left on the latch for ten minutes that turn into an hour.

So on a detached house we usually start at the side. A fail-secure electric strike in the side door's jamb means the door is locked every time it closes, without anyone turning a key. A keypad or reader beside the door means the family's hands can be full and the door still opens with a short code or a tap of a fob. A door position contact means the controller knows the difference between a door that is shut and a door that is only pulled to, and it can send a phone alert if the side door has been standing open for longer than a set time. The porch door then gets the visitor station with a camera.

The cellar door is the one owners forget. Many Midwood houses have an outside stair down to the basement at the side or back, closed by a steel or wood door or a pair of bulkhead doors. These rarely need a reader; nobody comes in that way on purpose. They need a contact, so the system can report the door opening when the house is empty, and a lock that cannot be lifted from outside.

The garage at the back of the lot

The garage behind a Midwood house is often older than the car in it, holds tools, bicycles and a spare freezer, and is closed with a padlock or a key nobody has seen for years. Putting it on the house system is straightforward if we can get a cable to it. On many lots the cable can follow the fence line in conduit or run overhead from the house eave to the garage; where neither is possible, a wireless bridge or a battery keypad lock on the garage's people door gives the same codes without trenching the driveway. The overhead door itself can join too, through its operator's input for an external opener, as long as the operator's own safety eyes stay in charge of closing.

Fobs Six Days a Week and a Key on the Seventh: Planning Midwood Doors Around Shabbos

In a neighborhood where many households keep Shabbat, a door that opens only electronically is a design mistake, not a feature. Chabad.org's guidance on doors says that "Doors that open with electronic key cards, fobs, or keypads may not be used on Shabbat," that the devices themselves "should not be handled," and that the answer is to arrange before Shabbat to use a door with a standard key or to have staff open the door. Peninei Halakha adds that a Jew should not press an intercom to ask a guard to open the door. We are installers, not rabbis, and we do not rule on what any household may or may not do.

The city has seen what happens when that is forgotten. In March 2007, Yeshiva World News reported that observant residents of Stuyvesant Town and Peter Cooper Village objected to a card-swipe system that was replacing keyholes, with one resident, Jeannette Shuck, telling reporters, "They are making us prisoners in our own homes," and residents at Peter Cooper describing waits of more than 30 minutes outside for someone to let them in. JTA's report two days later quoted a tenant who had stopped walking to synagogue for months. The owner's fix was simple and came late: in its spokesman's words, "The Orthodox Jewish residents will be given a key before sundown that will be returned on Sunday." A Midwood building should start where that one ended.

What a Shabbos-ready door looks like

The essentials are mechanical. Every controlled entrance keeps a keyed cylinder that opens the door with an ordinary metal key, whatever the electronics are doing. The electric strike or lock is fail-secure where the code allows, so the door stays latched in a power cut and the key still works. The door closes and latches by itself, so a family coming home from shul does not have to pull it shut. And the inside lever, knob or panic bar opens the door mechanically on the way out, as the Building Code requires for exits anyway. None of that costs extra in a well-planned job; it is how the door should be built.

On top of that sit the choices each building makes for itself. Some co-ops issue a plain key to every household that asks for one; others issue keys only to residents who request them for Shabbos and Yom Tov and keep a signed list. Some buildings put the front door on a schedule; door timers of the kind Locksmith Ledger describes can lock and unlock by time of day, and some controllers have a "First Man In" setting that keeps a door locked until the first authorized person arrives and leaves it unlocked after that. Whether any schedule is appropriate for Shabbat is a question for each household's or community's rabbi, and we set the door the way the building decides, not the other way round.

What we do not do

We do not sell a door as "Shabbos compliant." Practice differs between communities and families, and a label cannot settle it. We do not remove the key path, and we do not set a door to stand unlocked for a day without the board's written instruction, because an unlocked street door in a multiple dwelling also runs into the self-closing and self-locking rules in Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a. What we do is give the board, the owner or the congregation a written description of every way into the building, so the people who decide these questions are deciding with the facts in front of them.

Elevator Co-ops on Ocean Avenue and Avenue K: Lobby, Laundry, Garage and the Super

Midwood's apartment buildings are concentrated where the 2006 rezoning found them, along Avenue K, Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway and parts of Avenue N, with more on Avenue H and Avenue I. Many are co-ops, many are prewar, and most share a profile that the CityRealty listing for 1801 Ocean Avenue sums up: an elevator, a live-in superintendent, an intercom, a mail room, no doorman and an indoor garage with a waiting list. In a building like that the front door is only the beginning. The laundry and storage rooms are in the cellar, the garage has its own entrance, the super's workshop and the boiler room must stay closed to everyone else, and the roof door has its own rules.

The listing for that same building already mentions "keyless entry," which is common in Midwood co-ops and tells a board less than it seems to. Keyless entry may mean a modern encrypted system, or it may mean 125 kHz proximity fobs from twenty years ago that a kiosk at a mall can copy in a minute. It may be run from a computer in the super's apartment that nobody else can log into. Before we quote new hardware for a building with an existing system, we read a few of its fobs, look at the controller and its software, and find out who actually holds the administrator account. Often the readers and strikes can stay and only the credentials and the management need to change.

What the super should and should not carry

In most Midwood co-ops the live-in super has been the building's access system for decades: he lets in the plumber, finds the laundry key, opens the garage for a new shareholder and knows which apartments have elderly residents who cannot come down to the door. A good system takes the routine part of that off him without taking his role away. The super gets an administrator account for day-to-day changes, such as a lost fob, a contractor's temporary code or a new laundry schedule, while the board's managing agent or treasurer holds the master account and receives a monthly list of changes. Service doors such as the boiler room, the meter room and the roof bulkhead can move to the super's own fob, and the old ring of keys goes into a lockbox for emergencies.

Lockouts are a design failure, not bad luck

Brick Underground published a letter in 2018 from a tenant whose building's fob system failed when the internet went down or when it rained, with no backup entry, and the tenant lawyer Sam Himmelstein told the paper, "If you're being deprived of the ability to get into your own apartment, your lease is being breached." A vendor's account of a fob rollout at Queensbridge South described tenants locked out, jammed doors and "management communication was minimal," with some residents propping entrances open. Every one of those failures is avoidable: our controllers keep every door working locally, on battery, without the internet, every entrance keeps a key path, and residents get a written guide before the old keys stop working.

Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park: Readers and Keypads on Landmarked Houses

At the north end of the neighborhood, the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District was designated on March 18, 2008. The Historic Districts Council describes its houses as "characteristic of suburban residences erected by builders between 1890 and 1930," with "the overwhelming majority of houses" built and occupied by 1914, in Arts and Crafts, Colonial Revival and Dutch Colonial Revival styles. These are freestanding houses with porches, deep front lawns and original wood doors, and owners there ask the same question most landmark owners ask us: can a reader go on the house without a fight with the Commission?

Usually, yes, if it is done the way the Landmarks Preservation Commission's own rules describe. Its permit guidebook says that staff "can issue permits approving light fixtures and intercom panels if the installation meets the following criteria," that such panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," that an intercom may instead go on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, that there is a limit of one intercom panel per door opening, and that "Design, size, and finish of installations must be compatible with the architecture of the building." Manufacturer cut sheets are required with the application, while ordinary maintenance and repair need no permit at all.

In practice that points to small devices placed inside the door reveal or on the casing, finished to suit the house, and to an electric strike in the jamb that leaves the face of the original door untouched. We prepare the cut sheets and a photo showing exactly where each device goes, and the owner or their expediter files them.

The Rules Behind a Midwood Door

An entry system in a New York City building touches several sets of rules at once, and which ones apply depends mainly on how many households live in the building and whether the door is an exit. None of them make access control hard to install. They decide how it has to behave.

Doors that close and lock by themselves

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and an intercom with voice and a remote door release in buildings of eight or more apartments; in older buildings the requirement applies when a majority of tenants ask for it in writing. HPD adds that apartment and hallway doors "are required by law to swing closed and latch by themselves," that a violation "must be corrected within 14 days," and that under Local Law 71 of 2022 it selects 300 class A buildings a year for proactive self-closing door inspections. An electric lock does not replace a working closer; it depends on one.

Three or more households: the Tenant Data Privacy Law

HPD's summary of Local Law 63 of 2021 says owners of multiple dwellings who use smart access, which includes fobs, key cards, apps and biometrics, must get tenants' consent, give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use it to track tenants outside the building, to harass them or to monitor their relationships. A three-family house is a multiple dwelling under these rules; a one- or two-family house is not. We set up the system so the owner can meet the policy, and we hand over a draft of the notice the owner gives tenants.

Exits always open

The NYC Building Code allows electrically locked exit doors only with the right releases: unlocking on power loss and on fire alarm, and a manual "PUSH TO EXIT" button 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door. On most Midwood house and shop doors we use fail-secure strikes with a mechanical lever inside, which opens the door whatever happens to the power. Access control is low-voltage work, done here under New York State license #12000287431.

Business Access Control in Midwood

Midwood's businesses are strung along a handful of streets that the 2006 rezoning named: Coney Island Avenue down the western edge, Avenue M from Coney Island to Ocean Avenue, Avenue J from Coney Island Avenue to East 16th Street, and Kings Highway along the south. In a house of worship the practical plan is a front door on a prayer-time schedule, with video so the person at the door can be seen, and readers on the inner doors that should never be open to a stranger: the office, the room where coats, bags and phones are left, the stairs to the classrooms and the rooms where charity boxes or valuables are kept. Each of those doors locks itself when it closes and opens only for the people who should be there.

Many Midwood institutions pay for door work with security money, and the wording of each program matters. Rules that apply to Midwood business doors

  • Exits always open. An electrically locked door on an exit path must release on power loss and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation and needs the manual release the Building Code calls for; a shop, shul or school exit opens from inside by its lever or panic bar.
  • Apartments above. Where a shop shares a street door with apartments in a multiple dwelling, Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a expects that door to be self-closing and self-locking, so it cannot sit on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. A business's own staff doors are outside the Tenant Data Privacy Law; a residential door it shares with three or more households is inside it.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Midwood: Questions Answered

Detached and two-family houses

Our family only ever comes in through the kitchen door halfway down the side driveway, never the porch door. Does the first reader belong on the door we use or on the door visitors see?

On the door you use. The point of the first controlled door is to remove the daily risk, and in your house the daily risk is the kitchen door: it is opened many times a day, it is out of sight from the street, and it is the one most likely to be left unlatched while someone brings in groceries. A fail-secure strike there relocks the door every time it closes, a keypad or reader lets anyone in the family in with full hands, and a door contact tells you if it has been left standing open.

The porch door can come second, usually as a visitor station with a camera, because what you need at the front is to see and speak to whoever rang before anyone goes to the door. Both doors run from one controller in the basement, so the second door costs much less to add than the first. If the budget allows only one this year, we wire the basement end for both, so the porch door is a short job later rather than a second project.

After people forced their way into a house near East 13th Street and Avenue P in February, should we worry more about how strong our front door is or about how we decide whom to open it for?

Both, but they are separate problems with separate fixes. News12 reported only that "multiple people forced their way into the residence" early on February 18, 2026; it did not say whether a door was broken or opened to a knock. Against a determined forced entry the door, frame and lock are what count: a solid door, a reinforced strike plate with long screws into the framing, and a deadbolt that is actually thrown at night. An electric strike is a convenience lock, not a substitute for a deadbolt, and we say so.

Where access control helps is the other half. A video station lets you see and talk to whoever is at the door from upstairs or from a phone, so nobody has to open the door to find out who knocked at 1:30 in the morning. A door contact sends an alert the moment a door is forced, so the household knows immediately.

Our upstairs tenants have lived with us for twelve years on a key we cut for them. How do we bring in codes without it sounding as if we suspect them?

Present it as what it is: a change to the house, not to them. The usual reasons owners give are true and easy to say: nobody knows how many copies of the old key exist after twelve years of relatives, cleaners and repairmen; the new door relocks itself so nobody has to remember; and if anyone in either household loses a fob it can be turned off in a minute without changing anyone's lock. Owners who switch at the same time as their own family, on the same day, rarely get pushback.

Give the tenants something, too. Each adult in their household gets their own code or fob, they get a household account that can create guest codes for their own visitors inside limits you set, and their old key keeps working on the cylinder as a backup, which matters if anyone upstairs keeps Shabbat. In a two-family house the Tenant Data Privacy Law does not apply, but telling the tenants in writing what the system records, who can see it and for how long is still the respectful thing to do, and it heads off the question before it is asked.

Ocean Avenue, Avenue H and Avenue K buildings

Our 1930s building on Avenue H has an arched stone entrance and a pair of original iron-and-glass doors. Where does the cable go when there is no straight header to run it through?

Not through the arch. On an entrance like that we treat the stonework as untouchable and bring the cable up from the cellar instead: through the vestibule floor, inside an existing conduit or behind the vestibule's interior wall, to the hinge side of the active door leaf. Power crosses to the door through an electrified hinge that looks like the other hinges, or through a protected door loop, and the lock is either an electrified mortise lock in the leaf or a strike in the frame at the meeting stile, whichever suits the doors as they are.

The inactive leaf keeps its flush bolts so it can still be opened wide for moving day, and a contact on each leaf tells the system whether the pair is really closed. The reader and the lobby panel go on the vestibule wall or a slim post beside the steps rather than on the carved surround. Before quoting we check the doors themselves, because iron-and-glass pairs of that age have often been rehung or re-latched over the years, and the latch has to meet the strike every time before any electronics can do their job.

Our co-op is moving to fobs, and several families need a metal key for Shabbos. How do we keep track of those keys once everything else is electronic?

Treat the keys as credentials too, just paper ones. We key the lobby cylinder to a restricted keyway, so copies can only be cut through the locksmith or supplier the board authorizes, and the board keeps a simple register: each key's number, the apartment it went to, the date and the resident's signature. When an apartment is sold, the key comes back with the fobs at the closing, the same as the mailbox key.

The system can help with the rest. With a door contact on the lobby door, any opening that was not a fob, a code or an exit button is logged as a key or a forced opening, so the board can see if key use appears at times that make no sense, such as a weekday at 3 a.m., without tracking anyone on Shabbat. If a key is lost, the cylinder is re-pinned and new keys issued to the people on the register, which takes an afternoon and leaves every fob untouched.

Shops and offices on the avenues

Our Coney Island Avenue clothing shop does alterations in a back room where customers' garments wait for pickup. Can that room lock separately from the sales floor?

Yes, and it is one of the cheapest doors to add once the shop has a controller. The back room gets a strike or an electrified lever and a small reader, so it relocks every time it closes and opens only for the tailor, the owner and whoever else you choose. During busy weeks before a holiday or a wedding season, when there is more in that room than usual, the log tells you who went in and when, and a held-open alert tells you if someone propped the door while the floor was crowded.

The inside of that door always opens by its lever, so nobody can be shut in. If the same room holds the cash or the jewelry, it can require a manager's credential after a certain hour. And if the shop's back door is in the same room, the two doors can be set so the street door to the back cannot be opened from outside while the room is unattended. All of it runs from the one controller and the same staff credentials, so nobody carries extra keys.

We rent the floor above our Kings Highway store to an immigration and tax-preparation office whose clients come up all day. Should the stair door be buzzed from upstairs or run on a schedule?

Usually both, at different times. During the office's posted hours the stair door can unlock on a schedule, or better, stay locked with a call button that rings the office, so the staff see each client on a screen and release the door from their desks. Outside those hours it locks, and only the office's own staff codes open it. The office's tenant administrator can add and remove its own staff without asking you, and its clients never pass through your store.

What decides between an open schedule and a buzz-in door is what else the stair serves. If the same stair leads to apartments, the street door belongs to the residents too, and in a multiple dwelling it should stay self-locking with the office using the call button. If the stair serves only the office, a schedule is fine. Either way, your store's doors and the office's stair door sit in separate partitions on the controller, so neither business can open the other's door.

Shuls, masjids and yeshivas

During prayers at our masjid on Coney Island Avenue, coats, phones and wallets are left in a side room. Can that room lock while the main hall stays open?

Yes. The side room gets its own reader and an electric lock that relocks it every time the door closes, so the room is open only to whoever holds a credential: the imam, the caretaker, the volunteers who run the room, or each regular worshipper if you prefer. The main entrance can stay on its prayer-time schedule, and the side room stays locked whether the main hall is full or empty. The News12 report from June 2026 describes exactly the situation this answers: a man entered a Coney Island Avenue masjid late in the evening and took a worshipper's phone and wallet from a changing room.

For people who do not carry a fob, a keypad with a code that changes each week, or an attendant at the door during the busiest prayers, works as well. The room's door always opens from inside, and a door contact sends an alert to the caretaker if it is propped open. If the masjid also has an office or a donation box, those doors go on the same controller with a shorter list of people.

The women's section of our shul has its own entrance on the side street. Should that door keep the same hours as the main entrance?

Often not, and it is worth deciding deliberately. A side entrance that is used heavily at certain services and barely at others, and that opens onto a quieter street than the main door, is a good candidate for a narrower schedule: open for the times it is actually used, locked the rest of the time, with the women who use it regularly holding their own credentials or the door's keyed cylinder. Its schedule can follow the main door's or run on its own, and both are set in one place.

Because the shul's practice on Shabbat and Yom Tov decides how any schedule may be used on those days, we set what the rabbi and the board decide and we write it down, door by door. On a weekday the side door can also ring the office or the gabbai's phone with video, so someone arriving late is seen before being let in, and if the door is ever forced or left open, the alert goes to the same people who manage the main entrance.

The state's safety-equipment list for our yeshiva includes a "remote electronic door unlatching system." Does that mean the reader, the lock or both?

The list names the system, not the parts, and the parts that make a door unlatch remotely are a lock or strike that can release electrically, a power supply, the controller or relay that tells it when, and the button, screen or app the office uses to send that command. A visitor station that lets the office see and speak to someone before releasing the door fits next to it under "visitor intercom," and the command that locks every outside door at once fits under "central lockdown." The NPSE list we read does not say how installation labor is treated, so we price equipment and labor on separate lines.

How a particular purchase is classified is a question for the school's grant administrator and the State Education Department's guidance for the current year, not for us. What we provide is a device list with model numbers, cut sheets, the quantity for each door and a written description of what each item does, which is usually what an administrator needs to match items to the list.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Midwood

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each one is set against what a Midwood door actually needs. We do not attribute a specific claim to any one of those sources unless we quote it.

“Keyless means no keys at all.”

Quick answers often present fobs and phone keys as the end of the metal key, and marketing copy encourages the idea.

In Midwood that is the wrong goal. Many residents use a metal key from Friday evening to Saturday night and on Yom Tov, and the city expects a key-based way in anyway; amNY reported in 2019 that the Department of Buildings requires one.

A good Midwood system is keyless for daily use and keyed for the days that need it, with a register of who holds the keys.

“Access control is for apartment buildings and offices.”

Generic answers picture a card reader in a lobby. That misses most of Midwood, where Bisnow found most rentals in multifamily homes rather than rental buildings.

A detached or two-family house has the same problem as an office on a smaller scale: several people who should get in, a list that changes, and no way to take back a copied key.

On our Brooklyn list one controlled door is $1,500 to $2,500 with the strike, wiring and ten fobs, the same line a small office would use.

“Put the reader on the front door.”

Answers assume the front door is the main door. On a Midwood house with a side driveway it usually is not.

The family comes in through the kitchen or side door, which faces a fence, is out of sight and is the one left on the latch.

We start with the door that is used most and give the front door a visitor station, which is often cheaper and more useful than the reverse.

“A smart deadbolt does the same job.”

A battery smart lock is a fine product for one door used by one household that is happy to use an app.

It is a weaker fit for a two-family's shared door or a house with a side door, a cellar door and a garage. Each lock has its own batteries and app, it rarely reports whether the door is shut, and its account belongs to one person.

A wired strike, a reader and a controller with a battery keep every door on one list that belongs to the owner.

“Fail-safe locks are always safer.”

The phrase sounds reassuring, so answers recommend fail-safe hardware everywhere. A fail-safe lock unlocks when it loses power.

On a house side door or a shop back door, that means the door is open to the street in every blackout, and on Shabbat nobody may be watching the door at all.

What keeps people safe is a door that always opens from inside mechanically. With that in place, fail-secure is usually right for Midwood houses and shops, and fail-safe hardware is kept for the doors the Building Code or the fire plan calls for.

“Landmarked houses can't have readers.”

Owners in Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park sometimes read that any change to a landmarked front needs a long approval or is refused.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says its staff can approve intercom panels installed within or beside the door opening, one panel per opening, with a compatible design, size and finish, and that ordinary repair needs no permit.

A small device on the casing and a strike in the jamb, filed with cut sheets, fits that description.

“The hardware is the cost; installation is extra.”

Many guides lead with the price of a reader or a lock. In a Midwood house the labor is often the larger part.

Repairing a tired jamb, rehanging a door that has dropped, finding a route from a side door to the basement and making the door close and latch every time all take longer than mounting a reader.

Our Brooklyn list prices the installed door, labor included, with no monthly contract required for a local system.

Field Notes: Count the Doors Nobody Mentions

This describes how we approach a Midwood house, not a particular customer. On the phone, owners describe one or two doors: the front and the side. On the walk around the house we count every opening that leads inside. The porch door, the kitchen door off the driveway, the cellar stair at the back, the bulkhead over it, the basement window well that was turned into a door when the basement was finished, the garage's people door and, in a two-family, the tenant's entrance. Most houses have more doors than the owner listed, and the forgotten ones are usually the ones with the weakest locks.

Then we ask who has keys to each, and the answer for the forgotten doors is often "nobody knows." That count shapes the plan more than any product sheet. The doors people use get readers or keypads; the doors nobody should use get contacts and proper locks; and every door ends up on one list that the owner controls.

FAQ

Midwood Access Control FAQ

Does fitting an electric strike weaken the wooden jamb of an old Midwood house door?
+

Not if the jamb is sound and the strike is fitted properly. The strike sits in a pocket cut where the old strike plate was, and its faceplate is screwed through the jamb into the framing behind. Where the wood is soft or split from a century of locks, we splice in hardwood or use a strike with a longer reinforcing plate first, so the finished door is stronger than before.

Which side of the door does the reader go on, and does the door swing matter?
+

The reader usually goes on the latch side, where your hand reaches for the handle, at a comfortable height for everyone in the house. The swing matters for the lock rather than the reader: an inswinging house door takes a strike in the jamb, while an outswinging door needs a strike or lock rated for it. We check both on the visit.

Can the indoor answering screen go in the kitchen at the back of a detached house, far from the porch door?
+

Yes. The indoor screen connects to the system by cable or by the house network, not to the door directly, so it can go wherever the family spends its time, and a second screen can go upstairs. Calls can also ring phones, so a ring at the porch is answered from the kitchen, the bedroom or the shop.

How does the cable get from the porch door to the basement when the porch is a solid concrete slab?
+

Usually around the slab rather than through it. The cable can drop inside the house wall beside the door, follow the porch's side wall in a small conduit to a basement window, or run along the underside of the porch ceiling into the house. Coring the slab is the last option, and we price the route we would actually use.

If the controller ever has to be replaced, do we lose everyone's codes and fobs?
+

No, if the system is set up the way we leave it. Cloud-managed systems keep the user list online and load it into a replacement controller, and local systems are backed up at handover with a copy kept by the owner. Can the side door stay unlocked for ten minutes while we unload the car, then lock by itself?
+

Yes. A code or a button inside can hold the strike unlocked for a set time, such as ten minutes, after which it locks again on its own, and the door contact can still alert you if the door itself is left standing open. Nobody has to remember to lock up when the last bag is in.

How far from the door can the controller be?
+

Far enough for any Midwood house or co-op. A controller can sit a long way from the doors it runs, limited mostly by the cable type and the power the lock draws, and in practice the basement of a house or the cellar of an apartment building is always within range. For a garage at the back of a deep lot we check the run on the visit.

Will we need a different doorknob or lever when the strike goes in?
+

Usually not. An electric strike works with the latch you already have, so the knob or lever stays. What matters is that the outside handle is locked, so the strike decides who gets in, and that the inside handle always turns. If the current set is a passage set that never locks, we replace it with an entry or storeroom function set.

Can a door station be mounted on a wooden porch column instead of the house wall?
+

Often, if the column is solid and the cable can reach it. A station on a column beside the steps meets visitors before they reach the door and keeps holes off the house front. Hollow or decorative columns need a mounting plate fixed to the structure inside, and the cable usually comes down the column and under the porch floor.

Can we keep an emergency code for a volunteer ambulance corps that may be called for a parent who lives alone?
+

Yes. A code with no expiry can be created for that purpose, logged by name every time it is used and sent to the family's phones when it is, so you know if the door was opened for an emergency. Some families prefer a key lockbox as well, in case the responders do not have the code. We set up whichever the family chooses.
