The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Brooklyn Heights is a small place with a lot of front doors. It covers about a third of a square mile between Old Fulton Street, Cadman Plaza West, Atlantic Avenue and the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway along the East River, and about 25,000 people lived there at the 2020 census. Its streets were laid out early: after 1823 the land was divided into lots of 25 by 100 feet, and the number of houses grew from seven in 1807 to more than 600 by 1860. By 1890 it was almost completely built. The result, still standing, is block after block of narrow row houses with a stoop, a parlor door at the top and a second door under the stoop, interrupted by churches, carriage houses on back lanes and, later, apartment buildings and co-ops.

Those houses did not stay single-family. Once the Brooklyn Bridge opened in 1883 and the subway arrived in 1908, many of the big houses were divided into apartments or run as rooming houses. That history is why so many doorways in the Heights carry three, four or six bell buttons that were added one at a time over a century, often wired by different people in different decades to whatever transformer was handy in the cellar. A house that was built for one family and its servants now has several households sharing one parlor door, and each of them needs to know who is ringing before letting them in.

The third layer is the district itself. When the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Brooklyn Heights Historic District on November 23, 1965, it brought almost every one of those doorways under its review. The Commission's doorway guidelines list "installing intercom boxes" among the work that needs its approval, and its permit guidebook has a section devoted to installing light fixtures and intercom panels. So a buzzer here is not only an electrical question. It is also a question of where on a nineteenth-century doorway a modern station can sit, how big it can be and how its cable gets inside without marks on carved stone or cast iron.

The fourth reason is security at the first door. In August 2024 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that vandals got into the vestibule of a pre-war co-op on Orange Street at about three in the morning and that one of them smashed the glass in the lobby door with a hammer. Package theft from lobbies and vestibules is a recurring worry in buildings without a doorman, and Brick Underground's advice to residents of those buildings is blunt: "don't buzz anyone you don't know into the building. Getting past that first door is all a thief needs." Advice like that only works if every household can actually see or hear who is outside, and if the door they release locks again by itself as soon as the visitor is through.

Put those together and the typical Heights buzzer job looks different from one in a newer neighborhood. It is usually a small number of households, a doorway that has to stay handsome, an old house with plaster walls and a cellar full of abandoned wire, and an owner or board that wants the work done once, done neatly and approved where approval is needed. Most of what we install here is a first proper talk-back or video system for a divided house, a replacement for a co-op's aging lobby system, or a compact station for a single-family house whose owners are tired of walking down three flights every time the bell rings.

Six facts that shape a Brooklyn Heights buzzer installation

  • It is the city's first historic district. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated it on November 23, 1965, and its doorway guidelines name intercom boxes among the work that needs the Commission's approval.
  • One panel per doorway is the guidebook's pattern. The Commission's guidance allows light fixtures within or beside the door, up to two, plus one intercom panel, and asks that conduit be concealed through plain masonry with only about ten inches left visible, with staff approval.
  • The primary facade is the last resort. The guidebook says this kind of equipment cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, and the 2018 rules amendments ask for objects to be as small as possible.
  • The lots are narrow. After 1823 the land was cut into 25-by-100-foot lots, so a typical house has one narrow doorway, a high stoop and little wall to spare beside the door.
  • Many houses hold several households. After 1883 and 1908 many mansions were divided into apartments or rooming houses, which is why doorways carry rows of buttons added over decades.
  • Doors that do not close are common citywide. HPD's FY25 Self-Closing Door Proactive Program report counted 2,937 self-closing door violations written by HPD, and a buzzer is only as good as the door it releases.

Install or Repair in Brooklyn Heights

Need Repair in Brooklyn Heights? When One Part Is the Fix and When a Doorway Needs a New System

Plenty of installation calls from the Heights begin as repair calls. A tenant on the fourth floor of a divided house cannot buzz anyone in, the board of a co-op hears that half the handsets crackle, or the owner of a single-family house notices the bell has gone silent. Some of these are genuine one-part repairs: a tired transformer in the cellar, a strike whose screws have pulled out of an old wooden frame, a handset with a broken cord, a bell button that has corroded behind its brass plate. We price those as repairs and say so.

The pattern changes when the faults spread. When several floors fail within months of each other, when the trouble comes and goes with the weather, or when the system is old enough that its parts are no longer made, the cause is usually the cable from the cellar to the floors or the system as a whole. A repair then lasts a season. The answer is to meter every pair, keep the ones that test sound, replace the ones that do not, and put one system with one power supply on the whole house. One complaint on Yelp about intercom work near the Heights described an electrician "disconnecting, dismantling and throwing out a perfectly working exterior intercom panel." We would rather test first and tell you honestly that the panel on your doorway is fine, if it is, than remove a working part from a landmarked front. If a repair is all your doorway needs, our Brooklyn door buzzer team will price it that way.

Converted and newer buildings

Row houses kept as one home: hearing the stoop from the top floor

The simplest Heights job is also one of the most requested: a single family in a four- or five-story house, a bell button at the parlor door, and a bell or chime that only the people on the parlor floor can hear. The answer is a compact station at the door and indoor stations on the floors the family actually lives on, usually the kitchen floor, the parlor floor and the top bedroom floor, with phone answering added if they want to answer from upstairs without a station or from outside the house.

The work lives in the doorway and the cellar. The station has to sit where the Commission will accept it, which in practice means within the doorway, on plain surfaces, and as small as it can be while still having a camera and a speaker. The cable has to get from that station into the house through plain masonry rather than carved stone, which often means a short, careful hole behind the station itself, and then find its way down to the cellar and up to each floor inside the walls. In an old plaster house, that route is planned before anything is drilled, floor by floor, with the owner.

The release goes in the parlor door's frame. Many of these doors are original, tall and heavy, and many frames have been patched after old lock changes. A fail-secure electric strike needs solid wood around it and a door that drops its latch into the keeper every time, so the door is checked before the strike is chosen, and any frame repair is named on the quote.

Houses split into flats: one doorway, several households, one system

When a house was divided long ago, the doorway usually tells the story: a bell button for each apartment, some brass, some plastic, some added with a strip of tape for the label. Behind them, each button may have its own pair of wires running to its apartment, or several may share a cable that was spliced in the cellar decades ago. Before we promise anything, every pair is traced and metered from the cellar to each apartment, because the condition of those wires decides whether a new system can ride on them or needs new cable.

A divided house also has more people with an opinion. The owner may live on the parlor and garden floors and rent the floors above, or may live elsewhere and rely on a managing agent. Tenants may want a wall station, a phone app or both. A two-wire system can carry audio and video to each apartment over one pair and still let individual apartments add phone answering, which suits a house where the people upstairs range from students to long-time residents. We set out the options for the owner in one written quote, with each household's station listed so it is clear what everyone gets.

Prewar co-op lobbies: the board, the riser and the glass inner door

The co-op buildings on the main residential streets usually have the layout the city's rules were written around: a street door into a vestibule with mailboxes, a glass inner door into the lobby, and an elevator and stair beyond. The rule 1 RCNY §42-01 allows the street door into the vestibule not to self-lock when the inner door from the vestibule self-closes and self-locks, which often decides where the release goes and keeps the mailboxes reachable for the carrier. The Orange Street incident in 2024, when the inner door's glass was smashed, is a reminder that the inner door takes the abuse in such buildings, and its frame, glass and closer deserve attention alongside the electronics.

The riser is the other half. Many of these buildings already have a multi-pair cable running up a closet stack or the stair hall to every apartment. If its pairs meter sound, a new system can use them, which keeps cost and disruption down. Boards need to see that test result before they vote, along with a clear statement of what the building pays for, what each shareholder's apartment station involves, and whether any part of the system carries a monthly fee.

Converted carriage houses: finding the door on a back lane

City Lore's history of Grace Court Alley describes a one-block dead end off Hicks Street lined with mid-nineteenth-century carriage houses, two and three stories tall, built to serve the mansions on Joralemon and Remsen Streets, later turned into garages and then homes, most still with their hay cranes. Houses like these have their own buzzer problems. The old carriage opening is often now a large window or a garage door, the human door may be small and set to one side, and the walls are thick brick with no cellar under some of them.

The station goes where a visitor will look for it and where the Commission will accept it, which is usually beside the human door rather than across the old carriage opening. Power and cable come from inside the house by the shortest concealed route, and where there is no cellar, the power supply is mounted in a closet or utility space the owner can reach. If the garage door is used by the household every day, its own opener stays separate; the buzzer opens the door people walk through.

Late Federal houses in the north: wood doorways and very little wall

The oldest houses in the Heights, mostly in the northern blocks, are smaller and lower than the brownstones that followed. Their doorways tend to be wood rather than stone, with narrow casings and sometimes a fanlight above. There is often no plain masonry close to the door at all. That pushes the station toward the slimmest equipment we install, mounted on the casing or a plain board within the doorway, with its cable dropping straight through the floor behind it rather than across the front.

Converted and newer buildings: long runs and many apartments

The Hotel St. George complex, built in stages from 1885 to 1930 and once the city's largest hotel, has held co-ops since 1984 and student housing since the late 1990s. New York YIMBY reported in March 2026 that the former Jehovah's Witness headquarters at 25–30 Columbia Heights is planned as 661 rentals, with a 2029 completion target. Buildings of this kind need a directory or code panel, cable sized for long distances, and a clear split between which doors serve which residents. They are planned from drawings with the building's own team, and the doorway on a district building still follows the Commission's rules.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What Gets Checked on a Brooklyn Heights Stoop Before a Station Is Chosen

At a Brooklyn Heights house our technician does four things before anyone talks about brands. They take less than an hour, and between them they decide most of the price and the Landmarks plan.

First, the doorway itself. The technician photographs the parlor doorway straight on and from the sidewalk, and notes what each surface is: carved stone, plain stone, brick, wood casing, cast-iron railing, glass sidelight. The Commission's guidance treats these differently, and so do we. Plain masonry or a plain wood casing can take a station and a concealed cable hole; carved stone and cast iron are left alone.

Second, the doors. The street door is opened fully and let go. It should close by itself, latch on the first try and not bounce. The same is done with the vestibule door. A door that has to be pulled shut defeats any buzzer, so its closer, hinges and latch are noted before any electronics.

Third, the households. The technician counts buttons, mailboxes and apartment doors, and asks who actually lives where, because a house listed as three apartments may have a garden unit or a top floor shared by two people.

Fourth, the cellar. The technician finds the old transformer and the bell wiring, which in many Heights cellars sits among abandoned runs from earlier systems, phone lines and cable television. Each pair to each floor is metered. Sound pairs are reused; failed ones are replaced; the quote says which.

The Historic District Doorway

How the Landmarks Rules Shape a Buzzer on a Brooklyn Heights Doorway

Most of what makes a buzzer job in the Heights different from one a mile away comes down to a handful of sentences in the Landmarks Preservation Commission's documents. We read them closely because every station we mount on a district doorway has to live with them, and because owners who know them in advance make better choices about equipment.

What the Commission's documents actually say

The Commission's guidelines for restoring architectural features list "installing intercom boxes" under the work for which its approval is required. Its Permit Guidebook, Chapter 11, on health, safety and utility equipment, includes a section on installing light fixtures and intercom panels. That guidance says equipment of this kind cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists, that it should be put in discreet locations, that it should not be fastened through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and that conduit should be concealed through plain masonry, with only about ten inches left visible and only with staff approval. At a doorway, it allows light fixtures within or beside the door, up to two, plus one intercom panel. Chapter 8 of the guidebook, on yards and areaways, names Brooklyn Heights and points intercom installations back to Chapter 11.

The Commission's 2018 rules amendments addressed the same family of objects. They describe lighting, security cameras and intercom panels as objects common for security, and the proposed text would prohibit installing many of them on a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative or the location is required by an agency or another entity. Where they are installed, the work has to minimize or avoid damage to significant architectural features, and the object has to be as small as possible.

What that means for the station we choose

For a typical Heights house with one entrance on the street, the parlor doorway is the only place a visitor can ring, which is exactly the situation the "no other option" language anticipates. The station then goes within the doorway, on the plainest surface available, at a size that does what the household needs and no more. A three-household house does not need a station sized for twenty; a family that wants video does not need a panel with a keypad they will never use. Choosing the smallest station that still has a readable label for each household, a speaker and, if wanted, a camera is both good design and the best preparation for a filing.

The single-panel pattern matters too. If a doorway already has a doorbell camera, an old bell plate and a newer button, they should be consolidated into one station rather than added to. Old plates that no longer work come off, and their holes are filled, when the new station goes up.

What that means for the cable

The cable is where careless jobs on district houses show. A run of surface conduit up the brownstone, or a cable stapled across the brick to a hole by the cellar window, is exactly what the guidance is trying to prevent. We plan the route so the cable leaves the back of the station and enters the house directly through plain masonry or the door frame, and then travels inside the house to the cellar and the floors. Where the station has to sit on wood, the cable usually drops straight through the floor of the vestibule or the frame behind it.

Who files, and when the work happens

The Commission's permits are issued to the owner of the building, and we prepare what an owner or their architect or expediter needs for the doorway: the station's dimensions and finish, photos showing where it goes, a description of how it is fastened and how the cable gets inside. Installation on the doorway happens after the approval the building needs is in hand. Interior work is a different matter; the Commission offers an expedited Certificate of No Effect for specific kinds of interior work in historic-district buildings, which it says can be issued within three days, and owners with interior Buildings Department filings should ask whether that path applies to them. None of this is legal advice for a particular building; the Commission's own staff are the final word on what a given doorway needs.

The Door Comes First

A Heights Parlor or Vestibule Door Has to Close, Latch and Open From Inside Before It Gets a Release

Every entry system has two halves. One is electronic: the station, the indoor stations, the power supply and the cable. The other is mechanical: the door, its hinges, its closer, its latch and the release cut into its frame. Owners shop for the first half, but most failures at old doors are in the second, and in Brooklyn Heights the doors are old.

What a door has to do

The rule 1 RCNY §42-01 sets out what an intercom installation in a multiple dwelling of eight or more apartments must do. Entrances must have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors, and every self-locking door must be readily openable from the inside without keys. The bell and intercom must be at the main entrance so a person can reach the door when the unlocking buzzer sounds. Smaller houses are not bound by those words, but they describe a sound door for any building, and we treat them as the standard. Local Law 111 of 2018 added a duty for doors to interior corridors and stairs in residential buildings to be self-closing, and HPD's FY25 proactive program report counted 2,937 self-closing door violations, which shows how often doors across the city fail to close by themselves.

What happens on installation day

Before a release is cut into a frame, the door has to swing freely, close from any position and latch without help. Original parlor doors are tall and heavy; a closer set too weak lets them drift, and one set too hard makes a buzzed-in visitor think the door is still locked. Doors that have dropped on their hinges miss their keeper. Swelling in humid weather can make an old wood door stick. Each of these is corrected before the release goes in, so the electronics have a door they can rely on.

We use fail-secure electric strikes on street and vestibule doors. Allegion's guide explains the distinction: fail-safe products unlock when power is removed and fail-secure products stay locked, and with an electric strike a person inside can always leave by turning the lever whichever type is fitted. Allegion also notes that electromagnetic locks are only available fail-safe. A fail-secure strike keeps a Heights house locked during a power cut without ever trapping anyone inside, which is the right combination for a residential door.

The glass inner door

The inner vestibule door in many co-op buildings and some divided houses has a large glass panel. That is the door the Orange Street vandals smashed in 2024. A release can go into its frame like any other, but the frame must be sound, the glass must be secure in it, and the closer must pull it shut against the draft of the street door opening behind it. When a board is replacing the entry system, it is worth looking at that door at the same time, since an inner door that fails leaves the lobby open however good the panel outside is.

Rules and Responsibilities

Which Brooklyn Heights Buildings Must Have a Buzzer, Who Keeps It Working and Who Approves the Doorway

  • Eight or more apartments is the main threshold. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a and 1 RCNY §42-01 require self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system at the main entrance of class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968.
  • Most Heights buildings are older than 1968. For buildings put up before 1968, §50-a ties the duty to a written request or consent from tenants occupying a majority of the apartments. Since nearly all of the Heights was built by 1890, this is the provision that matters for many divided houses and prewar apartment buildings here.
  • Sources disagree about small buildings. One compliance guide says buildings with fewer than eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement though the self-closing, self-locking door requirement still applies; another vendor says the duty reaches most buildings of three or more units. The 8-apartment figure is the one written in §50-a; if your building is in doubt, ask HPD or your lawyer.
  • A system that exists must be kept working. The owner's general duty to keep the building in good repair covers an intercom once it is installed, and one compliance guide classes a non-working buzzer as a class B hazardous violation. HPD's page lists 90 days to correct a class A violation, 30 days for a class B, and 14 days for a self-closing door violation.
  • The Landmarks Commission approves the doorway. Its guidelines list intercom boxes among the work needing approval, and its guidebook sets out where panels and cable may go on a district building.
  • Permits from the Buildings Department are disputed. One installer says low-voltage work under 50 volts no longer needs an electrical permit under the 2025 Electrical Code; another says new intercom installations need DOB permits; a third says only line-voltage work, such as a new circuit for a strike or a magnetic lock, needs an electrical permit by a licensed electrician. We confirm what each job needs before it starts and say so on the quote.
  • Doors to halls and stairs must close by themselves. Local Law 111 of 2018 and HPD's self-closing door rule apply alongside the intercom rules, and a release must never stop a door from closing.

None of this is legal advice for a particular building. HPD, the Buildings Department, the Landmarks Commission or your own lawyer can say which rules apply to yours. What we do is install a system that meets those rules where they apply and leave written proof of what was installed and tested.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

The historic district and the doorway

The Landmarks Commission wants security objects kept as small as possible. How small can a station for a three-household Heights house really be?

Smaller than most people expect. A station for three households needs three labeled buttons, a speaker and a microphone, and, if the residents want to see the visitor, a camera. Slim surface-mounted stations that stack those in a narrow vertical strip are made for exactly this, and they fit on a door casing or a narrow pier of plain stone where a wide square panel would spill onto carved detail. The width of the plain surface beside your door is usually the number that decides the model.

What adds size is features nobody uses: a keypad for a house where everyone has keys, a large screen at the door, a card reader. We leave those off unless the household asks, because each one makes the station bigger and harder to approve. Where a house later adds a household, a station with one spare button position is often the best compromise between small now and useful later, and we discuss that before ordering.

Our Hicks Street house has one entrance, on the street front. The guidebook keeps equipment off a primary facade unless there is no other option. Where does that leave our buzzer?

In the doorway, placed with care. The guidance is written to push equipment to side and rear walls, areaways and other discreet spots when they exist. On a row house with a single street entrance, a visitor has nowhere else to ring, so the doorway is the option that remains. The work then turns to how discreetly it can be done there: within the door opening rather than on the wall beside it, on a plain surface, at the smallest size that works.

Sometimes there is a better spot. A deep vestibule whose outer doors stay open by day can hold the station on its inside wall, out of view from the street. An areaway gate with a plain post can serve a garden-floor household. We look at each of those at the walkthrough and show the owner which ones are practical for their visitors as well as for the Commission.

The house next door has a large black video panel that went up years ago. Does that mean the Commission will approve something similar on ours?

Not necessarily. A neighbor's panel may predate the current guidance, may have been approved for reasons specific to that house, or may never have been approved at all. The Commission reviews each building, and its present guidance asks for objects to be as small as possible and kept off decorative surfaces, so a large panel on the next stoop is not a precedent to rely on.

It is more useful as a lesson in what to avoid. If the neighbor's panel covers part of the stone surround or has surface conduit running from it, those are exactly the features a filing for your house should not repeat. We plan your station from the current rules and your doorway, not from what is already on the block.

Old systems in old buildings

The lobby panel and handsets in our Remsen Street co-op carry the Lee-Dan name. Does a system from that era limit what can replace it?

It limits what can be repaired more than what can be installed. Older audio systems of that kind used their own wiring arrangements, and parts for them can be hard to find, which is why so many buildings end up patching one handset at a time. A replacement does not have to match the old make. What carries over is the cable, if it tests well, and often the location of the lobby panel and the strike.

The test is the important part. Before your board decides, we meter every pair from the lobby and the cellar to each apartment. Aiphone's guidance on upgrades says that if existing wires are in good condition they can be reused, and its GT Series is designed for retrofit projects on existing wire. If the riser meters sound, a two-wire video system can usually run on it; if several lines fail, the quote shows which ones and what new cable they need.

Before anyone removes the panel on our stoop, how do we find out whether it actually works?

By testing it as a part of the whole system rather than judging it by its age. The panel's buttons, speaker and microphone can be checked on their own, the release circuit can be checked separately, and the cable and power supply in the cellar can be measured. A panel that tests well can stay while the stations upstairs, the power supply or the strike are changed, if those are what failed.

We say that plainly because it saves owners money and saves the doorway from new holes. On a district house, keeping a working station that is already approved, or already sits well on the doorway, can also avoid a new filing. Where the panel is the problem, the test sheet shows it, and the replacement is planned around the same opening where possible.

Our house was a rooming house for decades before it became four apartments, and some old room bells were never disconnected. Could that be why visitors ring the wrong flat?

It can. When a house is divided and re-divided, old buttons are sometimes rewired to new apartments, splices are made in the cellar to save running new wire, and abandoned bells stay connected to live circuits. The result is calls that ring in two places, buttons that ring the wrong floor and faint buzzes in rooms nobody uses. These faults are confusing because each button seems to work some of the time.

The cure is to trace every pair from the doorway to the cellar to each apartment, disconnect anything that leads nowhere, and label both ends of the runs that are kept. That tracing is part of every quote we give for a divided house, and the new directory is written from it, so each button reaches only the household named on it.

Co-ops, the St. George and bigger buildings

Our co-op apartment is in the old Hotel St. George complex, where other parts of the block house students. Who decides how our section's entry works, and can it be kept separate?

Your co-op's board, or whoever controls your section's entrance, decides; the student housing in other parts of the complex is run by its own operators. Since the complex has been divided into co-ops since 1984 and dormitories since the late 1990s, each part generally has its own doors and its own systems, and an entry system for your section can be planned for your residents alone.

What matters in a large, shared block is keeping the doors straight. Each entrance your residents use needs to call only your apartments, service doors need their own locks and releases, and any connection to a shared door has to be agreed with the other party. We plan from your board's drawings and a walk of every door, and the quote lists which doors are in scope and which belong to someone else.

After vandals smashed the glass of a co-op's inner door on Orange Street in 2024, our board is asking whether a video station would have stopped it or only recorded it.

Honestly, mostly recorded it. A station's camera at the street door shows who rang and, on some systems, who came in, which is useful afterward, but people determined to damage a door at three in the morning are not usually deterred by a lens. What reduces that kind of risk is the door itself: a sound frame, glass held securely, a closer that shuts it reliably and a lock that is not easy to slip.

Where video earns its place is the everyday decision. Residents who can see the person at the street door are less likely to release it for a stranger, and fewer strangers in the vestibule means fewer chances for anyone to reach the inner door. If your board also wants cameras in the vestibule or the lobby, we can add them on the same visit and keep them on their own recorder.

The new riser cable in our co-op runs entirely inside the building. Does interior intercom work in a district building go to the Landmarks Commission at all?

The Commission's attention is mainly on the exterior and the doorway, where its guidebook addresses intercom panels and conduit. Interior work is treated differently. The Commission offers an expedited Certificate of No Effect for specific kinds of interior work in historic-district buildings, which it says can be issued within three days, and that path is typically tied to interior work that also needs a Buildings Department permit.

For your board, the practical step is to treat the lobby panel and anything on the facade as Commission work, and to ask the managing agent or the building's expediter whether the interior portion needs any filing in your case. We provide the drawings of the cable route and equipment either way, so whatever is filed matches what is installed.

Getting the work done

The installers we talked to disagree about whether a Brooklyn Heights buzzer needs a Buildings Department permit. Who is right?

The disagreement is real, and the honest answer is that it depends on what the job involves. One installer's page says that since the 2025 Electrical Code took effect, an electrical permit is not required for low-voltage work under 50 volts. Another says new intercom installations need DOB permits and that electrical work must be done by a licensed electrician. A third takes the middle view that line-voltage wiring, such as a new circuit for a strike or a magnetic lock, needs an electrical permit.

Most buzzer systems run on low voltage from a plug-in or hard-wired power supply, and the wiring we install is low-voltage work done under our state license. Where a job needs a new line-voltage circuit, that part is done by a licensed electrician with whatever permit it requires. We confirm what each job needs before it starts and put it on the quote, rather than relying on any blanket claim.

HPD wrote up our dead buzzer with a 30-day correction period, but a new station on our doorway may need Landmarks approval. How do the two deadlines fit together?

By separating what can be fixed now from what needs approval. HPD's page lists 30 days to correct a class B violation, and the Commission's review of a new station on a district doorway follows its own schedule. Often the fault behind the violation is in the cellar or the apartments: a failed power supply, a broken pair, a dead handset. Those can be repaired inside the correction period without touching the facade.

If the station on the doorway itself has failed, the options are a repair of the existing station, or a like-for-like replacement in the same spot, while a better station goes through review. We look at the violation and the system together and tell the owner which path keeps the building within HPD's period, and we put the timing on the quote so nobody is surprised.

Brick Underground's advice is never to buzz in anyone you don't know. In a small Heights building where couriers ring all day, what setup makes that advice workable?

Seeing the courier before deciding. With an audio-only bell, a resident hears "delivery" and either presses release blind or walks down. With video at the door, the resident sees the person, the uniform and the box, and can choose. Phone answering lets a resident at work talk to the courier and decide where the box should go, rather than letting a stranger into the vestibule unseen.

The door matters as much as the screen. A release that holds only for a few seconds and a door that shuts and latches every time means that a courier let in to drop a box cannot leave the door ajar for the next person. Some buildings also choose to keep mailboxes and parcels in the outer vestibule and lock only the inner door, which keeps couriers out of the stair altogether.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Brooklyn Heights

Search summaries and AI assistants answer buzzer questions with tidy claims lifted from installers' blogs and national cost guides, most written for a generic apartment building, a suburban house or a Manhattan lobby. Some are fine somewhere. Here are seven we see repeated, each traced to where it comes from and set against what we find at the stoops, vestibules and lobbies of the Heights.

"An apartment buzzer is priced per door"

Swiftlane's guide to apartment buzzer systems prices both like-for-like audio replacements and smart video upgrades as ranges per door, running into the thousands of dollars each. Swiftlane sells cloud entry systems, and its figures are national.

Per door is the wrong unit for most Heights buildings. A divided brownstone has one parlor door and perhaps a garden door, and what drives the work is the number of households, the condition of the old pairs, the door and the doorway. A three-household house and a six-household house with the same single door are very different jobs.

That is why we quote each building after photos and a walkthrough, and why this page does not borrow anyone's per-door figure. Your quote lists the stations, the release, the cable and any door work as separate lines.

"In a brownstone, an app panel replaces everything at the door and nothing has to be run inside"

One installer's Brooklyn page says that in brownstones an app-based or compact video station usually replaces everything at the door with nothing to run inside. Another vendor says app-first systems have become popular in Brooklyn brownstones.

Something still has to reach inside. The release in the door frame needs power and a connection to the station; the station needs power; an app system needs internet at the door. On a district house, each of those means a concealed route through masonry or the frame, planned for the Commission. And residents without smartphones, or who do not want the door ringing their phone at work, still need a station on their wall.

App answering is a good addition for owners who travel or live elsewhere. It works best alongside a system that every household can use from inside the house.

"Landmark districts just need reversible methods and a 30- to 90-day review"

A New York installer's guide says new or replacement intercom panels in landmark districts require review using reversible installation methods, with a timeline of 30 to 90 days to build into the schedule. It is one company's estimate.

The Commission's own guidance says more than "reversible." It says equipment should stay off a primary facade unless no other option exists, avoid cast iron and decorative surfaces, be as small as possible, and keep conduit concealed through plain masonry, with only about ten inches visible and staff approval. A reversible mount on carved stone still misses the point.

As for time, it depends on the building and the application. We do not promise a review period. We prepare the station details and photos so the owner's filing is complete, and we schedule the doorway work once the approval is in hand.

"Any building with three or more units must have an intercom"

A security vendor's compliance page says the duty covers most residential buildings with three or more units. Other sources set it at eight apartments.

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a sets the requirement for class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and ties the duty for older buildings to a written request or consent from a majority of tenants. Since almost all of the Heights was built by 1890, that pre-1968 provision is the one many buildings here fall under.

Small buildings are not off the hook entirely: once a system exists, it has to be kept working, and self-closing door duties apply regardless. If your building is near the line, HPD or a lawyer can say which rule applies.

"A magnetic lock and an electric strike do the same job"

Summaries often treat the two as interchangeable door releases. A New York installer's page puts it more carefully: magnetic locks release when power is cut, while strikes come in versions that stay locked or release, and the right one depends on the door and its egress rules.

Allegion's guide adds that electromagnetic locks are only available fail-safe, so a magnetic lock on a parlor door unlocks whenever power is lost. It also needs release devices, signage and often a fire alarm connection under the building code. A fail-secure strike keeps the house locked in a blackout while the inside lever always lets people out.

On almost every residential door in the Heights, a strike is the right choice. A magnetic lock is a last resort, priced with its full package and named on the quote before anyone agrees to it.

"A buzzer never needs a permit" or "a buzzer always needs a DOB permit"

Both appear online. One installer says that under the 2025 Electrical Code an electrical permit is not required for low-voltage work under 50 volts, and asks readers to check with DOB. Another says new intercom installations need DOB permits and licensed electricians.

A blanket answer either way is risky. Low-voltage buzzer wiring is a different thing from a new 120-volt circuit, and fire alarm connections bring their own rules. The middle view, that line-voltage work needs an electrical permit, matches how most jobs are actually divided.

We check what each job needs before work starts and say so in writing. Where a licensed electrician is needed for a circuit, that is planned and named on the quote.

"Video can always run on the old intercom wire"

Summaries repeat that a video upgrade can use the wiring already in the walls. Aiphone's own upgrade guidance is more measured: if the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused. On r/accesscontrol, an installer suggested a path that would "keep the existing GT system, repair the damaged cable, and only upgrade those tenants that desire app connectivity."

The condition is the whole question. In a Heights house whose wiring was spliced and re-spliced through decades of division, some pairs are fine and some are not. A two-wire video system on one failing pair will show it at once.

So we meter before we promise. The quote shows which pairs are reused and which need new cable, and if new cable is needed in a plaster house, the route is agreed with the owner floor by floor.

Our house sits on one of the narrow 25-foot lots and the vestibule is barely deeper than the doors swing. Is there room for a release on the inner door?
+

Usually yes, because an electric strike sits inside the door frame and takes no floor space at all. Can the station be fastened into mortar joints rather than drilled into the brownstone itself?
+

Where the doorway is brick or has mortar joints in the right place, fastening into the joints is often the better choice, because a mortar joint can be repointed later while a hole in stone is permanent. On a solid brownstone surround there may be no joint where the station needs to go, in which case we look first for a plain wood casing within the doorway. Our Montague Street shop's lease gives the landlord control of the storefront. Who arranges the Landmarks approval for a buzzer at our door?
+

The building owner, since the Commission's permits are issued for the building and your lease gives the landlord control of the facade. Our doorway was restored with Landmarks approval some years ago. Does that earlier approval cover a new buzzer?
+

Not unless the buzzer was part of the approved work. An approval covers what was described in that application, and the Commission's doorway guidelines list installing intercom boxes as work that needs approval in its own right. The earlier file is still useful: its drawings and photos show the doorway's materials and help us find a plain surface for the station. Ask the owner or the architect who handled the restoration for a copy before we prepare the station details.

A neighbor told us to unplug the transformer in the cellar and plug it back in whenever the buzzer quits. Is that a real fix?
+

It is a sign, not a fix. If restarting the power supply brings the buzzer back for a while, the supply may be failing, overheating or overloaded by old connections that were never removed, or a loose splice may be making intermittent contact. Each of those gets worse, and a power supply on an outlet that anyone can unplug is a problem in itself. We measure the supply and the circuits, and the quote says whether a new battery-backed supply on its own is enough or the wiring needs work too.

Some installers say any building with three or more apartments must have an intercom. Is that right for our divided brownstone?
+

That claim appears on one vendor's page, but Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a sets the requirement at class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968, and for older buildings ties it to a written request or consent from tenants occupying a majority of the apartments. A brownstone of three to six flats built in the nineteenth century usually falls outside the requirement to install one. Once a system is in, though, the owner is expected to keep it working, and HPD or your lawyer can confirm what applies to your house.
