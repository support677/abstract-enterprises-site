Most houses in Bath Beach hold more than one household. This page is about designing alarms for exactly those buildings.

The Neighborhood

Why Bath Beach Properties Need Alarm Installation

Bath Beach sits at the southwestern corner of Brooklyn on Gravesend Bay. Wikipedia places it across 86th Street from Bensonhurst and New Utrecht, across 14th Avenue from Dyker Beach Park and its golf course, and across Stillwell Avenue from Gravesend, and puts its area at about 480 acres. It was part of the old town of New Utrecht and began as a seaside retreat for well-off families, named after Bath in Somerset, England; it even had its own amusement park. The beach itself is gone. It was paved over in the middle of the twentieth century for Shore Parkway, now part of the Belt Parkway, and land dug out during that construction in the early 1940s was used to fill the southwestern part of the neighborhood.

What replaced the summer houses is what matters for an alarm. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as mainly working-class, with semi-attached houses and small apartment buildings. BrickUnderground’s 2022 guide lists condos, co-ops converted from rentals (usually four-story walk-ups), one- to three-family houses, a scattering of Victorian houses more than a century old, and newer three-story condo buildings put up on lots where an old house was knocked down. A broker quoted there put it simply: “There’s no vacant land.” The city’s own numbers make the point about households. In the Department of Finance’s 2022 neighborhood sales summary, Bath Beach recorded 74 sales of two-family houses against 19 one-family and 32 three-family houses. The 2024 summary shows the same pattern at lower volume: 44 two-family sales, 12 one-family and 17 three-family, with a median two-family price of $1,252,000.

A house with two households is a different alarm problem from a house with one. The owner downstairs leaves for work at seven; the tenant upstairs works nights. Someone’s mother lives on the parlor floor and never touches a keypad. The front door, the stair hall and the cellar belong to everyone, but each apartment belongs to one family. Off-the-shelf systems assume one household, one code and one “away” button, and in a Bath Beach two-family that assumption produces either an alarm nobody arms because it is always inconvenient for someone, or an alarm that goes off every time the other household comes home. Both outcomes are common reasons people tell us an old system has been sitting dead on the wall for years.

The local break-in record also points somewhere specific. In January 2024 Brooklyn Paper reported that four people got into a home near Bay 23rd Street and 86th Street by climbing into a second-story window, then robbed and stabbed the man inside. The same paper reported a few weeks later that police caught a man on 23rd Avenue near Cropsey Avenue after he was seen on live camera forcing his way into an apartment, and that he left through a front window. In February 2026 News 12 reported that five men forced their way into an apartment near Bay 37th Street and 86th Street and took $10,000 from a tenant’s bedroom dresser, then came back two days later. A 2018 CBS New York report on a serial burglar described him forcing open a back window of a Bath Beach home. Windows, including upstairs windows, and individual apartments inside multi-family houses are exactly where a one-keypad, front-door-only alarm is weakest.

Then there is the commercial side. Wikipedia notes that Bath Beach’s shops cluster at the Ceasar’s Bay Shopping Center at the foot of Bay Parkway, on 86th Street and on Bath Avenue. The D train runs on an elevated structure over 86th Street, built under the Dual Contracts of 1913 and opened in stages in 1916 and 1917, and the stores below it live with vibration and noise that can fool the wrong kind of sensor. The Brooklyn District Attorney’s office listed a grocery store on Bath Avenue among the businesses burglarized in a 2022 spree, and Brooklyn Paper reported $50,000 of tools taken from a shipping container near Shore Parkway and Eugene R. Romano Lane in December 2023. Small stores, garages and yards need alarms designed for how they are actually broken into, not a residential kit with a sticker on the window.

Most Bath Beach owners who call us about an alarm also ask about cameras before the visit is over. The two work best when they are planned together: the alarm decides that something is wrong and makes noise, and a camera tells you and the monitoring station what it was. We cover cameras on our Bath Beach security camera installation page and can wire both on the same day.

Keypad Beeping, Siren Stuck or the App Says “Offline”? Same-Day Alarm Repair in Bath Beach

An alarm that misbehaves in a two-family house is everyone’s problem at once: the keypad chirp comes through the floor, the siren wakes the tenant, and the neighbors on the other side of the party wall hear all of it. Most of these calls turn out to be one of a short list of faults, and most are fixed in one visit of an hour or two. A keypad that beeps every minute is usually a low battery in a wireless sensor or in the panel itself, or a trouble message for lost power or a lost communicator signal. An app that says the system is offline usually means the cellular or internet communicator has dropped, often after a router change or because an old 3G radio was never replaced. A siren that keeps coming back after it is silenced usually means a door contact that no longer lines up because the door has sagged or been replaced.

Call (347) 934-8335 and tell us the brand on the keypad, what the screen or lights show, and whether anyone else in the building uses the same system. Take the earliest arrival window. If the alarm is sounding now and you cannot silence it, most panels stop on their own when the programmed siren time runs out; City law requires a building burglar alarm to be able to shut itself off within fifteen minutes, so a siren that runs longer than that needs attention the same day. We service most common panels, including systems put in by other companies, and if the panel is too old to be worth repairing, we tell you before any billable work starts.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Bath Beach

Every job below starts from the same question: who uses this building, at what hours, and how would someone get in who should not? The services are listed separately because they are priced separately, but most Bath Beach houses end up with three or four of them on one panel.

Partitioned alarms for two- and three-family houses

One panel divided into separate areas, so the owner’s floor, the tenant’s floor and the shared hall and cellar each arm on their own schedule with their own codes. It is the single most useful thing we do on the Bay streets, and it is covered in detail further down this page.

Window protection on every reachable floor

Contacts on the windows a person can reach from the ground, a porch roof, a garage roof, an awning or a neighbor’s extension, plus glass-break detectors for rooms with several windows. Second-story windows over flat roofs are surveyed the same way as ground-floor ones.

Door contacts and keypads at the right doors

Front door, vestibule door, rear door to the yard, cellar door and the door at the top of the cellar stair. Keypads go where each household actually comes in, which in a two-family is often not the same door.

Store and mixed-use alarms

Rolling-gate contacts, front glass protection chosen to ignore the train on 86th Street, back-door and roof-hatch contacts, a panic button at the register and a separate area for the apartments above, so the shop and the tenants never share a code.

Takeovers and upgrades of old systems

Many Bath Beach houses already have wired contacts in the doors and windows from an alarm installed decades ago. Where the wiring tests good, a new panel and a cellular communicator bring it back to life for a fraction of the cost of starting over.

Monitoring, app control and verification

Self-monitoring through the app, or a central station plan with no long-term contract, plus a camera or two so an alarm can be verified before anyone is called. Each household gets its own app login and its own notification list.

Cellar water, freeze and smoke add-ons

Water sensors at the boiler and the lowest point of the cellar, a temperature sensor where pipes have frozen before, and smoke and carbon monoxide detectors that report through the same panel. Useful in any house; especially useful on low blocks near the bay.

Garage, yard and container alarms

Wireless contacts on overhead doors and container doors, outdoor motion detectors aimed so passing cars on the parkway service road do not trigger them, and a siren at the yard rather than inside the house.

Repairs and false-alarm cures

Low batteries, failed communicators, misaligned contacts, motion detectors aimed at radiators or curtains, and sirens that do not shut off on time. We service other installers’ equipment too.

Many Bath Beach two-families pair the alarm with a new door station at the shared front door, so each household can see who is ringing before anyone opens the vestibule. That work is on our Bath Beach intercom installation page.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Bath Beach’s Architecture

Bath Beach does not have one house type; it has five or six that sit side by side on the same Bay street. The sources describe them in general terms, and our descriptions below are about how each type behaves for an alarm, not a claim about any particular address.

Attached and semi-attached brick two-families

The most common sale in the neighborhood, according to the Department of Finance. Shared party walls, one front door or two side by side, a stair to the upper unit, and a cellar under the whole house. City sales records from the late 2000s list many two-family houses on the western Bay streets built around 1930.

Three-family houses

Thirty-two sold in 2022 and seventeen in 2024. Three households means three alarm areas plus a common one, and more people with codes. The design question is less about sensors than about who is allowed to arm and disarm the shared doors.

Surviving Victorian frame houses

BrickUnderground notes Victorian houses more than a century old still standing; a Brownstoner listing describes a detached three-family Victorian on Bay 23rd Street built around 1910. Wood frame, many windows, porches, often re-sided. Lots of openings to cover, and wireless sensors are often the cleaner answer.

Walk-up co-ops and small apartment buildings

Usually four stories, converted from rentals. Individual owners want an alarm inside their own apartment, which means wireless sensors, a small panel inside the unit, and no wiring in common areas without the board’s approval.

Newer three-story condo buildings

Built in the last couple of decades on the lots of demolished houses, typically six to twelve units. Modern framing and drywall make wiring easy during a renovation; inside each unit the alarm question looks like any apartment.

Stores with apartments above

On 86th Street, Bath Avenue and parts of Cropsey Avenue and Bay Parkway. A store at street level with a gate, glass and a back door, and one or more apartments upstairs reached by a separate street door. Two completely separate alarm areas, often two separate owners of the problem.

How we wire each type

Brick two- and three-families. The cellar is the friend of the installer here. The panel and its battery go high on a cellar wall, away from the boiler and above any water line the owner has seen, and cable runs along the joists to the front and rear walls and up inside them to the first-floor openings. Second-floor windows are reached through closets stacked above each other, along baseboards, or with wireless contacts where a run would mean opening finished plaster. We do not drill through party walls; the house next door is somebody else’s property, and a party wall is usually the hardest masonry in the building anyway. Keypads for the upper household go at the top of their stair, not at the shared front door, so the upper unit is armed and disarmed from inside its own space.

Frame Victorians. These houses have the most windows per room of anything in the neighborhood and the least predictable wall cavities, because a century of renovations has moved plumbing, added insulation and boxed in old chimneys. We survey the openings first and count them, then decide room by room between hidden wired contacts and small wireless ones. A glass-break detector in a parlor with five windows often does more than five separate contacts, provided the room is not full of heavy curtains. Porches matter: an enclosed porch is a room of glass in front of the real front door, and we usually treat the porch door as the first entry point and give it a short entry delay so people can reach the keypad.

Walk-up co-ops and condos. Inside one apartment the design is simple: a contact on the entry door, contacts on any window reachable from a fire escape or a roof, a motion detector in the main room and a keypad by the door. Everything is wireless, nothing touches the common hall, and the system leaves with the owner if they sell. If the building wants an alarm on its own front door or roof door, that is a separate job for the board, quoted separately.

Stores with apartments above. The store gets its own panel or its own area with its own code, and the apartment door upstairs gets nothing from the store’s system. Gate contacts go on the rolling gate, the front glass gets protection chosen for the street it faces, the back door gets a heavy-duty contact, and any roof hatch or skylight is contacted too, because roofs connect along a commercial row.

Two Households, One Panel

One House, Two Households: How a Partitioned Alarm Works in a Bath Beach Two-Family

Most alarm panels sold today can be split into two or more areas, which the manuals call partitions. Each area has its own sensors, its own user codes and its own armed or disarmed state, and the panel keeps track of them separately. Very few systems in Bath Beach two-families are actually set up that way. The usual installation treats the house as one space, and the result is predictable: whoever leaves last is supposed to arm the whole building, the upstairs tenant does not know the code or is not given it, and within a year nobody arms the system at all.

A partitioned design starts by drawing the house as it is used. In a typical two-family there are three areas. Area one is the owner’s apartment: its windows, its own inner door, and a motion detector in its main room. Area two is the tenant’s apartment, set up the same way. Area three is the common space: the street door, the vestibule, the stair hall, the cellar door and the rear yard door if both households use it. The common area is programmed to arm only when both apartments are armed, so nobody coming home late sets off the hall, and it disarms when either household enters a valid code at the front door.

Each household gets its own keypad inside its own apartment, its own app login and its own list of people who receive alerts. The owner does not receive notifications every time the tenant comes home, and the tenant’s codes do not work on the owner’s floor. If the upstairs apartment is empty between tenants, its area can stay armed for weeks without affecting anyone else. If a relative lives on one floor and does not use phones, that floor can be set to arm automatically at a fixed time, with a key fob as a backup.

This arrangement also answers a question that comes up whenever a landlord installs an alarm: who controls it, and who sees what? In our setups the tenant controls the tenant’s area and the owner cannot disarm it remotely. The owner retains administrator access to the panel itself, to add or remove users when a tenant moves out, but the event history for the tenant’s area goes to the tenant’s app. Cameras in common areas are a separate subject with their own rules, and we keep them out of apartments entirely.

Three-family houses add a third apartment area and usually a more complicated common area, because the third floor often has its own rear stair or a separate door. The panel does not care; the planning does. Before we quote a three-family, we draw the doors and stairs on a sheet of paper with the owner and mark which household uses each one, and that drawing becomes the zone list.

Partitioning costs very little extra in hardware: a second keypad, a few more sensors and an hour of programming. It does take thought, and it is the reason we ask on the phone how many households live in the building before we say anything about price.

Upstairs Windows

The Second-Floor Window Problem on Bath Beach Blocks

The reason this page spends so long on windows is the local record. Brooklyn Paper’s January 2024 report on the home invasion near Bay 23rd Street and 86th Street said the four intruders got in by climbing into a second-story window. Its February 2024 report from 23rd Avenue near Cropsey Avenue described a man escaping through a front window after he was seen on live camera breaking into an apartment. A 2016 Brooklyn Paper item from Bath Avenue between Bay 16th and Bay 17th streets described an intruder who kicked in an apartment’s front door and then a bedroom door upstairs. Earlier reports in the same 62nd Precinct column describe entry through open bathroom and rear bedroom windows in neighboring Bensonhurst. None of this is a crime statistic, and we do not present it as one; it is a pattern of how entry happened in the cases that were reported.

A common installation protects the front door, the back door and perhaps a motion detector in the downstairs hall, and nothing upstairs. That leaves the upper household, and the upper windows of the whole house, uncovered. We survey reachable openings on every floor. A window is reachable if a person can stand under it on something solid: a porch roof, the roof of a one-story rear extension, a garage roof, a store awning on a mixed-use building, a fence or a trash enclosure, or a neighbor’s roof on an attached row. On a block of attached houses, a flat roof shared along the row makes every rear upper window a ground-floor window for practical purposes.

Reachable windows get a contact that trips when the sash opens. Where a family keeps windows open a few inches in summer, we can set a second magnet so the window can be locked open for air without disarming. Rooms with several windows get a glass-break detector as well, because a contact does not notice glass being broken while the sash stays shut. Upstairs motion detectors go in the hallway, not the bedroom, so the household can arm the floor at night and still move around.

The other half of the window problem is time. A burglar who comes in through a second-floor window is usually inside an apartment before anyone downstairs notices. That is why the upper household needs its own keypad and its own alarm area, why the siren must be loud enough to be heard upstairs as well as down, and why we put a small interior sounder on each floor of a two-family. A siren in the cellar of a brick house is close to useless to someone sleeping on the second floor.

Near the Bay

Low Blocks Near Gravesend Bay: Keeping the Alarm Panel Dry

Bath Beach was a beach. The southwestern part of the neighborhood is land filled in during Shore Parkway construction in the early 1940s, according to Wikipedia, and Bensonhurst Park and the Ceasar’s Bay center sit on that fill. During Hurricane Sandy in 2012, the National Weather Service recorded storm surge reaching inland to Avenue X into Bensonhurst, Gravesend and Sheepshead Bay and shutting down the Belt Parkway. We have not found a source that measures flooding block by block in Bath Beach, and we do not claim one. Owners can look up their own address on the city’s flood maps.

What we can say is that the standard alarm installation puts the panel in the cellar, next to the electrical service, at the lowest point of the house. That is the wrong place in any house whose cellar has ever taken water. A panel that floods loses its battery and its communicator at exactly the moment the house is most exposed, often with the owners away. On low blocks near Cropsey Avenue and Shore Parkway, and in any house where the owner tells us water has come in, we mount the panel and communicator on the first floor or high on a cellar wall, run power from a circuit above flood level where the electrician allows, and put water sensors at the floor drain, the boiler and the lowest corner.

Water sensors are programmed as 24-hour zones, which means they report whether or not the alarm is armed, and they can be set to text both households and the owner’s plumber at the same time. A temperature sensor near pipes that have frozen before costs very little and warns you before a burst pipe does. None of this replaces flood insurance or a sump pump; it simply means the alarm tells you about the water instead of drowning in it.

Before the Visit

A Checklist for Bath Beach Owners and Landlords Before the Alarm Visit

The visit goes faster, and the quote is more accurate, when a few things are settled before the technician rings the bell. None of this is required; it simply saves a second trip.

Talk to the other household first

In a two- or three-family, the alarm affects everyone who lives there. Tell the tenant or relatives what you are planning, ask which door they actually use to come and go, and find out their hours. A night-shift worker upstairs changes the design: their area may need to be disarmed during the day while the owner’s is armed, and the shared hall needs to allow for someone coming in at six in the morning. If the other household does not want an alarm in their apartment at all, the common area and your own floor can still be protected, and their area can be added later with no change to the panel.

Find the old alarm, even if it is dead

Look for a grey or beige metal box in the cellar or a closet, a keypad near a door, small round or rectangular plugs in door frames, and a siren box outside. Photograph the panel’s label and the keypad. Old wired contacts recessed into doors and windows are invisible until you look closely at the frame edge, and finding them in advance tells us whether we are reviving a system or starting fresh, which is the biggest single swing in price.

Know your cellar’s water history

Has water ever come into the cellar, during Sandy or any heavy rain? Where did it stand, and for how long? The answer decides where the panel goes and whether water sensors are a nice extra or a necessity. If the house has a sump pump, tell us; a sensor next to it reports a failed pump before the floor is wet.

Walk the back of the row yourself

From your rear upper windows, look down. Is there a porch roof, an extension, a garage or a neighbor’s roof you could step onto? From the yard, look up. Count the windows on each floor that you could reach with only a trash can to stand on. That count, more than the number of rooms, is what sets the number of window sensors.

Decide how you want to be told

Self-monitoring through the app, or a central station that calls you and dispatches help? Who should get a text when the cellar sensor trips? Should the tenant’s alarm events go to you at all? These are decisions for the household, not the installer, and making them before the visit means the system is programmed correctly on the first day.

For stores: get the landlord’s word on wiring

A store tenant on 86th Street or Bath Avenue usually needs the building owner’s permission to run cable, mount a siren outside or drill into the gate housing. Ask before the visit. If the apartments above are on a separate electrical service, tell us, because the store panel should be powered from the store’s own circuit, not a shared one.

Have the insurance question answered

If you want an insurance discount, call your agent and ask what proof they need: a certificate of installation, a certificate of central station monitoring, or both. We provide the installation certificate; the monitoring company provides the other. Knowing in advance avoids installing a self-monitored system and then finding out the insurer wanted central station monitoring.

Common Fixes

Six Alarm Setups We Often Redo in Bath Beach Houses and Stores

These are patterns we correct regularly across southwest Brooklyn. They are described from our working practice, not counted from any survey, and none of them means the original installer did anything illegal; they are simply designs that do not fit the building.

1. One code shared by everyone in a two-family

The panel can only tell you that “user 1” disarmed it, so nobody knows who came in, and when a tenant leaves the code stays live because changing it means telling everyone else. We split the house into areas and give every adult a personal code, so the log is readable and a departing tenant’s code is deleted without bothering anyone.

2. The keypad at the street door only

If the only keypad is in the vestibule, the upstairs household has to come down to arm or disarm. They stop bothering within weeks. A keypad inside each apartment, plus phone control, fixes it.

3. A siren in the cellar and nothing upstairs

Brick floors and closed doors swallow sound. A single siren next to the panel in the cellar may be barely audible in a second-floor bedroom. We add a small sounder on each floor and keep the main siren where it is loud enough to drive an intruder out.

4. Shock sensors on glass under the el

On 86th Street, vibration sensors stuck to a storefront window pick up passing trains. Owners respond by switching off the glass zone, which leaves the window unprotected. Replacing them with tuned acoustic glass-break detectors restores the protection and ends the false alarms.

5. A communicator that stopped talking years ago

Many older panels reported over a landline that has since been cancelled, or over a cellular radio on a network that has been shut down. The keypad still lights up and the siren still works, so the owners believe the system is monitored. It is not. We check the communication path on every takeover and replace the communicator where needed.

6. No siren timeout, or one set far too long

City law requires a building burglar alarm to stop within fifteen minutes. Panels left at a factory default, or set to ring until someone disarms them, break that rule and keep a row of attached houses awake. Setting the timeout correctly takes a minute at the keypad and is part of every service visit we make.

What a Bath Beach store alarm needs

Glass that ignores the train. A storefront under the elevated structure on 86th Street feels every train in its glass. Shock sensors stuck to the window, which detect vibration, are the wrong tool there; they are designed to notice a blow to the glass and a passing train is a long series of small blows. Acoustic glass-break detectors listen for the particular sound of breaking glass and are a better choice, but they still need testing with a real train going overhead before the job is signed off. We aim them across the store at the glass, keep them away from the door chime and the speaker, and adjust sensitivity during a train, not between trains. On Bath Avenue and Cropsey Avenue, away from the el, the trade-offs are ordinary.

Gate, door and roof. A rolling gate gets a heavy-duty floor contact so the alarm knows the gate has been lifted. The back door, often on a Bay street, gets a surface contact rated for steel doors. A roof hatch, skylight or rear window over an extension is contacted, because on a commercial row roofs connect, and a burglar who gets onto one roof can often reach several.

A separate area for the apartments above. On mixed-use buildings the store and the apartments upstairs are separate alarm areas, often with separate panels. The tenants upstairs never get the store’s code; the store owner never gets theirs. If one owner holds the whole building, the owner keeps administrator rights to both, but day-to-day codes stay separate.

Hold-up and panic. A silent panic button at the register, under the counter, sends a different signal from a burglary alarm, so the monitoring station handles it differently. We install them only after explaining exactly what happens when one is pressed, because a panic signal is treated as an emergency.

Opening and closing reports. Business owners can receive a message each time the store is opened or closed and by which code, which tells you whether the last employee really armed the system at night.

Rules that apply to every Bath Beach alarm

  • Sirens must stop on time. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 says an owner may not operate an audible burglar alarm on a building unless it is capable of terminating, and does terminate, within fifteen minutes of being activated. Every panel we install is programmed with a siren timeout inside that limit. On attached houses that share walls, neighbors hear every minute of it.
  • The installer must be licensed. Under General Business Law § 69-m, no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems in New York without a Department of State license, and a licensee must issue identification cards to the unlicensed employees who help. Our license number is 12000287431. The same section exempts owners who install a system on their own property, which is why a do-it-yourself kit is legal.
  • Fire alarm systems are a separate subject. Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors tied to a burglar alarm add protection but do not replace detectors required in each dwelling unit, and commercial fire alarm systems have their own code and filing requirements. That work is on our Bath Beach fire alarm installation page.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Bath Beach: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bath Beach owners, landlords and store owners ask most when we are standing in the house, grouped by subject. Shorter answers on pricing and scheduling are in the FAQ near the bottom of the page.

Owners, tenants and shared doors

Can the upstairs tenant in my Bay street two-family have an alarm code that only works on their own floor?

Yes, and we recommend it. On a partitioned panel each household’s codes are tied to its own area. A tenant’s code arms and disarms the tenant’s apartment and the shared front door, and does nothing on the owner’s floor. When the tenant moves out, the owner deletes those codes from the administrator account in a minute, without a service call.

The shared hall and cellar are set up to arm only when both apartments are armed, so neither household can lock the other out of the stairs, and either household can open the front door with its own code.

If the alarm goes off upstairs while I am at work, who gets the call?

Whoever you put on that area’s notification list. Most two-families we set up send upstairs events to the tenant first and the owner second, and downstairs events the other way round. A central monitoring station follows the same call list before it dispatches anyone. Shared-area events, such as the street door or the cellar water sensor, go to both.

My elderly mother lives on the first floor and will not use a keypad. What are the options?

Several. Her floor can arm automatically at a set time every night and disarm in the morning; she can carry a small key fob with two buttons; or the family can arm her floor from their phones. Motion detectors on her floor can be left out entirely so she can move around freely, relying on door and window contacts instead. A medical or panic pendant can be added on the same panel if the family wants one.

Is it legal for a landlord to put an alarm in the shared hallway of a two-family?

An alarm in common areas, made up of door contacts and a motion detector, is generally treated as part of the building’s equipment, and we install them often. What we will not do is put sensors or cameras inside a tenant’s apartment for the landlord, or give a landlord remote control of a tenant’s area. Lease terms and local rules on entry and privacy vary, and this is not legal advice; if there is a dispute in the building, talk to a lawyer before installing anything.

Windows, doors and sensors

Which windows in a brick row house actually need sensors?

Every window a person can reach without a ladder, on every floor. In practice that means all first-floor windows, cellar windows at the front and rear, and upper windows over a porch roof, extension roof, garage or awning. On attached rows with connected flat roofs, the rear upper windows usually count. Upper windows facing the street with nothing below them are usually covered by a hallway motion detector instead of individual contacts.

Will a motion detector go off because of the cat, the radiator or the train?

Not if it is chosen and placed properly. Pet-immune detectors ignore animals up to a stated weight, provided the pet cannot climb furniture near the sensor. Detectors are aimed away from radiators, heating vents and windows that catch direct sun. Train vibration does not affect a passive infrared motion detector; it affects shock sensors on glass, which is why we avoid them on 86th Street.

Can I leave a bedroom window open a few inches in summer and still arm the alarm?

Yes. We install a second magnet a few inches up the frame, so the window can be locked in a partly open position and still read as closed. We set the second magnet at whatever height the household’s window lock or stop allows, and keep the opening small enough that no one can reach through.

Our cellar has a separate door to the yard. Does it need its own sensor?

Yes. Cellar doors and cellar windows are some of the least watched openings in a house, and in a two-family the cellar is usually shared space. They go in the common area with a contact on the door and on each window, plus a motion detector if the cellar holds anything worth taking, such as tools or a bicycle.

Monitoring, police and noise

Does an alarm in Bath Beach call the 62nd Precinct directly?

No. Alarms do not dial the police directly. A professionally monitored alarm reports to a central station, whose operators try your call list and then request a police or fire response if they cannot reach anyone or you confirm a problem. A self-monitored alarm sends the alert to your phone, and calling 911 is up to you. Either way, the call that reaches the police comes from a person, not the panel.

How do I keep the alarm from waking up the neighbors on the other side of the party wall?

Mostly by avoiding false alarms in the first place: correct sensor placement, entry delays long enough to reach the keypad, and codes everyone in the household actually knows. Then by setting the siren to stop well within the fifteen-minute limit in City law and leaving it silent for trouble conditions such as low batteries. An exterior siren is optional; inside a brick row house, an interior siren is loud enough to drive an intruder out and does not ring down the block.

What is the difference between self-monitoring and paying a central station?

With self-monitoring the alarm sends notifications to the household’s phones and you decide what to do. It costs nothing monthly beyond any app fee. With central station monitoring, trained operators watch for your signals around the clock and call you and, when needed, emergency services. It costs a monthly fee, and our monitoring partners start at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract. Some insurers ask for central station monitoring before they give a discount.

Can a camera confirm an alarm before anyone is called?

Yes. When an alarm zone trips, the panel can tell a camera covering that door or window to save a short clip and send it to the household and to the monitoring station. A clip of a person climbing in a rear window is a much stronger reason for operators to request a response than a sensor signal alone, and a clip of the cat knocking over a plant saves everyone a trip.

Old systems and changes

The house came with an old alarm nobody has used in years. Is it worth saving?

Often the wiring is. The contacts recessed into doors and windows and the cable to the cellar usually outlast the panel by decades. We test every zone; where the wiring is sound, a new panel, keypads and a cellular communicator cost much less than a new system, and on our Brooklyn list a panel replacement with communicator, keypad and programming runs $800 to $1,500. Where the panel is a brand that locks out other installers, we tell you before any work starts.

We are converting the house from one family to two. When should the alarm be redone?

While the walls are open. A conversion usually creates a new apartment door, a new stair arrangement and new keypad locations, and pulling cable during the renovation costs a fraction of fishing it later. Tell us the contractor’s schedule; we wire during framing and come back to install devices after paint.

What happens to the alarm when we sell the house?

The equipment is part of the house; the accounts are not. Before the closing, the seller removes their codes, cancels monitoring and app subscriptions, and hands over the installer code or panel documentation. The buyer then sets up new users and a new monitoring plan. We do a short handover visit for new owners at the service rate, or as part of a larger job.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Roofs Before You Count the Sensors

This is how we survey, not a story about a particular house. On an attached Bath Beach block the first thing a technician does is not go inside. He walks to the corner and looks at the back of the row from the side street or the nearest gap, because that is the only place you can see how the rear extensions, porch roofs and garages line up. A one-story extension that runs the full width of the lot, with its neighbor’s extension butting against it, makes a walkway along the back of the row at the height of the second-floor windows. A garage roof at the end of a row does the same. Awnings over store windows on a mixed-use building do it on the street side.

Then he goes up to the second floor and opens each rear window and looks down. If he could step out onto something solid, so could someone else, and that window gets a contact. If the drop is straight to the yard, it gets covered by the hall motion detector instead. Only after that walk does the sensor count get written down. It is the difference between a quote built on a package and one built on the building, and on these blocks it usually adds two or three window contacts upstairs that a package would have missed.

Where the Bath Beach Facts Come From

The history, figures, rules and news on this page were checked against the sources below in October 2026. What is not sourced is ours: the prices, which come from the AESS Brooklyn alarm list, and every description of how we survey, design and install, which is our working practice and not a claim about any particular building.

  • Wikipedia, “BMT West End Line” — elevated line over New Utrecht Avenue, 86th Street and Stillwell Avenue under the Dual Contracts of 1913; openings June 24, 1916 and July 21, 1917; Bay Parkway station opened July 29, 1916

Where sources disagree, we say so. Wikipedia gives Bath Beach’s density as 62.3 people per acre, which does not match its own population and area figures. Sources give Engine 253’s address as both 2425–2427 and 2429 86th Street; we used the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s. The Bensonhurst Park playground project is reported at both $7.09 million in funding and $7.86 million in cost, and its completion is dated both January 2020 and December 2020.

Not everything could be checked. Our build environment had no access to Answer the Public, Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or related searches, Bing or DuckDuckGo suggestions, or Yelp reviews. We found no Reddit threads about Bath Beach alarms or break-ins. We found no source that measures flooding block by block in Bath Beach, and no source counting how many houses are attached versus semi-attached, so the page does not give those figures. The NYC Noise Code fact sheet from the Department of Environmental Protection does not mention burglar alarms; the fifteen-minute rule is cited from the code text itself.
