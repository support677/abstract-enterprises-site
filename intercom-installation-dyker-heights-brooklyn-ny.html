The Neighborhood

Why Dyker Heights Properties Need Intercom Installation

The simplest way to understand an intercom job in Dyker Heights is to stand on the sidewalk and count how far a visitor's voice has to travel. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood's housing as mostly detached and semi-detached one- and two-family homes with driveways and yards, and very few apartment buildings. CityNeighborhoods.nyc puts it more plainly: houses with yards and driveways instead of stoops. On a brownstone block in Park Slope the bell is an arm's length from the sidewalk. Here, the visitor often stops at a gate between two piers, looks up a walk to a porch, and has no way of knowing whether anyone inside heard a knock or a chime. A doorbell button on the porch does not help someone stuck at a locked gate, and a chime in the front hall does not reach the kitchen at the back of a deep house or the bedrooms on the third floor. An intercom puts a call point where the visitor actually stands, rings it wherever the family actually is, and lets them open the right door without walking out to it.

The second pattern is the side door. Because the lots have driveways, many families park beside or behind the house and come and go through a door near the end of the driveway, leaving the formal front door for guests and the mail carrier. That splits the house into two entrances that both need answering. Couriers tend to go to the front, relatives tend to go to the side, and an old doorbell rings the same chime for both. In a two-family, the upstairs household may have its own door at the side while the owner uses the front, or the reverse. We plan Dyker Heights systems with a separate station at each door that people really use, so the monitor inside shows which door is calling and opens only that one.

The third pattern is the number of households. Dyker Heights has been arguing for more than a decade about two-family houses turned into something else. In June 2014 the Brooklyn Eagle reported a Dyker Heights Civic Association meeting where residents listed the signs they watched for: a bathroom on every floor, several dryer vents, steel doors opening onto a shared hall, and multiple meters. In August 2016 the Fire Department and the Department of Buildings ordered everyone out of 6705 Seventh Avenue, a two-family that had been divided into a five-family, displacing 18 adults and 13 children and citing a lack of exits among the hazards. For an intercom, the lesson is straightforward. The number of buttons on the door station should match the number of legal households in the house, and when we find more bells than legal apartments, we say so in writing rather than wiring every one of them.

The fourth pattern is the small condo and apartment building, which is less common here but real. Listings show three-story condo buildings with four units on 65th Street (built 1992), nine on 73rd Street (2002) and twelve on 13th Avenue (1987), and the much larger Dyker Gardens condo at 1238 63rd Street, built in 2019 with 146 units and, according to its listing, an Aiphone video entrance station. Buildings like these share one street door among many owners, so the intercom becomes a decision for a board or an association, and anything with eight or more apartments falls under the state's intercom requirement for multiple dwellings. Those buildings need a lobby panel, a directory and a release that every owner can trust, which is different work from a gate station on a single-family house.

Seven Dyker Heights facts that shape an intercom before any model is chosen

  • Houses sit back from the street. Wikipedia records that 1899 restrictions on the development called for plots of at least 60 by 100 feet with houses set back, rules that stayed active until 1915. That setback is why a call point at the gate matters here.
  • The zoning keeps it that way. CityLand reports that the City Council approved a 159-block rezoning of Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton in July 2007, mapping detached-only districts (R3A and R3X) and detached and semi-detached districts (R4A, R4B and R4-1), with rowhouse zoning (R5B) only in limited areas.
  • The neighborhood sits on a hill. The top is at 11th Avenue and 82nd Street, about 110 feet up, so many houses have steps from the gate to the porch and a driveway that slopes to a garage. Wet, sloped ground is where buried cable and gate hardware fail first.
  • Suburban utilities came early. The Brooklyn Eagle's history notes that Johnson installed gas, water, telephone and electric lines when he started the development. In older houses we test any early bell wire or old telephone pair we find in the walls before trusting it with a new system.
  • About 44 percent of residents rent. LucidRents, citing Census data for ZIP 11228, puts the renter share at 44 percent, which matches the many two-family houses with an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other.
  • 13th Avenue is the commercial heart. The section from 86th Street to Bay Ridge Avenue was co-named Dyker Heights Boulevard in 2001, and Forgotten New York lists Fort Hamilton Parkway and parts of 11th Avenue as the other business streets.
  • Parcels go missing from porches. The Brooklyn Eagle reported a package stolen from outside a home near 12th Avenue and 76th Street in 2020. A deep porch out of sight of the house is exactly where a door camera helps.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Dyker Heights' Architecture

Dyker Heights has a wider range of house types than most Brooklyn neighborhoods of its size, because it was built in waves: the first suburban houses of the 1890s and 1900s, detached brick and stucco houses of the 1920s and 1930s, postwar semi-detached two-families, small condos from the 1980s onward, and recent rebuilds on old lots. Each type puts the intercom's pieces in a different place.

Early suburban houses

Large frame or shingle houses with deep porches and long front walks. The station usually goes at the gate or the top of the porch steps, and the indoor stations are spread across three floors.

1920s brick three-families

Brick houses with a shared hall and a community driveway, like the 1925 three-family on 12th Avenue in one recent listing. One street door, three households, three buttons.

Postwar semi-detached two-families

Two households in each half, usually with one entrance at the front steps and one at driveway level. Two stations or one station with two buttons, depending on where the doors are.

Stucco and villa-style houses

Mediterranean-style fronts with stucco, columns and ornamental iron gates. The mounting surface and the gate hardware drive most of the planning.

Small condo buildings

Three-story buildings of four to twelve units with a shared lobby door. A directory panel, apartment stations and a board-approved design.

Rebuilt houses on old lots

New houses with large glass entries, built where an original house stood. Cable can be planned into the framing if we are called before the walls close.

Early suburban houses: a long walk, a deep porch and three floors to reach

The houses from Dyker Heights' first decades were built for a suburb, and a number of them still stand on the blocks near the top of the hill around 11th Avenue. The Saitta House at 1135 84th Street, built in 1899 and listed on the National Register in 2007, is the best-known example; Wikipedia counts about half of the roughly 150 original houses as still standing. These houses often have a front porch that wraps a corner, a front door with glass panels, a side or back entrance near the kitchen, and three full floors plus a cellar. A visitor coming up the walk may be heard by nobody if the family is on the third floor or in the yard.

We usually propose a station at the gate or at the foot of the porch steps, depending on whether the gate is kept locked, and indoor stations on each floor the family uses, placed in a hall rather than a bedroom so the call is heard without waking anyone. Original front doors in these houses are often heavy wood with glass, and many still carry their original hardware, so the release is planned around the existing latch rather than by cutting a new hole wherever it is convenient. Where the owner wants the old bell button kept for its looks, the new station can go beside it and the button can be retired or connected to a chime only.

1920s brick three-families: one hall, three households and an old bell plate

Forgotten New York points out the small attached houses at 10th Avenue and 81st Street, mostly built in the 1920s, and listings across the western half of the neighborhood show brick two- and three-family houses from the same decade, including a 1925 three-family on 12th Avenue with a community driveway and a garage. These houses have one street door opening into a common hall and stair, with a household on each floor. The usual inheritance is a two- or three-button bell plate in the brick or the door frame, bell wire stapled along the cellar ceiling, and a release that may or may not still work.

The work is closer to a small apartment building than to a house. The station needs a button per legal household, each floor needs its own indoor station, and the release on the street door has to work for all three. We test every run of old wire before reusing it; bell wire from this era that passes a resistance and insulation test can sometimes carry a two-wire system, and wire that fails is replaced with modern cable. The hall door must close and latch on its own after each visitor, so the closer is checked and adjusted as part of the job, not left for later.

Postwar semi-detached two-families: two doors at two levels

In many of the postwar semi-detached two-families we are asked to quote, the owner's door sits at the top of a short flight of front steps and the second household's door is at or just below the level of the driveway. That arrangement means a visitor has to choose a door before ringing, and couriers often choose wrong. One station on the front steps with two labeled buttons works when both households share an entry hall. When the entrances are separate, two stations, one at each door, are clearer, and each household's monitor then opens only its own door.

Because each building in a pair shares a wall with its neighbor, we keep every hole, box and cable path on the owner's own side of the party wall, and we never route cable across the neighbor's porch or driveway. Where the two halves of the pair have a shared front walk, the station goes on the owner's house, not on a post the neighbor also uses. These houses also tend to have the electrical panel and the old bell transformer in a small cellar room, which is a reasonable place for the new power supply as long as it stays dry and labeled.

Stucco and villa-style houses: decorative fronts and iron gates

CityNeighborhoods.nyc lists Mediterranean villa and Renaissance Revival houses in brick and stucco among Dyker Heights' styles, and the rebuilt houses of recent decades often go further, with columns, balustrades, stone-clad piers and wrought-iron gates. These fronts are attractive and unforgiving. A station surface-mounted on stucco needs anchors set into the masonry behind it rather than the stucco skin alone, and the hole is sealed so water cannot get behind the finish and freeze. A flush-mounted station looks better on a smooth stucco wall but needs a back box, which means deciding early whether cutting into the finish is acceptable to the owner.

Ornamental gates bring their own questions. A station on a hollow iron post can be wired through the post if there is a way in at the bottom, while a solid stone pier usually needs a surface conduit painted to match or a core drilled through the pier. If the gate is meant to open from inside, the latch has to accept an electric release, and many decorative gates were hung with simple gravity latches that need replacing. We look at the gate, the hinges and the latch on the survey and price them as part of the job, rather than discovering on installation day that the gate cannot be released electrically.

Small condo buildings: a board, a directory and one shared door

Dyker Heights' small condo buildings are mostly three stories: CityRealty lists a four-unit building on 65th Street from 1992, a nine-unit building on 73rd Street from 2002 and a twelve-unit building on 13th Avenue from 1987. They share a lobby door, a set of mailboxes and usually an entry panel installed when the building was new. Many of those panels were designed to dial each owner's telephone, which made sense when every apartment had a landline and makes less sense now. Others use apartment handsets with a single release button, and some have lost their directory labels as units changed hands.

The decision belongs to the board or the association, so our quote is written to be read by people who were not at the survey: what each owner gets in the apartment, how visitors find a name, how deliveries are handled, who holds the administrator login, and how the door is kept self-closing and self-locking. Buildings with eight or more apartments must have an intercom at a self-locking entrance under the state's Multiple Dwelling Law, so for the nine- and twelve-unit buildings a working system is a legal duty, not only a convenience. A four-unit building is not covered by that requirement, but its owners usually want the same thing.

Rebuilt houses on old lots: plan the cable before the walls close

Wikipedia notes that many of Dyker Heights' original houses have been replaced, one of them by seven detached houses in 1928, and the replacement continues today with large new houses on old lots. These are the easiest intercoms to do well, if we are called at the right time. With the framing open, cable can be run from the cellar to every planned station, out under the walk to the gate in a conduit set before the paving goes down, and up to a monitor location in the kitchen and the primary bedroom, all without a single exposed wire.

After the walls close, the same house is harder, so we ask builders to call at rough-in and leave back boxes at the gate, the doors and each monitor spot, one conduit to the gate pier, and a home run for the power supply.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Dyker Heights Lot From the Gate to the Garage Before Picking a Station

On a Dyker Heights survey the technician starts on the sidewalk, not in the cellar. The first thing to settle is where a stranger actually stops. If the gate is kept latched, the visitor stops at the gate, and a station on the porch is useless to them. If the gate stands open all day, the porch is fine. Then the technician walks the path a courier would take and the path a relative would take, which are often different, and notes every door that either of them reaches.

Next comes the ground between the gate and the house. Is it a poured walk, pavers, a lawn or a planted bed? Does the driveway slope toward a garage at the back? Is there an existing conduit for a lamp post or a sprinkler that could carry an intercom cable? A planted bed can be trenched by hand and restored; a poured walk usually means routing along its edge or boring under it, and the price follows from that choice.

Only then does the survey go indoors: where the family sits in the evening, which floor each household lives on, where the old transformer hangs, and how many bells are on the existing plate compared with how many legal apartments the house has. By the time we get to the station model, most of the decisions are already made by the lot itself. That order of work is why our Dyker Heights quotes describe the route first and the hardware second.

Gates, Walks and Driveways

From the Gate Post to the Kitchen: Wiring an Intercom Across a Dyker Heights Front Yard

Option one: keep the station on the house

The cheapest outdoor run is no outdoor run. If the gate is usually left unlatched, or if there is no gate, the station goes on the house beside the door, and nothing crosses the yard. This is the right answer for many semi-detached houses and for detached houses where the porch faces the street closely. We say so when it fits, because a gate station is only worth its cost if visitors really stop at the gate.

Option two: a shallow trench through a lawn or bed

Through grass or a planted bed, cable goes in an outdoor-rated conduit laid in a hand-dug trench, then the soil and plants go back. The conduit is what makes the run last: it keeps a later shovel from cutting the cable and allows a damaged run to be pulled out and replaced without digging again. We mark the route on the wiring record, so whoever plants the next bed knows where not to dig. If the property already has a conduit to a lamp post or a sprinkler valve, we check whether it has room and is safe to share, since mixing intercom cable with line voltage in one conduit is not acceptable.

Option three: under a poured walk or driveway

When concrete or stone pavers run all the way from the gate to the house, the choices are to route around the edge of the paving, to bore under it, or to cut and patch a narrow channel. Routing around is usually the least disruptive. Boring under a walk is possible over short distances. Cutting is a last resort, because a patch in a decorative walk rarely matches. On a rebuild, the simplest answer is a conduit placed before the walk is poured, which is why we ask builders to call us at rough-in.

What happens at the gate end

At the gate, the cable comes up inside the post or pier where possible, or in a short conduit fixed to the back of it. The station is mounted at a height a standing adult and a child can both reach, with the speaker at face height. The New York City rule for multiple dwellings, 1 RCNY 42-01, puts push buttons no more than six feet from the floor and the speaking device between four and five feet, and we use the same heights at a house gate because they suit most people. If the gate is to be released electrically, the release is matched to the gate's latch, and its power comes from the house, not from a separate supply at the post.

Driveway gates with motors

Some Dyker Heights houses have sliding or swinging driveway gates with motor operators. Many operators have a terminal that accepts an outside open command, and an intercom with a spare relay can trigger it, so the person answering a call can open the driveway as well as the walk gate. Whether that is safe and allowed depends on the operator, its safety sensors and the maker's instructions. We look at the operator on the survey and, if its manual permits it, connect the intercom to the input the maker provides, without bypassing any safety device.

The side door and the garage

The run to the side door is usually short, through the foundation wall from the cellar. The run to a garage at the back of the lot is longer and goes either along the side of the house and the garage wall in conduit, or underground across the driveway's end. We often combine the garage run with any camera or lighting cable the owner is planning, so the yard is opened once. Each run ends in a labeled box, and the wiring record lists every one.

Rules and Unit Counts

Two Legal Apartments, Five Old Bells: How Dyker Heights' Conversion Fight Shapes an Intercom Quote

Illegal conversions have been one of Dyker Heights' longest-running neighborhood issues. The Brooklyn Eagle covered a Civic Association meeting in June 2014 where residents described two-family houses being rebuilt with a bathroom on every floor and steel doors opening onto a common hall, and neighbors worried aloud about two-families becoming buildings for thirty or forty people. Brooklyn Paper reported residents on 75th Street between 10th Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway watching two-family houses fill with new occupants, and nineteen Buildings Department complaints about one site in three months. In August 2016 the city emptied 6705 Seventh Avenue after inspectors found a two-family divided into five dwellings.

An intercom installer is one of the few trades that counts the doorbells on a house, so we have a policy. The station we install has one button for each legal household, based on the certificate of occupancy or, for older houses without one, the building's recorded use. If a house we are asked to quote has more bells than legal apartments, we tell the owner in writing and quote for the legal count. We do not install hidden buttons, extra handsets in rooms that are being rented separately, or stations on doors that lead to unapproved apartments. That protects the owner as much as anyone. A house wired for five households that is legal for two is a house that will look converted to the next inspector, whatever is happening inside.

There is a safety point too. Officials at 6705 Seventh Avenue cited a lack of exits among the hazards, and an intercom touches the exits directly. A release that holds a door locked when it should open, a lock that needs a key to get out, or a station mounted where it blocks a door from swinging fully are all things we design against. The street door of any building with apartments must close and lock by itself, and it must still open freely from inside without a key.

What New York rules ask of a Dyker Heights door

  • Eight or more apartments. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercommunication system at the self-locking entrance of Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and the Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 extends the requirement to older buildings of eight or more apartments. In Dyker Heights that covers buildings like the nine- and twelve-unit condos, not the one- and two-family houses that make up most of the neighborhood.
  • Fewer than eight. A two- or three-family house is not required to have an intercom, but in any multiple dwelling the building entrance door must be self-closing, according to HPD's ABCs of Housing. A three-family counts as a multiple dwelling, so its street door has to close by itself even if it has no intercom at all.
  • Mounting heights. Under 1 RCNY 42-01, push buttons go no more than six feet above the floor and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet.
  • Repairs. HPD's guidance says the building's manager, super or owner should repair broken locks or intercoms immediately. For a condo association, that means someone needs to be named as responsible before the system goes in.
  • Electrical work. The intercom itself is low-voltage work under our state license. A new outlet or a dedicated circuit for the power supply is electrical work, which a licensed electrician performs and, where needed, files.

None of this is legal advice. If you are unsure how many legal apartments your house has, the certificate of occupancy on the Department of Buildings' website is the place to start, and we are glad to look at it with you before quoting.

How we set up a 13th Avenue storefront with apartments above

The most common commercial layout on the avenue is a store at street level with one or two apartments above, reached through a separate street door beside the shop window. The residents' door gets its own station with a button for each apartment, and those buttons never ring the shop. If the store owner also owns the building, the owner can have a separate account or a separate monitor that receives calls from the residents' door after hours, without the tenants' calls ever passing through the store. The store's own entrance, if it needs a call point at all, is usually its back or delivery door, wired to the shop's phone or a station in the back room.

Compliance notes for Dyker Heights businesses and mixed-use buildings

  • Means of egress. No intercom release, magnetic lock or strike may stop people from leaving. Exit doors must open from inside without a key, a code or special knowledge, and any locking hardware on a required exit is chosen and installed with that in mind.
  • Accessible stations. Where a business serves the public, the call station is placed where a wheelchair user can reach the buttons, and the door it releases has hardware that can be operated without tight grasping or twisting.
  • Mixed-use buildings with eight or more apartments. The apartments' entrance falls under the same state intercom requirement as any other multiple dwelling, regardless of the store below.
  • Fire alarm interfaces. If a building has a fire alarm system that is meant to release locked doors, the intercom's locks are tied into it by the fire alarm contractor's design, not by the intercom alone.
  • Privacy. A door station camera records people at your door. We aim it at the visitor's position and, where the model allows, mask out a neighbor's window or the public sidewalk beyond the visitor.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Dyker Heights: Questions Answered

Gates, walks and side doors

Our house sits back from the sidewalk behind an iron gate between stone posts. Should the call station go on the gate post or beside the front door?

It depends on whether the gate is kept latched. If it is locked or latched most of the time, visitors stop at the gate, so the station belongs on the post, with a release on the gate and, if you want, a second station at the door. If the gate usually stands open, a station beside the front door is cheaper, needs no run across the yard, and works just as well. Many owners choose the gate because it keeps strangers off the porch, especially in December when the lights draw crowds onto the blocks in the 80s.

Most of our visitors come up the driveway to the side door, not the front. Can one intercom ring from both doors and tell us which one is calling?

Yes. Each door gets its own station, and the indoor monitors show a different label, and on most systems a different tone, for each one. When you press the release, only the door that called you opens. This is one of the most common layouts we install in Dyker Heights, because so many families use the side door day to day.

There is a concrete walk and a planted front yard between our gate and the house. How does the cable get to the gate without breaking them up?

Usually by following something that is already there. The cable can run in conduit along the base of a masonry fence or wall, through the planted bed in a shallow trench that is then replanted, or along the edge of the walk where it meets the lawn. Boring under a short walk is sometimes possible. Cutting the walk is the last choice, and we would tell you before proposing it.

Whatever the route, the cable goes in outdoor-rated conduit, so it can be replaced later without digging, and the route is drawn on the wiring record. If there is already a conduit to a lamp post, we check whether it can be used, which depends on its size and on keeping low-voltage cable separate from the lamp's power.

Can the same intercom open our motorized driveway gate as well as the front door?

Often it can. Many gate operators have an input for an outside open command, and many intercom stations have a second relay that can trigger it, so one call can be answered with either the walk gate or the driveway gate. Whether we connect them depends on the operator's make and the maker's instructions, and we never bypass the operator's safety sensors.

Send us a photo of the operator's label and its control board if you can reach it. That tells us before the visit whether the connection is simple, whether an interface module is needed, or whether the operator should be left alone and a separate remote kept for the driveway.

Our garage at the back of the lot has its own people door, and the kids come in that way after parking. Can it have a station on the same system?

Yes. A garage station is just another door on the system, with its own label and its own release. The longer run to the back of the lot goes along the house and garage walls in conduit or underground across the end of the driveway, and if the garage has no power, the station takes its power over the cable from the supply in the house.

Households, condos and the legal count

The house we bought is a legal two-family, but the old bell plate beside the door has five buttons. Should the new station have two buttons or five?

Two. We install one button for each legal household, which for a legal two-family means two, plus any separate office or garage buttons the house actually needs. Extra bells on an old plate are often left over from a time when rooms or a basement were rented separately, and wiring them into a new system makes the house look like a five-family to anyone who rings.

We will trace where the extra bells went, disconnect them at the transformer, and note it on the wiring record. If you are not sure how many legal apartments the house has, check the certificate of occupancy before the visit, and we can go through it with you.

Our small condo building from the late 1980s has an entry panel that dials residents' home phone lines. Can it be switched to cell phones?

Usually by replacing the panel rather than reprogramming it. Older telephone-entry panels were built to dial phone numbers over a building phone line, and keeping them running means keeping that line. Current panels call an app on each resident's phone over the internet, ring an apartment monitor, or call a regular phone number through the maker's service, and residents choose which they want.

For a small condo, the choice between those depends on how many owners still want a monitor in the apartment and whether the building has a reliable internet connection at the lobby. We price both versions so the board can compare. The existing door release and lock are tested and kept if they work.

Our condo association has to approve the spending at a meeting. What should the quote show so owners can vote on it?

Our board quotes list the lobby station, what each apartment receives, the door hardware, the cable and power work, the programming, and the total, followed by the choices the board needs to make, such as monitors or apps in the apartments and who will hold the administrator login. We also note whether the building has eight or more apartments and so must keep an intercom by law.

Porches, parcels and the December crowds

Can the door station keep a short clip of whoever rings, so we can see who came to the porch while we were out?

Many video stations can. Some save a still image or a short clip of every call to the indoor monitor, others save to the app, and some do both. That is useful on a deep Dyker Heights porch where nobody inside can see the door, and after the Brooklyn Eagle's 2020 report of a package taken outside a home near 12th Avenue and 76th Street, it is a common request.

A door station is not a security camera, though. It records when someone presses the button, and some models also record on motion, but it does not watch the yard all night. If you want the porch and the driveway covered continuously, a camera is the better tool, and we can run its cable at the same time as the intercom's.

During the December lights, visitors ring houses on our block until late at night. Can the gate station stop calling the bedrooms after a certain hour?

Yes, on most current systems. A schedule can send gate calls after a set hour only to one monitor downstairs, or only to a phone, or not at all, while the side door station that the family uses keeps ringing normally. The schedule can be turned on for the weeks from Thanksgiving to New Year's and off again afterward.

Wikipedia describes the light displays running from Thanksgiving to early January, with mid-December the busiest, and the crowds are concentrated on the blocks in the 80s between 11th and 13th Avenues. If your house is on one of those blocks, mention it when you call, and we will set the schedule up at handover so you are not working it out in the middle of December.

Offices, rectories and stores

My accounting practice uses the front rooms of our house on Fort Hamilton Parkway. Can clients ring the office without the bell sounding in the family's rooms?

Yes. The office gets its own button, or its own station if it has its own door, and that button rings only the reception desk or your office phone. The family's button rings only the family's monitors. Nobody upstairs hears a client, and a client never reaches the family by mistake.

After hours, the office button can be switched to your cell phone or turned off on a schedule. If you also want clients to be let in automatically during appointment hours, that is better done with a controlled lock on the office door than by leaving the street door unlocked.

Our parish rectory gets callers at all hours. Can the door station ring the office during the day and a priest's phone at night?

Yes. A station with a schedule can call the parish office during office hours and switch automatically to a priest's or a sexton's phone in the evening and at night, with the caller seen on video before anyone goes to the door. A separate button can be added for deliveries or for a food pantry if the parish runs one.

Our 13th Avenue bakery takes deliveries at a back door before we open. Can the driver call the baker from that door?

Yes. A station at the back door can call a phone or a handset in the kitchen, and the baker can see the driver, talk to him and open the door without leaving the oven. If deliveries come at the same time every morning, the station can be set to ring the kitchen only during those hours and the owner's phone at other times.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Dyker Heights

Each one is checked against what a Dyker Heights lot actually asks for.

"Wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY"

HomeAdvisor's cost guide says exactly that, and for a small apartment it can be true. A wireless kit pairs a door unit with an indoor unit, and nothing has to be drilled through a wall.

The same guide also says wireless systems can have network issues, and on a Dyker Heights lot the distance and the materials are the issue. A gate station sits outside, separated from the house by a yard, a porch, a brick or stucco front and often a storm door, and a battery unit on an iron post spends the winter in the cold.

Wireless also does not solve the release. A gate or door still needs a lock that can be opened electrically, and that lock needs power. Once a wire has to reach the gate for the release, it can carry the intercom too.

"A home intercom costs about $2,500 on average"

That figure is HomeAdvisor's national average, alongside a range it gives from $150 for a basic wireless system to $15,000 for advanced ones. A national average says little about one Brooklyn lot.

On our Brooklyn list, a video intercom for a one- or two-family house is $600 to $1,800, an audio buzzer system for a small building is $400 to $900, and a building-wide system for a walk-up or small condo of 4 to 20 units is $1,200 to $4,500.

What moves a Dyker Heights price inside those ranges is mostly the outdoor run to the gate, the number of doors on the system and the condition of the door hardware, not the brand on the station.

"Any two-family house needs an intercom by law"

It does not. The state intercom requirement in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a and the Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 applies to buildings with eight or more apartments.

A two-family is not a multiple dwelling at all, and a three-family is a multiple dwelling that needs a self-closing entrance door but no intercom. Most Dyker Heights houses fall into those two groups.

The reason to install one is practical: a household upstairs that cannot otherwise answer a gate, and a door that cannot otherwise be opened without walking down. The nine- and twelve-unit condo buildings are the ones where the law applies directly.

"Put the doorbell next to the front door"

Every generic guide puts the call button beside the front door, because on most houses that is where visitors stand.

Dyker Heights was laid out with houses set back from the street, and many lots have a gate at the sidewalk and a family door at the side. A button by the front door can be the one place a visitor never reaches.

The right spot is where a stranger stops. On a latched-gate lot that is the gate post; on an open lot it is the porch; and for family and regular visitors it may be the side door. Many houses need two of the three.

"Add as many buttons as you have doors or tenants"

Online answers treat the button count as a matter of convenience: one per apartment, one per tenant, one per room if rooms are rented.

In Dyker Heights, where the Civic Association, Community Board 10 and the Buildings Department have spent years on illegal conversions, a door with more buttons than the house has legal apartments is a signal anyone can read from the sidewalk.

We install one button per legal household and say so in the quote. That is simpler for visitors and keeps the house honest to every inspector who rings.

"A video doorbell does the same job as an intercom"

For a single household with one door facing the street, a video doorbell covers most of what an intercom does: you see and talk to the visitor.

It does not open the gate or the door, does not give a second household its own call, and does not connect a side door and a garage into one system unless you buy and manage several devices.

If the house has any of those, the intercom is the tool built for the job. If it has none of them, we will tell you a doorbell is enough.

"Intercom installation is a quick one-day job anywhere"

Cost guides quote labor as if every house were the same. Many indoor-only jobs are quick, and a station swap on existing wire can be done in hours.

A Dyker Heights job with a run under a walk to the gate, a strike on an original front door and a garage station across the driveway takes longer, and may need a second visit if a planted bed or a gate latch has to be dealt with first.

We give a realistic schedule in the quote, and we book a call back within 2 hours of a request, but we do not promise same-day installation, because the lot decides that, not the calendar.

How far ahead should we book if we want the gate station working before the Thanksgiving crowds arrive?
+

Call us in September or early October if you can. Our front door is the original wooden door from the 1920s. Can it take an electric release without being ruined?

Usually, yes. The release goes into the door frame on the latch side, not into the door itself, and it is chosen to match the existing latch so the door's original hardware stays in place. Can the indoor monitor go in the kitchen at the back of the house instead of beside the front door?
