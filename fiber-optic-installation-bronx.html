The Neighborhood

Why Bronx Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

Street maps say the Bronx is wired. The Center for an Urban Future's July 2025 report puts the share of the borough with access to high-speed service at nearly 99.8 percent. The same report says, in the very next breath, that problems remain — "including individual buildings not yet wired for broadband and limited competition in some areas." That sentence is the whole reason this page exists. Coverage is counted at the curb. Whether a signal can climb a 1930s stair hall on the Concourse, cross a courtyard to the far wing, or reach a security office in a different tower of the same complex is decided by the wiring on the owner's side of the wall, and that wiring is often decades old, shared with other systems, or simply missing.

Residents notice it first. On the Fios forum, a tenant near Tremont writes that service is "unavailable at my address, but available directly behind me and across the street." In a different thread, someone posts a photo from a Co-op City basement and asks what it shows; the reply points out the "incoming fiber and 2 distribution hubs" serving the building. Those are consumer conversations, but they describe the commercial reality too. A provider's glass can stop in a cellar a hundred feet from the people who need it, and the owner, the board or the managing agent is the one who has to close the gap.

What makes that gap distinctive in this borough is scale. The Bronx holds some of the largest residential developments in the country, large institutional campuses — Montefiore, Jacobi, Fordham, Lehman, Manhattan College — and one of the biggest wholesale food hubs in the world at Hunts Point. A building owner in Brooklyn might ask us to reach the top floor; a Bronx owner more often asks us to reach building number twelve, or the freezer room on the far side of a market unit, or the leasing office in a separate low-rise across the parking lot. The answer is a backbone designed for the whole site rather than a run between two points, and that is the work this page describes.

The borough is also building again. The City approved a plan in August 2024 to support roughly 7,000 new homes around four new Metro-North stations at Hunts Point, Parkchester/Van Nest, Morris Park and Co-op City, and the Kingsbridge Armory redevelopment is scheduled to begin construction in 2026. New buildings get one chance to put the right conduit, sleeves and telecom rooms in place before the walls close. Older buildings get many chances, but each one costs more than the last. We work on both, and most owners add copper drops, cameras or door access in the same visit — which is why our Bronx structured cabling crew often walks the site with us.

How we adapt the method to each building type

Courtyard buildings. We treat the cellar as the hub. The provider's handoff, a small wall-mount enclosure and the building's network switch go in one dry, lockable spot, and a separate riser climbs each wing through whatever vertical space exists — a closet stack, a disused chase, a pipe sleeve with room to spare. Riser-rated cable is used for every vertical run, and each floor penetration is sealed and photographed. Where wings connect only through the cellar ceiling, the horizontal runs between stair halls go on proper supports, high and clear of the steam and water lines that run through older cellars.

Deco and early-twentieth-century lobbies. These are approached like a museum room. We find routes above existing ceilings, behind service doors and through back-of-house spaces before we ever propose surface raceway in a public area. Where raceway is unavoidable, it is small, painted to match and fixed into mortar joints or plaster patches that can be repaired invisibly, not into stone or decorative metal. Interior work in a historic district normally needs no Landmarks review when it does not affect the exterior, but we still treat the finishes as irreplaceable.

Complexes. The fiber plan starts on a site drawing, not in a closet. We count buildings, measure the real path between them, confirm which basements are connected and where existing conduit runs, and then size one backbone with spare strands to every building. Single-mode is the normal choice because distances across a large complex quickly exceed what multimode handles comfortably at modern speeds, and the extra strands cost little at install time compared with a second project later.

Industrial and cold-chain buildings. Cable stays off the floor and out of forklift reach, armored wherever it passes a dock door or a racking aisle, and inside sealed enclosures near coolers and freezers. Where cable crosses into a refrigerated room, the penetration is sleeved and sealed on both sides so moisture does not track along the jacket. Owners who are also adding cameras and door access usually bring in our Bronx low-voltage team for the same visit.

Compliance callouts for fiber work in the Bronx

  • Match the cable to the space. OFNR riser cable in vertical shafts and stair-hall chases, OFNP plenum cable where a ceiling cavity returns air, and outdoor or indoor/outdoor cable for any section between buildings or along an exterior wall.
  • Elevator hoistways are off limits. The electrical code reserves hoistways for wiring used directly with the elevator, so a building's network fiber has to find another vertical path even when the shaft looks convenient.
  • Seal every penetration. Each hole through a rated floor or wall is closed with a listed firestop system and photographed for the building's records; in cold rooms we add a vapour seal on the warm side.
  • Permits versus approvals. The Buildings Department does not normally require a work permit for sub-50-volt cabling such as fiber, yet the job is never approval-free: alteration agreements, co-op or campus sign-off and the firestop rules all still govern how it is done.
  • Suspected asbestos stops the drill. Older Bronx cellars often have insulated pipes and old floor tile. If a route would disturb material that might contain asbestos, work pauses until the owner has it tested and, if necessary, handled by licensed professionals.
  • Accessible routes stay clear. Enclosures and raceway in lobbies and corridors are placed so they do not narrow an accessible route or create protruding objects at head height.
Large Residential Sites

One Backbone for a Bronx Complex of Many Buildings

Co-op City's operator describes four building types on the site: ten triple-core buildings of 26 storeys with 500 apartments each, ten chevron buildings of 24 storeys with 414, fifteen towers of 33 storeys with 384, and townhouse clusters. Parkchester was laid out by Metropolitan Life around a central heating plant, with 51 building groups planned for about 12,000 families. Other Bronx campuses are smaller but follow the same pattern: many structures, one owner, one management office and a security operation that needs to see every lobby. When owners of sites like these call us, the question is never "can you run a cable" — it is "how do we stop treating forty buildings as forty separate problems."

The answer starts with a drawing. We map every building, the existing utility routes between them, which basements already connect, where the providers' equipment lands today and where the management, security and maintenance offices sit. From that we choose a main distribution room — usually in a central, dry, secure building with good power — and design a star of single-mode fiber out to an enclosure in each building. Where buildings are linked underground or by continuous basements, the fiber follows that path in armored cable or innerduct. Where they are not, we look for existing conduit or propose new conduit along the shortest practical route, and we tell you at the walkthrough which sections would need civil work.

Strand count is the decision owners most often under-buy. A building that needs two strands today for its camera switch will want more when the laundry system, the intercom, the elevator monitoring or a second provider arrives. We normally recommend at least twelve strands to each building on a large site and more to buildings that host offices or community rooms, because adding strands later means a second pull through the same route — the expensive part of the job. Spare strands are spliced, tested and labelled on day one, so they are ready rather than theoretical.

Access is the other large-site reality. Work in occupied buildings is scheduled with the managing agent, the super and, on a co-op, the board's chosen representative. Notices go up in lobbies before we open a ceiling, hallways are protected, and noisy coring is kept to agreed hours. We work building by building, so each one is finished, tested and closed up before the next opens, and the site never has half-finished work in every lobby at once. Owners who want the same backbone to carry cameras and door entry usually pair it with Bronx access control installation and Bronx intercom installation.

Finally, large sites outlive their contractors. Every strand gets a label at both ends that matches a single route map, and the results file shows each building's link with its loss and OTDR trace. When a new provider, a new security company or a new property manager arrives in ten years, they will be able to read the plant without guessing.

Hunts Point & Cold Chain

Fiber for Hunts Point Markets, Coolers and Loading Docks

NYCEDC estimates that roughly 4.5 billion pounds of food pass through the Hunts Point Food Distribution Center each year and that about 12 percent of all food distributed to New York City comes from it. The Terminal Produce Market, opened in 1967, covers more than 100 acres with four primary warehouse structures and 26 merchants. In December 2025 the City announced an agreement to redevelop the produce market as a new intermodal facility backed by $405 million, with construction expected to begin in late 2026. For the businesses on the peninsula, the network is not an office convenience. Scanners, label printers, temperature monitors, dock cameras and ordering systems all have to stay up through overnight shifts.

That environment is hard on cable. Forklifts and pallet jacks catch anything that hangs low. Doors between coolers and loading areas open and close constantly, so condensation forms where warm, damp air meets cold surfaces. Copper runs stretched from a front office to the far end of a long warehouse often exceed the distance Ethernet supports reliably, and they pick up interference from refrigeration compressors and conveyor motors along the way. Fiber solves the distance and interference problems outright; the installation solves the physical ones.

Our approach in market buildings and distribution warehouses is consistent. Cable runs high, on supports fixed to structure rather than to racking that may be moved. Armored cable is used wherever it passes a dock, a door or an aisle. Enclosures near cold rooms are gasketed, and every cable entering a refrigerated space passes through a sleeve that is sealed on the warm side, so moisture cannot follow the jacket in and freeze at the connector. Switches and media converters at the far end get their own small battery backup, because a link is only as reliable as the power at both ends.

Power resilience is getting attention on the peninsula itself. The City's Hunts Point Energy Resiliency Project, supported by a $20 million federal Rebuild by Design award and brought to a $71 million total budget, is designed to provide backup power to food businesses in the distribution center and solar-plus-storage at two local schools. When a building's power comes back after an outage, the network electronics should come back with it. We document which outlet and which circuit feeds each piece of network gear, so the electrician and the facilities team know what must be on emergency power.

Timing matters more here than anywhere else in the borough. Most market businesses cannot stop for a day. We stage work in sections, run new fiber alongside existing links, test it fully, and switch over during the quietest window the business can give us — often mid-afternoon between the overnight receiving shift and the evening pick-up, not overnight when the market is at its busiest.

Your Questions Answered

Fiber Optic Installation in the Bronx: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bronx owners, boards, managing agents and business operators actually put to us on walkthroughs, grouped by the kind of property they come from. Each answer is written for this borough's buildings.

Complexes, co-ops and large rental sites

On a site with dozens of high-rises, which building should host the main fiber room?

Somewhere central, dry, secure and well powered — in that order of priority. Central keeps the longest link short. Dry means above any space that has flooded or that sits under a plumbing stack. Secure means a locked room that porters and contractors do not use for storage. Well powered means a dedicated circuit and space for a battery backup. On many Bronx sites the management office building or the building that already houses the site's security desk ticks all four boxes; on others the boiler or power plant building does, as long as the room is separated from heat and steam. We walk the candidates with you and explain the trade-off of each before anything is drawn.

Can a complex's fiber be installed in phases over several budget years?

Yes, and many large sites prefer it. We design the full backbone first, so the main room, the strand count to each building and the labelling scheme are fixed for the whole site, and then build it in stages — usually the central building first, followed by the buildings whose cameras, security desk or offices need the link most. Each phase is tested, labelled and documented on its own, so nothing is left half-finished between budget years, and later phases plug into the main room without rework. The proposal prices each phase separately so the board can approve one at a time.

Can fiber be run through an old steam or utility tunnel between buildings?

Sometimes, and it can save a great deal of digging. The questions are temperature, water and who controls the space. Where a tunnel carries steam, cable has to be kept well away from the pipes and rated for the heat it will see. Where a tunnel floods or sweats, the fiber goes into sealed innerduct or conduit on wall supports above the water line. And because tunnels are usually the domain of the site's engineering staff, nothing is fixed until they approve the route and the supports. We inspect the tunnel with the engineer before quoting, and if it is unsuitable we say so and price the alternative.

Prewar, courtyard and Art Deco buildings

In a courtyard building with several wings and stairwells, does each wing need its own fiber riser?

Usually yes, and it is cheaper than it sounds. Bronx courtyard buildings often share a cellar but have three or four separate stair halls, and apartments in one wing do not connect to the next above the ground floor. Trying to feed every wing from one riser means horizontal runs through finished hallways on every floor. Running a short riser up each wing from a common cellar enclosure keeps the horizontal work in the cellar ceiling, where it is out of sight and easy to maintain. Each riser can be small — a few strands is often enough — with a compact enclosure on the floors that need one.

How is fiber routed through an Art Deco lobby without harming the original finishes?

By going around the lobby wherever possible. Most lobbies have a service corridor, a mail room, a closet behind the elevator or a hung ceiling in a back hallway that can carry cable from the entrance side to the cellar stair. If the route must cross a public space, we use the smallest raceway that will hold the cable, paint it to match, and fix it only into surfaces that can be patched invisibly — never into terrazzo, marble, original metalwork or decorative plaster. We photograph the lobby before work begins so the board can see that nothing changed apart from what was agreed.

Is the elevator hoistway ever allowed as the route for fiber between floors?

No. The electrical code limits elevator hoistways to wiring used directly with the elevator, and inspectors and elevator contractors enforce it. Every building does have another path, though it may take some finding: a disused dumbwaiter, a closet stack aligned floor to floor, an abandoned chimney flue that has been properly assessed, a pipe chase with room, or a new sleeve cored through slabs in a utility closet. In the worst case an exterior route in conduit up a rear or courtyard wall may be the most practical choice. We present the options with their cost and disruption side by side.

What notice do residents need before fiber work starts in the hallways of an occupied building?

Enough to plan their day, and in writing. We give the managing agent a notice for each building listing the dates, the hours, the floors affected and any short shutdown of building services such as an intercom or a camera feed. Most boards and agents post it in the lobby and elevators several days ahead. If we need access to an apartment — rare, but sometimes the only route — that is arranged individually with the resident through management. Noisy work like coring is grouped into one block of time per floor so nobody hears drilling for a week.

Warehouses, markets and industrial buildings

How is fiber protected from forklifts and pallet jacks on a busy market or warehouse floor?

By keeping it out of their reach. Cable runs at height on supports fixed to the building's structure, not to racking that can be moved or struck, and comes down to a device only inside steel conduit. Where a route has to cross a dock opening it goes overhead with clearance for the tallest trailer and lift, never across the floor. Interlocking-armored cable adds a second layer along aisles and beside doors, and enclosures go on walls or columns above the height where carts and forks make contact. If a column already shows impact damage, we take that as a warning and choose another.

Our building has a sidewalk shed up for facade work — can fiber work still go ahead?

Usually, yes. Facade inspections and repairs can keep a sidewalk shed in front of a prewar building for months, but nearly all fiber work happens inside — in the cellar, the stair halls and the closets — and is not affected. What changes is anything on the exterior: a new conduit up a wall or a new entry through the front facade has to wait for, or be coordinated with, the facade contractor and the engineer responsible for the work. We plan routes that enter from the cellar or the rear wherever possible, so the shed does not hold the project up.

How do you keep rats and mice from chewing fiber in a basement or warehouse?

Rodents damage cable everywhere in the city, and food businesses and older cellars see more of it. Fiber in an exposed basement or warehouse route goes into metal conduit or uses interlocking-armored cable, and enclosures are closed with sealed cable entries rather than open knockouts. We also keep cable off the floor and away from the corners and wall bases where rodents travel. If a site has a history of chewed cable, we note the hotspots on the walkthrough and route around them where we can.

New buildings, venues and public institutions

Which fiber items belong on the architect's drawings for a new building, and which can wait for the fit-out?

Anything buried in concrete, masonry or the foundation belongs on the drawings: the conduit from the street or property line to the main point of entry, sleeves through every slab in the telecom closet stack, conduit between buildings on a multi-building site, and the size and location of the main telecom room and each floor closet. Those are cheap during construction and very expensive afterwards. The cable itself, the enclosures, the splicing and the testing can follow later, during fit-out, once ceilings are open but before they close. For the many new projects planned around the Bronx Metro-North station areas, the time to call is before the foundation drawings are final.

How is fiber supported across a hall with a very high, column-free ceiling?

With planning and the right lift. Halls like the Kingsbridge Armory's 180,000-square-foot drill floor, gyms, arenas and big warehouses leave no columns to follow, so cable has to ride the roof structure or a dedicated messenger or tray system fixed to it. We confirm with the owner's engineer what may be attached to the trusses, choose supports at spacing that suits the cable, and keep the route away from roof drains, heaters and sprinkler mains. Work at that height uses a scissor or boom lift, which has to be scheduled around the venue's events and floor finishes.

Can a Bronx school or library use E-Rate funding for internal fiber?

Often, yes. The Universal Service Administrative Company lists cabling among the Category Two internal connections that E-Rate can support inside instructional buildings of a school campus or non-administrative library buildings, with discounts of up to 90 percent depending on the applicant. The programme has its own competitive bidding rules and deadlines, so the school or library must run the process before work is ordered. We can provide the detailed scope, materials list and documentation a funded project needs; the application itself is the institution's.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fiber Optic Installation in the Bronx

Search for fiber installation costs and you will see confident summaries assembled from national cost guides and provider marketing. Each claim below is quoted from a published source. Each one is true in its own context and misleading if it is applied to a Bronx building without that context.

"Fiber optic cable installation costs average $4,500 for most homeowners"

That figure comes from Angi's 2026 cost article, which gives a typical homeowner range of $1,500 to $7,000. It is a national average built mostly from single-family homes, where the job is often a provider-style drop from the street to the house and a short run inside.

Very few Bronx fiber projects look like that. A six-storey courtyard building needs a cellar distribution point and several risers; a complex needs a site backbone; a market unit needs armored cable and sealed enclosures near cold rooms. Those jobs are priced by the number of buildings, wings, strands and terminations, not by an average house.

The useful takeaway from the national number is that small residential fiber work is not exotic. A Riverdale or City Island homeowner linking a house to a detached studio may well land in that range. A board looking after forty buildings should look at our published unit prices instead.

"Commercial fiber optic cabling services can scale into six figures for complex builds"

Ring and Ping's 2026 cost guide says exactly this, alongside a residential range of $1,500 to $6,000. It is accurate, but the summary makes large projects sound like a single mysterious lump.

In practice, a large Bronx project is a repeated set of known units. Each building on a site needs a link of a certain length, an enclosure, a number of fused terminations and a test per strand. Multiply those by the building count, add any civil work between buildings, and the total explains itself. We publish the unit prices on this page so a board or owner can check the arithmetic.

What genuinely pushes a project into six figures is usually not the fiber. It is trenching between buildings with no existing route, extensive coring through thick prewar slabs, or a requirement to work only at night. We identify those drivers on the walkthrough, before anyone is surprised by a total.

"Single-mode fiber is cheap at roughly $0.09 to $1.49 per foot"

Unio Digital's 2026 guide quotes that range for single-mode material, against $1.50 to $6.00 per foot for multimode, and the same guide puts installed interior runs at $1 to $6 per foot. The material figures are real and explain why single-mode has become the default backbone.

The trap is reading a material price as an installed price. On a Bronx job the cable on the reel is one of the smallest costs. Labour to route it through a cellar full of pipes, firestop each floor, fuse each strand, mount enclosures and test every fiber is what the client is paying for, and it barely changes whether the glass costs nine cents or a dollar a foot.

That is also why we advise buying more strands than you need today. Once the labour of a pull is paid, the extra glass in the same cable costs very little, and every spare strand is a future service that will not need a second pull.

"Verizon coordinates with the property manager/owner to wire and install the service"

Verizon's own New York page says this about apartment buildings not yet wired for Fios. It is accurate as far as it goes: a provider will work with an owner to bring its own service into a building.

What the provider installs is its network, for its customers. It does not build the owner a private backbone for cameras, intercom, door access, boiler controls or the management office, and its cable is not available for the owner's other uses. On a large site, it may also not serve every building, or may serve them on a different schedule.

The right picture for most Bronx owners is two layers: provider networks for resident or tenant internet service, and an owner-controlled fiber backbone for the building's own systems. The two can share pathways and rooms if they are planned together, and we are glad to coordinate with any provider the board chooses.

"No landlord or co-op board approval required, no wiring"

A Riverdale internet-provider guide makes this pitch for 5G home internet: plug-and-play, no installation, no approvals. For an individual resident who wants service quickly, that convenience is real.

It does nothing for the building. Cameras in the garage, door readers in the lobby, the super's office, the laundry room's card system and the management company's computers still need wired connections that do not depend on a cellular signal through masonry walls. Wireless home service also does not help a business that needs a stable link between two buildings or floors.

Boards sometimes hear "wireless" and assume wiring is obsolete. The opposite is true on large sites: the more wireless devices there are, the more access points and switches need a solid wired backbone behind them.

"In Manhattan and the Bronx, you rent the underground"

Low Voltage New York's 2026 cost guide makes this point about street conduit, referring to Empire City Subway, the Verizon subsidiary that has held a city conduit franchise since 1891. For anyone running fiber under a public street, it is an important fact.

Most Bronx owner-side projects never touch the street. Fiber between buildings on a complex, a campus or a single lot travels through the owner's own basements, tunnels, conduit or courtyards. The street conduit question arises only when two properties are separated by a public street and no provider service can link them.

When it does arise, we tell you at the walkthrough, explain the options — a provider circuit, a permitted crossing, or a wireless link — and price only what is realistic. We do not let a street-crossing problem hide inside a quote for work on your own property.

"Nearly 99.8 percent of the Bronx is now wired for high-speed internet"

The Center for an Urban Future reported this in July 2025, and an AI summary might conclude the borough has no wiring problem left. The same report, however, immediately notes that individual buildings are not yet wired and that competition is limited in some areas.

Availability statistics measure whether service reaches an address, usually the building's edge. They say nothing about whether a riser exists to the upper floors of the far wing, whether the provider's equipment in the cellar can reach the security desk in another building, or whether a market unit's office can talk to its warehouse.

So the statistic is good news — providers are at the door almost everywhere — and it shifts the work inside. In most Bronx buildings the missing piece is no longer the street; it is the owner's side of the wall.

What to Look For in a Bronx Fiber Proposal

Boards and managing agents usually collect two or three bids for anything that touches several buildings. These are the lines that make proposals comparable — and the gaps that make the cheapest one expensive later.

Where an owner's fiber fits is everything around that. Coordination is the key word. Mitchell-Lama and other regulated owners also tend to have funding cycles and agency approvals, so our proposals can be phased to match a capital plan rather than forcing one large spend.

One more practical point: residents notice when work happens in their building and assume it is about their internet. Clear notices that explain the work is for building security and operations, not a change to their service, prevent a great many calls to the management office.

Field Notes From Bronx Cellars and Market Floors

How our installers approach a Bronx site walk — standing practice, not a description of one particular job

The first stop on a Bronx walkthrough is the boiler room, not the network closet. It tells us how the building was built, where the old wiring went, how hot and wet the cellar gets and, often, where the only continuous path between wings runs. Supers know these cellars better than any drawing, so we ask them first.

The second habit is counting doors. In a courtyard building, every stair hall is a separate riser decision; on a complex, every building is a separate link. Writing that count down on the first visit keeps the proposal honest and stops the "one more building" surprise halfway through the job.

The third is asking when the place is quiet. In a market unit that might be mid-afternoon; in a co-op it might be a weekday morning; in a school it is a holiday week. Fiber work goes faster and more safely when it fits the building's rhythm, and the people who live and work there remember it.

FAQ

Bronx Fiber Optic Installation FAQ

Can fiber connect our boiler-room controls or building management system to the office?
+

Yes. Boiler controllers, heating sensors and building management panels usually sit in a cellar far from the management office or the engineer's desk. A pair of strands from the site backbone, with a small switch or media converter at each end, gives the controls a reliable wired path without a separate cellular modem. The controls vendor still configures their own equipment; we provide and test the link.

Do you carry fiber materials on the truck, or does everything have to be ordered?
+

Common items travel with the crew: standard indoor single-mode and multimode cable, pigtails, splice trays, small enclosures and patch cords. Armored, outdoor, high-strand-count or specialty cable and larger enclosures are ordered for the job once the proposal is approved, which is one reason the walkthrough measures routes before anything is bought. Repairs can often be finished on the first visit with what is on the truck.

What deposit is needed before a Bronx fiber job is scheduled?
+

Our service agreement calls for a 50% deposit to schedule the work and order materials, with the balance due on completion. On a large complex done building by building, the proposal shows exactly what is included so the board knows what the deposit covers before signing.

Can you audit an existing fiber installation before we buy or lease a building?
+

Yes. We trace and label the existing strands, inspect the connectors, run loss and OTDR tests and record where each cable goes. The report tells you which strands are good, which are dark or damaged and what it would cost to bring the plant up to standard, which is useful information to have before closing or signing a lease.

Can the network rack sit in the super's office or a closet instead of a dedicated room?
+

Often, in smaller buildings. A wall-mount cabinet can live in a closet or office as long as the space stays dry, has a dedicated power outlet, does not overheat and can be locked. We avoid boiler rooms and spaces under plumbing, and in larger buildings we recommend a proper room because the equipment and the number of strands grow over time.

Who handles firestopping on the holes you make — you or the building's contractor?
+

We do. Every penetration we make or reuse through a rated floor or wall is sealed with a listed firestop system and photographed, and the photos go into the closeout file. If the building has a preferred firestop product or contractor, we follow that standard.

Do you patch and paint the walls and ceilings you open?
+

We replace ceiling tiles, close access panels and make small plaster and drywall repairs around our work, and we leave surfaces ready for paint. Where a building needs a finish match in a lobby or apartment, the proposal says whether we paint or the building's painter does, so nobody assumes.

What happens if our cellar has old pipe insulation or floor tile that might contain asbestos?
+

We stop before drilling, cutting or disturbing it. The owner arranges testing by a licensed asbestos investigator and, if needed, removal by a licensed abatement contractor. Very often a route can be adjusted to avoid the material altogether, and we look for that option on the walkthrough.

Can you run fiber to a laundry room or package room for card readers and lockers?
+

Yes. Laundry payment systems, package lockers and door readers in those rooms often run on unreliable wireless or a long copper run. A short fiber link from the building's enclosure, or a copper drop from a nearby switch, gives them a stable connection that the vendor can rely on.

Can fiber run near the gas meters and gas piping in a cellar?
+

Yes, with care. Fiber carries light rather than electricity, so it adds no spark or heat, but we never fasten cable to gas piping or use it as a support, keep enclosures and racks away from meter areas, and never block access to meters or shut-off valves. Where the utility or the building restricts work in a meter room, the route goes around it.

Do we need an electrician as well as you?
+

Sometimes. We install the low-voltage fiber, enclosures and racks, but new dedicated outlets or circuits for network equipment must be installed by a licensed electrician. If the walkthrough shows that a room needs power, the proposal says so, and we coordinate with your electrician or one the building already uses.

Will you give a fixed price, or is the work charged by the hour?
+

New installation is quoted as a fixed price per line in the written proposal, based on the walkthrough. The hourly rate of $195 with a three-hour minimum applies to repairs, troubleshooting, labor-only work with owner-supplied materials and changes requested after the job starts.
