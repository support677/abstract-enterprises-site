The Neighborhood

Why Castle Hill's Towers, Co-ops and Avenue Storefronts Need Fire Alarms Built Around Elevators and Stairs

Castle Hill needs fire alarm work shaped by height. Unlike the blocks of five- and six-story walk-up and elevator buildings that cover much of the Bronx, this neighborhood packs a large share of its homes into big developments: a 21-story rental building on Lafayette Avenue, the Mitchell-Lama development called Jamie Towers, and the fourteen numbered buildings of the Castle Hill Houses. When people live twenty floors up, a fire alarm is not only about waking them. It is about recalling the elevators safely, telling the fire department exactly which floor and which room, and keeping the building's systems working after the fire is out.

The neighborhood occupies the peninsula south of Westchester Avenue between White Plains Road on the west and Westchester Creek on the east, running down to the East River at Castle Hill Point. Unionport is usually counted as the northern part of it, above Lafayette Avenue. The 2010 census counted 38,557 people here, and about 73 percent of homes are rented. The New York City Housing Authority finished the Castle Hill Houses at the end of the 1950s, adding more than 2,000 apartments at once, and the neighborhood's storefront streets grew around the elevated Pelham Line station at Castle Hill Avenue and Westchester Avenue.

The recent fire record shows why the elevator question matters. Late on December 3, 2025, a two-alarm fire burned through apartments on the 20th and 21st floors of 1965 Lafayette Avenue, a 21-story building with 353 rental apartments built in 1969. Firefighters had it under control in about an hour and only one person was hurt. Two weeks later, News 12 Bronx reported that the damage had taken four elevators out of service and that more than 300 residents were sharing a single service elevator or climbing more than a dozen flights of stairs, describing them as "stuck in their own homes." That is what a high-rise fire looks like on the morning after, and a large part of it has to do with how the fire alarm, the elevator controls and the building's repair plans fit together.

The towers are not the whole story. In January 2025, 60 FDNY and EMS members responded to a fire in an eight-story building on Seward Avenue between Pugsley and Olmstead Avenues. In March 2024 a fire on the second floor of an apartment building on Lyon Avenue sent two people to the hospital. On August 20, 2026, more than 140 fire and EMS personnel were called to a one-story commercial building on Castle Hill Avenue. And in July 2023, a fire that started in a three-story building on Castle Avenue in Unionport spread next door, partially collapsed a roof and injured seven firefighters and one civilian. Each of these buildings is a different fire alarm problem, and commercial fire alarm installation in Castle Hill has to start by naming which one you have.

Need Fire Alarm Repair in Castle Hill? The First Hour in a High-Rise

If the panel in a Castle Hill building is beeping, showing a trouble or supervisory light, or sounding with no fire, call (646) 490-0629 for emergency fire alarm repair, and call the central station that monitors the building before anyone presses a button. Emergency fire alarm service in a tower begins with four facts: what the display says word for word, which floor or device it names, whether the elevators have recalled to the lobby, and whether anything happened that day, such as a sprinkler shutdown, a contractor cutting into a hallway ceiling or a burst pipe. Our emergency fire alarm technician uses those facts to bring the right parts, because emergency fire alarm troubleshooting goes faster when half the cause is known before the van leaves. Many trouble conditions are fixed in one or two hours once the technician is on site.

Do not take out the batteries, switch off the panel's circuit breaker, or disconnect a smoke detector because it keeps tripping. Silencing a trouble buzzer is acceptable; disabling the system is not. If the system has to be out of service, the FDNY's fire watch rules apply until it is restored, and in a building of twenty floors a fire watch is a serious staffing job. If you need after hours fire alarm service, call anyway. Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Castle Hill's Building Types

Commercial fire alarm systems in Castle Hill fall into six shapes, and each is designed differently. The design professional starts from the occupancy and the code; our survey shows what the building will physically allow, from cable routes to where a panel can live above the flood line.

Twenty-story rental towers on Lafayette Avenue

A tower like 1965 Lafayette Avenue, 21 floors and 353 apartments, has everything a smaller building has plus an elevator bank, a fire command point near the lobby, stairs that firefighters use to reach the fire floor and mechanical spaces on the roof. The fire alarm in a building like this has to do jobs that a six-story building's system never does: recall the elevators to the right landing when a lobby or machine-room detector trips, send them to an alternate floor when the trouble is in the lobby itself, release the doors and shut down the fans the drawings name, and show the responding officer which floor is involved. When a fire damages a floor, every device that was wired through that floor has to be checked before the system is called normal again, and that is where a repair schedule meets the elevator contractor's schedule.

Jamie Towers is a 620-unit Mitchell-Lama development under state oversight, and in June 2026 Assembly Member Karines Reyes announced new state funding for it, split between operating costs and capital repairs such as roofs, steam pipes, plumbing and boilers. "I know there are challenges in the development and we haven't forgotten," Reyes told residents. Developments like this run capital work through a board, a managing agent and a state supervising agency, so fire alarm work has to be documented in a way that survives all three. Field Notes

Field Notes: The Elevator That Came Back Down

This is a typical scenario rather than a single job. A managing agent for a Castle Hill tower calls because, after a small fire on an upper floor, the elevators keep recalling to the lobby and will not return to normal service, and residents are angry. The panel shows a trouble and an alarm memory on one floor. The super has reset it twice.

The first thing we check is not the elevator. Water from the hose lines ran down the shaft and into the hallway ceiling below the fire floor, and the smoke detector in that floor's elevator lobby is wet. A wet detector can drift into alarm, and an elevator lobby detector is exactly the device that sends the cars home. Every reset brings the cars back for a few minutes before the detector trips again. The second thing we check is the wiring between that detector and the relay that talks to the elevator controller, because water that reached one device may have reached the junction box beside it.

The fix is ordinary: dry or replace the affected detectors and modules, megger the circuit, and test the recall from that floor with the elevator mechanic present so both sides see the same result. What makes it Castle Hill is the scale. In a 21-story building with four elevators, every hour of recall means hundreds of people on the stairs. That is why we bring the elevator company into the plan on day one, and why the closeout report lists every device on the fire floor and the floor below by address, with its test result.

Your Questions Answered

Castle Hill Fire Alarm Questions From Tower Owners, Co-op Boards, Merchants and Supers

These are the questions Castle Hill owners, boards, managing agents, store tenants and supers bring to us, grouped by subject. Each answer explains how the FDNY process applies; what your particular building requires always comes from your design professional.

Cost and budgeting

After the December 2025 fire on Lafayette Avenue, our tower's lender asked for a fire alarm budget line. How do we build one before any drawings exist?

Split it into the four pieces that are billed by different people: the design professional's sealed drawings, the FDNY filing and any resubmission, the installation, and the acceptance test with retests and closeout. Add the central station contract as an annual cost and a contingency for the elevator contractor's time, because in a tower the recall testing needs the elevator mechanic on site. A survey gives the lender a scope outline and a list of assumptions; a real fire alarm installation bid can only be written against the drawings. Anyone who gives a lender a single number for a 21-story building before walking it is guessing. Once drawings exist, a fire alarm system installation proposal lists every device count, interface and test the scope includes.

Our Mitchell-Lama board received state capital funds. Can fire alarm work come out of that money?

That is a question for your supervising agency and your board's counsel, not for us. What we can do is write a fire alarm system installation estimate that separates code-required items, such as correcting an FDNY deficiency or replacing an unsupported panel, from improvements the board may want, such as voice notification or new annunciators. Clear separation is what lets a board show where each dollar went when the state reviews the spending.

Quality and trust

How do we check that the company servicing our Castle Hill tower's panel is allowed to touch it?

Ask for two things in writing: the company's FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate and the name of the principal who holds the FDNY S-97 Certificate of Fitness. The FDNY requires any company that installs, inspects, tests or services fire alarm components, other than only smoke detectors, to hold the company certificate. For installation, also ask which NYC licensed electrician will be on the filing. A fire alarm company that cannot answer those questions should not be working on a high-rise system.

Residents on our upper floors say they did not hear anything during the last alarm. Was the system working?

Possibly yes. Many apartment buildings are designed without horns in every corridor, and in a residential high-rise the signal often goes first to the central station and the fire department, with notification limited to certain floors or spaces. Whether your building should have louder or wider notification is a design question. If residents expect something the system was never designed to do, the honest answer is to explain the design, and if the design is now out of date, to file an upgrade.

Who does what

Our super wants to swap the cellar smoke detectors himself during the boiler shutdown. Is that allowed?

Replacing devices on a building fire alarm is servicing the system, and the FDNY expects that work to be done by a certified fire alarm company. Even an identical replacement has to be the right listed model, programmed to the right address and tested, and the change has to show up in the building's records. The super's job is to notice and report; ours is to replace, test and document.

A tenant wants to open a dialysis and therapy office in our Castle Hill Avenue storefront. Who decides what fire alarm it needs?

The design professional, working from the occupancy, the occupant load and what the building already has. Medical uses can change the occupancy classification and the detection required. We survey the space, report what the building's existing system covers and what it does not, and then work to the drawings the professional produces. If the code does not require a system, we say so.

Technical

Why do our tower's elevators go to a different floor when the lobby smoke detector trips?

Because sending the cars to a lobby full of smoke would be dangerous. Elevator recall is normally designed so that detectors in the elevator lobbies and machine room send the cars to the main recall floor, and if the detector on that floor itself is the one in alarm, the cars go to an alternate floor instead. The drawings define which devices do what, and the acceptance test proves each path.

Water from firefighting reached three floors of our building. Do we have to test the whole system again?

Every device, circuit and module that water may have reached should be inspected and tested before the system is declared normal, and anything damaged should be replaced. Whether a full retest is needed depends on the extent of the damage and what was replaced. The building's records should show which devices were checked, by address, with the result. Doing less invites a ground fault or a false alarm weeks later.

Our panel near Westchester Creek keeps showing ground faults after storms. What is going on?

What causes a fire alarm ground fault? The most common cause is water or moisture reaching a cable or device, often at an exterior wall, a roof device, a parking-level detector or a junction box in a damp cellar. The low-lying blocks near the creek and the East River add flooding and condensation to the list. Ground fault troubleshooting means splitting circuits until the faulted section is found and fixing the cause, not just the symptom.

Owners, tenants and boards

A restaurant on Westchester Avenue wants to add a hood. Does our building's fire alarm have to change?

If the building has a fire alarm, the hood's suppression system is normally supervised by it, so yes, the building's system is modified and the change is filed. The lease decides who pays. Kitchen hood suppression monitoring means the panel reports when the hood system discharges or is impaired, so the central station and the FDNY learn about a kitchen fire even if no one calls.

The central station called our managing agent at 3 a.m. about a trouble signal. What should the agent do?

Write down exactly what the central station reported, ask the super to read the panel display, and call the service company. If it is a trouble and not an alarm, the building is not in danger from the signal itself, but the system may not be able to report a fire until the trouble is fixed. A trouble on a communicator is especially important, because it means the building cannot call for help on its own.

Complaints and problems

Our system was installed by a company that has since gone out of business and left no drawings. Can it be saved?

Often yes, but someone has to rebuild the records. We survey the system, identify the panel, check whether it is still supported by the manufacturer, and trace the circuits. If the FDNY has an approval on file, the design professional can work from it; if not, the system may need to be filed as new work. Either way, the result should be a set of as-built drawings that the next service company can use.

Neighbors in our co-op complained that the alarm test lasted all afternoon. Can testing be shorter?

Testing can be planned to bother people less: notice to residents in advance, testing floor by floor, scheduling noisy devices for set hours, and putting the central station on test only for the time it is needed. But every device has to be tested to NFPA 72 schedules, and a large development has many devices. The way to shorten testing is to keep the system healthy so retests are rare.

Addressable or conventional: what the choice means in a Castle Hill tower

What is an addressable fire alarm system? One in which each device has its own identity on a signaling line circuit. What is a conventional fire alarm system? The lease decides. The hood's suppression system is normally supervised by the building's fire alarm, so the building's system is modified and filed. Either party can pay for the design, filing and installation, but we need to know who the client of record is before writing the scope.

Do storage buildings near Westchester Creek need different detectors?
+

Often. High ceilings, unheated rooms, dust and vehicles make spot smoke detectors slow or prone to false alarms. Beam detectors, air sampling or heat detectors may suit those rooms better. The design professional chooses based on the space and the code.

What records should a Castle Hill board keep for its fire alarm?
+

The FDNY Letter of Approval, approved drawings and as-builts, every inspection and test report with device addresses, battery records, the central station contract, and any Notices of Defect with their corrections. Keep them in the building where the FDNY and the service company can find them.

How fast can a technician get to a Castle Hill building?
+

We book the survey or a service visit within 2 hours of your call. When a technician arrives depends on that day's workload and how urgent the problem is: a tower whose panel cannot report a fire comes before a routine inspection, and if a part has to be ordered we say so on the first call.
