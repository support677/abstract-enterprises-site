The Neighborhood

Why DUMBO Properties Need Access Control

Start with what the buildings were. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries the waterfront under and between the two bridges was a manufacturing district. Warehouses and factories made machinery, paper boxes, spices and Brillo soap pads; the cardboard box was invented in Robert Gair’s building on Washington Street, and the area was known for years as “Gairville.” The National Register of Historic Places listed the DUMBO Industrial District in 2000, with 95 contributing buildings, and the listing notes that the district includes the earliest large-scale reinforced concrete factory buildings in America. Benjamin Moore, Arbuckle Brothers, J.W. Masury & Son, E.W. Bliss and Brillo all made things here. Those buildings were designed for freight, not for front doors: wide floors, heavy columns, big elevators and loading entrances on the street.

Then the use changed. Artists looking for cheap, large lofts started moving in during the late 1970s, and the name “DUMBO” itself dates to 1978, coined by new residents who hoped an unattractive name would keep developers away. It did not. Developer David Walentas and Two Trees Management bought many of the buildings in the late twentieth century, and conversions followed: the Clocktower Building at 1 Main Street, a 1914 Gair factory, became 124 condominiums in 1998; the Sweeney Building at 30 Main Street, a 1908 factory, followed in 2002; 70 Washington Street, built in 1910–11, became the largest conversion at 259 residences in 2005. These are named only as examples of the building type; they are not AESS clients, and nothing on this page describes work in them.

Now DUMBO is also an office district. Wikipedia, citing USA Today in 2013, describes about 500 tech and creative firms employing more than 10,000 people within a ten-block radius, and the neighborhood is the center of what is called the Brooklyn Tech Triangle. Etsy has its headquarters in the old Gair building, and West Elm is headquartered here too. The DUMBO Improvement District’s 2023 annual report counts more than 1,000 creative offices and 103 independent businesses in the district. Many of those offices sit in the same kind of converted loft as the condos, sometimes in the same building, and several office buildings advertise lobbies with 24/7 access.

Put those three facts together and the access problem becomes clear. A DUMBO building may have residents who expect a quiet, locked lobby at night, office tenants whose staff arrive at 6 a.m. and leave at 2 a.m., couriers and food delivery all day, movers booked on the freight elevator, and a shop at street level that has nothing to do with any of them. Keys cannot sort that out. Neither can one shared code on a keypad. What works is a system in which every person carries their own credential, every door knows which groups it serves and when, and each group — the condo board, the landlord, each office tenant — can add and remove its own people without touching anyone else’s.

The street also matters. DUMBO’s streets are Belgian block, the cobblestones that appear in every photograph of Washington Street. The city finished a $108 million reconstruction of those streets and the utilities beneath them in 2025, restoring the cobblestones on 25 blocks and converting one more, Gold Street between Front and Water Streets, from asphalt to cobblestone. Old rail tracks from the Jay Street Connecting Railroad still show in some streets. For entry systems this means street-level work has to respect a landmarked streetscape: readers, gate operators and conduit go on the building side, not in the sidewalk or roadway.

And crime here looks like crime in any busy shopping district rather than in a residential side street. In 2025 Brooklyn Paper’s 84th Precinct reports included a beauty school on Pearl Street whose large glass door was shattered before dawn on August 17, with two cash registers taken, and an athletic-wear shop on Front Street that was robbed twice in two days in July, losing about $300 in shirts the first time and more than $6,000 in merchandise the second. Neither of those is a story about access control; one is a smashed door and the other is daytime theft from an open shop. We mention them because they show the real risks on DUMBO’s retail blocks, and because they help explain what an entry system can and cannot do, which we come back to below.

DUMBO at a glance: facts we used to plan this page

  • Small and dense: about 0.11 square miles with 4,975 residents in the 2020 census, ZIP code 11201, part of Brooklyn Community District 2, policed by the 84th Precinct at 301 Gold Street.
  • Two historic designations: National Register DUMBO Industrial District (2000, 95 contributing buildings) and the city’s DUMBO Historic District, designated unanimously on December 18, 2007 as New York City’s 90th historic district, with roughly 91 buildings between John, York, Main and Bridge Streets.
  • Buildings from the 1850s to the 1920s: the Historic Districts Council dates the earliest commercial structures to the 1850s; materials run from brick and heavy timber to reinforced concrete.
  • Offices next to homes: the DUMBO Improvement District lists office buildings such as 10 Jay, 20 Jay, 45 Main, 55 Washington, 68 Jay and 53 Pearl Streets and the Dumbo Heights campus at 81 Prospect, 117 Adams, 77 Sands, 77 Prospect and 175 Pearl Streets, plus coworking at 68 Jay, 155 Water and 195 Plymouth Streets.
  • Transit: the F train at York Street, the A and C at High Street, the B25, B67 and B69 buses, and NYC Ferry at the DUMBO/Fulton Ferry landing.
  • Waterfront exposure: DNAinfo reported on October 31, 2012 that Sandy left DUMBO businesses and residences under multiple feet of water, with standing water in shops at the end of Main Street.

Most DUMBO buildings that add readers end up pairing them with cameras at the same doors, and many also replace an old buzzer panel at the same time; we cover both on the same visit when the building wants it.

Repair

Need Access Control Repair in DUMBO? Same Day

Some calls cannot wait for a quote. A lobby reader that has gone dark on a Friday evening, a freight entrance that will not relock after a move, an office door that suddenly rejects every badge after an internet outage, a maglock that holds even when someone presses the exit button — those are repair calls, and we treat them as repairs. Ring (347) 934-8335, tell us the address, the door and what it is doing, and we give you a two-hour arrival window. We work on systems we installed and on systems we did not; many DUMBO buildings have readers from two or three earlier installers, and part of the first visit is often working out which controller still runs which door.

Most single faults are found and fixed within an hour or two on site: a strike power supply that has failed, a reader cable cut during a fit-out, a door closer that no longer pulls the door shut against the latch, a controller that lost its network address, or a credential database that was never backed up. If a part has to be ordered, we leave the door in a safe state — locked with a working key or exit device, never propped — and tell you exactly what is on order.

Behind those cards there is one principle we apply to every DUMBO building: the person who is accountable for a group of people should be the one who controls their access. The condo board controls residents. The landlord controls the base building and the freight elevator. Each office tenant controls its own staff. The shop controls its own employees. Modern cloud-managed systems can be set up with separate administrator accounts that each see only their own people and doors, and that is how we configure them.

Building Specialists

Access Control for DUMBO’s Architecture

Very few DUMBO properties are houses. Almost everything is a loft building of one kind or another, and each kind has its own door problems.

Converted factory condos

Twelve-story concrete or timber factories turned into condominiums from 1998 onward. Typical features: a staffed or unstaffed lobby, two or more freight elevators, wide corridors, stair doors on every floor and a cellar with old service rooms. The access work centers on the lobby, the freight cars and the stairs.

Rental loft buildings

Converted buildings run as rentals by one owner, often with a management office on site. These are Class A multiple dwellings, so the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to any fob, app or biometric entry and the owner’s duties under the Housing Maintenance Code still apply to every apartment door.

Mixed residential and office lofts

Buildings where offices or studios occupy lower floors and apartments sit above, or where both share one lobby. The design goal is two populations with two sets of rules that never see each other’s data.

Office loft buildings and campuses

Whole buildings of offices with 24/7 lobby access, such as those the DUMBO Improvement District lists along Jay, Main, Washington and Pearl Streets and the Dumbo Heights campus. Here the landlord runs the base building and each tenant runs its suite.

Warehouse retail and event buildings

Large converted warehouses with shops, food halls, offices and event space under one roof, Empire Stores on Water Street being the best-known example. Many doors, many operators and long public hours.

Newer waterfront towers and small buildings

Ground-up apartment buildings near the park and around the edges, plus a few small walk-ups and the NYCHA Farragut Houses to the east. Each gets a design that fits its size; a small building does not need a campus system.

How the materials change the work

Reinforced concrete. Early concrete factories have thick slabs and columns. Drilling for cable is slow, noisy and sometimes needs a structural check, so we look for existing paths first: old electrical risers, abandoned sprinkler or pipe sleeves, elevator machine-room chases and the gap above a dropped ceiling. Where no path exists, surface raceway painted to match is often the honest answer, and in an industrial interior it rarely looks out of place.

Heavy timber and brick. The older buildings have timber beams and brick bearing walls. Brick takes anchors well in the mortar joints; timber can be drilled but we keep holes out of the main beams. Lofts with exposed ceilings make cable visible, so we plan the run along existing pipe or sprinkler lines where it reads as part of the building rather than across an open ceiling.

Steel, glass and aluminum doors. Many lobbies were rebuilt with aluminum-and-glass storefront doors or heavy steel doors on pivots. A storefront door with a narrow frame has no room for an ordinary electric strike, so the usual choices are an electrified panic bar, an electrified mortise lock made for narrow frames, or a magnetic lock with the release hardware the Building Code requires. Steel doors can usually take a strike, but on fire-rated stair and corridor doors the strike has to be one that keeps the door latched when power fails.

Freight elevators. Freight cars in these buildings were designed for goods. Many now run with an attendant, a key switch or a car-call restriction. Elevator controls belong to the elevator contractor, so we work with them: we provide the reader and the dry contact that tells the elevator controller a credential was accepted, and the elevator company makes the connection inside the controller.

Cellars near the river. Many DUMBO buildings sit within a few blocks of the East River, and reports from 2012 describe water coming through ground-floor windows on Old Fulton Street and standing in shops on Main Street. We put controllers and power supplies on an upper floor or high in a ground-floor room where a building allows it, and never on the floor of a cellar.

Three groups, three different priorities

Residents care most about the lobby, the freight car and their own corridor, about packages, and about privacy. In a rental building the Tenant Data Privacy Law shapes what the system may record and for how long; in a condo the board’s rules do. Residents generally want the system to be invisible: a fob or phone that works every time, and a building that is quiet and locked at night.

Office tenants care about speed of change. People join and leave every month, contractors come for a week, and a company may take a second floor or give one back. They want to manage their own people, tie access to the same identity that runs their email, and produce reports for clients or auditors without asking the landlord.

Shops, galleries and restaurants care about the hours their doors are open to the public, the back of house, early deliveries and late cleaning. Their staff often work in shifts, and turnover can be high. They need codes or cards that can be issued in minutes and revoked the same day, and a door log they can match against their own camera.

The landlord or managing agent sits across all three. Their concern is the base building: the lobby schedule, the freight elevator, the service entrance, the stairs and the equipment rooms. A good DUMBO system lets each of these groups do its own job without stepping on the others, and that is the design brief we work from.

Historic District

Working Inside the DUMBO Historic District

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the DUMBO Historic District on December 18, 2007, by a unanimous vote. At the October 30 hearing that preceded it, the local council member and the borough president spoke in favor. The district covers roughly 91 buildings, mostly industrial, from brick and timber warehouses to reinforced concrete factories. Inside it, changes to the outside of a building, including equipment fixed to a façade, are reviewed by the Commission.

For entry systems the practical effect is modest if the work is planned for it. The Commission’s permit guidebook allows staff to approve intercoms and doorbells placed within or beside a door opening, attached at mortar joints or plain surfaces rather than ornament, with exposed conduit kept short and painted. Readers usually follow the same logic. In loft buildings the easiest route is to put the reader inside the vestibule or on the door frame rather than on the façade, to bring cable through the wall from the inside, and to choose black or dark bronze housings that disappear against old brick and steel. Ordinary repair and replacement of existing equipment does not need a permit.

Two DUMBO-specific points. First, many buildings here have large loading openings that were later filled with storefronts or glass. Where a reader has to go on that infill, we treat the infill as the place for equipment, not the original masonry around it. Second, the streets themselves are landmark-sensitive and were rebuilt at great cost. We do not cut the sidewalk or roadway for an entry system; gate readers and exit buttons on sidewalk gates are fed from the building side.

We describe each device and its location in writing as part of the quote, so if the building does need a Landmarks application the description is ready. The managing agent or architect usually files; we provide drawings and product sheets.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in DUMBO

DUMBO’s commercial life is not on one avenue; it is spread across a grid of short streets. These are the parts we plan for, and what tends to matter on each.

Jay, Main and Washington Streets: office lofts

The DUMBO Improvement District’s office listings include 10 Jay Street, 20 Jay Street, 45 Main Street and 55 Washington Street, buildings where design firms, record labels, publishers, architects and technology companies share converted floors. The common pattern is a landlord system at the lobby, open to all tenants around the clock, and each tenant’s own reader at its suite. The best setups let one credential open both, with the landlord and tenant each keeping their own administration.

Prospect, Sands, Adams and Pearl Streets: Dumbo Heights

The Dumbo Heights campus spans 81 Prospect, 117 Adams, 77 Sands, 77 Prospect and 175 Pearl Streets, and coworking operators have locations in it. Coworking and shared floors bring a high turnover of members and guests, so the work is less about hardware and more about how credentials are issued and taken back, and how guest passes are logged.

Water Street and Empire Stores

Empire Stores, a Civil War-era coffee warehouse renovated in 2017, now holds about 360,000 square feet of shops, food, offices and event space according to the BID, including West Elm’s flagship and the Time Out Market. Large mixed buildings like this have many operators behind one set of public doors, and the staff doors, stock rooms and offices behind them each need their own rules. Gleason’s Gym, the city’s oldest boxing gym, is also on Water Street, at number 130.

Front Street and the shops around the Archway

Front Street and the blocks around the Manhattan Bridge Archway and the Pearl Street plaza carry boutiques, cafés and restaurants that see heavy weekend crowds; the Archway hosts the Brooklyn Flea on Sundays from April to October and the BID’s summer events. The July 2025 thefts reported at a Front Street shop show the retail risk here is mostly daytime theft, which door hardware can help with only at the edges: a locked stockroom, a staff-only rear door, and in some cases a front door that the shop controls from the counter.

Studios, galleries and education

Galleries, artist studios, performance spaces such as St. Ann’s Warehouse, and schools such as the beauty school on Pearl Street whose glass door was broken in August 2025, all mix public hours with private rooms. Their doors need different behavior at different times of day, and records that show who unlocked what after hours.

Rules that apply to DUMBO entry systems

  • NYC Building Code §1010.1.9.8 (sensor release). An electrically locked egress door released by a sensor must unlock when the sensor detects someone approaching or when power is lost, needs a “PUSH TO EXIT” button 40 to 48 inches above the floor within 5 feet of the door that cuts power directly and keeps the door unlocked for at least 30 seconds, and must unlock when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate.
  • NYC Building Code §1010.1.9.9 (electromagnetic locks). A magnetic lock on an egress door is allowed where the door hardware has a built-in switch that cuts power to the lock in one obvious motion with one hand, unlocks on power loss, and releases with panic hardware where panic hardware is required. Locking devices are listed to UL 294.
  • Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021). Owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access (fobs, cards, apps, biometrics) must give tenants a written data policy, collect only minimum data with express consent, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, never sell it, and never require smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment.
  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. Entrance doors of Class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking, and buildings of eight or more apartments need an intercom from the main entrance with a door release in each apartment. Converted loft buildings fall on the post-1968 side of that line.
  • Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043. Each apartment entrance door needs a key lock, and in Class A multiple dwellings a heavy-duty latch set, a heavy-duty deadbolt operated by key outside and thumb-turn inside, and a chain guard. A reader on a lobby or corridor does not replace those.
  • Loft Law (Multiple Dwelling Law Article 7-C). Some former industrial buildings in the city are interim multiple dwellings under the Loft Board while they are legalized. If yours is one, tell us; changes to entrances and common areas in an IMD can involve the Loft Board’s process as well as the owner’s.

This is a practical summary, not legal advice. We design to these rules and point out where a building’s own lawyer or architect should confirm a detail.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in DUMBO: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up in loft buildings, office floors and shops like the ones in DUMBO. Answers are short first, with the detail after.

Our converted factory condo has a freight elevator that opens straight onto every residential floor. How do we stop it becoming a back door into the apartments?

Control the car, not just the lobby. In many factory conversions the freight elevator lands in a corridor or vestibule shared by several apartments, and sometimes directly at an apartment’s own door. If anyone who reaches the freight entrance can call the car and press any floor, the lobby reader is only half a lock.

The fix is in three parts. First, a reader at the freight entrance on the ground floor so the service door itself is not an open path. Second, a reader in the freight car, or at each landing’s call station, wired so the car accepts a floor only when a credential allows it. Residents get their own floor; movers and contractors get the floor on their booking for the hours on their booking; the super gets everything. Third, door-position contacts on the freight doors at each landing, so the software shows when a landing door is held open longer than a move would need.

Elevator controls are the elevator contractor’s territory. We supply and wire the reader and the relay output that tells the elevator controller a credential was accepted, coordinate the visit, and the elevator company makes the connection inside its controller and confirms the car still behaves correctly on fire service. Budget for both trades on the same day; it saves a second shutdown of the car.

Our loft building has apartments upstairs and offices on the lower floors whose staff come and go at all hours. How do residents and office workers share one lobby without sharing access?

One door, two groups, two schedules, two administrators. The lobby reader accepts both residents’ and office staff’s credentials, but each group is a separate access group with its own doors and hours. Office staff open the lobby and then only their floor or suite, the stair to their floor and their own bike room. Residents open the lobby and the residential floors, the residential amenity rooms and the mail area. Neither group ever appears in the other’s reports.

Administration follows the same split. The condo board or residential manager runs the residential group; the commercial landlord or each office tenant runs theirs. In a cloud system that means separate administrator logins scoped to their own people and doors. The base-building super can see everything for troubleshooting, but adds or removes people only on instruction.

Two details make this work in practice. The lobby schedule should follow the stricter of the two groups at night: if residents want the lobby locked overnight, the office group’s credentials still open it but the door does not sit unlocked for them. And if the offices receive late-night deliveries, they should go to a separate commercial entrance where one exists, rather than through the residents’ lobby.

Residents move in and out through our service entrance on booked days. Can that door open only during a booked move window?

Yes, by tying a temporary credential to the booking. When a move is booked, the manager creates a credential — a PIN, a QR code sent to the moving company’s phone, or a fob handed over at the desk — that opens the service entrance and the freight elevator to one floor for the hours of the booking. When the window closes, it stops working by itself.

Without that, service doors in loft buildings tend to get propped open for a whole moving day and then forgotten. Add a door-position contact and a held-open alarm to the service door so the manager gets a message if it stays open for more than a few minutes outside a booked window, and a camera covering the door so there is a picture if something goes wrong.

For buildings that take many deliveries of furniture and equipment, the same tool covers vendors: the installer of a new kitchen gets access on the days in their contract, no more. We set up the booking template with the manager so nobody has to remember the settings each time.

Our lobby door is a heavy aluminum-and-glass storefront door with a narrow frame. What kind of electric lock actually works on it?

Usually an electrified exit device or a narrow-frame electrified lock, sometimes a magnetic lock with code-compliant release. Narrow aluminum stiles have no room for the deep pocket an ordinary electric strike needs, and cutting the frame to make room weakens it.

The options, roughly in the order we prefer them: an electrified panic bar (the push bar on the inside withdraws the latch electrically when a credential is accepted, and always lets people out by pushing); an electrified mortise lock built for narrow-stile doors; an electric strike designed specifically for aluminum frames; or a magnetic lock. Magnetic locks are simple to fit but under §1010.1.9.9 of the Building Code the door hardware itself must have a switch that cuts the lock’s power in one motion, and the lock must release on power loss — and under §1010.1.9.8, a sensor-release maglock needs a motion sensor, a PUSH TO EXIT button and fire alarm release. That is more parts and more ways to go wrong.

Two more things about glass doors. The closer has to be strong enough to pull a heavy door shut against wind off the river, or every lock will look broken. And the pivot or hinge needs to be checked before anything is fitted; a sagging glass door that rubs the threshold will defeat any lock.

Three companies share our office floor. Can each one manage its own staff on the same door without seeing the others’ people?

Yes, with separate administrator accounts scoped to each company. The shared floor door belongs to all three, so each company’s staff are in an access group that opens it. Each company’s own suite door, meeting room or storage room belongs only to that company. Each company gets an administrator login that can add and remove its own staff, see its own reports and nothing else.

This matters most when one company grows, shrinks or leaves. The departing company’s administrator removes their staff, or the landlord removes the whole group in one step at lease end, and the other two are untouched. It also matters for privacy: one company’s staff comings and goings are its own business.

We set the naming convention at the start so reports stay readable: company name first, then person. And we agree with the landlord who holds the master account, because someone has to be able to fix things at 2 a.m. when a company’s own administrator is asleep.

The landlord’s lobby system and our suite reader are different brands. Can our employees carry one badge for both?

Often yes, if the two systems can read the same credential format. Most office readers can read more than one card technology. If the landlord issues a card whose format our suite reader can also read, we enroll the same card number in the suite system and the employee carries one card. If the formats are incompatible, a multi-technology reader at the suite usually solves it.

Phone credentials are the other route. Many current systems issue a mobile credential that the phone presents by NFC or Bluetooth. If both the landlord’s system and the suite system accept phone credentials, staff can use their phones at both doors, even though behind the scenes they are two credentials in two systems.

Before buying anything for a suite, ask the landlord’s building manager what card technology and format the lobby uses, and whether they allow tenant readers to share it. We can make that call for you. It is far cheaper to match the building’s card than to have staff carry two.

Can our office door access switch off automatically when HR offboards someone in our identity system?

With several cloud access platforms, yes. Many current systems can synchronize users from a company identity or HR system, so a person added there appears in the door system with the right access group, and a person suspended or removed loses door access at the same moment. Whether this is possible depends on the platform you choose, so we confirm it before quoting.

For tech and design firms this is often the main reason to replace an older standalone system. The old pattern — someone in operations remembering to delete a badge when a person leaves — fails exactly when it matters, during a hurried exit or a busy week. Linking the door to the same account that controls email and laptops removes that step.

Two cautions. Keep at least one local administrator account that does not depend on the identity system, so a sync problem cannot lock everyone out. And decide which identity groups map to which doors at the start, because the system will do exactly what the groups say.

Our building already has a 24-hour concierge at the front desk. Do we still need readers on the street door?

The concierge decides who should get in; the reader handles everyone who obviously should. A concierge cannot watch the door every second of a 24-hour day, take packages, answer the phone and deal with a resident’s problem at once. A reader on the street door lets residents in without the concierge having to buzz each one, and lets the concierge spend attention on visitors, couriers and anyone unfamiliar.

The more important doors in a staffed building are usually the ones the desk cannot see: the freight and service entrance, the garage or bike room door, the stairs from the cellar and the side exits. Those are where a reader, a door-position contact and a held-open alert do the most good, because they tell the desk something it would otherwise never know.

We set up the desk’s screen to show the doors it cares about and to issue visitor passes, while the board or manager keeps the ability to add and remove residents. That split keeps the concierge effective without putting the whole building’s credential list in the hands of whichever contractor staffs the desk.

Our building has entrances on two different streets. Can the quieter one be made exit-only without breaking the fire rules?

Usually yes, because exit-only is not the same as locked. A door that is an exit has to let people out freely. It does not have to let people in. The outside can be fitted with no handle, a blank plate or a reader for staff only, while the inside keeps its panic bar or lever so anyone can leave at any time.

Many loft buildings that fill a block have a second entrance on a side street that was once a loading door or a lobby for another company. Making it exit-only for the public, with a reader for residents or staff who need to come in that way, and a door-position contact plus an alert if it is held open, closes off the path that is easiest to slip through without anyone noticing.

What is not allowed is locking people in. Delayed-egress hardware, which makes a door wait a few seconds before releasing, is a separate legal category with its own rules and is rarely the right answer in a residential building. We check the building’s exit layout with the plan before recommending any change to a required exit.

Thefts at shops near us happened in daylight. Should a small DUMBO boutique keep its front door locked and buzz customers in?

Only part of the time, and only if it suits how the shop sells. The thefts reported at a Front Street shop in July 2025 happened while the store was open. A locked front door with a release button under the counter lets staff choose who comes in at quiet times, and some shops use it in the last hour of the day or when one person is working alone.

A buzz-in door also has costs. Customers on a busy weekend block will walk past a locked door. The door has to open from inside with no special knowledge, so people can always leave. And the release button must be somewhere the person at the counter can reach without leaving the register.

For most shops the better investments are elsewhere: a reader on the stockroom so merchandise is not one step from the shop floor, a staff-only rear or side door, and a camera that clearly covers the entrance. We can set the front door to switch between free entry and buzz-in on a schedule, so the shop does not have to remember.

Bikes keep disappearing from our building’s bike room. What will actually help?

Know who can open the room, and see who did. Bike rooms in loft buildings are often reached from a side entrance or the cellar, and often still open with one shared key that has been copied for years. A reader on the bike room door, a door-position contact and a camera inside covering the door turn “someone took my bike” into a list of the credentials used and a picture of the person.

Limit the room to the people who registered a bike. That alone shrinks the list from every resident or tenant to the handful who use it. If the room is shared between residents and office tenants, give each group its own access and, if the space allows, its own section.

Check the door itself. Bike rooms often have light doors with weak frames and hinges on the outside. A reader on a door whose hinge pins can be pulled is not much protection, so we fit security hinges or hinge studs where needed and make sure the door closes and latches on its own.

Our studio is on an upper floor of an office loft with no front desk. How do clients and couriers reach us when the street door is locked?

A visitor station at the street door that calls the right suite. A video door station with a directory lets a visitor find the studio’s name and call it; staff see the visitor on a phone app or desk screen and release the street door. The visitor’s path then needs one more decision: does the elevator take them only to the studio’s floor, or does a staff member meet them in the lobby?

For couriers, a simpler option is a package drop inside the vestibule that couriers can reach with a delivery code, so the studio is not interrupted every time a box arrives. Several delivery services use per-delivery codes, and the system records each use.

If the building has several tenants with no desk, the directory needs keeping up to date. We give each tenant’s administrator the ability to edit its own directory entry so the landlord is not chasing changes.

Terms used on this page

  • Access group. A named set of people who share the same doors and hours, such as residents, one office tenant or shop staff.
  • Administrator scope. The people and doors an administrator login can see and change; in DUMBO buildings each group usually gets its own scope.
  • Controller. The box that stores the rules and logs and switches the locks; it keeps working for valid credentials if the internet drops.
  • Credential. Whatever a person presents to open a door: a fob, card, phone, PIN or wallet badge.
  • Door-position contact. A small switch that tells the system whether a door is shut, so it can report doors that are forced or held open.
  • Electrified exit device. A panic bar whose latch can be withdrawn electrically for entry while always letting people out with a push.
  • Fail-safe and fail-secure. What a lock does when power is cut: fail-safe unlocks, fail-secure stays locked from outside while still allowing exit.
  • Held-open alert. A warning sent when a controlled door stays open longer than a set time.
  • Multi-technology reader. A reader that accepts more than one card format, used to match a landlord’s badge or to migrate from old fobs.
  • Relay output. A switched contact from the access controller that another system, such as an elevator controller or alarm panel, reads as a signal.
  • Request-to-exit. A sensor or button on the inside of a door that tells the system someone is leaving, so the exit is not logged as a forced door.
  • Smart access system. The Tenant Data Privacy Law’s term for keyless entry such as fobs, cards, apps and biometrics in multiple dwellings.

One equipment point is worth stressing for DUMBO. Many buildings installed fob systems a decade or more ago, and older 125 kHz proximity fobs carry no encryption; inexpensive copiers sold online can clone them. If your building still runs that kind of fob, a migration to encrypted credentials with multi-technology readers lets old and new work side by side for a few weeks while everyone swaps over.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in DUMBO

We could not see Google’s AI Overview answers from our build environment, so this section does not quote them. It corrects claims that appear again and again in generic cost guides and quick online answers, set against what DUMBO buildings actually need.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr often quote numbers for smart locks or for security doors and then let readers assume that is what an access system costs. A battery smart lock on a house door is a different product from a reader, controller, electrified lock and wiring on a commercial or multiple-dwelling door.

Our published Brooklyn price for a single controlled door is $1,500 to $2,500, including reader, electric strike, wiring and ten programmed fobs. Multi-door buildings run $4,000 to $15,000, and full buildings with elevator restriction and parking $15,000 to $40,000 and up. DUMBO is priced at that same Brooklyn base.

What moves a DUMBO job inside those ranges is the building, not the reader: narrow-stile glass doors that need exit devices, concrete that is slow to drill, freight elevators that need an elevator contractor, and how many separate groups need their own administrators.

“A magnetic lock is the easy universal answer.”

Magnetic locks are easy to mount, so quick answers often recommend them. In New York City, a maglock on an egress door comes with conditions: under Building Code §1010.1.9.9 the door hardware needs a built-in switch that cuts power in one motion, and power loss must unlock the door; under §1010.1.9.8 a sensor-released lock needs an approach sensor, a PUSH TO EXIT button and release on fire alarm.

That makes a maglock a system of several parts that all have to work, on a door people must always be able to leave through. On an aluminum storefront lobby door, an electrified exit device usually does the same job with fewer failure points.

We still use maglocks where they are the right tool, but we do not start with them.

“Put a reader on the front door and the building is secure.”

In a DUMBO loft building the front door is often the best-watched door. The freight entrance, the service door used by movers, the side street exit and the bike room door are where people actually get in unnoticed.

The freight elevator is the clearest example. If it can be called from an uncontrolled service entrance and sent to any floor, residents’ corridors are open no matter how good the lobby reader is.

We start every DUMBO survey from the door list, not from the front door, and price the doors that matter most first.

“Face recognition at the lobby is the modern way to go.”

Biometric readers are marketed hard. In a residential building in New York City, the Tenant Data Privacy Law sets rules for any smart access system, requires express consent before collecting reference data, requires destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized and forbids requiring smart access for a tenant to enter their own apartment.

In a mixed loft building, applying a biometric system to residents brings all of that into play. For most residential groups, encrypted fobs or phone credentials do the job without those questions.

Office tenants have more freedom but still have employee privacy to think about. We recommend biometrics only where a specific room justifies it.

“Cloud systems work without internet.”

Some quick answers promise that a cloud-managed door will keep working no matter what. Most good systems keep their rules on the door controller, so doors keep opening for valid credentials if the internet drops. What stops is remote management, live alerts and mobile credentials that need the cloud to validate.

For offices that rely on phones as badges, that difference matters. We fit controllers with local storage of credentials and logs and recommend a fallback card or fob for each person.

We also put the controller’s network connection on a battery backup so a short power cut does not also cut management.

“Any historic building can take any device.”

Generic answers rarely mention landmark rules. Inside the DUMBO Historic District, exterior equipment falls under Landmarks Preservation Commission review, and the Commission’s staff guidance favors small devices within or beside door openings, attached at mortar joints or plain surfaces with minimal exposed conduit.

In practice most DUMBO entry systems can be placed inside the vestibule or on infill rather than on the original façade, which keeps the work simple.

What matters is planning it that way from the start, not discovering it after the reader is on the wall.

“Ring or SimpliSafe is enough for an office.”

Consumer doorbells and alarm kits from ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe are good at what they are for: telling a homeowner someone is at the door, and alerting when a door opens while armed. They do not issue individual credentials, separate administrators per company, or control an electrified lock on a commercial door.

An office with staff turnover needs to add and remove people in seconds and see who used which door. A shared code on a consumer keypad cannot tell you who entered.

Consumer doorbells can still play a part as visitor cameras on a small studio door, as long as the door itself is controlled by a proper reader.

Our Installation Process

A single suite door and a whole loft building go through the same seven steps. In DUMBO, the step that saves the most trouble is the second one: agreeing who administers whom.

1. Door list and photos

You send the list of doors, who uses each and when, with photos of both sides and the latch edge. We read it before anyone visits and flag the doors that will need special hardware.

2. Groups and administrators

We agree in writing which groups exist (residents, each office, shop staff, vendors), which doors each opens, at what hours, and who administers each group.

3. Site survey with the building

A technician walks every door with the super or building manager, checks closers and frames, finds cable paths through concrete and timber, and notes freight-elevator and landmark issues.

4. Written plan and Brooklyn price

A door-by-door plan, the hardware for each door, the cable route, any elevator or landmark coordination, and a fixed written price from our published Brooklyn figures.

5. Install around building rules

Work booked to the building’s permitted hours and freight-elevator schedule, doors adjusted to latch before electronics, cable run and labelled, controllers mounted above flood level.

6. Enroll, schedule and test

Credentials enrolled per group, schedules set, held-open alerts tuned, and every door tested for entry, exit, power loss and fire alarm release where the code requires it.

7. Handover to each administrator

Each administrator gets a login scoped to their group and a short session on adding and removing people. The building gets a door schedule and a one-page summary of who administers what.

After handover: a short monthly review

Loft buildings change people faster than houses. Once a month, each administrator should open their list and remove anyone who has left: a former employee, an intern whose term ended, a contractor who finished, a resident who moved out. The building manager should look at the held-open alerts for the service and side doors and ask why any door is being propped. Twice a year, test that the lobby and exit doors release when power is cut, and check the controller logs are being purged on schedule if the building is residential.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start From the Freight Door

This is a note on how we survey loft buildings, not an account of any particular customer. When a board or manager asks us to look at a lobby, the first thing we ask to see is the freight entrance. We stand there for ten minutes and watch. Who comes through? How long does the door stay open? Can someone walking in call the freight car, and where can it go?

Then we ride the car to each floor and look at what is on the landing: an apartment door, a shared corridor, an office suite, a stair door. We write each landing down. In factory conversions the freight elevator is often the one piece of the building that still works the way it did when the building made things: it goes everywhere and anyone can drive it.

Only after that do we go back to the front door. Nearly always, the plan that comes out of this has fewer readers in the lobby than the board expected and more control at the service entrance and in the freight car. That is usually where the money does the most good.

Can staff open our office door with a badge stored in Apple Wallet or Google Wallet?

Where wallet badges are not available, most systems offer an app-based phone credential, and we always recommend a physical card or fob as a backup for each person.

Can door events be posted to our team's Slack or Microsoft Teams channel?
+

Many cloud access platforms can send alerts to email or chat tools, either directly or through a webhook or an integration service. Useful alerts for a DUMBO office include a door held open, a door forced, a badge used outside its schedule and the first entry of the day. We set up only the alerts someone will act on, so the channel does not turn into noise people ignore.

When our company's lease ends and we move out, who owns the suite readers and controller?
+

That is set by your lease, not by us. Some leases treat security hardware installed by a tenant as the tenant's property, to be removed at the end; others require it to stay with the space. We list every device we install on the handover sheet so the question can be settled with the landlord, and if you take the system with you we remove it, patch the holes and can reinstall it at the new office.

Can a reader work together with an automatic door opener on our lobby door?
+

Yes. The reader and the opener are wired so that a valid credential first releases the lock and then starts the opener, in that order, and the push plate for the opener only works when the door is unlocked or from the inside. Getting the timing right matters: an opener that pushes before the lock releases strains both. We coordinate with the opener's installer if it is a different company.

Our reader faces a busy sidewalk near the Archway. How do we protect it from stickers, spray paint and tampering?
+

We choose readers with metal or vandal-rated housings for street-facing doors, mount them with security screws, and where the layout allows set them in the vestibule or on the door frame rather than the open façade. A tamper input on the controller reports if a reader is pried off. Stickers and paint are best handled quickly: most reader faces clean with mild solvent, and we show the super which cleaners are safe for the lens and screen.

How can we tell whether our building's existing fobs are the easily copied kind?
+

Look at the fob or card for markings and check the system's documentation, or send us a photo of the reader and a fob. Readers and fobs that work at 125 kHz, often labelled proximity or prox, carry no encryption and can be copied with cheap devices. Credentials at 13.56 MHz that use encryption are much harder to copy. If your building is on the older kind, multi-technology readers let you move everyone to encrypted fobs over a few weeks.

Our company is preparing for a SOC 2 audit. What door-access records can the system give us?
+

A cloud access system can export who has access to which doors, when access was granted and removed, and a log of door events for a chosen period, which are the kinds of records auditors usually ask to see for physical access to offices and equipment rooms. We set up access groups so the server or network room has its own short list, and we show your team how to export the reports. What your auditor requires is for them to say; we make sure the system can produce it.

Can AESS work alongside our general contractor during an office fit-out?
+

Yes, and it is the best time to do it. We give the contractor the door hardware list and back-box positions early so frames are prepared for strikes or exit devices and cable is pulled before ceilings close. We then return near the end to fit readers, terminate and program. Our scope, schedule and insurance documents are provided to the contractor and landlord in whatever form they require.

Can our suite's intrusion alarm arm itself when the last person badges out at night?
+

Many access platforms can be linked to an alarm panel so that a chosen reader or schedule arms and disarms it. A common setup is that the first authorized badge in the morning disarms the alarm and the alarm arms automatically after the last person leaves in the evening, with a manual override for late workers. We check both the access system and your alarm panel support the link before promising it.
