The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Fiske Terrace was built fast and built to last. The land had been part of the Lott farm and then the estate of George Fiske, an oil merchant, when the T.B. Ackerson Company bought about 30 acres of it in 1905. According to Long Island Exchange, Ackerson laid out the streets, put water, sewer, gas and electric lines underground, and had roughly 150 three-story detached houses ready within eighteen months; the company liked to say it had turned the place from woods into city in that time. CityLand describes those houses as built in series of ten from stock plans, while Mary Kay Gallagher's neighborhood guide notes that other builders, C.F. Bond among them, put up many of the houses on East 18th and East 19th Streets. Flatbush Gardener writes that the district's 250 houses were largely finished and occupied by 1914. What no one installed in 1906, of course, was a single strand of data cable, and that is the gap this page is about.

Today almost every one of those houses is asked to do something its builders never pictured: carry a fast network to three or four floors. A family in a freestanding Fiske Terrace house may have a home office on the second floor, a teenager's room under the roof, a TV in the parlor, cameras at the side door and the garage, and a router sitting wherever the provider's technician happened to drop the line. Wi-Fi from that one router has to climb through two or three wooden floors, plaster walls and long hallways, and it rarely arrives on the top floor in good shape. A Yelp reviewer in a nearby Brooklyn search praised a technician for fixing "wonky WiFi" and cleaning up "all the cables littering my desk," and another, in a search for Ethernet cable, opened by saying a previous installer had run their home network wiring improperly. Both complaints are what we expect to find when a house this size has been networked one gadget at a time.

The harder part is that these interiors are worth protecting. Brownstoner's history of the neighborhood describes the original houses as having heavy oak ornamental mantels, staircases, beamed ceilings, built-in bookcases, bordered parquet floors and elaborate cabinetry. A house on East 18th Street was listed with a parlor that has a coffered ceiling, an arched brick fireplace flanked by glass cabinets and crown molding; a 1910s Arts and Crafts house on East 19th Street was listed with a working wood-burning fireplace between built-in bookcases. Elliman's local guide notes that original built-ins and beamed ceilings survive in many homes alongside updated kitchens and baths. In rooms like these the cable route is not a technical detail. A careless installer can cut into a beam, split a piece of trim or lift a section of an inlaid floor border that cannot be matched, and the whole value of a concealed network is that nobody can tell it is there.

Then there is the outside of the house, which belongs, in a sense, to the whole neighborhood. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District on March 18, 2008, and Brownstoner counts it as Brooklyn's 20th historic district. A resident commenting on that designation wrote that he would now need LPC approval "to paint my house or re-do my driveway." The commission's permit guidance says work that affects a building's exterior in a historic district needs its review, and that includes work in front, side and rear yards. So the moment a cabling plan includes a new entry hole in the siding, a conduit up a side wall, a camera on a porch post or a cable buried across the lawn to the garage, the landmark rules come into play. Inside, the plan is mostly between you and the house. We draw that line on every quote.

Finally, the way service reaches the houses is unusual. The Brooklyn Paper quoted Fred Baer of the Fiske Terrace Association explaining that "we have phone lines in the backyards of our homes," and the paper reported Verizon installing 20-foot poles in the district to guard access points to underground fiber, on East 18th Street between Foster Avenue and Glenwood Road among other spots. CBS New York covered neighbors in Midwood Park and Fiske Terrace "digging in for a fight" over an earthy brown Verizon Fios tower that went up in a single day. Whatever you think of the poles, they tell you two things a cabling plan needs to know: the provider's line may arrive at the back of the house rather than the front, and fiber is present on at least some of these blocks. Where your line lands decides where the network starts. On Ocean Avenue, by contrast, StreetEasy lists Fios as available at pre-war co-ops like 1212 and 1220 Ocean Avenue, and the cabling question there is how the service travels from the basement to each apartment.

  • The houses stand alone. The district is largely detached single-family houses in Queen Anne, Shingle, Colonial Revival and Arts and Crafts styles. The Ackerson series house: four levels and one good vertical path

The houses Ackerson built in series share a basic logic that makes cabling them predictable once you have done one. There is a cellar with open or lightly finished joists, a first floor with the parlor, dining room and kitchen, a second floor of bedrooms off a central hall, and a third floor of smaller rooms under the roof. The best route between those four levels is almost never the stairwell. It is a closet stacked over a closet, a chase beside the plumbing that serves the bathrooms, or the gap beside a chimney breast where the framing leaves a void. We look for the one path that runs from the cellar to the top floor without crossing a finished room, and we try to send every vertical run up it, because one well-chosen path means one set of holes through the floors instead of five.

The cellar then becomes the highway. From the bottom of that vertical path, cable travels along the joists to points directly below each first-floor jack, comes up through the floor inside a wall or a baseboard, and never shows in the room above. On the upper floors, cable leaves the vertical path at each level and travels in the floor or ceiling space to the rooms it serves. Because houses in the same series tend to repeat the same layout, a neighbor's house on the same block can tell us where the stacked closets are before we arrive. That does not replace a walk-through, since a century of renovations has made every house a little different, but it does make the first conversation much more specific.

The house by another builder: no series to copy

Not every house in the district came from Ackerson's stock plans. Mary Kay Gallagher's guide names C.F. Bond as the builder of many houses on East 18th and East 19th Streets, and the designation report describes a range of forms and styles, from foursquares and front-gabled houses to bungalows, Dutch Colonial Revival houses and a few Tudor Revival and stucco houses. Each of these has its own arrangement of closets, chimneys and stairs, and the vertical path we would use in an Ackerson house may simply not exist. In those houses the walk-through takes longer, and we often use a small inspection camera through an existing opening, such as a cold-air return or a gap behind a cellar stair, to see what is inside a wall before deciding to drill.

Some of these houses also have features that complicate the top floor. A gambrel roof makes the third floor larger but leaves only short knee walls at its edges; a bungalow form may have most of its rooms on one or two floors under a deep roof. A Tudor house may have more plaster and less open framing than its neighbors. None of this makes cabling impossible, but it changes what the cable can follow. Where no hidden vertical path exists at all, the fallback is a small, paintable surface channel in the least visible corner of a closet, never along a hall or up a stair, and we tell you on the walk-through if your house is one of those.

The two- and three-family house: one building, separate households

Not every house in and around Fiske Terrace is single-family. StreetEasy lists two- and three-family houses in the neighborhood, including 1525 Avenue H and 1704 Glenwood Road, both built in 1899, before the Ackerson development. In a house with a rented floor, the network question is not only how cable reaches the rooms but whose network it is. The owner may want the tenant on a separate provider, or may provide internet as part of the lease and want the tenant's devices kept apart from the family's cameras and computers. Either way, the cable plan starts by deciding where each household's equipment lives and which floors each cable serves.

The practical answer is usually a single cabinet in the cellar or a shared closet, with each floor's runs landing on clearly separate ports and, if the households share one provider, a router that keeps the tenant's network apart from the owner's. Where the tenant has their own provider, the provider's line is brought to the tenant's floor along a route we agree with the owner, rather than a new hole drilled through the front of the house every time someone switches companies. We also keep anything the building uses as a whole, such as a camera at the side door or a door station, on the owner's side of the cabinet, so a tenant moving out never takes the house's cameras offline with their router.

The house with a freestanding garage: a link across the yard

The Landmarks Commission's designation report notes that freestanding garages began appearing in the district around 1910, many of them catalog designs, set at the end of concrete driveways with grass strips down the middle. Today those garages hold cars, bikes, workshops and occasionally a studio or office, and owners want a camera, an access point or a wired desk out there. The distance from the house is usually short, but the route crosses the yard, and in a historic district that matters. The commission's permit guidance covers work in front, side and rear yards, so a trench across the lawn or a cable strung between the house and the garage is a different conversation from a run inside the house.

Our approach is to find the route that touches the yard the least. Sometimes an old utility path already exists, such as a conduit that once carried power to the garage, and a cable can follow it with nothing new in the ground. Where nothing exists, we lay out the options in writing: an underground run in conduit rated for burial, a fiber or outdoor-rated copper link, or, for a camera alone, a wireless bridge that needs only a small device at each end. We note which options are likely to need LPC review and leave that filing decision with you before any shovel goes in. Inside the garage, cable is kept high and away from tools and doors, because garages are where cables get caught on things.

The Ocean Avenue co-op: shared shafts and a board that decides

The apartment buildings on Fiske Terrace's eastern edge are a different world. Long Island Exchange notes that a number of Ackerson's houses were replaced with apartment buildings in the 1920s, mainly along Ocean Avenue and Avenue H. StreetEasy lists 1212 Ocean Avenue as a 48-unit, six-story pre-war elevator co-op from 1925 with an Art Deco lobby and a live-in super; 1220 Ocean Avenue as a 53-unit, six-story elevator building from 1938, classed as semi-fireproof; and RentHop lists 1290 Ocean Avenue as a 60-unit building from 1928. In these buildings an apartment owner controls what happens inside the apartment walls, but the basement, the shafts, the hallways and the roof belong to the corporation, and the board and managing agent decide what may pass through them.

Most apartment cabling in these buildings is modest: a jack at a desk, a cable to a TV, an access point in the middle of a long apartment, a clean route from wherever the provider's box landed. The work that needs the board is anything outside the apartment: a new run up a shaft, a hole into a hallway, a cabinet in the basement for building cameras or a door station. For that work we provide a scope letter, a certificate of insurance and a drawing of the route, and we keep the building's own systems in the building's own spaces, so a renovation in one apartment never cuts a camera line serving the lobby. Semi-fireproof construction also means concrete or masonry between floors in places a wood-frame house would have joists, so a hole through a floor is a cored sleeve that the building approves, never a quick drill bit.

The house in transition: the best time is when nobody lives there yet

Fiske Terrace houses change hands rarely and are usually updated when they do. The best moment for cabling is the gap between a closing and a move-in, or the weeks when a kitchen, a bathroom or a cellar is opened up for renovation. With the rooms empty, a crew can work in every room without moving furniture, and with a wall or ceiling already open, a cable can be laid inside it for almost nothing compared with fishing it in later. Even a single open ceiling in a first-floor room can become the route to half the house.

The trap is assuming the contractor doing the renovation will think about network cable. Unless someone asks, the walls close with no cable inside them and the new owner is back to fishing wire through finished plaster a year later. If you are buying or renovating, bring us in while the plans are still drawings. We mark where every jack and access point should go, run the cable before the insulation and wall board go up, leave a pull string in any spare path, and photograph each open wall with its cable before it closes, so you have a record of what is inside.

Field Notes: What a Technician Looks For Before Drilling Anything in a Fiske Terrace House

These notes describe how our technicians walk houses like the ones in Fiske Terrace. They describe our practice, not a story about any particular house or owner.

Find the line first. The walk-through starts where the provider's service enters the house, which on these blocks is often a wire arriving from a pole or a line in the back yard rather than from the street. That point fixes one end of the network, and its distance from the middle of the house decides whether the cabinet goes right there or a run carries the service to a better spot.

Go down before going up. In the cellar, the technician looks at the joists, the beam that carries the first floor, the plumbing stacks and any old cable already running along the ceiling. Old runs tell you where earlier installers found a way up, and the plumbing stacks usually point to a chase that reaches every floor.

Open the closets on every floor. Closets stacked one above another are the gift of these houses. The technician checks whether the second-floor closet sits over the first-floor closet, whether the third floor has a closet or a knee-wall space above them, and whether anything in the way, such as a duct or a heavy beam, blocks the path.

Name the things that must not be touched. Before any route is drawn, the technician asks you to point out the finishes that matter most: a beamed ceiling, a stair, a built-in, an inlaid floor border, a fireplace surround. Those are written on the sketch, and no route crosses them.

Check the outside without touching it. If the plan involves a camera, a garage, a porch or a new service entry, the technician notes what would be visible from the street or would disturb the yard, and flags it for a Landmarks check rather than assuming it is fine.

Leave with a sketch you agreed to. Before leaving, the technician sketches each route floor by floor with you, so the written quote describes exactly the path you have already walked and approved, and nothing surprises you on the day of the work.

Plaster, Oak and Parquet: How Cable Travels Through a Century-Old Fiske Terrace House

A cabling system has the same parts in a house as in an office: a place where service arrives, a cabinet where the main equipment lives, cable from that cabinet to each outlet, and the outlets themselves. What is different in Fiske Terrace is what lies between those parts. Here is how the materials and finishes in these houses shape the work.

Wood framing. The houses are framed in wood, which is good news for cabling. A hole through a wooden floor plate or a top plate is quick to drill with a long flexible bit, and cable can travel inside the hollow spaces between studs and joists. The limit is that a framed wall in an old house is rarely empty. It may contain blocking, old gas piping, abandoned plumbing or the bracing that a builder in 1906 thought was a good idea. Before drilling blind, we look inside with a small camera through an existing gap or a tiny inspection hole in a spot that will be covered by a faceplate anyway.

Original plaster. Walls in these houses are mostly plaster, which is harder and more brittle than modern wall board. A jack can still go into a plaster wall, but the opening has to be cut carefully, with a sharp tool and a steady hand, so the plaster around it does not crack or let go of its backing. Where possible we put jacks in baseboards, inside built-ins or in a closet wall rather than in the middle of a finished plaster wall, because those places hide the opening completely and avoid the risk of a crack spreading.

Oak trim and woodwork. The heavy oak mantels, stair parts, door casings and beamed ceilings described in the neighborhood's histories are irreplaceable in practical terms. We do not drill through them, notch them or fasten anything to their faces. Where a cable must pass a piece of trim, it travels behind it through the wall, beneath it through the floor, or around it through a closet. If the only route would require removing a piece of trim and putting it back, we say so on the walk-through and you decide, because even careful removal of century-old trim carries some risk.

Parquet and inlaid borders. Bordered parquet floors are one of the finishes Brownstoner names in these houses, and they are the reason cable comes up from the cellar rather than across the floor. The third floor under the roof. Rooms under the roof often have sloped ceilings and short knee walls with a crawl space behind them. Every hole through a floor gets sealed

When a cable passes from one floor to the next, it makes an opening in a floor that was built to slow the spread of fire and smoke between levels. In a wood-frame house that matters even more than in a masonry building. In the apartment buildings, sealing penetrations through shafts and floors is usually a condition the board or managing agent sets before allowing the work, and we record each one on the route sketch.

Keeping data away from old electrical and heat

Old houses can carry several generations of electrical wiring, some of it still live. Where a run passes near a boiler, a furnace flue or a radiator pipe in the cellar, it is supported on its own hooks at a distance from the heat. If the walk-through turns up electrical wiring that looks unsafe, we stop, point it out and leave it to a licensed electrician; it is not something our crew touches.

Rules and Licenses

The Landmark Line, the 2025 Permit Change and the Co-op Board: What Applies to a Fiske Terrace Cabling Job

We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what the published rules say and how we apply them, so you know what to ask before any work starts.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission and the outside of the house. The Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District was designated in 2008 under LPC file LP-2208. The commission's guidance says that work affecting the exterior of a building in a historic district needs its review, even on parts not visible from the street and in front, side and rear yards. Its guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment says that if an installation needs conduit, the conduit must be concealed. Its rule for small equipment allows staff-level approval when equipment stays off the primary facade unless there is no alternative, sits on plain material, matches the finish and keeps any visible conduit to a short, painted length. A separate LPC rule for wall-mounted mechanical equipment on secondary facades says conduit must not be visible from a public thoroughfare, penetrations must be no larger than three inches across and conduit must be painted to match. That rule is written for mechanical equipment, and we use it only as a guide to what the commission tends to accept for small exterior cable work. An installer's guide for brownstones adds that visible exterior conduit and camera mounts in historic districts may need a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter.

What this means in practice. Cable that stays entirely inside the house, through the cellar, closets and walls, is normally between you and your house. A new service entry, a cable on a side wall, a camera on the house, a device on the porch or a trench to the garage may need review. We design to stay inside wherever possible, keep any outside work small and at the rear or on a secondary side, and tell you in writing which parts of the job to raise with the commission before we do them.

The electrical permit and who may do the work. Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which took effect on December 21, 2025. A New York low-voltage hiring guide summarizes it this way: an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. For most house cabling that means no permit filing, but it does not mean anyone can do it.

Fire alarm wiring is separate. An NYC structured cabling guide notes that fire alarm wiring falls outside the low-voltage exemption and requires specialized licensing and separate permits. Network cable never shares a route with fire alarm wiring in our work, and if your project includes fire alarm work, it is planned and filed on its own.

Cameras and door stations. When the cabling includes cameras, a door station or other security equipment, AESS installs those systems under New York State license #12000287431, so the cable and the devices it feeds are one job under one licensed company.

Boards and managing agents. In the Ocean Avenue co-ops and rental buildings, the board or managing agent usually requires a certificate of insurance, a description of the work and approval for anything outside the apartment walls. We send ours before the visit and keep a copy of the approved route on site.

Period rooms and original woodwork

Our parlor has a coffered ceiling with oak beams. Where can a ceiling access point go without cutting into the original woodwork?

Usually not in the parlor at all. An access point does not have to sit in the room it serves; it has to sit where its signal reaches that room. In many of these houses the better spot is the ceiling of the front hall, the dining room or a closet next to the parlor, or the ceiling directly above in a second-floor hallway, where a small device and a single cable disappear into ordinary plaster. If the parlor really is the only good location, we look for a flat panel between beams that is plain plaster rather than wood, and bring the cable to it from above, through the floor of the room upstairs, so nothing is cut into the coffers. We never drill through a beam or fasten to its face. On the walk-through we test coverage from a few candidate spots before deciding, so the access point ends up where it works and where you cannot see the route.

The living room has a bordered parquet floor. Can a cable reach a desk in the middle of the room without lifting a single piece?

It can reach close to the desk without touching the floor, but not usually the middle of the room itself. The reliable approach is to bring the cable up from the cellar into the baseboard on the wall nearest the desk, drilled from below so the hole is hidden behind the base and shoe molding, and finish it with a small jack in the baseboard. A short patch cable then runs to the desk along the edge of a rug or under the furniture. Cable pulled along the agreed routes, terminated on a patch panel and on jacks, and labeled at both ends to match a printed port map.

Port map, test results and route sketch handed over, cabinet dressed and identified, work areas cleaned, and a walk-through of the house with you.

FAQ

Fiske Terrace Structured Cabling FAQ

Our parlor walls are original plaster. What does that change on the price of mounting a screen?
+

On our Brooklyn list, a wall mount for a screen up to 55 inches is $179.99 with the bracket sold separately, and a plaster wall adds $45, for $224.99 before the bracket. The extra covers locating solid framing behind the plaster and drilling carefully so the surface does not crack. If the screen goes above a fireplace, that adds $130 on top.

How do you keep ladders and boots off our parquet and the stair while the crew works?
+

We lay floor protection along every path the crew uses, put ladder feet on pads, and cover stair treads if anyone has to use the stair to carry material. Most material stays in the cellar and travels up the hidden vertical path rather than through the front hall. Built-ins near any work are covered too, and the protection comes up only when that room is finished.

Before you drill into finished plaster, how do we see exactly where a jack will come out?
+

The technician marks each jack position with low-tack tape and shows it to you before any opening is made. Where we need to see inside a wall first, we use a small inspection camera through a spot that the faceplate will cover anyway. Nothing is cut until you have agreed to the mark.

Our cellar is finished with a drop ceiling. Can it carry the main runs to the rest of the house?
+

Usually, yes, and it is one of the easier finishes to work with. We lift the tiles along the route, support the cable on hooks fastened to the joists above the grid rather than laying it on the tiles, and put every tile back. A finished plaster cellar ceiling is harder and may mean routing along the cellar walls or through a utility room instead.

Will the power in the house need to be turned off while network cable goes in?
+

No. Network cabling is low-voltage work that does not touch your electrical panel or circuits, so the lights, heat and appliances stay on throughout. If the plan includes a new electrical outlet for the cabinet, that part is work for a licensed electrician, and we tell you in advance if it is needed.

Can the router and a printer live inside a built-in cabinet instead of on a shelf?
+

Often they can, if the cabinet has some ventilation and a way for cable to reach its back. We bring the cable up through the back or bottom of the cabinet from below, add a small jack inside, and check that the equipment will not overheat with the doors closed. We do not cut visible openings in the face of original cabinetry.

Our block is short and dead-ends at the train cut. How does the crew handle getting material to the door?
+

A house cabling job needs only a van's worth of cable boxes, a few ladders and tools, so we bring everything in one trip, unload quickly and keep the van out of driveways. If your driveway is free and you are happy for us to use it, mention it when booking; otherwise we plan around the street.

We want the soundbar under the new TV with nothing dangling. What are the options?
+

A soundbar mounted to the wall below the screen is $129 on our Brooklyn list, or $79 placed on a console. On a hollow framed wall, the in-wall power and cable kit at $169 hides the cords inside the wall; on masonry, a paintable cord cover at $59 or a route through a nearby built-in keeps things tidy.

There is an old TV bolted to the wall in the den. Can you take it down on the same visit?
+

Yes. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list, and we do it before mounting the new one so the crew can see the condition of the wall and the old mounting holes. We tell you before mounting if the old holes affect where the new bracket can go.

Can we choose faceplates that look less modern against the period trim?
+

Yes. Jacks and plates come in several colors, and in many rooms the best choice is a small plate low in the baseboard in a color close to the trim, where it nearly disappears. Tell us on the walk-through which rooms matter most and we will show you the options before ordering.

Our house has a deep front porch. Can the Wi-Fi reach it without an access point outside?
+

Usually it can. An access point on the ceiling of the front hall or the room just inside the porch, close to the wall the porch shares with the house, often covers the porch well. Mounting a device outside on the porch would be exterior work in the historic district, so we try the indoor position first and test coverage on the walk-through.
