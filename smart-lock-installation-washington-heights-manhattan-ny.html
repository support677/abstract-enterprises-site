The Neighborhood

Why Washington Heights Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Respects Who Lives Here

Washington Heights is a neighborhood of long tenancies and full households, and that changes what a smart lock is for. The NYU Furman Center's 2026 profile of Manhattan Community District 12, which covers Washington Heights and Inwood, counts about 182,700 residents and a homeownership rate of 12.3 percent against 32.7 percent citywide. Nearly nine in ten households rent. Almost one resident in five is 65 or older, and more than a third of households are a single person living alone. Wikipedia's history of the neighborhood describes it as set apart among Manhattan neighborhoods for its high residential density despite the lack of modern construction: the housing is overwhelmingly the five- and six-story apartment houses that went up after the IRT subway reached 157th, 168th and 181st Streets between 1904 and 1906, and the larger elevator buildings of the 1920s boom north of 181st Street.

That stock means most of the doors we are asked about belong to someone else. A rent-stabilized tenant in a pre-war walk-up off St. Nicholas Avenue does not own the door, the frame or the lock the landlord fitted. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law gives that tenant a right to install a lock of their own in addition to the owner's, provided a duplicate key is given to the owner on request. So the job is almost never "replace the lock". It is: keep the owner's lock working, add a smart lock the household controls, and do it without changing the parts of the door the landlord is responsible for maintaining.

The question of who controls access is not abstract here. In November 2018, Patch and CBS2 reported on 765 Riverside Drive, a six-story building near West 155th Street, where management introduced an electronic fob for the lobby door but approved fobs only for residents named on a lease and asked for a $50 deposit. A resident who had lived there for 50 years told CBS2 he had to send a copy of his marriage certificate before his wife could be considered for a fob. Then-Council Member Mark Levine said the policy broke city rules on keys and pointed tenants to a complaint with the state housing agency. The lobby is the landlord's business. The apartment door is where a household can decide for itself that a spouse, a parent or a home health aide gets in, and a smart lock is the tool that lets them do it without copying keys.

The buildings themselves add a second layer of work. Doors in pre-war Washington Heights apartment houses have been painted, rehung and re-keyed many times over a century; strikes sit in frames that have shifted; two or three locks of different decades are often stacked on one door. The Furman profile records 238.7 serious housing code violations per 1,000 privately owned rental units in the district in 2025, among the measures it tracks year to year. Up on the ridge, Hudson Heights is a pocket of 1920s to 1940s Art Deco and Tudor co-ops such as Hudson View Gardens and Castle Village, where a board rather than a landlord sets the rules. And south of 162nd Street, the Jumel Terrace Historic District and the apartment houses of Audubon Park bring the Landmarks Preservation Commission into any question about what shows on a facade.

Finally, there is the way people come and go. NewYork-Presbyterian and Columbia University Irving Medical Center sit on the high ground at 168th Street, hospitals run around the clock, and plenty of uptown households include someone whose working day ends long after dark. The Furman profile puts the car-free commute share at 84.4 percent, so most residents walk home from the A train at 168th, 175th, 181st or 190th Street or the 1 train along Broadway, then climb. A lock that wants a phone unlocked at 3 a.m. on a dim fifth-floor landing is the wrong lock. One that opens with a fingerprint or a short code, logs who came in, and still takes a key is the right one.

A Washington Heights smart lock has to sit beside the landlord's lock rather than replace it, hand codes to the real household rather than to the lease, fit a door that has been painted and patched for a century, open quickly for someone coming off a night shift, and stay within co-op rules up on the ridge and landmark rules down by Jumel Terrace. How the method changes by building

Walk-ups. We expect paint on every edge and a strike that has been moved at least once. Before choosing a lock we take the door off its latch, see whether it closes on its own weight, and measure what the existing bores will accept. The smart lock goes where it can share an existing hole or the cleanest new one, never through the landlord's cylinder. A small kit of spare batteries goes on the inside of the door's closet shelf for the household, because no one wants to make an extra trip down five flights for AA cells.

Pre-war elevator buildings. These usually have a building-wide key arrangement held by the super, and long masonry corridors that swallow radio signal. We test Bluetooth and Wi-Fi at the door before recommending anything, keep the owner's key path intact, and fit the lock so nothing on the corridor face looks out of place among dozens of identical doors.

Hudson Heights co-ops. The shareholder owns shares and a proprietary lease, not the door, so the managing agent comes first. We prepare a short scope note with the lock's specification and how the building keeps emergency access, then wait for the answer. Many boards care most about the corridor face; inside-only retrofits are often the easiest yes.

Jumel Terrace rowhouses. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules treat repairing or replacing door hardware such as hinges, knobs and handles as ordinary repair — but they carve out ornate historic exterior hardware on special doors. So we photograph what is there, keep anything ornate in place, put the electronics on the inside face, and choose a keypad whose finish disappears against an old door rather than shouting from the stoop.

Newer buildings and shopfronts. Newer apartment doors take smart locks easily, but the management agreement decides. On storefronts, a residential deadbolt rarely fits a narrow aluminium stile, so the choice is between a commercial keypad lock built for that door and a reader with an electric strike in the frame. Most shop owners who add staff codes also want cameras that show the door — our commercial security systems for Manhattan businesses cover both.

Compliance callouts for uptown business doors

  • Magnetic locks on egress doors: the NYC Building Code, section 1010.1.9.9, allows electromagnetic locks on egress doors and doors to tenant spaces in business, mercantile and certain other occupancies only with door-mounted hardware that has a built-in switch, can be worked with one hand and an obvious motion, cuts power to the lock directly, and releases when power fails.
  • Fire-labelled doors: the label on a rated door or frame limits what may be cut on site. NFPA 80 allows round holes for listed hardware; a rectangular strike pocket or other cutout counts as a field modification that the listing lab must approve (Lori Greene's iDigHardware columns walk through this). If a back door or stair door carries a label, we work inside those limits.
  • Permits for wiring: a reader, strike and controller run on under 50 volts. Section 28-105.4.7 of the city's Administrative Code says such signalling, alarm, communication and data wiring needs no electrical permit when installed by a licensed master or special electrician or by a qualified person as the city electrical code defines one. The 120-volt feed to a power supply is separate electrician's work.
  • Landmark facades: in the Jumel Terrace and Audubon Park historic districts, anything new on the exterior is an LPC question; repairing or replacing ordinary door hardware is treated by the Commission's rules as ordinary repair, except ornate historic hardware on special doors.
  • Accessible operation: a keypad mounted too high or an exit lever that needs a firm twist fails customers using wheelchairs or living with arthritis, so we set heights and choose hardware that opens without a tight grip or wrist turn.

We quote these provisions so owners can look them up, not as legal advice. Shops that add staff codes usually want to see the door as well — see our commercial security camera installation in Manhattan.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Washington Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions uptown households, co-op shareholders and shop owners actually put to us, grouped by what is really being asked. Where a law or a published rule decides the answer, we name it so you can read it yourself; nothing here is legal advice.

Codes for everyone who really lives here

A relative lives with us but isn't on the lease — can she have her own code on a lock we put in?

Yes. A smart lock that you add to your apartment door is yours to program, and the people who get codes are whoever the household chooses: a mother who moved in after a hospital stay, an adult son, a cousin staying for the year. The lease decides who the landlord deals with; it does not decide who you may let through your own door. We set each person up with their own code rather than one shared number, so the activity log shows that she came in at 4:10 and her grandson at 6:45, and so you can change one person's code without disturbing anyone else. For a relative who will never use a phone, the code is the whole system; we tape nothing to the door and give her a printed card to keep in her purse. What the household must still do is honor the owner's rights: the landlord's lock stays in place and the owner can ask for a duplicate key to the lock you added.

Our building gives lobby fobs only to people named on the lease — does a unit smart lock have to follow that rule?

No; those are two different doors. The street or lobby door belongs to the building, and the owner runs whatever system it chose there, within city and state rules. When residents of 765 Riverside Drive were told in 2018 that only leaseholders would get lobby fobs and that each fob needed a $50 deposit, then-Council Member Mark Levine told CBS2 the policy violated city rules on keys and suggested a complaint to the New York State Division of Housing and Community Renewal if management would not fix it. That is a building dispute, and we stay out of it. Your apartment door is different: a lock you add there answers to you. So a household member without a fob can still have a personal code upstairs, even if, for now, someone has to buzz them in downstairs. If the lobby problem is the bigger one, the building's owner or board can talk to us about the lobby system separately.

If the leaseholder moves out or passes away, how does the rest of the household keep control of the lock?

Plan for it on day one. Smart lock apps have one owner or administrator account and any number of users; if the only administrator is the person whose name is on the lease and their phone is gone, the household is left with working codes and no way to change them. We set up a second administrator — usually the adult who handles family paperwork — on a separate phone and email, and we record on the handover sheet which account owns the lock. Codes keep working when an account changes hands, so nobody is locked out while the family sorts things out. What the lock cannot settle is the tenancy itself: whether a family member can stay on in a rent-stabilized apartment is a question for the state housing agency or a tenant lawyer, not for an installer. The lock should never become the reason someone loses their home, so we will not program a lock to shut out a person who lives there.

The owner's lock and the owner's duties

Our door already has two locks of different ages — which one should the smart lock replace?

Usually neither of the landlord's. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law section 51-c lets a tenant install and maintain a lock in addition to the lock the owner supplied, of no more than three inches in circumference, provided the tenant gives the owner a duplicate key on request. So the first job at a door with two locks is to work out which is the owner's and which a previous tenant added. If the second lock is an old tenant lock that nobody has a key for, it is the natural place for the smart deadbolt: we remove it, check that the bore and backset suit the new lock, and fit it there, which means no new hole. If both locks belong to the owner, we leave them alone and look for the cleanest spot for a third, or suggest an inside-only retrofit on a lock you already control. Either way the owner's cylinder and key keep working exactly as before.

The landlord's lock on our apartment door is broken — should we just swap in a smart lock ourselves?

Report it first. The city's Housing Maintenance Code makes the owner responsible for a working lock and key on each apartment entrance door, and the LawHelpNY guide to HPD violations lists a defective entrance-door lock or knob as a class B hazardous condition. You file it through 311 as an apartment maintenance complaint and HPD inspects. If you simply take the broken lock out and fit your own in its place, you may end up paying for the owner's repair and muddying the record of a condition the landlord was required to fix. The better pattern is to let the owner repair or replace their lock, then add your smart lock alongside it. If you are locked out tonight because of the broken lock, that is an emergency: call the building's emergency contact first, and if no one answers, 311. We can come the same or next day for the smart lock once the door is secure.

Can the landlord bill us, or raise the rent, because we added a lock of our own?

The same section of the Multiple Dwelling Law that lets a tenant add a lock also says that a lease clause requiring the tenant to pay for exercising that right is void. In plain terms, the owner is entitled to a duplicate key to your additional lock on request, but not to a fee for your having it. A lock you buy and have fitted is not an improvement the owner made, so it is not the kind of work that can be passed on in the rent. What you do owe the owner is a door left in good order: if you take the smart lock with you at the end of the tenancy, the hole should be filled with a cylinder or cover plate and the door left looking as it did. We note on our invoice that the lock was supplied and installed for the tenant, which helps if anyone asks later. If a charge does appear, the state housing agency and tenant organisations are the people to talk to.

The street door downstairs no longer latches — is that ours to fix or the owner's?

It is the owner's, and it is urgent. LawHelpNY's HPD checklist lists "no lock on entrance door to building" as a class C immediately hazardous condition, which the guide says the landlord has 24 hours to correct once a violation is issued. Tenants should report it through 311 as a public area maintenance complaint, which covers doors and locks that are broken, missing or not self-closing. A smart lock on your apartment is not a substitute for a working street door, and a tenant should not pay to repair the building entrance. If the owner or the board decides to upgrade the street door rather than just fix it, our Manhattan access control and door buzzer teams work on lobby keypads, fob readers and strikes; a single-door keypad or fob reader with an electric strike is $1,875–$3,125 installed at the Manhattan rate, priced for the building, not the tenant.

Old doors and changed buildings

Decades of paint have built up on our door and frame — does that matter for a new lock?

It matters more than the choice of brand. Thick paint narrows the gap between door and frame, fills the strike pocket and sometimes glues the hinges so the door sags. A motorised deadbolt then has to push its bolt past paint and into a strike that is no longer where the bolt lands, and it either jams halfway or strains until the batteries die. Before fitting anything we cut back paint along the latch edge and the strike, open the strike pocket to full depth, and check that the door closes and latches on its own weight. If the frame has been painted over the old strike, we re-seat it rather than enlarge the hole. In buildings old enough to have lead paint under the newer layers, we keep the scraping to the minimum the lock needs, contain the dust and clean up with a HEPA vacuum. If the door itself is warped, we say so before quoting, because no lock fixes a door that does not close.

I rent a stabilized apartment in a building that went co-op — whose rules apply to my door, the board's or the law's?

Both, in different ways. Many uptown buildings, especially in Hudson Heights, converted from rentals to co-ops from the 1980s onward, and tenants who stayed on kept their rent-stabilized leases; the NYU Furman Center estimates about 16,000 such units citywide. As a tenant, your landlord is the owner of your unit's shares — often the original sponsor or an investor — and the tenant protections that apply to your apartment come with you, including the right in the Multiple Dwelling Law to add a lock of your own. The co-op's house rules govern the building and its shareholders, and the managing agent will still expect any contractor on site to carry insurance and work within building hours. In practice we ask the managing agent's office what it needs to see, keep the corridor face of the door unchanged where we can, and give the unit owner a duplicate key. We will tell you if a request goes beyond what the rules allow.

Coming and going on a working day

I come home from a night shift at 3 a.m. — what is the quickest way in that doesn't depend on fishing for keys or a phone?

A fingerprint reader or a short personal code, with the phone as a backup rather than the main key. After twelve hours on a ward, nobody wants to unlock a phone, wait for Bluetooth and tap a screen on a dim landing. A fingerprint deadbolt opens with a touch; a keypad opens with four to eight digits you can enter by feel if the keypad is backlit. We enroll two fingers per person, in case one is cut or bandaged, and test them with dry and with washed hands. Auto-unlock features that open the door as your phone approaches exist, but in a walk-up where the phone may lose signal in the stairwell they are less dependable, so we do not rely on them. We also set the lock to relock itself a short time after the door closes, so a tired person who forgets to turn the bolt is still locked in safely. The mechanical key remains in your pocket for the day everything else fails.

I already carry keys for the street door, the inner door and the mailbox — is a keypad on the apartment worth it?

It depends on who else needs to get in. If you are the only person using the apartment, a smart lock removes one key from a ring that still has three; the convenience is real but small. Where it earns its place is with everyone else: the aide who comes four days a week, the nephew who picks up the mail when you travel, the cleaner, the relative who arrives from the airport. Each of them needs a key to the apartment today, and copies of keys travel. With a keypad on your door, the only key that has to be copied is the street-door key the building already controls, and the apartment code can be time-limited, changed and logged. If the building later adds a code or fob at the street door, the whole trip from sidewalk to sofa can be keyless; until then, the smart lock still takes the most sensitive key off the ring.

People, trust and technology

Someone who used to stay with us still has a key — can we change the lock to keep them out?

It depends on whether they lived there. If the person only visited, changing codes or re-keying your own lock is your decision and we can do it the same day. If the person lived in the apartment for 30 consecutive days or more, New York City's unlawful eviction law, Administrative Code section 26-521, makes it unlawful to remove them by changing the lock without supplying a key, and section 26-523 attaches penalties of $1,000 to $10,000 per violation. That applies to households as well as landlords. In that situation, talk to Housing Court, a legal aid organisation or, where there is a safety risk, the police and a court order first. Where a court has excluded someone, we can change every code and the mechanical cylinder in one visit and leave you a record of what was changed. We will not change a lock to exclude someone who lives there without that footing.

What happens to our lock if the company behind its app stops supporting it?

The lock should still work as a lock, and choosing for that is part of our job. Companies change: Latch, the app-based lock found in many New York buildings, rebranded as DOOR in August 2025 and said existing Latch hardware would continue to be supported. Not every brand handles a change so smoothly. So we favour locks whose keypad codes and fingerprints are stored in the lock itself and keep working with no internet, which keep a mechanical key cylinder, and which support open standards such as Matter or Z-Wave so another app or hub could take over. We also write down the master or programming code on your handover sheet, because with most keypad locks that code lets you add and delete users at the door even if the app is gone. The features that depend on the company's servers, such as remote unlocking and phone alerts, are the ones you could lose; the door would still open for the people who live there.

Six families of lock cover almost every uptown door. The right one depends on whether the door is yours, how many people need to get in, and how much of the door the rules let us touch.

The workhorse for rental doors and walk-ups: a backlit keypad outside, a thumb-turn inside, codes stored in the lock and a key cylinder for emergencies. Easiest for relatives who will never install an app.

Features that matter more uptown than in the brochures

  • Codes stored in the lock: the door keeps opening for the household when the internet, the router or the app company does not.
  • A real key cylinder: the owner's duplicate key, and your own fallback when batteries die.
  • Enough user slots: large households and regular helpers can need a dozen or more codes; check the lock's limit before buying.
  • Language settings: some keypad locks speak their prompts in more than one language — Yale's Assure Lock manuals, for example, describe a setting for English, Spanish or French voice prompts.
  • Auto-relock with a door sensor: the bolt throws only when the door is actually shut, which matters on doors that do not always close on their own.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Washington Heights

We could not read Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so the claims below are ones published by national cost sites, home magazines and manufacturers — the material AI summaries draw on. Each is fine for a suburban house. Each needs correcting for an apartment door between 155th Street and Dyckman Street.

"Smart lock installation costs $250 to $400"

Those numbers come from Fixr's national guide (January 2025 update): $300 on average, covering a Wi-Fi lock plus one accessory, with a handyman billing $50 to $100 an hour. The picture behind them is a house door used by one family and drilled to standard sizes.

An uptown apartment door is rarely that door. Before a lock goes on, the paint along the edge may need cutting back, the strike may need re-seating, and the owner's lock has to be identified and left alone. Add the walk up the stairs with tools and the time spent setting codes for five or six people, and the job looks nothing like the national average.

That is why we do not publish a flat figure for an apartment-door smart lock. We quote it in writing after a free walkthrough, and the written figure is the price. Building and storefront doors, which are more predictable, carry published AESS prices at the Manhattan rate.

"Any renter can swap the old lock for a smart lock"

Plenty of articles tell renters to replace the deadbolt and put the old one back at move-out. In a New York multiple dwelling, the owner must provide the lock on each apartment entrance door, and the tenant's right in the Multiple Dwelling Law is to add a lock of their own in addition to the owner's.

Swapping the owner's lock out removes the owner's means of emergency access and can turn a simple upgrade into a dispute. The law also limits a tenant's own lock to three inches in circumference and expects a duplicate key on request, details most national guides never mention.

Our default for renters is therefore to add, not swap: a second smart deadbolt, or an inside-only retrofit on a lock that is already yours, with the owner's lock untouched.

"A smart lock means everyone needs the app"

Manufacturers lead with their apps, and AI answers repeat it. In a neighborhood where almost one resident in five is over 65, an app-first lock is a lock half the family will not use.

Keypad and fingerprint locks keep their codes in the lock, so a parent or an aide only needs a number or a finger. The app becomes a tool for the one or two people who manage the codes and read the log.

City law points the same way for landlords. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act says owners of multiple dwellings may not require tenants to use a smart access system to get into their own apartments. We apply that spirit to households too: nobody should need a smartphone to come home.

"A smart lock secures your home"

A lock secures one door. In an apartment house it is the last of several: the street door, often an inner vestibule door, the stairs or elevator, then your own. If the street door does not latch, strangers are already inside the building before your smart lock is involved.

That is why a defective lock on a building's entrance door is a class C immediately hazardous condition on the HPD checklist published by LawHelpNY, with a 24-hour correction period once the violation is issued. The fix belongs to the owner, through 311 and HPD.

What a smart lock adds is control and a record at your own door: who has a code, when it was used, and whether the bolt is thrown. Treat it as part of the building's security, not a replacement for it.

"Wi-Fi smart locks connect from anywhere in the home"

Product pages show a lock and a router in the same open-plan room. August's own support guidance says its Wi-Fi bridge should be within 10 to 15 feet of the lock, and that the distance to the router depends on what is in between.

In a pre-war apartment, what is in between is brick, plaster on lath and sometimes a steel-clad door, and the router may be in a room at the far end of the apartment. The hallway outside may pick up a dozen neighbours' networks and very little of yours.

We test the signal at the door with a phone before choosing a bridge, a Thread border router or a Bluetooth-only lock, and we place the bridge where it is reliable, not where it looks tidy.

"Half an hour and a Phillips screwdriver is all it takes"

Bob Vila's testers reported needing only a Phillips screwdriver and about half an hour, and on a modern door with standard bores that can be true.

On an uptown door, the screwdriver comes out after the real work: finding which lock belongs to the owner, cutting through a century of paint, moving a strike that no longer meets the bolt, packing out a door that is thicker or thinner than the lock's listed range, and making sure a steel-clad fire door is not modified in ways its label does not allow.

If you prefer to install the lock yourself, the walkthrough is still useful: we will tell you whether the door is ready for it, and a programming-only visit for a lock that is already fitted is available at a published rate.

"You can't put modern locks on a landmark building"

It is easy to assume that landmark status rules out keypads on the rowhouses of the Jumel Terrace district altogether. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules are more specific than that.

Section 2-14 of the LPC rules lists repairing or replacing window or door hardware, such as hinges, knobs and handles, as ordinary repair that does not need a permit — but it excludes ornate historic exterior hardware on special doors. Replacing a door itself, or altering a facade, is a different matter that does need the Commission.

So the practical rule is to keep any ornate historic hardware in place, put the electronics on the inside face, and choose a keypad that is modest on the outside. Where something new would change the facade, the job pauses for the LPC question before any drilling.

Household Planning

A Code Plan for a Long-Tenure Uptown Apartment

The lock takes an hour to fit. The plan for who holds which code is what makes it worth having, and in a household that has been in the same apartment for twenty or forty years, the plan has more people in it than the lease does. This is how we lay it out with you at the door.

1. The people who live here

Every resident gets a personal code or fingerprint, named in the app with their first name. Shared codes are where control slips away: once "the family code" has been told to a friend, a delivery driver and a former tenant, nobody can say who knows it. Personal codes mean any one can be changed without disturbing the rest, and the log tells you who actually came in.

2. The two administrators

One person runs the app; a second person, on a different phone and email, can take over. In many uptown households that is a parent and an adult child, or two siblings. We write both names on the handover sheet, and we note the lock's programming code so that codes can be managed at the keypad if a phone is lost.

3. Regular helpers

A home health aide, a babysitter, a cleaner, a neighbour who feeds the cat. Each gets a code that works only on the days and hours they are expected, where the lock supports schedules, and that is deleted the day the arrangement ends. If an agency changes the aide, the old code goes and a new one is made in a minute, without a locksmith.

4. Visitors and one-off arrivals

Relatives arriving for a holiday, a contractor sent by the landlord with your agreement, a friend collecting a parcel. One-time or short-dated codes cover these. They expire on their own, so nobody has to remember to delete them.

5. The owner's key

The landlord's lock keeps its key, and the owner can have a duplicate key to the lock you added, as the Multiple Dwelling Law provides. We cut or set aside that key on the day, so the arrangement is ready if the owner asks. That is what keeps an added lock on the right side of the lease.

6. The day something fails

Batteries are the usual failure, and the lock warns well before they die. We tell you where the spare set lives, which side the emergency power terminals or key cylinder are on, and who to call. A household that knows its fallback never has to wait on the landing for a locksmith.

7. When the household changes

Households uptown change shape over the years: a son marries and moves to the Bronx, a niece starts college in another state, an aide retires, a grandchild moves in. Each change is a two-minute job in the app — delete one code, add another — and we suggest a short review twice a year, perhaps when the clocks change, to remove anyone who no longer needs a way in. Nothing about the owner's lock or key changes when your own list of codes does.

Terms

Uptown Building Terms You Will Hear on a Lock Job

  • Multiple dwelling: in New York law, a building lived in by three or more families living independently. Most apartment houses uptown are multiple dwellings, which is why the Multiple Dwelling Law and the city Housing Maintenance Code apply.
  • Section 51-c: the part of the Multiple Dwelling Law that lets a tenant add a lock of up to three inches in circumference to the apartment door, with a duplicate key for the owner on request.
  • Class A, B and C violations: HPD's categories for housing conditions, from non-hazardous to immediately hazardous. A missing peephole is class A; a defective apartment-door lock is class B; no lock on the building's entrance door is class C.
  • Rent-stabilized: generally an apartment in a building of six or more units built before 1974, with regulated rent increases and a right to renew, as the city's Tenant Support Unit explains.
  • Proprietary lease: the agreement between a co-op and its shareholder that, with the house rules, sets what may be changed in an apartment.
  • Retrofit lock: a smart unit that fits over the inside of an existing deadbolt, leaving the outside and its key as they were.
  • Strike: the plate and pocket in the frame that receive the bolt. On old doors it is the part most often out of line.
  • Kalamein door: a fire-rated door with a metal skin, common in pre-war apartment houses; holes in it are limited to what its label allows.
  • Special door: in the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules, a historic door whose ornate hardware is not covered by the ordinary-repair exemption.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Uptown Landings

How our installers approach a smart lock on an older uptown apartment door — working practice, not an account of one particular job

The first thing we look at is not the lock but the door's history. On an older apartment door there are often more holes than locks: the outline of a removed rim lock, a filled bore from a deadbolt two tenants ago, a strike that has been moved up a quarter-inch. That history tells us where the next lock can go without weakening the door, and which existing lock belongs to the owner.

Then we close the door and let go. If it does not latch on its own, nothing else matters until it does. In buildings this age the cause is usually paint — on the hinge side, on the latch edge, in the strike pocket — and occasionally a hinge screw that has lost its grip in the frame. Ten minutes with a scraper and a longer screw saves a motorised bolt from a life of straining against the strike.

Before programming, we sit down with whoever runs the household and write the code plan on paper. It is the part of the visit families remember: who gets which code, who is the second administrator, where the spare batteries live, and which key goes to the landlord if asked.

Last, every code, finger and key is tried from the landing with the door shut, and the app over mobile data.

FAQ

Washington Heights Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Do we have to register a lock we add to our apartment door with the landlord or the city?
+

There is nothing to file with the city for a lock on your own apartment door. What the Multiple Dwelling Law expects is practical: the owner may ask for a duplicate key to the lock you added, and you should hand one over when asked. Co-op shareholders should also tell the managing agent, because house rules often require notice of work on a unit door.

Our building is about to get a new lobby entry system — should we wait before fitting a lock on our apartment?
+

Usually not. The lobby system and your apartment lock are separate, and a stand-alone smart lock on your door keeps working whatever the building installs downstairs. The exception is an owner who plans to offer apartment locks that enroll in the same platform; ask management first, and if that is the plan we can wait and choose a compatible model.

Can we send a one-time code to a relative arriving at the George Washington Bridge Bus Station before we get home?
+

Yes, if the lock reaches the internet through its own Wi-Fi or a bridge. You create a single-use or short-dated code in the app from wherever you are and text it to them; it stops working after one use or at the time you set. A Bluetooth-only lock cannot be reached remotely, so if this matters we plan for a bridge at the walkthrough.

Can you fit a door viewer or digital peephole at the same visit?
+

We can add a digital door viewer beside the smart lock where the door allows it. If your apartment door has no peephole at all, that is the owner’s job: the HPD checklist published by LawHelpNY lists a missing peephole in an entrance door as a class A violation, which you can report through 311.

The building handyman offered to put our smart lock in — what should we check first?
+

Ask whether the owner’s lock will stay untouched, whether the bolt will run freely into the strike once paint and alignment are corrected, whether a rated door’s label allows the holes planned, and who will set up the codes and a second administrator. If those answers are clear, a simple job may be fine; if not, our walkthrough or a programming-only visit can fill the gap.

How far ahead should we book a Washington Heights installation?
+

Ask for the next walkthrough slot as soon as you know the door you want done; the installation follows once the lock is in hand and any co-op approval has arrived, which for co-op doors takes as long as the managing agent needs. Repairs go into the next open 2-hour window, often the same or next day.

Is there anything you won’t install on a rental apartment door?
+

Yes. We will not fit a double-cylinder deadbolt that needs a key to get out, because HPD says apartment doors should open from inside without a key. We will not remove or disable the owner’s lock, aim a camera down the shared hallway without the owner’s agreement, or program a lock to keep out someone who lives in the apartment.

How do we know the person at the building door is really your installer?
+

We confirm the technician’s first name and the 2-hour arrival window with you before the visit. If the person at the door does not match what we told you, do not let them in; call the Manhattan line, (929) 560-0737, and we will tell you who is on the way.
