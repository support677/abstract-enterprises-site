The Neighborhood

Why Long Island City Properties Need Cat6 Repair That Starts at the Structure

Long Island City was built to make things, not to hide cable. In 1907 the Degnon Terminal Company bought 362 building lots in the low meadows south of Jackson Avenue, and within a decade the district was full of reinforced-concrete plants served by their own rail sidings; by 1918, according to local historian Jason D. Antos writing in Give Me Astoria, more than 70 of them were turning out war material. Many of those frames are still standing. The Loose-Wiles “Thousand Window Bakery” of 1909 is now LaGuardia Community College. The Standard Motor Products Building at 37-18 Northern Boulevard, built in 1919, now rents office, flex and light-industrial space to creative tenants. Buildings like these have heavy concrete floors, tall ceilings with no plaster under them, and wide column bays — the listing for the SMP lofts advertises concrete floors and ceilings of twelve to fourteen feet. When an office moves in, nobody builds a ceiling to hide the network. The Cat6 goes up on hooks, into conduit or onto tray, in plain sight.

That changes how cable fails. In a Manhattan tower or a Queens rowhouse, a Cat6 run spends its life in the dark behind drywall or above ceiling tiles, and it fails at the terminations or where somebody drills. In a Long Island City loft, the same cable is exposed for most of its length, and its health depends on its supports. Hooks get overloaded as each new tenant adds runs on top of the last tenant's. Conduit along a block wall gets packed until the next pull scrapes the cables already inside. Painters spray the whole ceiling, cable included, to get the black industrial look. And every partition built for a new tenant is metal stud, punched with holes whose edges will cut a jacket that is pulled carelessly across them. A link that tests badly here is usually telling you about one of those things.

The second force is turnover. The Long Island City Partnership counts some 25 million square feet of commercial and industrial space, a daily workforce of more than 100,000 and more than 40,000 residents in the area it serves. Offices, studios, showrooms, labs and workshops come and go in that space constantly, and each fit-out leaves cable behind — some still in use, some abandoned on the hooks, some cut short where a wall moved. The city's 2001 rezoning of 37 blocks started the shift to towers around Court Square and Queens Plaza; the OneLIC plan approved by the City Council on November 12, 2025 rezones roughly 54 more blocks, projects about 14,700 new homes and more than 3.5 million square feet of commercial and industrial space, and keeps an industrial subarea south of the Queensboro Bridge where new loft-like manufacturing buildings are expected. More construction around occupied buildings means more trades in more ceilings, and more Cat6 that gets moved, nicked or cut.

The third is water. Hunters Point, Dutch Kills and the streets along Newtown Creek sit low. During Sandy in 2012, the Newtown Creek Alliance recorded that some companies' bulkheads were underwater before the storm made landfall and that basement and ground-floor electrical systems were destroyed. The federal coastal plan discussed for the area includes floodwalls along Hunters Point and a storm gate across Newtown Creek. For Cat6 that means cellar demarcation rooms, ground-floor network closets and floor boxes on slab that have been wet before and may be wet again. And the towers that grew up after 2001 add a fourth pattern of their own: residents asking on r/longislandcity how good the in-unit Ethernet wiring is and whether the apartment's cables go to a switch of their own — a question that matters when a jack dies and nobody knows where it lands.

So a Cat6 repair in Long Island City starts with the structure. We test the link, find the fault by distance, and then go and look at what the cable is sitting on or running through at that distance: a hook, a sleeve through a slab, a stud, a conduit elbow, a column, a wet floor. Fixing the cable without fixing that is how the same link fails again three months later. Most businesses here also pair a Cat6 repair with commercial security cameras in Queens or a check of their door hardware, because the cameras and readers ride the same exposed pathways and suffer the same damage.

What We Repair

Cat6 Repair Services in Long Island City

Grouped by where the cable lives in a Long Island City building, these are the repairs we are called for. Every job ends the same way: the link passes a certification test for its category, and you get a short written note of what failed, why, and what was changed so it is less likely to happen again.

Exposed-Ceiling Run Repair

Cat6 hanging under concrete or open steel, where one run has been crushed in an overfilled hook, kinked at a beam edge or nicked by another trade's lift. We find the damaged span by distance, replace or re-route it and fix the support that caused it.

J-Hook and Support Rework

Hooks spaced too far apart, loaded far beyond their rated fill, or holding three generations of tenants' cable. We add supports, split bundles, take abandoned runs off the hooks and re-dress the live ones so nothing sags or bears on an edge.

Single-Cable Conduit Replacement

One failed run inside steel conduit along block, brick or a column. We work out whether the bad cable can come out on its own, pull its replacement with lubricant and a controlled pull, and test the neighbours afterwards to prove they were not harmed.

Metal-Stud Partition Damage

Jackets sliced where a run was dragged through stud knockouts without bushings, or crushed in a track when the board went on. We open the smallest section we can, replace the damaged length and fit grommets wherever the new cable crosses steel.

Floor-to-Floor Crossings

Runs between levels of a former factory through old sleeves or new core holes. We reuse sleeves where they are clear, arrange scanning before any new hole is cored, and firestop every penetration we make or reopen.

Painted and Coated Cable

Runs caught in a spray-painted ceiling or coated by a fireproofing or sealant crew. We test them, tell you which ones still pass and which have been compromised, and replace the ones that need it with cable that can stay exposed.

PoE Bundle Problems

Cameras, access points and phones that reboot or drop when many powered runs share one tight bundle on a hot ceiling. We test under load, split oversized bundles and move the worst runs, following the cabling industry's PoE bundling guidance.

Jack, Keystone and Panel Re-Termination

Most single-link faults still end at a termination. We cut back past any damage, re-seat the cable on a new keystone or panel module of the same category, and keep each pair's twist close to the contacts so crosstalk stays within limits.

Move-Out and Hand-Back Surveys

Testing and labelling the cable in a loft before a lease ends, so you know which runs are live and worth leaving, which are abandoned and must come out, and what your landlord is likely to ask about at hand-back.

Five structures, five different repair approaches

Open ceilings. On an exposed ceiling we walk the run with a light and a ladder at the distance the tester gave us. The usual finds are a hook loaded past its capacity, a gap of eight or ten feet between supports where the cable has sagged onto a sprinkler pipe or a duct flange, a bend pulled tight round the edge of a beam, or a scuffed jacket where a lift basket brushed the bundle. The repair is the damaged span plus the support: new hooks at sensible spacing, a split bundle, a bend eased out. Because everything is visible, the finished work also has to look tidy; we dress replacements to follow the existing lines and match the jacket colour where we can.

Conduit on masonry. Older lofts often have Cat6 in steel conduit run along block or brick walls and down columns to wall boxes. If the conduit is lightly filled, one cable can be withdrawn and a new one pulled in. If it is packed, pulling one out can drag the jackets of the others across the threads and edges of each fitting, so we test the neighbours before and after and, where the fill is too high, run the replacement in a new pathway instead.

Metal-stud partitions. Every new suite inside an old loft is framed in steel studs. The punched holes in those studs have sharp edges, and the track at floor and head can pinch a cable when board is screwed on. Damage tends to sit at the first or second stud from a box. We open a small section, replace the length and fit snap-in grommets at each crossing.

Slabs and sleeves. Between floors, the cable usually passes through a sleeve cast or cored into the slab. A clear sleeve with space is the cheapest route for a replacement. A full or blocked one, or no sleeve at all where the run needs to go, means a new hole, and in an old concrete building that means scanning the slab first and firestopping afterwards.

Towers and suites. Above hung ceilings in office towers and in apartment media enclosures, repairs look much like anywhere else in New York; the difference in Long Island City is usually the building's process — freight-elevator bookings, insurance certificates and hours — which we sort out before the visit.

Codes and building rules that come into a Long Island City Cat6 repair

  • NYC Electrical Code: the city's code is now the 2020 National Electrical Code with New York City amendments, in full effect since December 21, 2025 (NYC Department of Buildings). Its communications-cable rules govern how cable is supported, separated and passed through rated walls and floors.
  • Abandoned cable: the NEC requires the accessible portion of abandoned communications cable to be removed, while cable tagged for future use may stay (article 800.25, as explained by EC&M). In lofts with layers of old tenants' cable, this decides what comes off the hooks when we rework a pathway.
  • Firestopping: where a replacement run passes through a fire-rated floor or wall, the opening is sealed with a listed firestop system. In multi-storey concrete lofts that means most floor crossings.
  • Concrete penetrations: new holes through a slab or bearing wall are the building owner's call. Most managers want the slab scanned and the location approved before coring, and some require their own engineer's sign-off.
  • Fire alarm devices: drilling and coring raise dust that can set off smoke detectors. Covering detectors or placing a system on test is arranged with the building's fire-alarm company or management, not decided by us on the day.
  • Licensing: AESS holds New York State low-voltage licence #12000287431 and carries full insurance; certificates of insurance naming the building and managing agent are sent before the visit when a building asks for them.
Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in Long Island City: Questions Answered

These are the questions office managers, studio owners, lab tenants and loft landlords in Long Island City ask once a Cat6 link has started failing. They follow the cable: across the open ceiling, through the conduit, studs and concrete, and finally out of the building at the end of a lease. Booking and practical points are in the FAQ further down.

Open ceilings and the hooks that hold the cable

The Cat6 across our loft's open ceiling hangs in J-hooks that are packed full — can overloaded hooks be what is failing the links?

Yes, and it is one of the most common causes we find in converted lofts. A J-hook is a non-continuous support: the cable rests on a small curved saddle every few feet and hangs free in between. The pathway standard, ANSI/TIA-569, puts those supports no more than five feet (1.5 metres) apart, and manufacturers rate each hook for a fill — nVent, for example, calculates its hook capacities at a 70 percent fill. Belden notes that when hooks are too far apart the cable sags, which leads to abrasion and stress. In a loft that has had three or four tenants, each one tends to have added runs to the same hooks without adding hooks. The bottom cables in an overfilled saddle carry the weight of everything above them, and over months their pairs are pressed out of shape right at the support. That kind of deformation shows up as return-loss and crosstalk failures at a repeating distance — the distance to the hooks.

The tester tells us where to look; the ladder confirms it. Typical signs are cables bulging over the lip of the hook, a flattened jacket when a bottom cable is lifted out, and long sagging spans where a hook has been removed or never installed. The repair is in two parts. First the damaged runs: if the jacket is flattened but the run still certifies once the load is taken off, it can stay; if it does not, the damaged span is replaced or the whole run is re-pulled, depending on length and access. Second the support: extra hooks to bring spacing down, bigger or additional hooks so no saddle is overfilled, and abandoned cable taken off entirely so the live runs have room. We then re-certify every link that shared the overloaded hooks, not only the ones that were reported.

Painters sprayed our open ceiling black, cables and all — do painted Cat6 runs need replacing?

Not automatically, but they need testing, and some will. The cable makers are clear that cable is not meant to be painted: Belden's guidance on altering communications cable says plainly not to paint it, because paint can damage the jacket, shorten its life and void the product warranty, and Legrand's support pages say the same for Cat5e and Cat6. There is also a fire-rating point: a cable is listed with the jacket it left the factory with, and a coat of paint is something its listing never considered. On an exposed loft ceiling, where the black spray finish is part of the look, it happens all the time.

What we do is practical. We certify every painted link to see whether performance has changed — a thin latex overspray on a sound cable often makes no measurable difference, while heavy coats, solvent-based paints and paint that has run into a jack or a patch panel can. We check terminations, faceplates and patch cords for paint inside the contacts, which causes intermittent connections far more often than paint on a jacket does. Then you get a list: links that pass and can stay, terminations and cords that need replacing because paint got into them, and runs that should be replaced because they fail or because the building's insurer or engineer wants unaltered listed cable in that space. Where runs are replaced, we can supply cable whose jacket colour suits the ceiling, so the next paint job does not need to cover it.

Can a big bundle of PoE-powered Cat6 on our exposed tray run hot enough to cause faults?

It can, particularly in a bundle of dozens of cables feeding cameras, access points, phones and door readers on the top floor of an uninsulated loft. When power is sent over the pairs, each cable warms a little; packed together, the cables in the middle of the bundle cannot shed that heat. The industry bulletin on the subject, TIA TSB-184-A, approved in March 2017, covers currents up to 1,000 mA per pair and power up to 100 W, warns that the temperature rise can push cables past their rated temperatures, and recommends limiting bundle sizes. Warmer copper also loses more signal, so a long run that only just passes in winter can fail on a July afternoon under a sun-baked roof.

We test the affected runs under load and, where we can, at the time of day the faults occur. If heat is the problem, the repair is to break the bundle up: split it across two or three pathways, spread it on wider supports, move the highest-power devices onto separate runs, and keep powered bundles away from heating units and skylights. If a run has been cooked long enough to fail outright, it is replaced. For new powered runs in a hot space we will suggest Cat6A, which handles heat better, but most existing Cat6 can be kept once the bundle is no longer an oven.

Conduit, studs and concrete

One Cat6 in the steel conduit along our brick wall has failed — can it be pulled out without damaging the cables that still work?

Usually, if the conduit is not packed. Steel conduit on masonry and down columns is common in Long Island City lofts, where there is no hollow wall to hide cable in. The risk in pulling one cable out of a shared pipe is friction: the cable you are removing drags against the others at every elbow and fitting, and the replacement does the same on the way in. In a lightly filled conduit with gentle bends that is not a problem. In a pipe that has been filled to the brim, or one with tight elbows and set-screw fittings whose edges have never been reamed, it can scuff jackets or deform pairs on cables that were working fine.

So before anything is pulled, we certify every cable in that conduit and save the results. If the fill and the bends allow it, we remove the failed run, attach the replacement to a pull line, lubricate it and pull it in by hand with steady tension. Then we certify the neighbours again and compare. If the conduit is too full, or the run has failed because the pipe itself damaged it, we leave it in place, tag it as abandoned or remove it when the run is quiet, and route the replacement in a new pathway — a fresh conduit or a surface raceway beside the old one. Either way you see the before-and-after results for every cable in the pipe.

Our office partitions are built on metal studs — could the stud openings be what is cutting into the Cat6 jacket?

Very possibly. Every suite carved out of a Long Island City loft is framed in galvanised steel studs and track, and each stud has punched openings through which cable is run. Those openings have sharp edges. Snap-in plastic bushings or grommets exist precisely to stop cable being cut or frayed where it passes through, but on low-voltage runs they are often skipped; electricians on the Mike Holt forum point out that a damaged data cable will not cause a fire or a shock, which is why inspectors seldom chase it, but that the cable will fail. A run pulled tight around a corner of a knockout, or pulled through quickly with a tug at the end, can be sliced down to the pairs, and the same happens where the track at the floor or head pinches a cable when the board is screwed on.

The tester's distance-to-fault reading usually lands within a stud bay or two of a box, which is where the cable turns and is most likely to have been dragged. We cut a small, neat opening in the board at that spot, inspect the cable, and replace the damaged length or the whole run if the damage is close to the jack. Every steel crossing on the replacement gets a grommet, and the wall is closed up and ready for the painter. If several runs in the same partition were pulled the same way, we test them all, because a cable that has been nicked but not yet cut through is often the next one to fail.

A replacement run has to go between floors of our old factory building — does the concrete have to be scanned before anyone drills?

If a new hole is needed, yes, and in most managed Long Island City buildings the landlord will insist on it. Concrete floors and walls in these buildings can carry reinforcing steel, embedded electrical conduit and, in some later structures, post-tensioned cables. Scanning firms such as GPRS explain that ground-penetrating radar shows rebar, post-tension cables and conduit from one side of the slab and lets the operator mark the safe spots on the surface; their concern is that striking a conduit is a shock risk and cutting a tensioned cable is a structural one. X-ray needs access to both sides and clears the area, so radar is the usual choice in an occupied building.

Before arranging any of that, we look for a route that avoids new holes: an existing sleeve with space in it, a shaft or chase that already runs between the two floors, or a path down an exterior or column face. Many repairs between floors can reuse what is there. Where a new penetration really is needed, we agree the location with the building, the slab is scanned and marked, the hole is cored, and the opening is firestopped after the cable goes through. Scanning, coring and firestop are listed separately on the written estimate, and nothing is drilled until you and the building have approved it.

Will drilling for a replacement run set off our building's smoke detectors?

It can if nobody plans for it, so we plan for it. Drilling block or brick and coring concrete both throw fine dust, and smoke detectors in the area are designed to react to exactly that kind of airborne particle. Fire departments advise covering detectors during construction to keep dust out of the chamber and removing the covers when the work is done. In New York City, though, a building's fire-alarm system is not something a cabling contractor switches off on its own: if a system is impaired or out of service, FDNY rules can require a fire guard, and owners can face violations for an alarm that is not working as it should.

So when a repair involves drilling, we tell the building in advance where and when, and management or its fire-alarm vendor decides how the nearby detectors are handled — covered for the duration, the zone put on test, or the work scheduled when the vendor is on site. We drill with dust extraction, keep the work area small and clean up as we go. Most repairs never get this far: re-terminating, replacing a span on a hook or swapping a cable in a conduit makes no dust at all.

When the lease ends

When we move out of our loft, which of our Cat6 has to come out and which can stay for the next tenant?

That depends on two documents and one rule. The documents are your lease, which may require you to remove your cabling or to leave it, and any work letter or alteration agreement from your fit-out. The rule is the electrical code's treatment of abandoned cable: under the NEC, the accessible portion of communications cable that is no longer used and not tagged for future use has to be removed, while cable that is tagged for future use may stay. In Long Island City lofts, where most of the cable is accessible because it hangs on an open ceiling, that rule bites harder than in a building with closed walls.

What we do before a hand-back is test and label every run, mark which ones are live, which are dead, and which are worth keeping for the next tenant, and give you a list you can send to the landlord. Runs the landlord wants to keep are certified, labelled and tagged; runs that must go are cut back and taken off the hooks; and any jacks or panels left behind are tidied so the space looks cared for. If your lease is silent, the landlord's building manager usually has a view, and it is cheaper to ask before the last month than to be billed for removal after you leave. We are not lawyers, so we do not interpret the lease; we give you the facts about the cable so whoever does can decide.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in Long Island City

We could not view Google's AI Overview for these searches from our build environment, so we do not quote it. Instead, each heading below is a claim that turns up again and again in AI answers, cost guides and forums about Cat6 repair, and each is followed by what we actually find in Long Island City buildings.

“Cat6 doesn't really wear out — if it worked once, the problem is your equipment”

The cable itself is durable, and plenty of faults are in switches, cords or settings. But the claim assumes the cable has been left alone inside a wall since the day it was installed. That is not how an exposed loft works. Runs on open ceilings live through repainting, new sprinkler branches, HVAC work, scissor lifts, tenants adding cable to the same hooks, and a summer roof that bakes the top floor.

So we never assume. The first step on any visit is to certify the link and look at the margin. If it passes with room to spare, we tell you to look at the device and show you how. If it fails, the tester shows where along the run, and in a Long Island City building that point usually lines up with something physical you can see from a ladder.

The practical lesson is that a cable that “worked once” is evidence about the past. A saved certification result from the day of repair is evidence about now, and it is what lets you separate a cable fault from an equipment fault next time.

“A Cat6 repair is just a new jack on the end”

Re-termination fixes a lot, and it is where we start when the fault is at the end of the cable. Cost guides that quote a flat “jack replacement” price are describing that kind of fault.

In this neighbourhood, though, many faults sit in the middle of the run: under a crushed hook, at a stud opening, at a conduit elbow, at the edge of a beam. Putting a new jack on a cable that is damaged twenty metres away does nothing except use up slack. The tester's distance reading decides whether the end or the middle is the problem.

When the middle is the problem, the repair is a replaced span or a new run, plus a fix to whatever damaged it. That is why our written estimates list the support or pathway work separately from the cable work.

“Just splice the damaged section with a coupler”

Inline couplers and splice kits are sold for exactly this, and forum answers suggest them because they are quick. On an exposed ceiling it is tempting: the damaged section is right there.

The trouble is that each extra connection adds loss and crosstalk, field-made splices are rarely as good as the link they interrupt, and Fluke Networks' own troubleshooting guidance warns that couplers should not be used in a data installation. A coupler hanging in an open ceiling also becomes the weak point the next time somebody brushes the bundle.

Where a short replacement span is the right answer, we bring both ends back to a proper termination point — a surface box or a consolidation point — and certify the whole link. More often, on runs of normal office length, a new continuous run is cleaner and costs little more.

“Wireless is good enough now, so why repair cable at all?”

For laptops and phones, often it is. In Long Island City the counter-argument is the building: thick concrete floors and brick walls absorb radio, and towers packed with neighbours' networks crowd the airwaves. Brick Underground, writing about New York apartments, quoted a technician saying the city's walls are full of piping, plumbing, cement and brick, and that running Ethernet is the most effective fix.

The bigger point is that the wireless itself rides on cable. Every ceiling access point in a loft office is fed by a Cat6 run, usually hanging on the same hooks as everything else, and when that run degrades, the Wi-Fi in that part of the floor degrades with it.

So for workstations, storage, studio gear, lab instruments, cameras and the access points themselves, the cable is the foundation. Repairing it is usually quicker and cheaper than redesigning the wireless around a broken run.

“Fixing Cat6 is a cheap per-drop job anywhere”

National guides do publish numbers in that range. Fixr's guide, updated in January 2025, gives $125 to $250 per drop for new Cat6 and labour of $45 to $75 an hour. Those are national figures for new installation, not New York City repair.

A repair here is billed differently: time to diagnose and fix, plus any replacement cable. Our published figures are $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and replacement runs from $175 per drop. In a loft, extras such as new supports, scanning before coring, or firestopping are quoted line by line, because they depend on the building rather than the cable.

The useful comparison is not per-drop price but outcome: a certified link, a fixed cause, and saved results. A cheap visit that re-terminates a cable crushed in the middle will be repeated.

“Any electrician can fix a network cable”

Electricians are skilled people, and many pull data cable well. The claim misses what makes a Cat6 repair a Cat6 repair: proving the link meets its category afterwards, which takes a certification tester and the knowledge to read it.

It also misses the scale. In Long Island City, licensing for low-voltage work and the building's insurance and approval process apply just as they do for electrical work, and building managers ask for both.

The fair version of the claim is that whoever fixes the cable should test it to category and hand you the results. If they cannot, you do not know whether the repair worked.

“Dead runs on the hooks do no damage, so leave them”

Out of sight, out of mind, the argument goes, and an extra cable cannot hurt anything. In closed buildings, it is easy to believe.

On an open loft ceiling the dead cable is the reason hooks are overloaded, the reason live runs are hard to trace, and the reason a repair takes longer than it should. The electrical code also requires the accessible portion of abandoned communications cable to be removed unless it is tagged for future use.

When we rework a pathway, we take dead cable off the hooks as part of the job where it can be reached, label what is kept for future use, and leave the ceiling lighter than we found it.

Reading the Ceiling

What We Look For on an Open Long Island City Ceiling

When a building has no ceiling tiles, the whole pathway is in view, and a trained eye can read a lot about a failing link before the tester is even unpacked. This is the walk a technician does along an exposed run in a Long Island City loft once the fault has been placed by distance.

Spacing between supports

We pace the run and note every support. Gaps longer than the five-foot maximum in the pathway standard show up as sag: a loop of cable drooping between hooks, sometimes resting on a sprinkler branch, a duct seam or the flange of a steel beam. Where the sagging cable touches something hard, the jacket wears, and where it hangs heavy, the pairs at the next hook take the strain. These spots get extra supports even when the cable on them still passes.

Fill in each hook

An overloaded hook is easy to spot: cables spilling over the lip, cables wedged in so tight they cannot be lifted, or a single hook carrying two bundles that clearly came from different tenants. We count, compare to the hook's rating and plan to split the load across new supports, taking off any cable that is no longer used.

Bends at beams and columns

Old factory ceilings are full of edges: beam flanges, column capitals, the lips of concrete drop panels and the corners of steel angle bracing. A run pulled tight around one of these edges takes a sharp bend and a pressure point at the same place. Category cable has a minimum bend radius for a reason; bend it tighter and the geometry of the pairs changes, which is exactly what return-loss and crosstalk tests detect. Easing the bend with an extra support or a short detour is often enough.

Neighbouring trades

Open ceilings are shared space. We look for new sprinkler heads and branches, recently moved ducts, fresh conduit from an electrician, new light fixtures on chains and lift tyre marks on the floor below. Damage almost always lines up with the newest work in the ceiling, which also helps when a landlord or contractor asks who caused it.

Paint, coatings and water marks

Overspray on the jackets, paint in the open faces of jacks, sealant or fireproofing on bundles near the slab, and tide marks on the walls of ground-floor or cellar rooms. Each one tells us which links to test with extra care and whether a termination or a run needs replacing rather than cleaning.

What the cable says about itself

The printing on the jacket tells us the category, the fire rating and often the maker and the date. Mixed categories on one link, uncertified bargain cable, and riser-rated cable used where something else was specified all show up on the jacket before they show up on the tester. Where the print does not match the plate, we say so in the report.

After the Repair

Keeping an Exposed Cat6 Pathway Healthy After It Is Fixed

A repaired link on an open ceiling only stays repaired if the pathway around it stays in good shape. These habits save Long Island City tenants and landlords the most repeat visits.

  • Add a hook when you add a cable. Whoever pulls the next run should add supports rather than load the existing ones. If the hooks are already at their rated fill, a new pathway beside them is the answer.
  • Take dead cable down. When a run is retired, cut it back and remove it from the hooks, or tag it clearly if it is being kept for future use. Every dead cable makes the next trace slower and the next hook heavier.
  • Brief the painters. If the ceiling is going to be sprayed, ask the painting contractor to mask jacks, panels and cable bundles, or schedule the network work afterwards. Masking costs very little compared with replacing runs.
  • Tell other trades where the cable is. Sprinkler fitters, HVAC crews and electricians working on the same ceiling should know which bundles are live. A simple marked-up floor plan taped inside the network closet helps.
  • Keep powered bundles loose. As cameras, access points and readers are added, do not keep packing the same bundle. Split powered runs across pathways, especially near the roof.
  • Protect the bottom of the building. In Hunters Point and near the creek, keep closet equipment and terminations off the floor, and look at the cellar or ground-floor closet after any heavy rain.
  • Keep the results. The saved certification results from the repair are your baseline. When something fails again, comparing a new test to the old one shows immediately whether the cable has changed.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Under Long Island City's Concrete Ceilings

From Anwar Timothy, licensed low-voltage contractor: The first thing I tell a technician going into a converted loft is to look up before plugging anything in. In a closed building you trust the tester and go straight to the end of the cable. In a loft the tester gives you a distance, and then you count hooks. More often than not, the number lands on a support that has been asked to carry too much, a bend round a beam that someone pulled tight to save a few feet, or a spot where a lift or another trade has been working. Fix the cable there and leave the hook as it was, and you will be back.

The second thing is to be honest about the old cable. In buildings that have had several tenants, there can be more dead cable on the ceiling than live. Nobody enjoys paying to remove somebody else's leftovers, but it is the thing that makes every future repair cheaper and the ceiling safer to work on, and the code expects accessible abandoned cable to come out. I would rather explain that at the start than leave a ceiling that will cost the next person twice as much.

And the third: in a neighbourhood that changes as fast as this one, write everything down. Labels at both ends, saved results, a note of what was removed. The person who reads it next might be your own IT provider, a new tenant or us.
