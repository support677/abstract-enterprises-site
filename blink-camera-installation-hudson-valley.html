The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need Blink Camera Installation

The Hudson Valley was settled as a scatter of farms, manors and river landings, and most properties still spread out the way they did. The Historical Marker Database records that the Dutch set up a trading post near the future site of Albany in 1614, made a permanent settlement at Fort Orange in 1624, and settled Esopus, today's Kingston, in 1653. The same marker notes that the Van Rensselaer patroonship and the Livingston, Philipse and Van Cortlandt manors gave the valley an aristocratic shape in the seventeenth century, and the Hudson River Valley Heritage Area writes that by 1750 most of the region had been settled as a network of small local communities that still survives. What that history leaves behind, for a camera installer, is distance. A house here is rarely one box on a small lot. It is a main house, a garage that may stand on its own, a shed or a barn, a gate or a mailbox at the end of a drive, and walls of fieldstone, brick or old timber between them. Every one of those details decides whether a battery camera that talks over Wi-Fi will work.

Blink is built around a simple chain, and that chain is what makes the valley tricky. According to Blink's own support pages and independent troubleshooting guides, Blink's battery cameras do not connect to your router directly. They talk to a small hub called a Sync Module, and the Sync Module talks to your Wi-Fi. Blink states that its Sync Modules can communicate with cameras up to 100 feet away, and it tells owners to keep the module away from thick walls and from materials such as concrete, brick, stone or metal. In SafeHome's testing, the claimed 100 feet shrank to a strong connection at only about 70 to 80 feet once walls and obstacles were in the way. Now picture a Kingston Stockade house, where Wikipedia notes colonial Dutch stone houses still stand on all four corners of Crown and John Streets, or a farmhouse in Dutchess with a barn across the yard. A camera that worked perfectly on the kitchen table during setup can drop offline the day it is screwed to the barn, because two feet of stone or eighty feet of open yard now sit between it and the module. That is the most common reason a self-installed Blink system disappoints, and it is the first thing we measure.

Then there is the weather. Blink rates its Outdoor cameras to operate from minus 4 to 113 degrees Fahrenheit, according to an answer on Blink's Amazon product page, and its Indoor and Mini cameras only from 32 to 95 degrees, for indoor use only. Valley winters test those numbers. On Amazon's own forum, one owner reported a Blink Outdoor that would not record at 15 degrees outside. On a Best Buy question page, another owner said their version 4 cameras gave up at minus 11 Celsius, would not stream on demand and eventually disconnected. Battery guides that cite Blink say the cameras are meant to run on AA 1.5 volt lithium non-rechargeable cells and list extreme heat and cold among the things that shorten battery life. Put those facts together and you have the classic January call: the driveway camera that went silent the week the temperature dropped, or the barn camera whose batteries were the wrong type from the start. Choosing the right cells, keeping a camera out of the wind where we can, and deciding when wired power is worth it are part of every valley install we do.

Power and internet are the last two links, and both fail more often here than in the city. Central Hudson reported 669 active outages affecting 41,323 customers across the region at noon on the Sunday after the July 4, 2026 storms, according to Times Hudson Valley, and later said the most heavily affected areas were in Dutchess and Ulster. Mid-Hudson News reported that the utility restored power to more than 24,000 customers after a nor'easter in September 2026. A Blink battery camera keeps its own charge through an outage, but without a working router and Sync Module it has nowhere to send a clip. Some properties have no fast internet to begin with. Hudson Valley One reported in 2023 that 1,070 Ulster County properties still had no broadband, and that in Shandaken, with 2,866 residents across 120 mountainous square miles, even cell service struggles. Mid-Hudson News reported in September 2025 that eleven providers had won nearly 400 million dollars in state funding to reach every documented unserved location, including parts of Putnam, Dutchess, Orange, Ulster and Westchester. Until that work reaches your road, a Wi-Fi camera is only as good as the connection the house already has, and we check that connection before we sell you on any plan.

Why do people in the valley buy cameras at all? The local news answers that directly. In a January 2025 alert reported by Hudson Valley Post, the New York State Police said troopers were investigating residential burglaries in Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam counties, and one of their tips was to install or activate security systems and cameras. Patch Mid-Hudson Valley reported that State Police in Poughkeepsie asked anyone with home cameras to check their footage from midnight to 5 a.m. after car break-ins, naming residents of Hoofprint Road in Union Vale, Fountain Place and Weatherford Lane in Millbrook, and Horseshoe, Verbank and Maple Hill Roads and Rodrigo Court in the town of Washington. News 12 reported that neighbors on and near Tower Hill Drive in Port Chester showed doorbell-camera footage of incidents around 4 a.m., and that the Ulster County Sheriff's Office warned of burglaries and car break-ins on Union Center Road in Esopus. In Scarsdale, Hudson Valley Post reported, a resident doing yard work on Bradford Road found a surveillance camera hidden in the bushes and aimed at the home, and Scarsdale police described organized burglars placing such devices for days before a break-in. A camera only helps in those moments if it was recording, at night, in the right direction, and if the clip is still there when the police ask. That is an installation question as much as a product question.

We want to be honest about the gaps in what we could find. We searched Reddit, local news, Yelp and hyperlocal outlets for posts about Blink installations specifically in the valley and found none. We found no Yelp complaints about Blink installs here, and no public source that says whether a small battery camera on a historic house triggers a landmark review in any particular town. Where this page talks about stone walls, barns, long drives and cold mornings, it draws on Blink's published specifications, owners' reports, and the public record of what the valley's buildings and weather are like, not on a survey of valley Blink owners.

Hudson Valley facts that shape a Blink install

  • Six counties, six price points: AESS covers Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster, priced at 1.25, 1.30, 1.40, 1.45, 1.53 and 1.53 times our Brooklyn base respectively, because lots are larger, runs are longer and travel is further than in the city.
  • Room to spread out: the USDA's 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 620 farms on 99,652 acres in Dutchess, averaging 161 acres, and 486 farms on 73,026 acres in Ulster, averaging 150 acres. Barns and outbuildings are normal here, and so are cameras that need to reach them.
  • Historic fabric everywhere: the Hudson River National Historic Landmark District, designated in 1990, is described by Hudson River Heritage as the largest such district on the mainland of the contiguous United States, and Wikipedia notes only 2 percent of its property by acreage is not considered historic.
  • Storms that take the router down: Central Hudson reported 41,323 customers affected after the July 4, 2026 storms, and more than 24,000 restored after the September 2026 nor'easter.
  • Internet dead zones: 1,070 Ulster properties without broadband in 2023, and an older WPDH map found that most unserved spots were apartment complexes, townhomes and rural neighborhoods.
  • Police who ask for footage: the Village of Croton-on-Hudson invites residents and businesses to register private cameras voluntarily, and contacts registrants only if a crime happens nearby.

Six Counties

From the Sound Shore to the Catskills: How the Same Blink Job Changes County by County

The Hudson Valley as AESS serves it runs from Westchester's border with the Bronx up to Ulster County and the edge of the Catskills. Wikipedia describes the wider region as stretching from the Capital District south to Yonkers, and the Hudson River Valley Heritage Area groups Westchester and Rockland as the Lower Hudson, and Putnam, Orange, Dutchess and Ulster as the Middle Hudson. A Blink camera is the same box in every one of those counties, but the property it lands on changes as you go north. In the south, lots are tighter, neighbors are close and the question is mostly where a camera may point. Further up, the questions become how far, how cold and how reliable the internet is. Westchester: close neighbors and a camera that has to stay on your side

Much of southern Westchester is built on village and town lots where the next house is a few yards away. The Blink jobs there look most like city work: a video doorbell, a camera over the driveway, one on the back door, maybe one in the garage. The challenge is aim. A camera that sees your front walk often also sees a neighbor's front window or a public sidewalk, and Blink's activity zones and privacy settings exist for that reason. The local news here is also the most camera-heavy in the region. News 12 reported doorbell footage from neighbors on Tower Hill Drive in Port Chester, and Rye police have reported 99 vehicle thefts since 2020, all involving vehicles left unlocked with the key fob inside, according to News 12. A driveway camera does not stop that, but it records what happened, and placing it so it sees the car doors rather than only the street is the difference between a useful clip and a blur of headlights. Westchester also brings the Scarsdale reminder: burglars placing their own hidden cameras in bushes. When we set up a Westchester system, we look at your hedges and side yards with that in mind.

Rockland: suburban streets, village centers and room at the back

Rockland mixes suburban neighborhoods, village downtowns, growing commercial centers along roads such as Route 59, Route 304 and Route 202, and rural properties further from the river. For a Blink system, that usually means a house with a modest front yard and a deeper back lot, often with a shed, a pool fence or a detached garage at the far end. The front of the house is easy. The back is where the 100-foot Sync Module range gets tested, especially if the module sits in a front room or a basement on the far side of the house. Rockland is also bear country. The New York State Department of Environmental Conservation describes the Southern Black Bear Range as extending to southern Orange and Rockland counties at the New Jersey border, and lists bird seed and feeders as the most common attractant. A camera pointed at a trash enclosure or a bird feeder will record a lot of animals; we set the zones so that a bear at the bins does not drain the batteries on the camera watching your back door.

Orange: river cities, warehouses and farm country on the same map

Orange County holds the widest range of property in the region, from Newburgh's East End Historic District, which the city describes as the second largest designated historic site in New York State and which is lined with Italianate row houses, to farms and newer subdivisions further west. In a Newburgh row house, a Blink job is closer to a city job, with brick party walls that block the Sync Module's signal from one end of the house to the other. On a farm, it is a distance problem. Orange is also, by one measure, the stormiest county in the state: an Earth Networks report on 2020 lightning ranked Orange first in New York, with 275,059 lightning pulses and 41 thunder days. Lightning does not usually hit a battery camera, but surges and outages knock out routers, and a Blink system with no router has nowhere to send clips. Orange is served mostly by Orange and Rockland Utilities, which says it serves about 300,000 electric customers across New York and northern New Jersey, and by Central Hudson in villages such as Montgomery, Maybrook, Cornwall and Highland Falls.

Putnam: wooded hills and the outage that takes the whole county down

Putnam's houses tend to sit back from the road on wooded lots, often uphill or downhill from the driveway, with trees in every line of sight. Trees matter to a wireless camera: wet leaves in summer absorb signal that passes freely in winter, so a camera that tests well in March can struggle in July. Putnam is also where a single storm can switch off most of the county at once. The Daily Voice reported that at 10:15 a.m. on August 5, 2020, during Tropical Storm Isaias, NYSEG had 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers out, and Central Hudson 3,621 of its 5,160. When the power comes back, the cameras have to find the Sync Module and the module has to find the router again, all without anyone home to press a button. We test exactly that recovery before we leave a Putnam job, and we talk through whether a small battery backup on the router is worth it for your house.

Dutchess: estates, farms and the roads the State Police named

Dutchess carries much of the Hudson River National Historic Landmark District, the estates of the old Great Estates region, and more than 600 farms. It is also where State Police asked residents of named rural roads in Union Vale, Millbrook and the town of Washington to check their cameras for footage between midnight and 5 a.m. after car break-ins. Those requests show what a Dutchess camera needs to do: see a car in a long, dark driveway at three in the morning and keep the clip long enough for someone to ask for it. That rules out a lot of careless placements. A camera mounted high on a barn gable, aimed across a wide yard, captures motion but not a face or a vehicle. A camera on a fence post by the gate, closer to where a car must slow down, often captures far more. Distance from the house is the limiting factor, which is where Blink's Extended Range Sync Module, covered below, comes in. Dutchess is Central Hudson territory, and the utility named Dutchess and Ulster as the hardest-hit areas after the July 2026 storms.

Ulster: stone walls, cold mornings and the internet question

Ulster gives us the hardest walls and the coldest air. Kingston's Stockade District, per Wikipedia an eight-block, 32-acre area of Uptown Kingston, mixes homes and businesses among colonial stone houses, and the city's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work on historic properties that can be seen from the street before a building permit is issued. Out in the towns, the issue is connectivity. Hudson Valley One reported 1,070 Ulster properties with no broadband in 2023, and in September 2025 the county announced a state ConnectALL award, with Verizon's match, to reach nearly 1,300 unserved homes, businesses and community facilities in remote areas. Blink cameras need working Wi-Fi to send clips and alerts. On a road that is still waiting for service, the honest answer may be that a Blink system cannot do what you want yet, and we will say that before you spend money on cameras.

The time lost on a Blink job in the valley is almost always lost to surprises: a Sync Module that turns out to be an older model, a router in a basement on the far side of the house, a doorbell chime that cannot power a wired doorbell, or an internet connection that slows to a crawl in the evenings. Walk the route a stranger would take

Before deciding where cameras go, walk your property the way a visitor or a stranger would: from the road, up the drive, to the front door, around to the back door, to the garage and the barn. Note where a person has to slow down, turn or stop, such as a gate, a step, a door or a parked car. Those are the points where a camera gets a usable face or plate. In the valley, owners often want to cover everything at once, the whole lawn, the whole field, the whole tree line, and end up with cameras that trigger on every branch and still miss the person at the door. A short list of choke points gives better clips and longer battery life.

Think about winter before you think about angles

A spot that is perfect in October can be useless in February. Notice where snow drifts against the house, where ice drips from the gutter line, which side of the house takes the prevailing wind, and where the plow piles snow at the end of the drive. A camera under an eave on the sheltered side of the house stays clearer and warmer than one on an exposed corner. If a camera has to face the weather, say so, and we will plan its power and mounting around that.

Stone houses: getting signal through walls built to last

The valley's colonial stone houses are the hardest buildings we put a Blink system into, for one simple reason. Blink's own guidance is to keep the Sync Module away from stone, brick and concrete, and in a stone house almost every wall is one or the other. The fix is not more power, because the cameras cannot shout louder. It is geometry. We place the module where the signal can leave the house through a window, a door or a wood-framed addition rather than through solid masonry, and we measure at each camera spot with the doors shut. Often the answer is a module in a back porch, a frame wing or an attached garage rather than the living room where the router sits. Mounting is the second question. Fieldstone is uneven, and mortar in old walls can be soft. We look for a flat face of stone, or better, a wooden lintel, a porch beam or a piece of trim that will take a screw without anyone ever noticing it, because a Kingston historic district house is reviewed on how it looks from the street. Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work on historic properties visible from the public right of way before a building permit is issued. We found no source that says whether a small battery camera alone triggers that review, so on a district house we ask you to check with the city before we mount anything on the street side.

River estates: big footprints and the drive that goes on and on

The Hudson River National Historic Landmark District, the Historical Marker Database notes, takes in four towns, four hamlets, two villages and more than forty riverfront estates, including names such as Montgomery Place, Wilderstein and Wildercliff listed by Wikipedia. An estate-scale property breaks a Blink system's assumptions in several ways at once. The house may be more than 100 feet across, so one Sync Module cannot reach both ends. The carriage house, the garage and the gate lodge sit apart from the main house. The approach drive may run for hundreds of feet through trees. We plan these jobs as a set of zones: the main house, each outbuilding, and the drive. Each zone gets a module where the signal survey says it should go, and the farthest spots are where Blink's Extended Range option is weighed against its lower picture quality. On a property where cameras matter that much, we will also tell you plainly if a wired system would serve you better than Blink, and we write that into the quote.

Farms: metal, open ground and animals that never stop moving

Farm buildings bring metal siding, metal roofs and steel doors, and Blink-focused guides note that metal doors and walls both cut range and increase battery drain, because a camera struggling for signal works harder on every clip. They also bring livestock, wildlife and machinery moving through every frame. A camera on a barn door that faces the paddock will trigger hundreds of times a day. We place farm cameras on the approaches, not the fields: the lane, the gate, the barn door from the side where people walk up, and the spot where a truck would back in. Activity zones are drawn tight, and sensitivity is set for a person, not a cow. Where the barn has power, a wired camera saves the owner from changing batteries every few weeks during calving season or harvest.

Suburban lots: the neighbor is closer than the barn ever was

On the suburban streets of Westchester and Rockland, distance is easy and privacy is not. A doorbell on a front door that faces the street records the street. A driveway camera may look straight into the house across the way. Blink lets us draw activity zones and, on many models, privacy zones that black out part of the picture. We use both, and we check the view from the app with you before we leave. Suburban houses are also where the wired doorbell question comes up most, because many have an existing chime. Blink states that its wired doorbell needs a chime system supplying 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz and no more than 40 VA; if the wiring does not qualify, the app shows a "wired power not supported" message and the doorbell falls back to battery behavior. We check the chime before we promise a wired doorbell.

Townhouses, condos and apartments: the board owns the wall

In attached homes, the outside wall and the common hallway usually belong to the association or the landlord, not the owner or tenant. Blink itself suggests plug-in cameras such as the Mini for living areas and battery doorbells and outdoor cameras for doors and patios in rentals, and no-drill mounts exist for some doorbells, with limits on door thickness and gaps. We work inside those rules: indoor cameras placed to look out, doorbells on approved mounts, and written confirmation from the board or landlord before anything is fixed to a common wall.

Your Questions Answered

Blink Camera Installation in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

Winter, cold and batteries

Will a Blink Outdoor camera keep recording through a January night in Ulster or Dutchess when it drops far below freezing?

Usually, but not always, and the published rating is not the whole story. Blink rates its Outdoor cameras to operate from minus 4 to 113 degrees Fahrenheit, according to an answer on its Amazon product page. Owners report trouble before that limit: one on Amazon's forum said a Blink Outdoor would not record at 15 degrees, and another on Best Buy's question page said version 4 cameras gave up at minus 11 Celsius, stopped streaming on demand and eventually disconnected. Cold slows battery chemistry, and a camera that is also struggling for Wi-Fi spends more power on each clip. We reduce the risk in three ways: the right lithium cells, a mounting spot sheltered from wind under an eave where the view allows it, and a strong signal path so the camera is not straining. For a camera that must never miss a cold night, wired power removes the battery from the equation.

Do Blink batteries really die faster in the cold, and which ones should go in?

Yes, and the type matters. Battery guides that cite Blink say its cameras are designed for AA 1.5 volt lithium non-rechargeable batteries and list extreme heat and cold among the factors that shorten battery life. A battery vendor's guide adds that lithium cells hold steady across a very wide temperature range, while alkaline cells lose significant capacity in the cold. In practice that means the alkaline batteries in the kitchen drawer are a poor choice for a barn or driveway camera in the valley, and rechargeable AAs are not what Blink specifies either. We install fresh lithium cells and note the date in the handover sheet, so you know when the camera was last changed. If a camera is draining far faster than its neighbors, that usually points to weak signal or a busy activity zone rather than the battery itself, and we look at both.

Can a Blink Mini watch our unheated mudroom, enclosed porch or barn through the winter?

Not reliably. Blink states that its Indoor and Mini cameras operate from 32 to 95 degrees Fahrenheit and are intended for indoor use only. An unheated porch, a garage or a barn in the valley spends much of the winter below freezing, so a Mini there is outside its rating for months at a time. For those spaces we use an Outdoor model instead, even though it sits indoors, because its rating is wider and it is built for damp air. The Mini stays in heated rooms, where it does a good job looking out a window at a driveway or watching an entry hall. If you already bought Minis for the barn, we will tell you which spots they can safely move to.

Signal, distance and staying online

Why does our Blink Outdoor 2K+ keep dropping offline when it worked fine the day we set it up?

Blink's own guide to offline cameras points to three usual causes: weak signal, low batteries and unstable Wi-Fi, and tells owners to check battery and signal in the app and confirm a stable 2.4 GHz connection. In valley houses the most common version is the first one. The camera was paired on the kitchen table, then mounted outside a stone or brick wall, or past the point where the Sync Module's roughly 100-foot range holds up. Signal that is barely enough on a dry day fails on a wet one, or once the trees leaf out. Batteries then drain faster as the camera keeps reconnecting, which makes the problem worse. We read the signal from the mounted position, move the Sync Module or add a second one where the readings call for it, and check the router's 2.4 GHz band, which some newer routers combine with 5 GHz in a way that confuses smart devices.

Our barn and front gate are several hundred feet from the house. Can Blink reach that far?

Sometimes, with the right module and a compromise on picture quality. In Tom's Guide's test of Blink's Extended Range feature, Extended Range let a camera work up to 250 feet from the house at up to 720p, and Extended Range Plus reached 400 feet but dropped the picture to 360p. Deal-forum listings note that one Sync Module XR handles up to ten devices but only two of them can use the extended range, an account can have up to two XR modules, and the feature works only with the Blink Outdoor 4 and Outdoor 4 Floodlight. At 360p, a camera at the gate will show that a car arrived, but probably not its plate. Where the barn has power and Wi-Fi of its own, a standard Sync Module placed in the barn is often the better answer, with full picture quality. We measure both options on site before you buy.

We bought new Blink cameras, but our older Sync Module has the USB stick for local storage. Will they work together?

That is a question owners ask on deal forums, and the honest answer is that it depends on the exact combination, so we check before you buy rather than after. What the published listings do show is that local storage depends on the module, not the camera: an Outdoor 4 bundled with the Sync Module Core can only save clips to paid cloud storage, while the Sync Module XR adds extended range and a slot for local recording. If keeping clips at home without a subscription matters to you, tell us which module you own and which cameras you want, and we will confirm in the Blink app and Blink's own documentation that the pairing and the storage you expect will actually work.

Our road still has no broadband. Is there any way to run Blink cameras out here?

Not without some internet connection at the house, because Blink cameras send their clips and alerts through the Sync Module and your Wi-Fi. Hudson Valley One reported 1,070 Ulster properties without broadband in 2023, Hudson Valley 360 later counted roughly 700 Columbia County addresses without internet, and state funding is now paying providers to reach the remaining unserved locations in parts of Putnam, Dutchess, Orange, Ulster and Westchester. If your house has a satellite or cellular internet connection, Blink can work over it, but clips use data and upload speed matters, so we look at your plan's limits first. If the house has no connection at all, we will tell you plainly that Blink is the wrong camera for now, rather than selling you a system that can only record motion nobody ever sees.

Power and outages

Can our Blink cameras be wired or solar-powered so nobody climbs a ladder every few months?

Many of them can, but the options differ by model. Battery guides that cite Blink say the Outdoor 3rd Gen works with Blink's weather-resistant 13-foot power cable and adapter pack, the Outdoor 4 with its Weather Resistant Power Adapter, and the Blink Solar Panel Mount powers the Outdoor 3rd Gen only. For doorbells, the wired option runs from a qualifying chime transformer, and the Wired Doorbell 2K+ can use Blink's plug-in adapter from an indoor outlet, which Blink notes has no battery backup. Wired power needs an outlet within reach; where there is none, a new outlet is work for a licensed electrician, which we arrange or leave to yours. Solar on a valley lot depends on winter sun, not summer sun, so we look at where the panel would sit in December before recommending it.

After a long Central Hudson or O&R outage, will our Blink cameras come back by themselves?

Battery cameras keep their own charge, so they usually come back as soon as the Sync Module and the router do. The weak link is the router. Some come back cleanly after a power cut; others need to be restarted, or come back before the internet provider's line does and leave the Sync Module unable to connect. Outages here can be large: Central Hudson reported 41,323 customers affected after the July 4, 2026 storms, and the Daily Voice reported NYSEG with 34,150 of 38,693 Putnam customers out during Isaias in 2020. Before we leave a job, we switch the router off and on and watch every camera reconnect without anyone touching it. If yours does not recover cleanly, we tell you, and a small battery backup on the router and Sync Module keeps the system uploading through short outages.

Privacy, rules and the law

Is it legal to leave the microphone on our Blink cameras in New York?

New York follows a one-party consent rule for recording conversations, and that is where outdoor microphones get risky. A Justia Ask a Lawyer answer from October 2025 warns that a camera that records voices when you are not there, with no participant consenting, can count as eavesdropping, a class E felony, and that leaving the microphone on while away risks recording delivery drivers, neighbors or visitors. Video inside private rooms is a separate issue: Penal Law 250.45 makes it unlawful surveillance to record a person in a bedroom, bathroom or changing room without consent. We are not lawyers, so we set cameras up conservatively: outdoor microphones off unless you decide otherwise after advice, and no camera pointed into a private room, including rooms in a guest suite or rental unit.

Do we need a permit or a historic-district sign-off to put Blink cameras on our house?

For most valley homes, a battery camera screwed to the house is not something a building department regulates, but historic districts are the exception to check. Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews exterior work on historic properties visible from the public right of way before a building permit is issued, and its ordinance bars material changes to a property's appearance in a landmark district without approval. We found no source that says whether a small camera alone counts, so we do not guess. On a district house, we suggest asking the city or village first, keeping street-side cameras small and discreet, and mounting on trim rather than stone where we can. Any wired power that needs a new outlet is electrical work, which may need a permit and an inspection from the town.

Our village police keep a list of private cameras. Should we register our Blink system?

It is up to you, and it costs nothing in the places that offer it. The Village of Croton-on-Hudson asks residents and businesses to register private camera systems voluntarily, and says registrants are contacted only if a crime happens near their camera. Registration does not give police access to your cameras; it simply tells them whom to ask. If you register, it helps to know how long your Blink plan keeps clips, how to download and share one, and which camera covers what, and we leave you a written sheet with exactly that. Other departments may run similar programs; if yours does not, the same preparation still makes you ready when State Police ask a whole road to check its footage, as they did in Dutchess.

What Blink can and cannot do

State Police asked our road to check cameras between midnight and 5 a.m. How should a Blink be set so it would have caught something?

The requests Patch reported in Dutchess, for Hoofprint Road, Weatherford Lane, Verbank Road and others, tell you what a valley camera needs to do: wake up for a person or a car in the dark, record clearly enough to be useful, and keep the clip until someone asks. That means a camera close to where vehicles must slow down, such as the turn into the drive or the spot beside the parked cars, rather than high on a gable looking at the whole yard. It means night vision tested from the real position, because a porch light can wash out a picture, and an activity zone that covers the driveway, not the road. It means the system armed overnight on a schedule, and storage set so clips survive long enough to be requested. We set and test each of those before handover.

After the Scarsdale reports of hidden cameras in bushes, can our Blink cameras help spot someone watching the house?

They can help, if they cover the right ground. Hudson Valley Post reported that a Scarsdale resident found a surveillance camera in the bushes aimed at their home on Bradford Road, and Scarsdale police described organized burglars placing trail cameras or similar devices for days before a burglary. A Blink camera will not detect another camera, but it will record whoever walks into your hedge line or side yard to place one. We make sure at least one camera's zone covers the side and back approaches, not only the front door, and that motion alerts from those zones reach your phone while you are away. During setup, we also look along hedges and fence lines with you; it costs nothing and is worth doing on any property in the region.

Is Blink actually a good security system for a valley property, or should we be looking at something wired?

Blink is good at what it was designed for: affordable, battery-powered cameras that send motion clips and alerts to your phone, with no wiring and, as HomeGuide notes, no professional monitoring or police dispatch. A deal-forum commenter summed up a common view, that Blink works best in low-traffic areas where wired cameras would be hard to install. For a suburban house, a weekend home or a small barn, that fits well. For an estate drive, a busy farmyard or a business that needs every minute on record, a wired system with continuous recording usually serves better. We install both kinds of system, so we have no reason to push one. We look at what you need to see, how often things move in front of the cameras, and how reliable your power and internet are, and then give you a straight recommendation.

Our Installation Process

Every valley Blink job follows the same eight steps, whether it is a single doorbell in Westchester or a house, barn and gate in Dutchess. The order matters because the two things that sink wireless camera systems here, weak signal and the wrong power, can only be fixed cheaply before anything is mounted.

1. Send photos, the county and the camera list

Photos of each door, drive and outbuilding, your town and county, your internet provider and the Blink models you own or want. We match the job to your county's price page before you book.

2. Agree coverage and privacy limits

What each camera must see, what it must not see, such as a neighbor's windows, the road or a guest room, and whether microphones stay off.

We walk the property with the app, read 2.4 GHz signal at each spot with the doors closed, and decide where the module goes, away from stone, brick, concrete and metal.

Lithium AA cells, a weather-resistant cable or adapter, a solar panel on the model that supports it, or a chime transformer checked for 16 to 24 volts AC for a wired doorbell.

Activity zones drawn to the paths people use, sensitivity tuned against deer, branches and passing cars, and an arming schedule that fits the household.

Every camera triggered, day and night clips checked, the router restarted to confirm everything reconnects alone, and signal read again from each mounted position.

A walkthrough of the app, shared access and storage, and a written sheet listing each camera, its location, its power source and its battery date.

What moves the total within a county

Within any one county, the price of a Blink job is driven by a handful of things you can see for yourself. The number of cameras is the first. Whether each one runs on batteries or needs wired power is the second, because a power cable routed and protected along a wall takes more time than a battery camera on a bracket. The third is the wall: siding and wood trim are quick, while fieldstone and brick need masonry drilling and anchors. The fourth is distance, which decides whether one Sync Module is enough or whether the barn needs its own, or the gate needs the Extended Range module. A doorbell adds a check of the chime transformer. The Hudson Valley Blink price page sets these out line by line, and your county's page applies its scale to each line, so you can total a job before you call.

What is not part of our price

Blink's own cloud storage plans are billed by Blink, not by us, and we explain what each plan keeps before you choose. New electrical outlets are work for a licensed electrician; if one is needed, the quote names it and who will do it. If a historic commission or association requires drawings or an application, that is between you and them, though we will give you the device photos and mounting details they ask for.

FAQ

Hudson Valley Blink Camera Installation FAQ

Does Blink send its own technician to install cameras at a Hudson Valley house?
+

No. Blink sells its cameras as do-it-yourself products, and NerdWallet's review lists no professional installation available. If you want someone to plan the Sync Module placement, power and mounting, that is done by an independent installer such as AESS, working with the cameras Blink sells.

What should we look for when hiring someone to put up Blink cameras in the valley?
+

Ask for a New York State license number, a written scope that lists every camera, its power source and the Sync Module plan, and a clear statement of who does any electrical work. Ask whether they will test signal from the final mounting spots and test recovery after a router restart. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and leaves a written handover sheet on every job.

Can you put our Blink cameras on a fieldstone or brick wall without wrecking it?
+

Yes. We drill into the face of sound stone or brick rather than soft mortar, use anchors made for masonry, and keep holes small enough to fill neatly if the camera is ever moved. Where the house has wooden trim, a porch beam or a lintel near the spot, we often mount there instead, which is gentler on an old wall and less visible from the street.

Can our new Blink doorbell run off the old doorbell wiring in our farmhouse?
+

Only if the chime system supplies what Blink asks for: 16 to 24 volts AC at 50 to 60 hertz and no more than 40 VA. Old farmhouse chimes often fall outside that. If the wiring does not qualify, Blink's app reports that wired power is not supported, so we test the chime first and fall back to a battery doorbell or Blink's plug-in adapter where needed.

Do we have to pay Blink every month to keep clips from the cameras?
+

It depends on your Sync Module and what you want to keep. Security.org notes Blink's plans include 60 days of cloud storage, and listings show that an Outdoor 4 paired with the Sync Module Core saves clips only to paid cloud storage, while the Sync Module XR adds a slot for local recording. We explain the options before you choose; any plan is billed by Blink, not by us.

How many Blink cameras can one Sync Module handle on a big property?
+

The real limit on a valley property is usually distance and walls, not numbers. For the Extended Range model, listings state one Sync Module XR takes up to ten devices but only two can use extended range, and an account can hold up to two XR modules. We plan modules by zone, house, barn and drive, from the signal readings rather than the camera count.

Where should the Sync Module sit in a stone house when the router is in the basement?
+

Somewhere it can reach both the router's Wi-Fi and the cameras without passing through the thickest masonry. Blink advises keeping the module away from stone, brick, concrete and metal. In practice that is often a ground-floor room with windows facing the cameras, a frame addition or a porch, chosen after we measure signal at each camera spot with the doors closed.

Will a solar panel keep a Blink Outdoor going on our shaded lot in Putnam?
+

Possibly, but check two things first. Battery guides citing Blink say the Solar Panel Mount powers the Outdoor 3rd Gen only, so an Outdoor 4 needs a power adapter instead. And a panel must get real winter sun, which wooded lots and north-facing walls often lack. We look at where the panel would sit in December before recommending it over a power cable.

Can we take our Blink cameras with us when we sell the house?
+

Usually, since battery cameras come off their mounts easily, but agree it with the buyer in writing, because some contracts treat anything fixed to the house as staying with it. We can remove the cameras and mounts, fill the holes, and reinstall the system at your new property. Remove the devices from your Blink account before handing over the house.

Our camera by the trash and bird feeder records animals all night. Can that be fixed?
+

Mostly, by moving the zone rather than the camera. The DEC lists bird seed and feeders as the most common bear attractant, and the Southern Black Bear Range reaches southern Orange and Rockland, so busy feeders and bins draw constant visitors. We tighten activity zones to the paths people use, lower sensitivity and adjust the angle so animals at the bins no longer drain the batteries on your door camera.

Which of your price pages applies to a house right on a county line?
+

The county the property actually sits in, which is the one named on your tax bill, not necessarily the one your mailing address suggests. Postal town names cross county lines in places. Tell us the street address when you book and we will apply the right county's page, so the quote and the paperwork match.

Do you cover the whole Hudson Valley, including places north of Ulster and Dutchess?
+

AESS's Hudson Valley service covers six counties: Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster. The wider region reaches up to the Capital District, but those counties are not on our Hudson Valley price pages. If your property is outside the six, call and ask; we will tell you honestly whether we can help.
