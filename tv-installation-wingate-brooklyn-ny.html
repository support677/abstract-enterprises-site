The Neighborhood

Why Wingate Properties Need TV Installation

Wingate has had other names and other edges. In the nineteenth century much of this ground was known as Pigtown, after the small pig farms run by Italian and Irish immigrants, and BKReader's 2024 profile notes it was for a time one of Brooklyn's pork-producing districts. The name Wingate came later, borrowed from George W. Wingate High School and the park beside it, both named for George Wood Wingate, the lawyer and Civil War veteran who ran the Public Schools Athletic League. The edges are still argued over. Wikipedia draws the neighborhood from Empire Boulevard south to Winthrop Street and from New York Avenue east to Troy Avenue, inside Community Board 9. Cityneighborhoods.nyc stretches the southern line to Clarkson Avenue. Listings on the same street sometimes call it Crown Heights, East Flatbush or Prospect Lefferts Gardens. We cover all of it at the same Brooklyn rate, so the label on your lease does not change your price. What changes the job is the building, and Wingate's buildings arrived in layers that each leave a different wall behind the TV.

NYC Parks tells the clearest part of that history. The land where the park and the school campus now sit once held Kingston Avenue Hospital, later absorbed into Kings County Hospital Center across Winthrop Street. The city bought the site in 1952, the high school was built there in the mid-1950s, and the park opened in 1957, taking the name Wingate Park in 1987. Around that campus, the residential blocks were filled mostly between the wars. When we pulled the city's PLUTO land-use records for the rectangle bounded by Empire Boulevard, Troy Avenue, Winthrop Street and New York Avenue, they listed 941 residential lots. Of those, 340 were two-family houses, 274 were walk-up apartment buildings, 234 were one-family homes, 33 were condominium lots and 32 were elevator buildings. Four hundred forty-eight of the lots were built between 1920 and 1939, and 160 before 1920. Another 154 date from 1990 onward. That last group is the brick infill and the new condos you see on Rutland Road, Empire Boulevard and Brooklyn Avenue, and their walls are nothing like the ones a block away.

For anyone hanging a TV, that spread of dates is the whole story. A house from the 1920s or 1930s usually has plaster walls, and plaster in an older Brooklyn house may sit on wood lath, on metal lath or directly on brick, each of which holds a screw differently. A brick two-family may put the living room TV on an exterior wall of solid masonry. A prewar elevator building such as 486 Brooklyn Avenue, a six-story building from 1930 with 107 apartments, can have plaster partitions inside and masonry around the outside and the shafts. The postwar towers on the edges, like the sixteen-story building at 636 Brooklyn Avenue from 1959 and the twenty-story building at 710 East New York Avenue from 1968, often add concrete and block. And the buildings finished in the last few years, such as the 22-unit condo at 401 Rutland Road from 2019 and the 27-unit rental at 698 Empire Boulevard from 2021, are more likely to have drywall on metal studs, which looks just like drywall on wood until a screw spins in it. A stud finder that works perfectly in one apartment can be useless in the next.

The people in those buildings shape the work as much as the walls do. A broker-style source estimates that about eight in ten Wingate residents rent, which puts a landlord, a managing agent or a board between many households and the drill. Census figures for ZIP 11203, which covers Wingate and a much larger stretch of East Flatbush, show 44.1 percent of employed residents working in education, health care and social assistance, against 29.3 percent for Brooklyn as a whole, and 20.9 percent leaving for work before 5 a.m., between noon and 4 p.m. or between 4 p.m. and midnight. Living next door to Kings County Hospital and SUNY Downstate means many households run on shifts. For a TV installer that has two practical effects: someone may be sleeping in the next room while we drill, and the best hour for the visit is not always the obvious one. We ask about both when we book, and we plan the noisy part of the job, the drilling, so it happens in one short burst rather than spread across the visit.

There is also the street calendar. Wingate Park hosts free summer concerts that the Borough President's office says began in the late 1970s; Brooklyn Paper quoted the Borough President saying more than 5,000 people came to one year's show, with the entrances on Brooklyn Avenue and Winthrop Street. And Empire Boulevard, Wingate's northern edge, is part of the J'Ouvert route on Labor Day morning. NY1 reported that in 2026 Empire Boulevard was closed from Flatbush Avenue to Nostrand Avenue, and Nostrand from Empire to Rutland Road, from 11:30 p.m. on the Sunday until 11 a.m. on Monday, September 7, with the parade itself running along Eastern Parkway. We do not book TV jobs that need a van on those streets that morning, and we tell customers on the blocks nearest Empire to expect closures around concert evenings and the carnival weekend. None of this is unusual for Brooklyn; it simply decides when a technician with a 75-inch box can actually reach your door.

Same-Day Repair in Wingate

Need Repair in Wingate? A Screen That Leans, a Bracket Pulling Out of Plaster, or Wires Hanging Where the Old TV Was

Most of the urgent TV calls we get from older Brooklyn buildings fall into three kinds, and Wingate's walls make all three likely. The first is a screen that has started to tip forward or sit lower on one side. In plaster, that usually means the anchors were chosen for drywall: plastic expansion plugs or small toggles that grip the half-inch of plaster but nothing behind it. Plaster is hard on the surface and crumbly underneath, so a plug that felt tight on the day slowly crushes the material around it each time someone pulls the screen out on an arm. The second is a bracket on brick that was screwed into the mortar joints instead of the brick, or into brick with the wrong anchor, and has started to work loose. The third is the leftover mess after someone moved out: an empty bracket, a hole with a cable coming out of it, a power strip taped to the wall behind where the TV used to be.

If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back. Take the weight off the bracket if two people can safely lift the TV down, unplug it, and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list and removing an old bracket is $45; if the bracket comes off and the same TV goes back on a properly anchored mount in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type, with the take-down added. When the old holes are too close to where the new fixings need to go, a plywood backer board ($85) spreads the load across sound wall and covers the damage, and it can be painted to match. We tell you before we start which of those the wall needs, and if we find the problem is in the wall itself, a crack running through the plaster or a loose brick, we say that plainly so you can bring in the landlord or a mason.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Wingate's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every Wingate TV call. They differ in what the walls are made of, how far the TV has to be carried, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled. Those three things decide the plan long before anyone talks about brands.

The brick two-family

The most common lot in Wingate by PLUTO's count: two stacked apartments, plaster inside, brick outside, often with the owner living on one floor and renting the other.

The walk-up apartment house

Three- and four-story buildings from the 1920s and 1930s on the side streets. Stairs only, narrow turns, plaster partitions and sometimes brick behind the living room wall.

The prewar elevator building

Six-story buildings like 486 Brooklyn Avenue, built in 1930 with 107 apartments. Elevators help, but management rules and mixed wall construction come with them.

The postwar tower

Taller buildings on the edges, such as 636 Brooklyn Avenue (sixteen stories, 1959) and 710 East New York Avenue (twenty stories, 1968), where concrete and block are common.

The one-family row house

Two-story attached houses from between the wars and later brick infill such as the "Fedders" houses Brownstoner described on Rutland Road.

The new condo or rental

Buildings from 2019 on, like 401 Rutland Road and 698 Empire Boulevard: drywall on metal studs, large windows, and an office that wants paperwork before the visit.

The brick two-family: two households, one set of walls and an owner who often lives downstairs

The two-family house is the backbone of Wingate. In most of them, the living room runs across the front of the house, and the longest uninterrupted wall, the one everybody wants for the TV, is often either the party wall shared with the house next door or an interior wall with the stair behind it. Both matter. A party wall in a brick row is usually solid masonry under its plaster, which is excellent for holding a heavy screen but means a hammer drill and masonry anchors, and on our list the brick add-on is $65 instead of the $45 for plaster on framing. An interior wall beside the stair is usually framed, with plaster on wood lath, and a fixing there has to land in the framing or use an anchor rated for hollow plaster. We find out which by scanning and then by drilling one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it, because the color of the dust tells the story: white or grey powder for plaster, red for brick, grey grit for block, and a sudden drop into nothing for the hollow behind lath.

The second thing about a two-family is that the tenant and the owner are often neighbors. If you rent the upstairs unit, the owner may live below you and may hear the drill, and may well be the person who decides whether the TV can go on the wall at all. We suggest a two-line text to the owner before the visit, saying which wall, roughly how big the holes will be and whether wires will go inside the wall or in a cover. Owners who are asked first rarely object; owners who find out from the noise sometimes do. If you own the house and are setting up the rental unit, mounting the TV for the tenant on a wall you chose, with the wires in an in-wall kit, is one way to stop five different tenants drilling five different sets of holes over the years.

The walk-up apartment house: the stairs come first, then the wall

Walk-ups are the second most common kind of building in Wingate, and for a TV installer the stairs are half the job. A 65-inch screen in its box is long and awkward, and a 75-inch one is longer still; in a narrow stair with a turn at each landing, the box sometimes has to go up on its edge, which is fine for a TV that is properly packed and a problem for one that has been unboxed and re-wrapped in a blanket. Our list adds $40 for a third floor or higher with no elevator, and for screens of 86 inches and up it already includes a second technician, because one person cannot safely carry and lift a screen that size. If your TV is still in the store or in a delivery truck, have it delivered in its original box and leave the box intact until we arrive; it is the safest way to get it up the stairs.

Inside, the walls of a 1920s walk-up are usually plaster, and the question is what is behind it. Partitions between rooms are commonly framed in wood with plaster on lath. The walls along the outside of the building and between buildings are often masonry. In many apartments the best TV wall is one of the long side walls of the living room, which in a walk-up is often a party wall shared with the next building. That gives a very solid fixing but a long drill through dense material, and we check the other side's layout where we can, because nobody wants a bit coming through the neighbor's kitchen. We also look for the old things that run through these walls: gas lines to the kitchen, radiator risers in the corners, and surface conduit added during electrical upgrades. A scanner finds most of them, and the pilot hole finds the rest before the big bit goes in.

The prewar elevator building: an easier carry, a building with rules

The elevator buildings hold far more apartments than their number of lots suggests. By PLUTO's unit counts, the 32 elevator lots in the rectangle we checked hold roughly half of all the homes. A building like 486 Brooklyn Avenue, six stories and 107 apartments from 1930, makes the carry simple, but it usually comes with a management office or a super who wants to know who is coming, when, and with what insurance. Many buildings of this kind set working hours for contractors and ask for a certificate of insurance naming the building and the managing agent. Ours is free, and we send it before the visit. If the building needs the elevator padded or reserved, we need to know a day ahead.

The walls in these buildings are mixed. Interior partitions in buildings of this age were often built of hollow clay tile or gypsum block with plaster over them, and the outer walls and shafts are masonry. Hollow tile is the wall that catches out handymen: it looks and sounds like solid masonry, but a standard sleeve anchor can break through the thin face of a tile into the void behind, and a plastic plug simply spins. The answer is an anchor designed for hollow masonry, placed with a slow drill so the tile face does not shatter, and sometimes a plywood backer that spreads the load across more fixings. We price that wall as masonry on our list. If the building has a rule against opening walls for wiring, the cables go in a paintable cord cover ($59) or down into a console instead of through an in-wall kit.

The postwar tower: concrete, block and long hallways

The taller buildings at Wingate's edges, such as 636 Brooklyn Avenue from 1959 and 710 East New York Avenue from 1968, were built later and differently. Walls between apartments and along corridors are often concrete block, and some of the exterior walls are poured concrete. Concrete and cinder block are a +$75 add-on on our list, because they need a hammer drill, carbide bits and anchors set to a specific depth, and because a concrete wall gives no way to fish cables inside it. That last point decides the cable plan in most of these apartments: the wires either run in a surface cord cover painted to match, or the TV goes on a wall with the console directly below it so the cables drop out of sight behind the furniture.

Some of these buildings also have drywall partitions added during renovations, on wood or metal studs, and the living room may have one of each. We scan every wall, and if the one you chose is block but the one beside it is a framed partition with room for an in-wall kit, we show you both options and the price difference before deciding. Heavier full-motion arms on block need more care, because an arm pulled out from the wall levers its whole weight on the top anchors. On block, we use the full set of fixing points on the bracket plate and avoid placing anchors in the hollow cores or the mortar joints.

The one-family row house and the brick infill

Wingate's one-family houses come in two broad generations. The older ones are attached two-story row houses from between the wars, with plaster walls and a stair along one side. The newer ones are the brick infill houses built since the 1990s, which Brownstoner described in 2011 as the "new construction brick Fedders houses" that surround the older row house at 665 Rutland Road. The nickname comes from the through-wall air conditioner sleeves these houses often have, and that sleeve is worth noticing, because it usually sits in the front wall right where a TV would otherwise go.

In the older row houses the TV usually ends up on the long party wall, or over the mantel where a house has one. A fireplace that is purely decorative is still masonry, and above-fireplace mounting is a +$130 add-on on our list because it means drilling into a chimney breast and usually a higher screen, which needs a tilting bracket or a pull-down mantel mount (+$160) so you are not looking up all evening. In the newer houses the interior walls are commonly drywall, sometimes on wood studs and sometimes on metal ones, and the front and party walls are block or brick behind the drywall. That is why we never assume: two walls in the same living room can carry two different add-ons, and you see both prices before choosing.

The new condo or rental: steel studs, big windows and a management office

The newest buildings in and around Wingate include the 22-unit condo at 401 Rutland Road from 2019, the 27-unit rental at 698 Empire Boulevard from 2021, and a condo at 537 Brooklyn Avenue listed as built in 2026. Buildings of this generation are where we most often find drywall on light-gauge steel studs. Steel studs are a +$30 add-on on our list, because a lag bolt that would bite into a wood stud just strips a thin steel one. The right fixing is a toggle or a steel-stud anchor rated for the load, spread across at least two studs, and on full-motion arms we often add a plywood backer behind or over the drywall so the arm has something solid to pull against.

New buildings bring two other things. One is glass: living rooms with a wall of windows leave fewer places for a screen, and the one solid wall may be shared with the corridor or with a neighbor. The other is paperwork. A condo board or a rental management office may ask for a certificate of insurance, set working hours, and want to know whether you are opening the wall. An in-wall power kit goes through the drywall between studs and puts a recessed outlet behind the TV, which most offices accept when it is installed to code; a paintable cord cover needs no opening at all. We check with you which one your building allows before we arrive, not after.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Reads a Wingate Wall in the First Ten Minutes

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like Wingate's. They are our practice, not a story about any one address.

Look before touching. Before the scanner comes out, the technician looks at the room: which walls face the street, which one is shared with the next building, where the radiator is, where the outlets are and whether they are on the baseboard or high on the wall. In a building from the 1920s, an outlet that sits in a metal box on the surface of the wall usually means the wiring was added later in conduit, and conduit runs somewhere we must not drill.

Knock and scan. A knock along the wall at screen height tells a lot. A scanner set to deep mode confirms where framing or metal is, but on plaster over metal lath a scanner can read metal everywhere, so the knock and the next step matter more than the beep.

One pilot hole, hidden. The technician drills a single small hole where the bracket plate will later cover it and reads the dust and the feel. Plaster gives white powder and then either wood (lath), a sudden void, or red or grey grit (brick, tile or block). That one hole decides the anchor, and if it changes the add-on from what we quoted, you hear it before any more drilling.

Tape, then sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen on the wall. The technician asks you to sit where you usually watch, and checks the height, the glare from the window and whether a door or radiator gets in the way. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket costs a wall.

Drill once, quietly where possible. If someone in the next room sleeps by day, the drilling is done in one burst, after warning them, and everything noisy that can be done outside the apartment is.

Wall by Wall

Plaster, Brick, Block or Steel: What Holds a TV in a Wingate Wall and What Costs Extra

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV from 32 to 55 inches on a flat or tilting bracket on drywall over wood studs, labor only. Almost nothing in a 1920s Wingate house is drywall over wood studs, which is why we explain the wall add-ons before anything else. Each one exists because that wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time, not because older buildings are an excuse to charge more.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). This is the classic wall of a Brooklyn house from before the Second World War: thin wooden strips nailed to studs, covered in two or three coats of plaster that can add up to an inch thick. It is strong in compression and weak in pull. A screw into lath alone, or a plastic plug in plaster alone, can feel solid and still fail over months. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into the stud behind, with a pilot hole drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward, or uses a toggle rated for the load where the bracket cannot reach a stud. Studs in these houses are not always on modern spacing, so we find them one at a time rather than measuring from a corner.

Plaster on metal lath or on masonry (priced by what is behind it). In some prewar buildings the plaster sits on expanded metal mesh, which confuses scanners, and in others it is applied straight onto brick or tile. Once the pilot hole tells us what is behind the plaster, we price the wall by that material: plaster on framing at +$45, plaster on brick at the brick rate.

Solid brick (+$65). Party walls and exterior walls of Wingate's brick houses and walk-ups are commonly solid brick under the plaster. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor goes into the brick itself and not the mortar joint, which is softer and can crumble around a fixing. We use a hammer drill, sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and we keep the holes a safe distance from the edges of each brick. On older, softer brick we may use more fixings at lower load each.

Concrete or cinder block (+$75). Block walls appear in the postwar towers, in basements and in some of the newer infill houses. Hollow block has thin faces with voids behind them, and an anchor placed into a void holds very little; we place fixings into the solid webs or use anchors made for hollow block. Poured concrete is the hardest drilling of all, and a hidden steel bar inside it is the one thing that stops a hole dead, in which case we move the bracket slightly rather than fight it.

Drywall on steel studs (+$30). Common in buildings from 2019 on and in renovated apartments. Steel studs are thin, so lag bolts strip out; toggles made for steel, spread across two studs, or a plywood backer board ($85) solve it.

Tile or marble (+$95). Rare in a living room but common in a kitchen or bathroom, where some customers want a small screen. Tile needs a diamond or carbide bit, water to keep it cool and a slow start so the glaze does not crack.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The most common request after "mount it" is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right and a wrong way to do it. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole above the screen and pull it out of a hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed inside walls; Gizmodo's widely shared summary of the rule quotes the code's ban on cords "concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings" and points out that an outlet behind the TV is the proper fix. A cord inside a wall can be crushed by a fixing, overheat where it is bundled, and cannot be inspected.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into the existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate opening for HDMI and other low-voltage cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits plaster on lath and drywall walls and does not suit brick, block or concrete. For those walls, the choice is a paintable cord cover ($59), which runs neatly on the surface and can be painted to match, or a cable run to a cabinet or another spot up to 25 feet away ($99). Where the wall is brick but the TV sits directly above a console, the cables can often drop straight behind the furniture and never be seen at all.

In a rental, an in-wall kit means two neat openings in the wall that will need patching when you leave, and a cord cover means two or four small screw holes. Either is easy to restore, but it is the kind of thing your landlord may want to agree to first, which brings us to leases.

Renters and Owners

Leases, Landlords and Deposits: Mounting a TV When Someone Else Owns the Wall

With most Wingate homes rented, the question of permission comes up on a large share of our calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what we have learned from the public guidance and from doing this work, so you can ask the right question before booking.

Start with the lease. Brick Underground's "Realty Bites" column put the question to a New York real estate attorney in 2022, who said the first place to look is the lease's alteration clause, and that a landlord can restrict wall hanging, though it is uncommon. If your lease is silent on alterations, the column said, a blanket ban may be hard to enforce. If it does restrict fixtures, ask your landlord in writing and keep the reply. A landlord who knows you are using a licensed, insured installer, and that the wires will be in a cover rather than chased into the wall, is usually easy to persuade.

Understand what counts as damage. The same column quoted a property manager saying a nail-sized hole is generally fine but a larger hole is damage, and that a tenant who removes a mounted TV and leaves significant damage must repair it or pay for the repair. A TV bracket leaves four to six holes, each larger than a nail hole, and an in-wall kit leaves two openings. All of that can be patched and painted, and the column's advice was simple: patch before you move out, ask for a move-out inspection and photograph the repaired wall. It also noted that landlords must itemize any deposit deductions and return the rest within two weeks of move-out.

Consider not drilling at all. If your lease is strict, or you expect to move soon, putting the TV on its own stand on a good console is a legitimate choice. On our list a TV placed on its stand is $119.99 at the Brooklyn rate, and that includes connecting the devices. A soundbar placed on the console is $79. You get the same tidy cables, the same setup and none of the patching.

If you own the building. Owners of Wingate two-families and small walk-ups sometimes ask us to mount TVs in rental units between tenants. The advantage is control: one well-placed bracket, a proper in-wall kit, a tidy wall and a note in the lease that tenants use the existing mount rather than adding their own. Our list prices each TV the same way, and a certificate of insurance can be made out to you.

If you live in a co-op or condo. Board-run buildings may have an alteration agreement or house rules that cover wall work, working hours and contractor insurance. Hanging a TV is usually treated as minor work, but opening a wall for an in-wall kit can be treated differently. Check with the managing agent, send us whatever they require, and we will send the certificate of insurance before the visit at no charge.

Compliance notes for Wingate businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. Under the 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, objects mounted on a wall with their leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor must not stick out more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a corridor or a waiting room can break that rule when it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. The National Electrical Code does not allow flexible cords to be concealed in walls, floors or ceilings. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through an in-wall power kit with its own rated cable, or a cord cover on the surface.
  • Insurance paperwork first. Commercial landlords and managed buildings often require a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent before work starts. Ours is free and sent ahead of the visit.
  • Exits and signs stay clear. We do not mount screens where they would block an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or the swing of a door, and we keep cables off the floor in any route people use to leave.
  • Heavy screens need rated hardware. Every bracket we install is rated for more than the TV's weight, and on masonry we use anchors rated for the load. A business screen that is pulled, leaned on or bumped needs that margin more than one in a living room.

TV Installation in Wingate: Questions Answered

Walls that fool a stud finder

Our building on Lefferts Avenue went up around 1930, and my stud finder beeps along the whole living room wall. What does that mean, and can the TV still go there?

A stud finder that beeps everywhere on an old wall usually means it is reading something continuous behind the plaster: metal lath, a masonry wall, or dense plaster that confuses its sensor. It does not mean the wall is unsafe, only that the tool cannot help. In a building of that age the wall may be plaster on metal mesh over framing, plaster on hollow clay tile, or plaster directly on brick, and each takes a different anchor. Our technician confirms which one with a single small pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it, then prices the wall by what is behind the plaster: +$45 if it is framing, +$65 if it is brick, +$75 if it is block. In nearly every case the TV can go where you want it; what changes is the fixing, and on hollow tile sometimes a plywood backer to spread the load.

The only free wall in my Rutland Road walk-up is the one shared with the building next door. Is it safe to drill a party wall for a 65-inch TV?

Usually yes, and a party wall of solid brick is often the strongest wall in the apartment for a heavy screen. The care goes into depth and position. We drill only as deep as the anchor needs, which is a few inches into a wall that in older Brooklyn buildings is commonly much thicker than that, and we keep the holes in the brick rather than the softer mortar joints. Before drilling we look for anything that runs along that wall, such as a chimney flue, a radiator riser or electrical conduit, and we ask what is on the other side if you know, so we are not drilling toward a neighbor's kitchen cabinets. The brick add-on is $65 on our list, and a 65-inch TV adds $30 for its size, so the wall work comes to $274.99 before any bracket or extras.

There is a through-the-wall air conditioner sleeve right where I wanted the TV in my brick row house. Can the screen go above it or next to it?

It can sometimes go beside it, but rarely right above it. Brownstoner described the newer brick houses on Rutland Road as "Fedders houses," a name taken from the through-wall air conditioner sleeves they commonly have, and in many of these houses the sleeve sits in the front wall at about the height of a seated person's eyes. A TV directly above the sleeve would be too high to watch comfortably and would sit in the warm, damp air the unit throws out. A TV beside it is possible if the wall leaves enough room, but the masonry around a sleeve opening carries a lintel or frame we will not drill into. More often the better choice is the side wall opposite the window, which also keeps the afternoon glare off the screen. We show you the options with painter's tape before deciding.

Hospital hours and the people in the next room

Someone in our apartment sleeps through the day after hospital night shifts. How loud is the drilling, and how long does it go on?

The loud part is shorter than most people expect. On plaster over framing, the drilling for a bracket takes a few minutes. On brick or block, a hammer drill is loud for each hole, and a typical bracket needs four to six, so think of two or three short bursts over perhaps ten minutes, plus a few more if an in-wall kit or a soundbar bracket is going in. The rest of the visit, the scanning, measuring, hanging, cable work and setup, is quiet. Many households near Kings County Hospital and SUNY Downstate work shifts; census figures for ZIP 11203 show a fifth of commuters leaving for work before 5 a.m., in the early afternoon or in the evening. Tell us when you book, and the technician will do all the drilling together, warn the sleeper first, and close the door between them.

We want a TV on the wall facing the bed, but the only cable outlet in the apartment is in the living room. Does the cable box have to come with it?

Not necessarily. There are three common answers, and the right one depends on the building. If you stream rather than use a cable box, the bedroom TV needs only power and Wi-Fi, and a streaming device set up on our list costs $49. If you want live cable in the bedroom, the provider may be able to supply a second box or an app that runs on the TV, which is their service, not ours. And if the box must stay in the living room, we can run a cable to another spot up to 25 feet away for $99, which in a walk-up may mean following the baseboard in a cover rather than opening walls you do not own. We look at which of those your lease and your building allow before suggesting one.

Two-families, walk-ups and the people who own the walls

My landlord lives on the ground floor of our two-family and asked exactly what you will be doing to his wall. What should I send him?

Send him four things: which wall, the size of the TV, the type of bracket, and where the wires will go. A typical bracket leaves four to six holes about the width of a pencil, all hidden behind the TV while it is up. If you choose an in-wall power and cable kit, add that it means two neat openings, one behind the screen and one low on the wall, carrying power in the kit's own rated cable, not the TV's cord pushed inside the wall. If you choose a paintable cord cover, say that it is fixed with a few small screws and can be removed and patched later. Tell him the installer is licensed and insured, and that a certificate of insurance naming him can be sent before the visit at no charge. That answers almost every question an owner has.

We bought an 85-inch TV for our fourth-floor walk-up apartment. Does a screen that big change the price or the crew?

It changes both. On our Brooklyn list, a screen from 76 to 85 inches adds $100 to the base price, and the fourth floor with no elevator adds $40. Above 85 inches, the list adds $175 for 86 to 97 inches and $220 for 98 inches and up, and both of those sizes come with a second technician. The last tenant left a bracket on the wall and a hole with a wire coming out of it. Can we reuse that bracket for our new TV?

Sometimes, but only after it has been checked, and often it is not worth the risk. We look at three things: whether the bracket is rated for your TV's weight and fits its bolt pattern, what it is fixed into and with what, and whether the wire coming out of the wall is a TV's power cord fished through the wall, which is not allowed under the electrical code. If the bracket is sound and right for your screen, we hang the TV on it and price the job as a mount on that wall. If it is not, removing an old bracket is $45 on our list, and the holes it leaves are either reused, filled, or covered by a plywood backer ($85) under the new bracket. A cord inside the wall gets pulled out and replaced by an in-wall kit or a surface cover.

Sound, neighbors and the rest of the household

Our downstairs neighbor in a prewar elevator building complains about bass. Can the soundbar be set up so it bothers them less?

Yes, and placement matters more than people think. Bass travels through structure, so a soundbar or subwoofer sitting on a floor or a hollow console passes more of it into the floor below than one fixed to the wall under the TV. Mounting the soundbar on its bracket ($129 for a wall-mounted soundbar, or the Sanus soundbar bracket at $84.99 on our list), keeping any separate subwoofer off bare floorboards on an isolation pad, and setting the bass and night modes during setup all help. We run the room setup with you so you hear the difference at your usual volume. A soundbar cannot be made silent to the neighbors, but most complaints come from bass that is set high and sitting on the floor, and that is easy to fix.

My mother lives with us on the ground floor of our two-family and struggles with the small buttons on modern remotes. Can the setup be made simpler for her?

It can. A universal remote programmed so one button turns on the TV, the box and the soundbar together and the volume controls the right device is $99 on our list, and we pick models with larger buttons where possible. During setup we hide the inputs she never uses, set the TV to start on the channel or app she watches most, and turn off features that pop up unexpectedly. Then we sit with her and go through it, and leave a short written note beside the TV. If she watches lying down or from a chair across the room, we set the screen height and tilt for that, not for the person booking the visit.

The management office at our new building on Empire Boulevard asked whether the TV wiring will go inside the wall. What exactly gets opened, and what will they want to see?

With an in-wall power and cable kit, two openings are cut in the drywall between studs: one behind where the TV will hang, for a recessed outlet and a cable pass-through, and one near the floor, for the power inlet that plugs into your existing outlet. The kit carries power inside the wall in its own rated cable, which is the code-compliant way to hide power; nothing is connected to the building's wiring. Offices usually want to know that no fire-rated wall, such as one shared with a corridor, is being opened, that the contractor is insured, and that work happens within their hours. We send the certificate of insurance, tell you which wall the kit will go into, and if the office will not allow any opening, we use a paintable cord cover instead, which needs only a few small screws.
