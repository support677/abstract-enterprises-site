The Neighborhood

Why Windsor Terrace Properties Need Intercom Installation

Windsor Terrace is a small, narrow neighborhood squeezed between Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery, with Park Slope on one side and Kensington and Borough Park on the other. Its main corridors are Prospect Park West, Prospect Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway, and its housing comes in a few clear layers: small frame houses from the nineteenth century, roughly 700 brick row houses put up by the Calder company between 1902 and 1919, apartment buildings from the 1920s on and around Prospect Avenue, a wall of six-story co-ops facing the park on Prospect Park Southwest, and a handful of condos and rentals built since. Almost every one of those buildings already has some way of letting a visitor in. That is exactly why intercom work here is different from work in a neighborhood of new towers. The job is almost never "install the first intercom." It is "replace the one that has been failing for ten years without making a mess of a building people love."

Start with the park-facing co-ops, because they are where most Windsor Terrace intercom money gets spent. Corcoran lists 135 Prospect Park Southwest as a pre-war co-op built in 1929, six stories with two elevators, a central laundry room, a full-time superintendent and a common courtyard. CityRealty lists 185 Prospect Park Southwest, between Reeve Place and Greenwood Avenue, as a postwar co-op from 1959 with 58 apartments, an intercom and an elevator. A building like that has had a lobby panel for decades, and behind it a riser: a bundle of thin pairs climbing from the cellar to every floor, joined at junction boxes on the way. Every renovation since has been a chance for someone to cut, splice or paint over a pair. When the board finally decides to replace the panel, the most valuable thing an installer can tell them is how much of that riser still works.

The row houses are a different problem in a smaller package. Brownstoner's histories of the Calder rows describe houses sold as one-family homes and as two-families with an owner's duplex and a floor-through rental above. Many have a single bell, or a bell and a buzzer that someone added later, and a front door the upstairs household cannot open without walking down. As those houses change hands, and StreetEasy named Windsor Terrace the third most in-demand neighborhood in the city in January 2026 with searches up almost 45 percent, new owners want the upstairs tenant or the in-laws on the top floor to see the visitor and buzz the door from where they live. That is a two-household intercom on a house built for one bell.

The last layer is new construction, and it is growing. New York YIMBY reported in June 2026 that sales had launched at The Windsor, a nine-story, 98-condo building at 23 Ocean Parkway, with a part-time attended lobby, a "cyber doorman" and a package room, and occupancy expected in the fourth quarter of 2026. The City Council approved the Arrow Linen rezoning on Prospect Avenue in February 2025, for about 250 homes with 100 permanently affordable. Buildings like these are built with networked entry systems from the start, but they still need someone to plan the garage door, the package room, the side entrance and the hand-off between the remote attendant and the residents. Between the old risers, the one-bell row houses and the new remote-attendant lobbies, every kind of intercom job in the city turns up on a few dozen Windsor Terrace blocks.

There is one more reason, and Brick Underground put it in the words of a working locksmith. In its intercom repair guide, Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith said: "Intercoms are getting outdated. Now everyone wants more security. They want a camera to see in front of the door before allowing someone in." That is the request we hear most often in Windsor Terrace buildings: keep the door, keep the building's look, and let residents see who is there before they press the button.

Seven Windsor Terrace facts that shape an intercom plan

  • Two co-op eras side by side. On the same park-facing stretch of Prospect Park Southwest, Corcoran dates 135 to 1929 and CityRealty dates 185 to 1959. Both sit on the older side of the city's 1968 intercom line, which changes what the law asks of them.
  • A condo with its own garage. CityRealty lists 207 Prospect Park Southwest, The Windsor Terrace Apartments, as a 1990 condo with 35 units, nine stories, an intercom, no doorman and a full-service garage. A second vehicle entrance means a second door to plan.
  • Walk-up co-ops with grand lobbies. 6sqft describes 175 Prospect Park Southwest as a pre-war co-op with an Art Deco lobby, a courtyard and community garden off it, stroller parking in the lobby and no elevator to the top floor. A visitor there may be four flights from the person they are calling.
  • Remote attendants are arriving. The Windsor at 23 Ocean Parkway lists a part-time attended lobby, a "cyber doorman," a package room and cold storage among its services, per New York YIMBY's June 2026 report.
  • A rule tied to the building's year. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom at the self-locking entrance of buildings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968; for older buildings, the doors and intercom are required when tenants of a majority of the apartments ask in writing.
  • Mounting heights are in the rules. The Department of Buildings' rule 1 RCNY 42-01 puts push buttons no more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet.
  • App entry carries paperwork in rentals. HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners of multiple dwellings using smart access systems, including key fobs, need express consent, a written retention and privacy policy, and must destroy authentication data within 90 days.

Many Windsor Terrace boards and owners pair a new intercom with a camera over the lobby door or a fob reader on a garage or courtyard gate, and the survey is the right time to plan them together, because the cable path, the equipment closet and the power supply are shared. If that interests you, say so in your first message and we will price both on the same quote.

Repair or Replace

Need Intercom Repair in Windsor Terrace? Half the Directory Silent, Video Snow on the Lobby Screen, or a Two-Family Bell That Only Rings Downstairs

Plenty of Windsor Terrace calls begin as a repair and end as a decision. A board member writes that the third and fifth floors stopped ringing, or a new owner of a row house says the upstairs station has been dead since they closed. The first thing we do is find out whether this is a fault in a system worth keeping or the symptom of a system near the end of its life, because the honest answer changes what we quote. A 2008 Brownstoner forum thread captures the dilemma exactly: a co-op of two buildings with 16 apartments each was told by its management company that the whole system had to be replaced, while the board member posting believed "only a few buzzers on the outside of the building do not work." The replies split, some saying full replacement was right, others describing quick, cheap repairs. Both can be true. The only way to know is to test.

The commonest pattern in the park-facing co-ops is a column of apartments that goes quiet together, say every "C" line from the third floor up. That almost always points to one pair or one junction box in the riser rather than to the stations in the apartments, and it often follows work in one apartment: a kitchen renovation, a new closet, a painter who closed a junction box behind a coat of paint. We trace it with a tone generator and probe from the panel to the dead stations, open the box where the trouble starts, and either repair the splice or move that line to a spare pair. If the riser has spares and the rest of it tests well, that is a repair and a good sign for a future upgrade.

The second pattern is the lobby screen that shows snow, rolling lines or a picture that comes and goes. On older video systems that usually means the video is riding on a pair that was never meant for it, or on a splice that has started to corrode. Sometimes a new coaxial or twisted-pair run for the video alone fixes it. Sometimes the honest answer is that the camera module and the screens have reached the end of what parts are still sold for, and repair money would be better spent on a system that carries video over the same pairs digitally.

The third pattern belongs to the row houses: a two-family bell that rings downstairs but not up, or rings both and opens neither. In a house where somebody added a second button decades ago, the second household's wire often runs along a baseboard, up a closet and through a floor, and it is the first thing a renovation cuts. If the wire is gone, the decision is between a new run and a system that reaches the upstairs household over Wi-Fi or a phone app, and we test the signal where the upstairs station would go before recommending the second. The fourth pattern is the release that clicks and does not open: an intercom that is working perfectly, on a door whose strike, closer or latch is not. We measure the voltage at the strike, check the door by hand and say which part is at fault.

Repairs are booked like any other request, with a callback within two hours. If the repair will cost a large share of what a new system would, or if parts for the existing panel are no longer made, we price both side by side so the owner or the board can decide with the numbers in front of them.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Windsor Terrace's Architecture

For intercom work, a building comes down to four things: what is already wired, where the visitor stands, how far each household is from the door, and who decides. Windsor Terrace has six building types that differ on all four. The cards name them; the notes underneath explain how each one changes the job.

Park-facing elevator co-ops

Six-story co-ops on Prospect Park Southwest from 1929 and 1959, with lobby panels, elevators, a superintendent and a riser that has been spliced for decades.

Walk-up co-ops with grand lobbies

Pre-war buildings like 175 Prospect Park Southwest, with an Art Deco lobby, a courtyard and residents four flights up from the door.

Calder rows and two-families

Brick and limestone row houses from 1902 to 1919, one family or an owner's duplex with a rental above, often one bell for everyone.

Frame houses and cottages

The older wood houses on the small streets near the expressway and Green-Wood, with stud walls, low cellars and bell wire of every age.

Apartment houses over stores

1920s and later buildings on Prospect Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway with a shop at street level and a separate residential door beside it.

Condos old and new

The 1990 condo at 207 Prospect Park Southwest with its garage, and new buildings like The Windsor with remote attendants and package rooms.

Park-facing elevator co-ops: the riser decides the budget

In a six-story co-op with a lobby on Prospect Park Southwest, the panel the board sees is the smallest part of the job. The expensive part is getting a signal from that panel to every apartment, and the building already has a way to do it: the riser. If most pairs in it test good, a system designed to run over existing pairs can give every apartment a new station, and often video, without opening a single hall ceiling. If many pairs fail, the choices are new cable up a stair hall or an elevator-adjacent closet stack, or a system that reaches residents' phones and keeps wall stations only for those who want them. The quote prices each path that the test allows.

Elevator co-ops also have a superintendent and a board, and both shape the work. The super knows where the junction boxes are, which apartments were gut-renovated and which closets are stacked, and a good riser test starts with an hour of his time. The board decides when residents are told, whether apartment stations are replaced in every unit or only offered, and who pays for upgrades inside an apartment. We write the changeover notice for the board to send, schedule apartment visits in two-hour windows, and leave the old panel live until the new one is tested from the street, so nobody spends a night without a working door.

Walk-up co-ops with grand lobbies: four flights from the door

Some of the most beautiful lobbies in the neighborhood belong to buildings with no elevator. 6sqft's article on 175 Prospect Park Southwest describes an Art Deco lobby, a courtyard and garden off it, stroller parking in the lobby and a top floor reached only by stairs. For an intercom that last fact matters more than the décor. A resident on the fourth floor will not walk down to see who rang, and a courier will not wait while they try. The station in each apartment has to show the visitor clearly, the release has to hold long enough for the visitor to push the door, and the residents who are out during the day are usually best served by phone answering as well.

The lobby itself needs care. A panel in an original stone or bronze surround should fill the existing opening if possible, because a new opening in historic material is permanent. Where the old panel is smaller than anything sold today, a cover plate that frames the new panel can protect the stone around it. A courtyard entrance adds a second door to plan: if residents bring strollers and bicycles through the courtyard, that door may need its own station and release, tied to the same directory as the front.

Calder rows and two-families: one bell, two households

Brownstoner's histories of Windsor Terrace describe Calder's rows going up between 1902 and 1919, many sold as two-families with an owner's duplex below and a floor-through above. The intercom in a house like that is usually a bell, sometimes a bell and a second button someone added, and almost never a way for the upstairs household to see the visitor and open the door. When a new owner buys the house, or the upstairs floor gets a new tenant or a parent moves in, that is the moment to fix it: a two-button station outside, a station for each household, and a release each of them can use.

The cable path is the question. Some houses still have the old bell wire climbing a closet to the second floor, and if it tests good, a two-wire video system can often use it. In houses that have been renovated, the old wire is often gone, and the honest choices are a new run, usually up a closet stack or along the cellar ceiling to a riser behind a stair, or a station on Wi-Fi with phone answering upstairs. We test the Wi-Fi signal at the exact spot the upstairs station would go, because brick party walls and plaster can turn a strong signal at the router into a weak one two floors up.

Frame houses and cottages: easy walls, old wire of every age

Windsor Terrace still has small wood-frame houses from its nineteenth-century beginnings, on the side streets near the expressway and toward Green-Wood. Frame houses are kind to installers: stud walls, stacked closets and floors that can be fished. They are also where wire of every era survives, from cloth-covered bell wire to telephone cable someone repurposed. We test whatever is there before we plan around it. The outdoor station usually goes beside the door on the trim or a backing plate, so that siding or shingles are not cut, and the supply lives in the cellar on a wall well above the floor.

Apartment houses over stores: two doors, two schedules

On Prospect Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway, a building often has a store at street level and apartments above, with the residential door a few feet from the shop's entrance. Those two doors belong to different people and run on different hours, and the intercom is for the apartments only. We keep the residential system entirely separate from the shop's alarm, cameras and door hardware, run its cable through the building's own path rather than through the store's ceiling where possible, and put the panel where a visitor will see it and not mistake it for the shop. If the building has eight or more apartments, the city's intercom rules apply to that residential door like any other.

Condos old and new: a garage door, a package room and an attendant

CityRealty lists 207 Prospect Park Southwest as a 1990 condo with 35 units, an intercom, no doorman and a full-service garage. A building like that has at least two ways in, and residents and their guests use both. A visitor who drives in should be able to call an apartment from the garage side, and a resident who walks in through the garage should not be able to leave that door open behind them. In a new building like The Windsor at 23 Ocean Parkway, with a part-time attended lobby, a "cyber doorman" and a package room, the plan is about hand-offs: which calls go to the attendant when one is on duty, which go to the remote service, which go straight to residents, and who opens the package room for a courier.

In condos the governance question is as important as the hardware. The administrator account belongs to the condominium, not to whichever unit owner made the first call or to a developer who has moved on. Each unit gets its own users. Any monthly service fee is named on the quote with who pays it. And when a unit sells, the board removes the seller and adds the buyer, which is one of the reasons we hand the cable map, the logins and the warranty terms to the board's own file at the end of every job.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Test the Riser Before Anyone Picks a Panel

This is a working habit, not a story about one building. When a Windsor Terrace board asks us to replace a lobby intercom, the first visit is not about panels at all. We go down to the cellar with the super, find where the old system's power and wiring start, and then test every pair, apartment by apartment, from that point up. We write down which apartments pass, which fail, which are on spare pairs and which junction boxes have been opened before. It is slow, unglamorous work, and it is the single most useful thing a board can have before it votes.

The reason is money. A system that can reuse the riser saves the cost of pulling new cable to every floor and of opening hall ceilings, and it keeps the work out of residents' apartments except for swapping the station on the wall. A system that cannot reuse it costs more but may give the building network features the board wants. Neither choice is right in the abstract. The test tells us which choice the building can actually make, and the quote prices the paths the test leaves open.

The second walk is from the sidewalk. We stand where a visitor stands, at the foot of the stoop or in front of the lobby door, and look at where the panel is, how high it sits, whether the afternoon sun off the park hits the camera, and whether a delivery driver can find the directory without a flashlight. Then we follow a resident's route to the farthest apartment and count the flights. Those two walks, one up the riser and one up the stairs, settle most of the design before anyone opens a catalog.

Keeping the Wire

What a Windsor Terrace Co-op Can Keep When It Replaces an Old Intercom

Most boards assume a new intercom means new wire to every apartment, and the dread of hall ceilings opened, plaster dust and contractors in every unit is often what keeps an old, failing system in place for another five years. The evidence from buildings much like Windsor Terrace's co-ops says the assumption is often wrong. The neighbor to the north is the clearest example. Security Sales & Integration reported on the Park Vanderbilt, a seven-story, 148-unit co-op built in 1963 in Park Slope, which replaced an aging audio-only intercom with video door stations: two at the lobby entrances and one at the service entrance. The installer reused most of the building's existing cable.

The people involved were specific about what that saved. "By using the existing cable – with the exception of two units – we were able to save $20,000 on the job," the installer's vice president, Dan Arnold, told the magazine. The building's property manager, Tom Randazzo, described the other benefit: "It looked good and saved us the need to replaster and paint in each unit." For a board in a 1959 co-op on Prospect Park Southwest, those two sentences are the whole case for testing the riser first. If most pairs pass, the money goes into stations residents will use, not into plaster.

Smaller buildings report the same. A Brownstoner forum post from the owner of a six-apartment Brooklyn co-op describes replacing "our old buggy analog system" with six indoor video stations, one outdoor main station with a camera and two new door strikes. The poster praised the crew for using a small vacuum while drilling for the new station template, which is the kind of detail that matters in a building where people live during the work. The post said nothing about new cable, and in our experience a six-apartment building on a sound riser rarely needs it.

What can a Windsor Terrace co-op usually keep, then? The riser pairs that pass the test. The apartment station locations, which means the new stations go where the old ones were and cover the old holes. Often the lobby opening, if a new panel can fill or cover it. Sometimes the door strike, if it is the right type and in good condition, though a new system is a good time to replace a tired one. What it almost never keeps is the old power supply and control unit, which are matched to the old stations, and the old panel, which is usually the part residents are most eager to see go.

And what has to change? Any pair that fails gets repaired or replaced, or that apartment is served another way. A riser whose junction boxes have been painted shut, crushed or flooded needs those boxes opened, cleaned and labeled. And if the board wants features that need a network, such as phone answering, video recording of each call or remote administration from a managing agent's office, those need a network connection to the panel and sometimes to each floor. We tell the board which features the reused riser can support and which need new cable, so the vote is about real options, not a brochure.

The Law

1929, 1959, 1990: How a Windsor Terrace Building's Year Changes What the Intercom Rules Ask

Boards and landlords often ask whether their building "has to" have an intercom, and in Windsor Terrace the answer depends on two numbers more than anything else: how many apartments the building has, and the year it was erected or converted. The key date is January 1, 1968. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a says class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after that date must have automatic self-closing and self-locking entrance doors, and that those "containing eight or more apartments" must also be equipped with an intercommunication system at the self-locking door to the main lobby, one that lets each apartment's occupant release that door.

For buildings older than 1968, the same section works differently. Subsection 3 requires the doors and the intercom in older buildings when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments "request or consent in writing" to the installation, on forms the department prescribes. Brick Underground's guide to broken buzzers summarizes the practical effect: pre-1968 buildings must offer these features only "if requested by a majority of tenants," and it describes the cost as falling on the tenants. The Department of Buildings' entrance-door rule, 1 RCNY 42-01, also covers older buildings of eight or more apartments and sets the minimum system as a bell or buzzer, or a speaking and listening device.

Now put Windsor Terrace's buildings against that line. 135 Prospect Park Southwest, built in 1929 by Corcoran's record, and 185 Prospect Park Southwest, built in 1959 by CityRealty's, are both pre-1968. 207 Prospect Park Southwest, the 1990 condo, and The Windsor at 23 Ocean Parkway, a new building, are on the post-1968 side and well past eight units. A Calder two-family, or a small building over a store with three or four apartments, is below eight and outside the intercom requirement altogether. In practice, almost every park-facing co-op already has a working intercom, has had one for decades, and is replacing it. The law question for them is rarely whether to have one. It is what they owe once they do.

That part applies to every building with a system. An industry summary of HPD practice by Connextivity says HPD classifies a non-functioning intercom as a class B hazardous violation, and puts class B penalties at $75 to $500 plus $25 to $125 a day until a certificate of correction is filed. In a co-op, shareholders are tenants of the corporation, and a broken intercom is the corporation's problem to fix. That is one more reason to plan a replacement before the old system fails outright: a planned changeover is a board decision on the board's schedule, while a dead system with an open violation is a deadline set by someone else.

The same rule fixes where things go on the wall. In buildings that 1 RCNY 42-01 covers, no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet. We follow those heights on every Windsor Terrace job, including row-house stoops that the rule does not technically reach, because they suit a visitor standing on a step as well as on a lobby floor. None of this is legal advice for a particular building. If an HPD order, a lease or a shareholder dispute is involved, the paperwork belongs with the board's attorney or managing agent, and we give them a written description of the system and its test results.

Attendants and Packages

Part-Time Attendants, Cyber Doormen and Package Rooms: Planning Entry for Windsor Terrace's Newer Buildings

The newest Windsor Terrace buildings answer the door in a way the old co-ops never did. New York YIMBY's June 2026 report on The Windsor, the nine-story, 98-condo building at 23 Ocean Parkway between Coney Island Avenue and East 8th Street, lists a part-time attended lobby, a "cyber doorman," a package room and cold storage among its services. That combination is becoming common in new Brooklyn buildings: a person at the desk for part of the day, a remote service the rest of the time, and an intercom underneath both that has to know which one is on duty.

The weak point is the hand-off. When the attendant goes home, calls from the lobby panel have to start going to the remote service or straight to residents, and the switch has to happen every day without anyone forgetting. When a resident is expecting a contractor, the remote service needs to know whether to let them in. When a courier arrives with a package, somebody has to decide whether the package room opens for them, and the building needs a record of who opened it. None of that is complicated, but every piece of it has to be written down and configured, or residents end up with a lobby that rings nobody at seven in the evening.

For buildings like this we plan the call routing as a schedule: who receives calls from the lobby panel at each hour of each day, what happens if nobody answers, and how a resident can override it for a specific visitor. We plan the package room door as its own entry with its own credentials and its own log, separate from the residential doors, so a courier who is let into the package room cannot walk on into the building. And we plan the garage, if there is one, so that a car entrance does not become the door that bypasses everything else.

The Arrow Linen rezoning on Prospect Avenue, approved by the City Council in February 2025 for about 250 homes with 100 permanently affordable, points to more buildings of this kind in the neighborhood's future, though nothing had been built at the time of the Council's announcement. When a developer or general contractor is at the design stage, the cheapest moment to get entry right is before the walls close: cable to every door that will ever need a station or reader, a dedicated closet for the equipment and its battery backup, and a written description of the routing so the first board inherits a system it understands.

Owners of smaller new buildings face a smaller version of the same question. A small new building with a store at street level, such as the three-story, six-unit building with commercial space whose permit was filed for 1259 Prospect Avenue in April 2026, has no attendant and no remote service, but it still has residents who work, travel and order deliveries. For those buildings, phone answering on every apartment, a release that each household controls, and a clear directory that does not send couriers to the shop are usually enough. The question to ask at the design stage is the same: who answers this door at every hour, and how do they open it?

App Entry and Privacy

Phone Answering, Fobs and the Tenant Data Privacy Law in Windsor Terrace Rentals and Co-ops

When Windsor Terrace owners replace an old intercom, many choose a system that lets residents answer the door on a phone, open it with an app or a fob, and give a guest a temporary code. In a neighborhood of walk-ups and two-families where the person being called is often several flights up or not home at all, that is a genuine improvement. In a rental building, it also comes with a legal duty that many small landlords have never heard of: the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021.

HPD's page on the law describes it as applying to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access (keyless) systems, "including but not limited to key fobs." It requires owners to get express consent, either in writing or through a mobile application, before collecting the data those systems use; to give tenants a data retention and privacy policy; and to destroy authentication data no later than 90 days after it was collected or generated, unless it is anonymized. HPD's page also says an owner may not require a tenant to use a smart access system to gain entry to the tenant's own apartment.

For a Windsor Terrace landlord renting out the upper floor of a two-family, the law may not apply, since a two-family house is not a multiple dwelling; for a walk-up over a store on Prospect Avenue with three or more apartments, it may. In a co-op or condo, shareholders and unit owners who rent their apartments out bring tenants into the picture too. We are not the right people to say exactly how the law applies to your building; an attorney or managing agent is. What we do is the part that belongs to the installer: we choose systems whose settings let the owner limit what is stored and for how long, we write down what the system keeps, and we set up each household's users so that the owner does not see every door release in a tenant's apartment unless both agree.

There is a practical side to the same rule. A system that requires a smartphone leaves out residents who do not use one, and Windsor Terrace has long-time residents and older parents living with grown children. Every system we install keeps a way to answer without an app, a wall station or a call to an ordinary phone line, and every door keeps a working key. Phone answering is an option for each household, not a condition of getting in.

Churches and community rooms

Mixed-use building owners

Landlords with a store below and apartments above, who need the residential intercom kept apart from the tenant business's systems.

Managing agents

Upstairs offices are the most common business request. A practice on the second floor of a Prospect Avenue building cannot leave the street door unlocked during office hours, because it is also the door to the apartments above. A station at the street door that calls the practice's reception desk, a release the receptionist controls, and a schedule that sends calls after hours to an answering phone or to nobody solves most of it. If the building's residents share the same panel, the practice gets its own directory entry and its own button, and residents' calls never ring the office.

Restaurants and bars ask about back doors. Staff need to know who is knocking at the delivery door before they open it, especially early in the morning or late at night, and a simple audio or video station at the back door that rings the kitchen or the manager's phone does that. Schools, day care centers and churches need something stricter at the front: every visitor seen and spoken to, a release that works only from inside, and a log of who was let in and when. We set those systems up with the school or the church deciding the rules, and we do not presume to make them.

Managing agents are the commercial clients behind many residential jobs. A firm that runs several co-ops or rental buildings in the neighborhood wants to add and remove residents from its office, look at a call log when a tenant says the door never rang, and avoid sending a technician for every move-in. Cloud-administered systems do that, at the cost of a subscription; systems administered on site do not, but have no monthly fee. The quote names both options where both fit, and says who would pay the subscription.

Compliance points for Windsor Terrace business and mixed-use doors

  • Free exit. A door with an electric release must still open from inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. We match the strike or lock to the door and test the exit with the power cut on every job.
  • Accessible entry. Where an entrance has a two-way communication system, choose a station with both audible and visual signals and mount it within reach of a visitor in a wheelchair. In buildings 1 RCNY 42-01 covers, buttons stay no higher than six feet and the speaking device between four and five feet.
  • Doors tied to a fire alarm. If a door's lock is connected to a building fire alarm, that connection belongs to the fire alarm system and its approvals. We identify those doors at the survey and coordinate with the fire alarm side rather than rewiring it.
  • Rated doors. Doors between public areas and stairs are often fire-rated, and release hardware on a rated door must be listed for that use.
  • Separate systems in mixed-use buildings. The residential intercom is the landlord's; the store's alarm and cameras are usually the tenant business's. We keep the two apart in wiring, power and accounts.
  • Cameras at the door. A video station records whoever stands in front of it. Decide who can view the footage and how long it is kept, and tell staff, residents and regular visitors that the station has a camera.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Windsor Terrace: Questions Answered

These are the situations Windsor Terrace boards, owners, managers and tenants are most likely to bring us, grouped by the kind of building. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Park-facing co-ops and their risers

The riser junction box for our line seems to be hidden behind a closet wall a shareholder built during a renovation. Can it be found without opening the wall?

Usually, yes. A tone generator on the pair at the cellar end and a probe on the floors above will show where the signal runs and roughly where the box sits, and the apartments above and below often reveal the stack's path through their own access panels. If the box really is sealed inside a finished wall, the choices are a small access opening, which the shareholder's own contractor would make and finish since AESS does not patch walls, or bypassing the buried box with a new short run from the nearest reachable point. We show the board and the shareholder both options with the cost of each before anything is touched.

Does sending video over our 1959 building's old riser pairs give a worse picture than new cable would?

Not necessarily. Systems built for existing two-wire runs send the picture digitally and are designed for exactly this kind of old pair, so on wire that passes its test the picture is generally as good as the camera at the lobby panel allows. What degrades video is not age as such but bad splices, corrosion and very long runs, and those show up in the test. Where one apartment's pair is marginal, we can move it to a spare pair or give that apartment a short new run, rather than rewiring the whole building to fix one line.

Our panel sits in a hole cut through the original limestone of the lobby entrance. If the new one is smaller, what happens to the opening?

We look for a panel whose face or a matching cover plate fully covers the existing opening, so the stone does not have to be filled or cut again. Many manufacturers sell surface frames and adapter plates sized for replacing older panels for exactly this reason. If nothing covers it cleanly, the opening needs a stone repair by a mason before or after the panel goes in, and the quote says so. We would rather put a slightly larger panel on a neat cover plate than cut new stone at a building whose entrance is part of why people buy there.

Our co-op has two elevators. Could the new intercom cable simply run up an elevator shaft to every floor?

No. Elevator safety codes reserve the hoistway for the elevator's own equipment and wiring, and an elevator inspector would treat intercom cable there as something to be removed. The usual paths in a Windsor Terrace co-op are the existing riser route, stacked closets, a stair hall or a pipe chase, and in older buildings sometimes a disused shaft that the board confirms can be used. We find those at the survey with the superintendent, who usually knows the building's vertical paths better than any drawing.

Calder rows and two-families

We are turning our Calder two-family back into a one-family house. What should happen to the second bell and the upstairs station?

That depends on how you will use the top floor. If it becomes bedrooms and a home office, a station up there is still useful, because the bell at the front door is hard to hear two floors up, and you can keep the second button outside or replace the two-button station with a single-button one. If you are taking the house down to one household entirely, we remove the second button and its station, label what stays in the wall and leave the run in place, so a future owner who rents the floor again can reuse it. Converting the house's legal status is a matter for your architect and the Department of Buildings, not for the intercom.

The intercom wire for our attached row house seems to come through the neighbor's cellar. Who owns that wire, and can we reuse it?

In rows built together, it was not unusual for a builder or a later contractor to run wire along a shared cellar route, and over a century those arrangements are forgotten. A wire that passes through a neighbor's cellar is on the neighbor's property, and we do not work in someone else's house without their permission. The cleaner fix is almost always a new run inside your own house, from your cellar up your own closet or stair, so your intercom never depends on a neighbor's renovation. If the neighbor agrees and the old wire tests good, we can leave it labeled as a spare, but we do not design your system around it.

Our upstairs tenant pays for her own internet. If her station or app needs a network, whose connection should it use?

For a station that only rings, talks and releases over its own wire, neither, because a two-wire system needs no internet at all. Phone answering does need a network connection at the outdoor station or the controller, and the cleanest arrangement is that this connection belongs to the owner, since the owner owns the door. The tenant then uses her own phone and data to answer, and never has to share her Wi-Fi password with the owner or the owner's with her. We set the system up that way and note on the handover sheet whose network the intercom uses.

Condos, garages and new buildings

Our condo's garage is run by an outside parking operator. Should the garage staff be able to let residents' guests into the building?

Usually not, and that is a decision for the board, not the installer. A garage attendant is there to park cars; letting guests from the garage into the residential halls puts the operator's staff in the position of deciding who enters the building. The common design gives the garage-side door its own station that calls residents directly, the same way the lobby panel does, so a guest who drove in calls the apartment and the resident releases the door. The operator's staff keep access to the garage only. We write the board's choice into the system's settings and the handover notes.

Our new building's listing promised a "cyber doorman." What does that actually do, and what still depends on the intercom?

"Cyber doorman" and "virtual doorman" are marketing names for a remote service: staff off site watch the lobby camera and answer the lobby panel, usually during set hours, and can let in visitors and couriers according to the building's instructions. New York YIMBY lists one at The Windsor on Ocean Parkway alongside a part-time attended lobby. Everything that happens when the service is off duty, or when a resident wants to answer their own guest, still runs on the intercom: the lobby panel, the apartment stations or app, the release and the package room door. Ask the sponsor or board for the service's hours and what it is allowed to do, and check that residents can answer directly at any hour.

A year after the board took over, our condo's developer still holds the intercom's administrator login. How do we get it transferred?

Start in writing with the developer or sponsor, asking for the administrator account and any subscription to be transferred to the condominium, with the board's resolution attached. Most cloud-managed systems also let the manufacturer or its dealer move an account once ownership is shown, and a deed, offering plan or board minutes usually serves. We can help with the dealer side, take over servicing once the account is in the board's name, and set up the board's own administrator users so no single owner or former developer controls the door again.

Walk-ups and buildings over stores

Visitors to our fourth-floor walk-up keep leaving before the door unlatches. Can the panel show them that the door is open?

Yes. Many current panels show a visual signal, a light, an icon or a message on the screen, and play a tone when the release is activated, so a visitor knows to push the door now. Pair that with a release time set long enough for the visitor to reach the handle, and a strike that releases cleanly under the pressure of the closer, and most of these complaints stop. We test the release from the farthest apartment while someone stands at the door, because the problem often turns out to be a strike that only releases on the second try.

The transformer for our building's intercom is in the store's basement, which the business tenant keeps locked. Should it be moved?

In most cases, yes. If the residential intercom's power and controller sit in space the commercial tenant controls, every service call depends on the store being open and the tenant being cooperative, and a store renovation can cut the supply without anyone upstairs knowing. When we replace the system we put the new supply and controller in space the landlord controls, such as the residential cellar, a hall closet or a utility room, on a labeled outlet. If no such space exists, the quote names what would be needed, and any new outlet is work for a licensed electrician.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Windsor Terrace

Quick answers about intercoms, from search summaries and lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, are written for an average house or an average apartment building anywhere in the country. Windsor Terrace's park-facing co-ops, one-bell row houses and remote-attendant condos are not average. Here are claims that come up often, checked against the sources on this page and our own survey routine.

"Installing an intercom costs about $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's cost guide says installing an intercom system "costs an average of $2,500, including intercom materials and labor," with a normal range of $1,200 to $8,500, and that figure is what many summaries repeat.

An average across houses and buildings nationwide tells a Windsor Terrace board very little. The same guide's own range runs from a simple single console to systems costing $15,000 or more, and a two-household station on a Calder row house and a lobby system for a 58-apartment co-op have almost nothing in common except the word intercom.

What moves a Windsor Terrace price is the riser test, the number of apartments and doors, whether the lobby opening can be reused and whether the board wants phone answering. We quote each building on its own after the survey, at our Brooklyn rate, and put every part on its own line so the board can see where the money goes.

"Plan on one to two hours per intercom station"

HomeAdvisor estimates labor at roughly one to two hours per intercom station, and summaries turn that into a simple formula: count the stations, multiply.

In a building that reuses its riser, swapping an apartment station can take far less than that, because the wire is already at the wall. In a building where pairs fail, one apartment's new run can take longer than the station itself. And the time that matters most in a co-op is not on the wall at all: it is scheduling access to dozens of apartments, each in a two-hour arrival window, around shareholders' work and the superintendent's day.

Brick Underground's intercom guide gives more useful benchmarks from working installers: Daniel Vasquez estimated a five-apartment building could be done in a day, and Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech about two days for a 100-unit building. We give each Windsor Terrace board a written schedule that counts apartment visits, not just stations.

"Replacing an old intercom means rewiring the whole building"

Many answers assume that a new system needs new cable to every apartment, and that is the picture that keeps boards from voting for an upgrade.

The evidence from a building very like the park-facing co-ops says otherwise. At the Park Vanderbilt, a 148-unit Park Slope co-op from 1963, the installer reused most of the existing cable when it replaced an audio-only system with video door stations, and its vice president, Dan Arnold, said that reusing the cable "with the exception of two units" saved $20,000 on the job.

Whether a Windsor Terrace riser can be reused is a question for a pair-by-pair test, not for an assumption either way. Where most pairs pass, the board can have video without opening hall ceilings; where many fail, new cable is the honest answer and the quote shows why.

"Every building with eight or more apartments must have an intercom"

Summaries of New York's rules often state the eight-apartment threshold as a single, flat requirement for every building of that size.

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a is more specific. For class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968, buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom at the self-locking lobby door. For older buildings, the doors and intercom are required when tenants of a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing, and Brick Underground's summary describes that cost as falling on the tenants.

That nuance matters in Windsor Terrace, where the park-facing co-ops at 135 and 185 Prospect Park Southwest date to 1929 and 1959. In practice both already have intercoms, and what they owe is keeping them working: an industry summary of HPD practice says a non-functioning intercom is a class B hazardous violation.

"Video intercoms cost about $500 per apartment"

A per-apartment figure appears in some answers, including the New York Times number cited by Brick Underground of around $500 per apartment for video intercoms in large buildings.

Per-apartment pricing hides the parts of the job that do not scale with apartments. Swiftlane's 2026 cost guide makes the point directly: cost scales with entrances and doors rather than with unit count, and wiring condition, video versus audio and software are separate drivers.

A Windsor Terrace condo with a lobby, a garage door and a package room has three entrances to plan whatever its unit count, and a co-op with a sound riser may spend less per apartment than one whose riser fails. The quote shows the lobby and door work separately from the apartment work, so the board can see both.

"A virtual doorman replaces the intercom"

Marketing for remote doorman services sometimes reads as if the service is the entry system, and summaries repeat it.

A remote service answers the lobby panel and watches the camera during the hours it is paid to. The Windsor at 23 Ocean Parkway, as New York YIMBY describes it, pairs a "cyber doorman" with a part-time attended lobby and a package room, which is exactly the point: several layers, all sitting on top of the intercom.

When the service is off duty, when its connection drops, or when a resident simply wants to let in their own guest, the panel, the apartment stations and the release still have to work on their own. We plan the routing between attendant, remote service and residents as a written schedule, so the lobby never rings nobody.

"A video doorbell gives an upstairs tenant the same thing as an intercom"

For small houses, quick answers often suggest a consumer video doorbell as the cheap modern replacement for an old buzzer.

In a Calder two-family, a doorbell camera rings one account and opens nothing. The upstairs household may see the visitor on a phone, but still has to walk down a flight to open the street door, and a second doorbell beside the first means two devices on one door frame, each with its own account and its own batteries.

A two-button station with a station for each household, and a release each can use, is what actually changes the experience for the floor-through tenant or the parents on the top floor. Where the old bell wire tests good, it can often carry that system, so the difference in cost is smaller than people expect.

FAQ

Windsor Terrace Intercom Installation FAQ

What happens to our old lobby panel, apartment handsets and transformer once the new system is working?
+

We take them out as part of the job once every apartment has been tested on the new system, unless the board wants to keep something, such as an original brass panel face for the building's archive. Old stations are removed from the walls, and any wire that is no longer used is labeled as abandoned at the junction boxes so the next contractor does not mistake it for a live pair. The old equipment leaves with the crew. If an old station leaves a larger opening than the new one covers, the quote says so in advance, since AESS does not patch or paint walls.

If our co-op later changes intercom companies, do the directory and the residents' app accounts stay with the building?
+

They should, and we set things up so they do. The administrator account is opened in the co-op's name with the board's email, not ours, and the directory, the residents' users and any subscription belong to the building. If the board later hires another company, it can give that company its own administrator login without anything being rebuilt. We also hand over the cable map and the model list, so a new company can service the system without starting from scratch.

Our 1929 building has an old dumbwaiter shaft running to every floor. Can intercom cable use it?
+

Sometimes, and it can be an excellent path when it is available. A disused shaft that the board confirms is abandoned and empty is often the cleanest vertical route in a pre-war building. Before we use it we check what else is in it, whether it is part of the building's fire separation, and how any openings we make through its walls would be sealed, because penetrations through rated walls must be firestopped. The board, and if needed its architect or engineer, makes the final call, and the quote lists the shaft route and an alternative.

Should a new lobby intercom be connected to the building's backup generator, if we have one?
+

Most Windsor Terrace buildings do not have a generator, so the usual protection is a battery backup on the intercom's own supply, sized to keep the panel, the releases and the apartment stations working through a short outage. If your building does have a generator or an emergency circuit, plugging the intercom supply into an outlet on that circuit is a sensible addition. That outlet is work for a licensed electrician, and we tell the board exactly what the supply needs so the electrician can provide it.

Can a dog walker or cleaner who serves several apartments in our building get a code that only works on certain days?
+

On most networked and app-based systems, yes. A resident can issue a code or a phone credential that works only on chosen days and hours, or only until a set date, and cancel it at any time. Each resident who uses the same walker issues their own credential, so the building's log shows whose visitor came in and when. On simpler systems without app features, the walker rings each apartment as an ordinary visitor, and the resident releases the door.

How does a visitor with low vision find the right apartment on a new lobby panel?
+

Choose a panel with large, high-contrast text, physical buttons or a keypad with a raised mark on the five key, and audible feedback when a call is placed and when the door releases. Many panels let a visitor dial an apartment number directly instead of scrolling a directory, which is easier for someone who cannot read a screen. We mount the panel at heights within the city's rule for buttons and speaking devices, and we light the area around it so the panel is easy to find at night.

Our co-op has steps up to the lobby door and a ramp at the side. Where should the panel go so a wheelchair user on the ramp can reach it?
+

Where both routes meet, if there is such a spot, usually on the landing in front of the door at a height a seated visitor can reach. If the ramp arrives at a different door, that door needs its own station and release, tied to the same directory, so the visitor on the ramp is not sent around to the steps. We measure both approaches at the survey, check that the door can be opened by someone in a wheelchair once it is released, and note on the quote if a powered door opener would be needed.

Who in our building should learn to add and remove residents on the new system, and how long does that take?
+

Usually the superintendent and one board member or the managing agent, so there is always someone who can do it. On most systems adding a resident, removing one or changing a directory name takes a few minutes once someone has been shown how. We walk those people through it at the handover on the live system, leave written steps for the specific model, and remain available if a question comes up later. A building with no super can keep all of it with the managing agent.

Do the new apartment stations go where the old handsets hung?
+

In most apartments, yes, because the wire is already there and residents are used to the spot. We choose stations whose back plates cover the old footprint where possible, so the wall is not left with marks around the new unit. If a resident wants the station moved, for example from the entry hall to the kitchen, we price that as a separate item for the apartment, since it means a new run inside the unit.

We are a small Windsor Terrace co-op with no managing agent. Who should sign the quote and handle the deposit?
+

Whoever the board authorizes, usually the president or treasurer, signing for the corporation rather than personally. The quote and invoice are made out to the co-op's legal name, and equipment is ordered once the board approves the quote and the deposit is paid. If your bylaws require a shareholder vote for spending of that size, hold the vote first; we can provide the written scope and the itemized quote for the meeting.

Does a new intercom in a Calder two-family change anything for the home inspection when we sell?
+

It mostly helps. A buyer's inspector will ring both buttons, check that each household's station answers and that the release opens the door, and a system that does all three, with a cable map and model list in the house file, is one less question in the report. What matters at closing is that any app accounts and administrator logins can be handed to the buyer, so we set them up in the owner's name and leave written steps for transferring them.

Will a lobby call still reach me if my phone is set to Do Not Disturb?
+

Not always. Phones treat an intercom app like any other app, so Do Not Disturb or a focus mode can silence its calls unless you allow that app through. Most phones let you add the intercom app to the list of apps allowed to ring, and we show residents where that setting is at the handover. A wall station in the apartment rings regardless of your phone, which is one reason we suggest keeping one in households that rely on deliveries or visitors at all hours.
