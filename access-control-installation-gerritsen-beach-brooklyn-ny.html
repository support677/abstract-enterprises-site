The Neighborhood

Why Gerritsen Beach Properties Need Access Control

Gerritsen Beach sits at the southern tip of Brooklyn between Sheepshead Bay and Marine Park, bounded by Plumb Beach Channel and Shell Bank Creek on its water sides and by Avenue U and Knapp Street on land, with Gerritsen Avenue as the only road in and out. Wikipedia counts 4,797 residents in the 2020 census. City Planning’s 2017 Resilient Neighborhoods report counts roughly 1,900 buildings holding more than 2,680 homes, and describes almost all of them as one- and two-family detached houses, mostly two stories, many first built as summer bungalows. For a door installer that ratio matters more than any other number on this page: about 1.4 homes per building. Gerritsen Beach is a neighborhood of front doors, not lobbies.

That changes what access control means here. In a Manhattan tower the job is managing hundreds of residents through one entrance. On a Gerritsen Beach court the job is managing a handful of people through two or three doors, and the people are the complicated part. A house may be shared by an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other. A grown child may check on a parent most evenings. A cousin may use the boat on weekends. A mail carrier, an oil delivery and a meter reader may all need the side gate. Every one of them has historically been handed a key, and keys copy, wander and outlive the reason they were given out. A keypad code or a fob can be issued to one person, limited to one door, and switched off in a minute without calling a locksmith.

The rebuild after Hurricane Sandy multiplied the keys. Wikipedia records that almost every home on the peninsula was affected when Sandy came ashore on October 29, 2012, and City Planning counted more than 200 households in the city’s Build It Back program by the spring of 2017. Replacement houses are still being filed: New York YIMBY reported a Build It Back permit for a new two-story, two-family home of 1,402 square feet at 54 Noel Avenue, replacing an existing house, and Marketproof reported a new building permit filed on May 26, 2023 for a two-story, two-unit building at 35 Keen Court, replacing a one-story structure built in 1920. Each lift and each rebuild put house keys in the hands of excavators, carpenters, plumbers, electricians, inspectors and expediters, for months or years. When the family moved back in, most of those keys were never collected.

The peninsula also has a particular relationship with power. CBS New York reported on November 14, 2012, more than two weeks after the storm, that nearly half of Gerritsen Beach was still without power, and a New York State Senate release from November 7 of that year described homes that needed an electrician’s inspection before electricity or gas could be restored. An electric lock that has never been asked what it should do in a blackout will answer the question at the worst possible moment. On a house at the end of a narrow court, the right answer is almost always a door that stays locked from outside, opens freely from inside, and still takes a plain metal key, and that has to be chosen, not assumed.

Finally, there is the question of who has the keys to the places the community shares. Bklyner’s Ned Berke, writing about Gerritsen Beach in 2010, described the Gerritsen Beach Property Owners Association as a hybrid of civic group and corporation that collects dues, runs programs and owns property, and remarked that nobody seemed to know who held the keys to the parks. That line was about local politics, not hardware, but it describes the door problem of many volunteer-run halls, clubhouses and church buildings: keys are handed from one committee to the next and the list of holders is lost. A system that names every credential and keeps a short record of which one opened the door fixes that without anyone having to change a lock.

Access Control for Gerritsen Beach’s Architecture

The house decides the hardware. City Planning splits the peninsula into an older section south of the Gotham Avenue canal and a newer one to the north, with different lot sizes, street widths and building types, and the waterfront lots along the canal are different again. These are the six kinds of property we plan for most often.

Winterized bungalows on the old courts

One- and two-story frame houses from the 1920s on the lettered courts of the old section, many built to the lot line with the door a step from the walk. Usually one front door and one back or side door, both wood or steel in a wood frame, with siding and studs behind the trim.

Houses lifted after Sandy

The same houses jacked up onto new foundations, with the front door now at the top of a stair and a flood-vented enclosure where the ground floor used to be. Two very different doors: a living-level entrance and an enclosure door near grade.

Replacement two-family houses

New two-story, two-unit buildings put up through Build It Back or privately, such as the filings at 54 Noel Avenue and 35 Keen Court. New frames, new wiring and usually two entrances, one for each household, sometimes sharing a vestibule.

Brick houses north of the canal

Attached and semi-detached brick houses on the wider streets of the newer section toward Avenue U. Masonry beside the door, a basement or cellar below, and a door arrangement closer to the rest of southern Brooklyn.

Canal-front lots with docks

Narrow waterfront lots on the Gotham Avenue canal, City Planning gives 24 by 66 feet, where the house has a street side and a water side and the rear gate leads to a private dock. Two fronts to secure, one of them reached by boat.

One-story stores and halls on Gerritsen Avenue

Attached single-story commercial buildings along the only shopping street, plus the community, church and club buildings near it. Aluminum or steel storefront doors at the front, a steel back door, shared side walls and a roll-down gate on many.

What the materials mean for the install

A bungalow door is usually the easiest door in Brooklyn to electrify, and the hardest to make look tidy. The frame is wood, the wall behind the trim is studs and sheathing, and a strike can be mortised into the jamb in an hour. The difficulty is the outside. With the house built to the lot line and the door a step from the court, there is often no porch, no recess and no masonry to mount anything in; the reader or keypad sits on vinyl or aluminum siding, and the cable has to come through the wall from inside without leaving a gap where rain runs in. We mount on a solid block behind the siding, seal the entry from above, and keep the device off the line where the court walk splashes the wall.

Raised houses add a second problem: the door moved and the wiring did not. When a house is lifted, the cable that once ran from the basement to the front door is either cut at the old sill or left dangling in the new enclosure, and the front door now sits several feet higher than it did. We do not reuse anything that sat below the old floor line. The controller goes on the living level, the cable to the front door runs inside the new framing, and the enclosure door, if it is controlled at all, gets hardware chosen to survive getting wet. In the brick houses north of the canal the job is more conventional: masonry openings, steel frames, a basement for the controller, and a closer that usually needs attention before any strike will hold.

One House, Two Households: Dividing a Two-Family’s Doors

A two-family house is the most common access control job on the peninsula after a single front door, and the one where people disagree most about what they want. The owner usually lives on one floor and rents the other, or two generations of one family share the house. Either way there are two households, and each wants to come and go without asking the other, and to hand out entry to its own guests, without being able to see or change the other’s list.

The answer is to treat the house as two small systems on one controller. Each household gets its own group of codes and fobs, its own door if the house has two entrances, and its own administrator login. Doors the households share, such as a common vestibule, the side gate or the back yard, accept credentials from both groups. The owner’s login can manage the shared doors and the tenant’s door if the lease says so; the tenant’s login manages only the tenant’s own people. Nobody needs to know anybody else’s code, and when the tenant moves out, the tenant’s group is deleted in one step while the owner’s family notices nothing.

The legal picture is simpler than people expect. New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law treats a building as a multiple dwelling when three or more families live in it, so a true two-family house is not a multiple dwelling. That means the entrance-door and intercom rules of section 50-a do not reach it, and neither does New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, which governs smart access in multiple dwellings. A two-family owner still has to give a tenant a working key to their own home, and we recommend following the city’s privacy rules anyway: collect only a name and a credential, keep the entry record short, never use it to keep tabs on a tenant, and give the tenant a non-app way in. The NYCLU’s Lee Rowland put the principle plainly in amNewYork in 2019: “It’s fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data.” A system built that way never becomes a dispute.

Whose electricity runs the door

Many two-families here have separate meters, and a controller has to be fed from somewhere. Our practice is to power the shared doors and the controller from the owner’s side, because the owner is responsible for the building, and to put the tenant’s door on the same controller rather than a separate supply on the tenant’s meter. If the house is owned by two related households that split the bills, we label the circuit at the panel and in the job file so nobody switches it off by mistake. The outlet itself is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician; the low-voltage side is ours.

When the house changes from one household to two

Several of the replacement houses filed since Sandy are two-unit buildings standing where a smaller, older house used to be, and some owners divide an enlarged house after the rebuild. If you are adding a second household, plan the second entrance and its cable while the walls are open. A second door station added to a finished house is entirely possible, but on a court where the house sits on the lot line the cable route is the part that costs money.

Keys Nobody Can Account For: Halls, Clubhouses and Volunteer Doors

Gerritsen Beach runs a great deal of its own life. It kept Brooklyn’s last volunteer fire department, organized in 1922 after the Abbey Court fire of 1921, according to Wikipedia; it has a property owners’ association that, as Bklyner described it, collects dues, runs programs and owns property; and it has church halls, a library branch and the ballfields. Every one of those organizations has doors, and almost every one has the same problem with them: the keys are passed from volunteer to volunteer, from one year’s board to the next, and within a few seasons nobody can say with confidence how many copies exist.

Bklyner’s 2010 piece quoted one association member saying, “What goes on in Gerritsen Beach stays in Gerritsen Beach.” That is a fair description of a close community, and also of the way keys circulate in one. Rekeying a hall every time a committee changes is expensive and rarely done. Fobs solve it differently. Each member, volunteer and staff member gets a credential with their name on it in the system, the organization issues and withdraws them itself, and the door keeps a short record of which credential opened it and when. When the treasurer’s term ends, the treasurer’s fob stops working that evening, and nobody has to ask for a key back.

The same system handles the patterns volunteer buildings actually have: a meeting room that opens for a group on Tuesday evenings only, a storage room the youth league uses in season, a kitchen only certain volunteers may enter, and a front door that unlocks for a public event and locks itself again afterwards. Records belong to the organization, not to us, and we set retention short by default. For a church or hall with a public exit, the Building Code rules on free egress apply in full, and we design every door so it opens from inside without a fob, a code or a key.

What we ask an organization before we quote

Who should be able to add and remove people, and does that person change every year? Which doors are used by the public, and when? Which rooms hold anything of value or anything dangerous? Is there a secretary or a manager who can receive a held-open alert, or should alerts go to nobody but the log? Answers to those four questions usually decide the hardware more than the doors themselves.

What the Front Door Does When the Peninsula Loses Power

Every electric lock has to be told what to do when it loses power, and the choice comes in two flavors. A fail-safe lock unlocks when power is cut; a fail-secure lock stays locked. ButterflyMX’s guide to outages puts it in one line each: “Fail-safe locks unlock when power is lost,” and “Fail-secure locks remain locked when power is lost.” Most systems ride through an outage on a backup battery for a few hours, the same guide notes, and when that battery is finally exhausted each door falls back to whichever state it was set up for.

On a Gerritsen Beach house the right setting for an outside door is fail-secure, combined with a mechanical key outside and a handle inside that always opens the door by hand. Fail-safe hardware on an outside door means the house unlocks itself in a long blackout, and the 2012 outage on this peninsula lasted weeks for many households, not hours. A fail-secure strike keeps the door shut no matter how long the power is out, the inside lever still lets anyone leave instantly, and the family gets in with a key the way they always have. Magnetic locks, which are fail-safe by nature, have no place on a house door here; we fit them only on commercial doors where the Building Code’s release rules can be met in full.

Battery smart locks behave differently: they keep working through any outage until their own batteries run down, but if the house’s Wi-Fi is off, any remote features stop with it. That makes a good keypad deadbolt with a key cylinder a sensible choice for a bungalow’s side door or an enclosure door, as long as someone checks the batteries before winter. For wired systems we size the controller’s battery for the doors it runs, put it where water cannot reach, and show the owner how to test it by switching off the supply for a few minutes.

After a flood, before the power comes back

The Senate release from November 2012 described homes that needed an inspection before Con Edison would restore electricity. If a coastal flood ever reaches a controller, power supply or lock on your property, treat it the same way: leave it off, use the key, and have it checked before it is switched on again. Salt water left in a low-voltage power supply corrodes it from the inside even after it looks dry. This is one more reason we keep every powered part of a Gerritsen Beach system on the living level of the house.

Stores, offices and service businesses on the stretch where the peninsula meets the rest of Brooklyn, with more traffic and more passers-by than the southern blocks. Front doors on schedules, back doors on codes, and an office door that only managers open.

Businesses with a public front and a restricted back room. A reader on the door between them, a short entry record for the room, and a front door that opens freely from inside as an exit must.

Community and church buildings

Halls, meeting rooms and church buildings near the avenue, with members, volunteers and outside groups who need different doors at different times. Credentials issued by the organization, and public exits that always open from inside.

Small stores, short staff lists, real exposure

A store of 940 square feet does not need a building management system. It needs three things a brass key cannot give it: a separate code for each person who opens the back door, the ability to delete one person’s code without changing anyone else’s, and a short list the owner can read of which code opened the door at 6:40 on Sunday morning. A keypad or reader on the back door, an electric strike or electrified panic device, a door contact and a small controller in the back room cover that. On our Brooklyn list a keypad on a personnel or service door is $1,500 to $2,000. Front doors usually stay on their mechanical lock and roll-down gate, opened by the first person in, because a storefront door that unlocks on a timer only makes sense if someone is behind the counter when it does.

Package and delivery traffic is the other pressure on these doors. The SafeWise 2025 Package Theft Report ranked New York City the second-worst metro area in the country for package theft, with an estimated 6,374,072 incidents and $248,221,997 in losses over the year it covered, and gave New York State the highest household incidence of any state, 965 incidents per 1,000 households. Those figures are citywide and statewide, not a Gerritsen Beach count. They are still the reason a store owner here asks whether a regular driver can be given a code that opens the back room for a delivery window and nothing else. The answer is yes, and the code expires the day the supplier changes drivers.

Rules that apply to Gerritsen Beach business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code an electrically locked door on an exit path must release when power fails and when a fire alarm or sprinkler system activates, and a magnetic lock needs release hardware built into the door that works with one hand. Every store, office and hall exit we install opens from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above a store. Where three or more families live in a building it is a multiple dwelling. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a the street entrance of a newer class A building must be self-closing and self-locking, so a door shared with apartments cannot sit on a daytime unlock schedule for the store.
  • Tenant data. In a multiple dwelling that uses fobs, codes or apps, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to the residential doors: consent, a written privacy notice, minimal data and no selling it. A store’s own staff door is not a residential door.
  • Permits and trades. Doors & Hardware magazine notes that under the model building code the permit exceptions “do not include access control systems or the installation of electrified hardware,” and that requirements vary by jurisdiction. In New York City the low-voltage wiring is our work; the 120-volt outlet for a power supply is a licensed electrician’s, and exit-door hardware has to meet the Building Code. We tell each owner which applies before the job is priced.
  • Licensing. AESS works under New York State license #12000287431 and carries insurance; a landlord or association that needs a certificate naming it gets one before the first visit.

Access Control in Gerritsen Beach: Questions Answered

Bungalows on the courts

Our bungalow’s door opens a step from the court walk, and neighbors pass with carts, bikes and strollers all day. Will a keypad sticking out from the siding get knocked off?

It can, if the wrong device goes in the wrong spot. City Planning describes the old section’s streets as narrowing to about 15 feet including the sidewalks, and Urban Omnibus noted that many homes there are built right to the property line, so the wall beside your door may be the edge of the walk. A deep keypad deadbolt or a bulky reader that projects two inches from the siding is exactly at handlebar height. We choose the slimmest device that does the job, mount it on the hinge side of the door where the opening door shields it, and keep its face flush with the trim where the siding allows.

Where even that is too exposed, the reader can go on the inside face of the door frame, facing across the opening, or the credential can be a fob read through a flush reader set into the trim. As a last resort the keypad moves to a protected spot such as the side of a stoop or the post of a front gate, and the lock stays in the door.

Our two-family’s second entrance is around the side, down a passage about three feet wide between our house and the next one. Can a reader go there without blocking the passage?

Yes, but the passage changes the hardware. In a gap that narrow, anything mounted on the wall at elbow height will be brushed by every person and every trash can that goes through, and a door that swings out into the passage limits where a device can sit at all. We usually put a flush or very slim reader on the latch-side jamb, inside the line of the door frame, and run the cable inside the wall rather than along it, so nothing projects into the passage.

The passage is also dark, damp and often shared with the neighbor’s side of the property line. A backlit reader helps at night, the device should be rated for outdoor use with the opening sealed from above, and if the passage itself belongs partly to the neighboring lot, nothing gets fixed to their wall. We check the property line on site before we drill.

Raised and rebuilt houses

Every trade used the same lockbox code during our lift. When the next phase of work starts, what should replace it?

A lockbox with one shared code is a key with no record attached: anyone who ever knew the code can still open the box, and nobody can say which trade was last inside. For a house that will be worked on again, say to finish the enclosure, add solar or build out the second unit, a keypad lock on the work door is a much better tool. Each contractor gets a code with their company’s name on it, valid on weekdays during working hours, ending on the day their part of the job is due to finish.

If something goes missing from the site, the lock’s record shows which codes were used that week. When the work is done you delete the contractor codes and the door goes back to being a family door, with nothing to collect and no lockbox left on the railing for anyone to pry open. It is the same hardware we would fit for the family afterwards, so nothing is wasted.

The enclosure under our raised house floods in big storms. Is it smart to put an electric lock on that door at all?

Often it is not, and we say so. A flood-vented enclosure below the design flood elevation is built to let water in and out. Any powered lock, reader or supply in that space will eventually be wet, and salt water ruins electronics even after they appear to dry out. If the enclosure only holds bikes, tools and the recycling, a good mechanical lock or a battery keypad deadbolt rated for outdoor use, with a key cylinder, is the sensible choice: when it fails after a storm, you replace a lock, not a system.

If the enclosure is the way the family actually comes and goes every day, we can control it, but we keep the controller and power supply upstairs, choose a lock with no electronics in the door if possible, and accept that the reader outside is a part that may need replacing after a serious flood. What we will not do is put the house’s controller or battery in the enclosure to save cable.

Our raised house has a front door at the top of the stair, but we really come and go through the enclosure and an inside stair. Which door should carry the reader?

The one you use. Access control is meant to replace the key you carry every day, so the reader belongs on the door you actually walk through, and the other door can stay on a key and a door contact that tells you whether it is shut. If that daily door is the enclosure door, the same flood caution applies, so the usual arrangement is a weather-rated keypad deadbolt on the enclosure door and a controlled lock on the inside door at the top of the interior stair, which is dry and inside the house.

The front door at the top of the outside stair then becomes the door for visitors and deliveries. A video station there, calling your phone, lets you answer a visitor without walking around to the front, and it can release that door for someone you recognize. Both doors can share one controller on the living level.

Two-family houses

Our tenant works nights and we keep day hours. Can the shared side gate send alerts to one household and stay quiet for the other?

Yes. Notifications in most current systems are set per user and per door, not per house. You can have the gate alert your phone when it is opened outside the hours your family normally uses it, while the tenant gets nothing about the gate and only receives alerts about her own entrance, or none at all. Her comings and goings at 3 a.m. stop pinging your phone, and your morning school run stops pinging hers.

We set it up on the hand-over visit with both households present if possible, so each sees exactly what the other will and will not be told. The owner’s login can always look at the gate’s history if something happens; nobody gets a running feed of the other household’s movements.

The internet in our two-family is in the tenant’s name. If she cancels it or moves, do our doors stop working?

The doors should not, and we design so they cannot. A controller in the house makes every lock-and-unlock decision on its own; the internet only carries remote management, phone keys that are checked online and notifications. If the tenant’s service ends, codes and fobs keep opening the doors and the locks still behave as set. What you lose until a connection is restored is the ability to change codes from your phone and the alerts.

Even so, a door system should not live on someone else’s account. The cleanest arrangement is a small cellular or separate owner’s connection for the controller, or the owner’s own internet service if the owner lives in the house. We ask about this on the site visit and put the answer in the plan.

Visitors on our tight court keep ringing the wrong household. Can the door station make it obvious which home they are calling?

Yes. A station with a separate, clearly labeled button for each household, or a short directory showing two names, removes the guessing. Each button calls only its own household’s phones or indoor screen, so a guest for the tenant never wakes the owner. Where the two households have separate doors, each door can have its own small station and the confusion disappears altogether.

Labels are worth thinking about. Some households prefer a first name or “Upstairs” to a surname on a station facing a public court. Whatever is printed, couriers and guests can be told it in advance.

Yards, sheds and the water side

We keep fishing gear and an outboard in a shed at the back of our canal lot. Is a keypad on the shed door worth more than a good padlock?

For most sheds, a good padlock on a solid hasp is the honest answer, and it costs far less. A keypad lock earns its price on a shed when several people need it at different times, such as family members who share a boat or a neighbor who borrows the trailer, because each can have a code you can delete, and when you want a record of when it was opened. The weak point on most sheds is the door and hinges, not the lock, so a keypad on a flimsy door adds little.

If you do want a keypad, a battery keypad lock rated for outdoor use is better than running cable across a yard that floods. Mount it on the side away from the prevailing weather, and plan to check the batteries and the seal each spring before the season starts.

Gerritsen Avenue stores and community buildings

Our store on Gerritsen Avenue is one room of under a thousand square feet with a back door to the yard. Is a reader on that back door worth it for a business this small?

Usually, if more than two people have keys. The back door of a small store is the one opened early for deliveries and late for trash, by whoever is working, and it is the door you cannot see from the counter. A keypad or reader with a code per person, a strike that relocks the moment the door closes, and a contact that reports the door standing open gives a store this size the same control a chain store has, at the price of one door: $1,500 to $2,000 on our Brooklyn list for a keypad on a service door.

If only the owner ever opens the back door, a solid deadbolt and a camera may be all you need, and we will tell you so. The store’s own exit rules still apply either way: the back door must open from inside without a key whenever the store is occupied.

We run the store as a family and everyone uses the same back-door key. Do family businesses really need separate codes?

They benefit from them more than most. In a family business the people change slowly but the jobs change all the time: a nephew who worked summers moves away, a cousin who did the books retires, a part-time helper is hired for the holidays. With one shared key, each of those changes is either ignored or means a new lock. With separate codes, each person’s access is added and removed on its own, and the record shows which family member opened up on a given morning without anyone feeling accused.

Separate codes also make it easier to give the hired help less than the family has: the stock room and the front, say, but not the office. That is a setting, not new hardware.

Volunteers lock up our hall after evening events. Can the board find out the next morning whether the door was actually left locked?

Yes, with two parts. A door contact tells the system whether the door is closed, and the lock tells it whether it is locked. Together they can send a message if the door is still unlocked, or standing open, at a set time after the event should have ended, and the morning report shows exactly when the door last closed and whose fob was used last. The door can also be set to relock itself at a fixed hour no matter what, so a forgotten unlock never lasts the night.

Exits are not affected: the hall’s doors still open from inside at any time. The record belongs to the organization, and we keep it short by default, enough to answer the next morning’s question and not much more.

Our church hall rents its room to outside groups. Can a renter’s code work only during their booking?

Yes. A code or fob can be given a start and end time, down to the hour, and limited to the doors the group needs: the street door and the hall, say, but not the office, the kitchen or the sanctuary. A recurring group can get a weekly schedule; a one-time party gets a code that works for that evening only. If a booking is cancelled, the code is deleted and nothing else changes.

Give each group its own code rather than reusing one, so the record shows which group was in the building. And keep at least one member of your own organization able to override the schedule, so a group running late is not stuck outside a locked door at the end of its booking.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Gerritsen Beach

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides of the kind published by Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. We do not attribute any claim to one of those sites unless we quote it; each is set against what a door on this peninsula actually needs.

“Access control is for office buildings and apartment lobbies.”

Most guides are written for property managers, so they assume a lobby, a front desk and dozens of tenants.

City Planning counted about 1,900 buildings and some 2,680 homes in Gerritsen Beach, almost all one- and two-family houses. Here the people who need doors managed are a family, a tenant, a few relatives and the trades.

A house-sized system is one door, a handful of codes and a small controller, and it is priced on the bottom line of our Brooklyn list, not the top.

“Fail-safe locks are the safer choice.”

The word “safe” misleads. A fail-safe lock unlocks when power is lost; a fail-secure lock stays locked, as ButterflyMX’s outage guide spells out.

On an outside house door, fail-safe means the door opens itself in a blackout, and CBS New York reported nearly half of Gerritsen Beach still without power on November 14, 2012.

Life safety comes from the inside handle, which opens a fail-secure door by hand at any time. That is the combination we fit on houses.

“Installed access control costs $1,500 to $3,500 a door, plus software.”

Ranges like that are real; one national cost guide gives $1,500 to $3,500 per door for a standard card or mobile system and lists cloud software from about $3.50 to $15 per door per month.

They describe commercial doors with networked readers. Our Brooklyn list starts one controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the wiring and ten fobs at $1,500 to $2,500, and a local system has no monthly fee unless you choose cloud management.

A battery keypad deadbolt on a shed or enclosure door costs far less than either, and we say so when it is the right answer.

“Smart locks are weatherproof.”

Many are sold as weather-resistant, which is not the same as suited to a canal lot.

All Security Equipment’s guide to outdoor ratings recommends IP66 or IP67 for coastal climates because “salt accelerates metal corrosion,” and IP67 or IP68 where equipment may be submerged.

No rating makes a reader survive a flood that covers it. That is why the powered parts of a Gerritsen Beach system go on the living level, and why a door in a flood-vented enclosure gets the simplest hardware that will do.

“A landlord can switch everyone to an app.”

amNewYork reported in 2019 that the city’s Department of Buildings treats fobs, cards and metal keys as keys and that technology may not simply replace them; tenants in a Hell’s Kitchen building sued when lobby keys were taken away.

New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law later barred owners of multiple dwellings from requiring smart access to enter a home.

A Gerritsen Beach two-family is not a multiple dwelling, but its tenant is still entitled to a key to the apartment, and we design every system with a non-app way in.

“Once the reader is on, the door is secure.”

A reader only controls a door that closes and latches by itself.

Houses lifted onto new foundations settle, rebuilt frames move as new lumber dries, and closers fitted during a rebuild are often never adjusted. A strike that lined up on hand-over day can miss its latch a season later.

We fix the door first, add a contact so the system knows whether it is really shut, and check it again on the follow-up visit.

“Wi-Fi smart locks are all a house needs.”

For one door and one household, a good keypad deadbolt with a key cylinder is often enough, and we fit them.

It stops being enough when two households share doors, when a gate and a shed join the front door, or when a store needs staff schedules. One app account for a whole two-family means one household sees and controls the other’s list.

A small controller with separate logins keeps them apart, keeps working without the internet, and can still use battery locks on the doors that do not justify cable.

Before hurricane season and before the heat comes on: a five-minute check

Before a storm, make sure everyone in the house has a working key, and walk through the list of codes and delete the ones that belong to people who no longer need them.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before the Doors

This describes how we approach a Gerritsen Beach house, not a particular customer. Before we look at a single door, we ask the owner to write down everyone who has a key: each person in the household, the tenant and anyone the tenant has given a copy to, the relative who checks the mail, the neighbor who feeds the cat, and every contractor who worked on the lift or the rebuild. On houses that went through the years after Sandy, the list is usually longer than the owner expected, and a few names on it come with a question mark.

That list decides the job. It tells us how many groups the system needs, which doors each group should open, and whether the old cylinders should be rekeyed on day one so that the keys with question marks stop working. Only then do we walk the doors: whether each one latches on its own, where a slim reader fits beside it, and which household’s circuit can power it. A keypad on a door that half the street still has keys to protects nothing.

FAQ

Gerritsen Beach Access Control FAQ

What does a keypad and electric strike on a court bungalow’s front door cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

It falls on the single controlled door line, $1,500 to $2,500, which covers the keypad or reader, the strike, the wiring, a battery-backed controller and ten fobs. A bungalow with a wood frame and an easy route for cable sits toward the bottom of that range; a door on the lot line with no basement or attic access sits higher. If a battery keypad deadbolt is all the door needs, we will say so, and that costs less. Sales tax is added and the written figure follows the visit.

Do we have to give up our deadbolt to add a keypad and an electric strike?
+

No. The strike goes into the frame for the latch, and the deadbolt stays exactly as it is. Most households leave the deadbolt open during the day and turn it at night or when they leave the house for a long time, which gives the door a mechanical lock that no code can open. Just remember that while the deadbolt is thrown, the keypad cannot let anyone in, including a family member without a key.

Can someone copy our fob the way a hardware store copies a house key?
+

Older 125 kHz proximity fobs can be copied with an inexpensive cloner, and installers and residents discuss exactly that on Reddit’s access control forums. That is why we fit encrypted credentials on new systems, which a cheap cloner cannot read. Even so, treat a fob like a key: if one is lost, delete it from the system the same day, which takes a minute and costs nothing.

If the batteries in our keypad deadbolt die completely, are the codes we set lost?
+

On the keypad locks we fit, no. The codes are stored in memory that does not depend on the batteries, so fresh batteries bring the lock back with its codes intact; confirm this for any model you buy yourself. While the batteries are dead the key cylinder opens the door, which is why we never fit a keypad lock without one. Most of these locks warn you with a light or a beep weeks before the batteries run out.

After the install, who keeps the master or installer code for our system, you or us?
+

You do. At hand-over the owner, landlord, store or organization receives the administrator login and any installer or master code, written on the job sheet, and we keep no hidden access of our own. If you want us to make changes remotely later, you add us as a user for that job and remove us afterwards. We recommend storing the master code somewhere other than the house itself.

How many separate codes and fobs can one house system hold?
+

Far more than a house needs. A battery keypad deadbolt typically holds a few dozen codes, depending on the model, which suits one household and its helpers. A controller-based system holds hundreds or thousands of credentials across all its doors, which comfortably covers a two-family, its relatives and contractors, or a hall with a long list of members and outside groups.

We took our keypad lock off before the house was lifted. Can it go back on the new door?
+

Often, yes, if the new door is prepared for it. A keypad deadbolt needs the right bore hole and backset in the door, and a door bought during the rebuild may have been drilled for something else. We check the lock for corrosion and worn seals after its time in storage, fit new batteries, reset it to clear every old code, and enroll the family again. If the new door is steel or much thicker than the old one, a different model may be needed.

Our back door to the deck is a sliding glass door. Can it be put on a code?
+

It can, but it is rarely the best place to start. Code locks made for residential sliders are limited, and electrified hardware for sliding doors is mostly commercial and costly. For most houses we leave the slider on its own lock with a security bar, add a contact so the system reports whether it is shut, and put the code on a hinged door instead. If the slider is the only way in from the deck, we can price a proper lock for it.

Can the camera in a door station show our visitors without recording the house across the court?
+

Yes. Many stations and cameras let us mask parts of the image so they are never shown or recorded, and on a narrow court that usually means blocking out the neighbor’s door and windows opposite. We also aim the station at the space where a visitor stands rather than straight across the court. Your neighbors see a small device by your door, not a camera pointed at their living room.

We rent our bungalow. What do you need from our landlord before putting a keypad on the door?
+

Written permission, since the door and frame belong to the landlord. We also ask the two of you to agree on three things: whether the landlord gets a code or keeps a key, what happens to the hardware when you move out, and whether the original lock goes back on at that point. A keypad deadbolt that keeps the existing key cylinder is usually the easiest thing for a landlord to approve.

Do we need to be home for the whole installation?
+

Not the whole time. An adult needs to be there at the start, to confirm the door and the cable route, and at the end, to enroll the household’s codes and fobs, learn the app and watch every door being tested. In between, many households leave us to work. For a two-family we ask for someone from each household at the hand-over so each sets up its own group.

If a lock or station has to be special-ordered, we tell you before the deposit that it is a special order, and confirm the installation date only once the part has arrived.
