The Neighborhood

Why St. George Homes Need Sonos Installation

St. George is the part of Staten Island that was planned first and built on hardest. Wikipedia describes it as the most densely developed neighbourhood on the island and the seat of both the borough government and Richmond County, packed onto about four-tenths of a square mile where the Kill Van Kull meets Upper New York Bay. The street grid goes back to 1835, when the surveyor James Lyons laid out New Brighton around the existing hills for the New Brighton Association; Hamilton Avenue, St. Marks Place and Westervelt Avenue still follow that plan. The district took the name St. George in the 1880s, after the developer George Law gave up his waterfront rights so that Erastus Wiman could build a combined railroad and ferry terminal, and Wiman kept his promise to "canonize" him.

Everything since has been layered on top of that start. Summer hotels and Italianate villas came in the 1840s and 1850s; a Civil War building boom brought Second Empire double houses to Westervelt Avenue and St. Marks Place; the railway and ferry terminal of 1886 drew Queen Anne, Shingle and Colonial Revival houses up the hill. After consolidation in 1898 the borough's first president, George Cromwell, moved the county seat here, and Carrère and Hastings designed Borough Hall in 1906, the library in 1907 and the courthouse in 1919. The 1920s and 1930s added apartment buildings, the St. George Theatre and an Art Deco federal building on Bay Street, and, by Wikipedia's account, many of the older houses were divided into small apartments. Since 2001 the waterfront has added a ballpark, an outlet mall and, in 2025, the Residences at Lighthouse Point.

What that history leaves a Sonos installer is a neighbourhood where the building, not the room, sets the rules. In the city's 2020 count for the St. George neighbourhood tabulation area, 70.2 percent of non-vacant homes were renter-occupied. A renter's soundbar bracket needs a landlord's blessing and has to come off cleanly when the lease ends. A Mitchell-Lama tenant answers to management and the rules of a regulated building. An owner inside the St. George Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in 1994 and which covers 78 houses and St. Peter's Church, needs the Commission's approval for anything that changes the outside of the house, including the side and back yards, even where nobody on the street could see it. And the newest rental buildings by the terminal come with their own house rules, managed Wi-Fi and concrete you cannot casually drill.

None of this stops anyone having good sound. It changes the order of work. Most installers arrive with a product plan and discover the rules halfway through the visit; on this hill that is how you end up with a speaker on a porch that needs an LPC permit nobody applied for, or a hole in a rental wall that costs part of a security deposit. We do it the other way round. The first ten minutes of every St. George call are about who decides, and only then about Arc Ultra versus Beam, or ceiling speakers versus a pair of Era 100s on stands. The system you end up with is the best one that fits inside the permissions you actually have.

There is a practical side too. The Staten Island Ferry, which runs free around the clock from the terminal at the foot of the hill, carried about 16.7 million riders in 2025 by the American Public Transportation Association's count cited on Wikipedia. Households that leave on an early boat and come back after dark want a system that works without fuss in the hours they are home, takes a router swap in its stride, and can be set up on a single visit without them taking a day off. That shapes how we schedule, how we document what we installed, and how much we lean on wired connections that do not need tending.

In between sit hundreds of older houses and small apartment buildings with their own mix of owners, landlords and boards. The rest of this page takes each group in turn.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for St. George's Architecture

Walk from the terminal up Hyatt Street to St. Marks Place and on to Westervelt Avenue and you pass through roughly a century and a half of building. Each type below comes with its own walls, its own wiring paths and its own person who signs off. The descriptions of the houses come from the Landmarks Preservation Commission's 1994 designation report and Wikipedia; the installation notes are our working practice, not claims about any particular address.

Queen Anne and Shingle-Style Houses

The houses that give the historic district its character, built mostly in the 1880s and 1890s, many by the Staten Island architect Edward A. Sargent, with corner towers, projecting bays and porches set into the slope. Expect plaster on wood lath, deep porch ceilings and attics reachable through a hatch. Interior work is the natural route; the porch is exterior work in the Commission's eyes.

Second Empire Double Houses

Clapboard frame houses with mansard roofs from the boom of the 1860s and early 1870s, the period the Commission credits with bringing the double house to the district. One family's living room shares a wall with the next family's, so Sub placement and rear-speaker aim matter more than in a detached house.

Colonial Revival and Craftsman Houses

Later houses in the district and on the streets around it, from the gable-ended houses the Staten Island architect Peter Veitch designed, two of them on Hamilton Avenue, to a group of four Craftsman houses the designation report singles out. Simpler plans and accessible basements make wired Amp locations easier than in the towered houses.

Greek Revival Survivors

Two houses on Richmond Terrace, at 272 and 404, are the last of the mid-nineteenth-century "Temple Row" along the waterfront. Houses this old get the lightest touch we offer: wireless products and furniture placement first.

Art Deco and Inter-War Apartment Buildings

Masonry apartment houses such as the Ambassador Apartments of 1932 at 30 Daniel Low Terrace, plus older houses divided into flats in the 1930s. Plaster over masonry, shared risers and a managing agent or landlord who decides what may be fixed.

Castleton Park's Towers

Two 20-storey buildings with 454 Mitchell-Lama rental apartments on St. Marks Place, renovated with new windows, kitchens, bathrooms and elevators in a programme completed in 2017. Tenants work within management's house rules; we plan no-drill or landlord-approved systems.

Lighthouse Point and the New Waterfront

A 13-storey mixed-income rental of 115 apartments built on the former U.S. Lighthouse Service depot, with in-unit laundry, a fitness centre, a rooftop lounge and a shared outdoor deck. New construction, managed common areas and a leasing office that sets the rules.

Civic and Office Buildings

Borough Hall, the courthouses on Richmond Terrace, the 1931 federal building at 45 Bay Street and the offices above the St. George Theatre. Many are landmarks; tenants usually need building-management approval and a certificate of insurance before any work.

Houses Split Into Flats

Single-family houses divided into two or three apartments, a pattern Wikipedia dates to the 1930s here. Separate networks per flat, an owner who may live upstairs, and walls whose wiring nobody has mapped in decades.

Plaster, party walls, masonry and concrete: working notes by type

Plaster on lath in the district houses. The original walls and ceilings of an 1890s house are almost always plaster over wood lath, and the ceilings are often the most fragile surface in the building. We locate joists and lath direction before cutting, score the plaster before the saw goes in, and choose in-ceiling speakers whose mounting dogs suit a thick, uneven ceiling. On the Staten Island list plaster carries a $45 surcharge for a wall-mounted soundbar; in-ceiling speakers are priced zone by zone after we have looked at the ceiling, because some are sound and some are not.

Towers, turrets and bays. The corner towers and projecting bays that the Commission singled out in its designation report make lovely reading rooms and awkward listening rooms. Curved or angled walls throw sound around unevenly, and a bay full of windows is the worst place for a soundbar. We usually put the main speakers on the flat walls of the room and let the tower or bay be the quiet end, then run Trueplay to even out what is left. That is a working rule rather than a promise; a room can surprise you, and we listen before we commit.

Party walls in double houses. In a pair of 1860s houses the wall between the two living rooms is the one wall you should not use for a Sub or for in-wall speakers, because it carries low frequencies straight next door. We place the Sub on the outside wall or in the room's interior, aim rear speakers away from the shared wall and keep in-wall speakers to the walls you own outright. If an attic or crawlspace runs across both halves, see the question on that below.

Masonry apartment buildings. Pre-war and inter-war apartment blocks combine plaster on masonry with hollow internal partitions, and the masonry walls are the ones with the solid fixing. Brick or stone adds $65 to a wall-mounted soundbar on our list and concrete or cinder block adds $75. Risers belong to the building; we do not run speaker cable through shared shafts without the building's written approval, and in most rentals we do not need to.

New construction by the water. Recently built rental buildings tend to have concrete floors and ceilings, metal-stud partitions and finished walls the leasing office expects back in the condition it handed over. Metal studs add $30 to a wall-mounted bar on our list. More often the right answer in a new rental is a soundbar on the console ($79 to place and connect) and wireless rears, with the system set up properly for $125, and nothing drilled at all.

Approvals

The Permission Map: Who Signs Off Before Anything Is Fixed

Sonos itself needs no permission. A speaker on a shelf, a soundbar on a console and a Sub on the floor are furniture, and you can have them tomorrow in any building on the hill. Permission comes in as soon as something is attached, cut or run: a bracket into a wall, a speaker into a ceiling, a cable through a floor, anything at all on the outside of a house in the historic district. The diagram below is how we work out, on the phone, which of those apply to you.

Owners outside the historic district

If you own a house that is not in the district and not an individual landmark, you are the only person who has to agree to interior work, and our licence covers the low-voltage side. The city's Administrative Code, at §28-105.4.7, says an electrical permit is not required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring designed to run at less than 50 volts for signalling, communication, alarm and data circuits, as long as a licensed electrician, special electrician or qualified person does it. Speaker cable from a Sonos Amp and the network cable to a Sonos product are both low-voltage. What does need an electrician is any new outlet, moved outlet or new circuit, and we name those in the quote so you can book one.

Owners inside the St. George Historic District

The Commission's permit guidebook says permits are required for exterior work that affects a building in a historic district "even on parts of the building that are not visible from the street, as well as work in front, side, and rear yards," and for interior work when the project requires a permit from the Department of Buildings. Read together with §28-105.4.7, that is why we design most district jobs as interior-only low-voltage work: speakers inside the rooms, cable inside the walls and floors, nothing through the exterior skin of the house. Porch, yard and wall-mounted outdoor speakers are possible, but they are exterior work, and the house owner applies for them through the Commission's online system, Portico, before we fit them.

Renters, in any building

Your lease and your landlord decide what can be fixed to the walls. If the answer is "nothing," we build the system so that nothing is: products on furniture, wireless surrounds, a Sub on the floor, cable along skirting in removable raceway or not at all. If the landlord agrees to a bracket, we ask you to get that in writing and we keep a copy with your job notes. In regulated buildings such as Mitchell-Lama developments, management sets house rules for tenants; we work to whatever management tells you in writing.

Businesses and offices

Commercial tenants normally need the landlord or building manager to approve work and to receive a certificate of insurance first; ours costs nothing and we send it before the visit. In landmarked office buildings the same Landmarks logic applies to anything touching the outside, and the building's own managers usually set the rules for the inside.

Landmark Houses

Music in a Landmark House Without Touching the Facade

The St. George Historic District is a small, dense place. The Commission's designation report describes it as "the best preserved and most historic section of residential New Brighton," one of the earliest planned suburban developments in the city, with houses "designed to take advantage of the hilly topography" and "picturesque features such as corner towers, projecting bays, and porches." Those are the very features owners most want music in, and they are also the ones the rules protect. Here is how we plan a district job so that it can go ahead without delay.

Start with what is purely interior

Every room inside the house can have Sonos without a Landmarks application, provided the work does not need a Buildings Department permit. In practice that means ceiling and wall speakers on an Amp, soundbars on brackets, and network cable between floors, all done as low-voltage work. The guidebook also excludes interior work that might affect the exterior, such as partitions behind windows or through-wall equipment, from its fastest review track, which is a useful reminder: we keep speakers and cable away from window openings and exterior walls where a cut could show outside.

Route cable the way the house was built

Balloon-framed and platform-framed Victorian houses usually offer a path from basement to attic through closets, chases and the cavities beside chimneys. We look for those first, because a cable that rises inside a closet corner needs no new holes in finished plaster. Where a run has to cross a ceiling, we open the smallest access we can, at the speaker position itself where possible, so the patch is hidden behind the speaker grille. Plaster repair and repainting around access holes is for your plasterer or painter; we make the openings neat and tell you exactly where they are.

Treat the porch as a separate project

The porch is where a district house's owners most often want outdoor music, and it is exterior work. The guidebook leaves no room for doubt that work on the outside of the building and in the yards needs a permit. So we quote the porch separately, give you the product details, photos of the location and a drawing of where the cable would run, and you file through Portico. Staff-level permits have published deadlines once an application is complete: 20 working days for a Permit for Minor Work and 30 for a Certificate of No Effect. Meanwhile the inside of the house can be finished.

Use portable speakers where fixing is the problem

For a back garden or a porch you only use in summer, a weatherproof portable speaker can be the honest answer. Sonos rates the Roam 2 at IP67 and the Move 2 at IP56, figures recorded in our silo research from Sonos's own specifications; neither is fixed to anything, so neither is an alteration. They join the rest of the system over Wi-Fi when they are in range, and they come inside in October.

One caution we give every district owner: we are installers, not preservation consultants. The Commission's staff answer questions by phone and email, and the guidebook encourages owners to check the designation report and historic tax photos for their house before planning. If your project is part of a larger renovation with an architect, we work to their drawings and their filings.

Apartments

Sonos for Apartments Between the Terminal and St. Marks Place

Most of St. George's homes are apartments, and most of those are rented. The good news is that Sonos was designed for exactly that situation: every product except the Amp and the architectural speakers is self-contained and plugs into an ordinary outlet. The work in an apartment is less about wiring than about three other things, and they are different here from a Manhattan high-rise or a Brooklyn brownstone floor-through.

The walls you share

The city's 311 records give a sense of what apartment neighbours notice. We queried NYC Open Data for 2025 noise complaints in ZIP 10301, which covers St. George along with Tompkinsville, New Brighton and parts of the hills behind them. Loud music and parties led with 1,398 complaints, but the second-largest category was "Banging/Pounding" with 572, more than loud talking, engine idling and construction combined. Banging travels through structure, not air, and a Sub sitting on a hard floor or a bar bracket bolted to a party wall is exactly the kind of source that can turn a film soundtrack into a neighbour's complaint.

So in apartments we put the Sub on a firm floor away from the wall you share and away from the corner, use Sub Level and the Night Sound setting for late viewing, and set volume limits on the products that face shared walls. Where a landlord allows a wall-mounted soundbar, we choose the wall that backs onto your own room or a corridor rather than the one that backs onto your neighbour's bedroom. The products are the same as anywhere; the placement is what changes.

The network you do not control

Newer buildings often provide Wi-Fi as an amenity, and older ones are crowded with dozens of neighbouring networks. Sonos's own system requirements, recorded in our silo research, rule out guest networks and networks with a log-in portal, and do not support wireless range extenders. If your building's network is one of those, the fix is usually a router of your own on your apartment's connection, with at least one Sonos product wired to it. We check what your lease and your provider allow before recommending anything.

Houses that became flats

A good share of the apartments on the hill are not in purpose-built blocks at all. Wikipedia notes that by the 1930s many of St. George's older houses had been converted into small apartment buildings, and plenty still are. In those buildings a top-floor tenant may sit under an attic that belongs to the owner downstairs, the internet may arrive in a hallway closet that serves every flat, and the walls between units are often the original interior partitions of a single-family house rather than anything built to separate neighbours. We treat each of those as a question to ask, not a reason to drill: who owns the attic, whose router is in the closet, and which wall backs onto somebody else's bedroom.

The day you move out

A renter's system should be designed for its last day as well as its first. We keep a record of every hole we make, use the smallest anchors the load allows, and favour raceway that comes off cleanly over cable fished through a wall. When you leave, a short visit takes down brackets and leaves holes ready for the painter, and the Sonos products go into boxes for the next place. Bringing that system onto a new network across the harbour, or across the street, is a $125 setup visit on our list.

St. George's business district is a civic centre first and a shopping street second. Its customers are jurors and lawyers, commuters with ten minutes before a boat, theatre audiences, ballpark crowds and the people who work in the borough's government offices. Music in those businesses has to suit short visits, sudden rushes and, in many cases, rooms where people are trying to talk quietly. These are the corridors we serve.

The route south from the ferry toward Tompkinsville, with the 1931 Art Deco federal building and post office at 45 Bay Street near its start and restaurants, bars and shops further along. Mixed buildings with apartments above ground-floor businesses.

Richmond Terrace Civic Strip

Borough Hall at 2–10 Richmond Terrace, the former Richmond County Courthouse at 12–24, the Family Court at 100 and the 120th Precinct at 78, with law offices, agencies and services in the streets behind. Quiet reception music and privacy matter more here than volume.

Hyatt Street and the Theatre Block

The St. George Theatre at Hyatt Street and Central Avenue, the Staten Island Savings Bank building of 1936 at 13–15 Hyatt Street, and the stretch where the seasonal greenmarket sets up. Pre-show crowds come and go in waves.

Central Avenue and Stuyvesant Place

The St. George Library Center at 5 Central Avenue, the Richmond County Supreme Court at 26 Central Avenue, opened in 2015, and the Staten Island Museum at Wall Street and Stuyvesant Place, with offices and practices nearby.

Empire Outlets and the Waterfront

The outlet centre beside the terminal, opened in May 2019, with a dining list that includes Empire Beer Garden and the MRKTPL culinary market. NYCEDC began a new planning process for it and the former New York Wheel site in June 2025.

Lighthouse Point Commercial Space

About 60,000 square feet of commercial space at the new complex, where silive reported a College of Staten Island tech incubator, a Learning Experience early childhood centre and a Club Pilates studio among the tenants.

How we set music up for a St. George business

Zones by how people use the room. A café near the terminal has a counter where the queue forms and a few tables where people sit; a law office has a reception area and rooms where clients talk. We put each into its own Sonos room or group so the counter can be brighter than the tables, and so reception music never plays inside an office where the door is closed. Ceiling speakers on an Amp suit dropped ceilings; where the ceiling is plaster or the landlord says no, Era 100s on brackets or shelves do the job.

Limits and schedules, set once. Each product gets a volume limit so staff cannot turn the room past what suits it, and Sonos alarms can start a chosen station or playlist at opening. We leave a one-page sheet behind the counter with the limits, the schedule and how to change a playlist, so the opener and the closer do the same thing.

A business music account, not someone's phone. Personal streaming accounts are licensed for personal, non-commercial use, as our Bronx research recorded from Spotify's own terms. We connect a business music service the owner chooses, signed in on the system rather than on an employee's phone, so the music does not stop when that employee goes home.

Rules that apply to music in a St. George business

  • Commercial music limits. NYC311's guidance on noise from bars, clubs and restaurants says that between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. their music may not exceed 42 decibels measured inside nearby homes, nor 7 decibels over the surrounding sound level measured on the street 15 feet or more from the source. Administrative Code §24-231 adds a separate test for bass inside a nearby home, which is why a Sub belongs off the shared floor or wall in any business with apartments overhead.
  • Landmarked buildings. Several civic buildings on Richmond Terrace are designated landmarks; anything fixed to their exteriors goes through the building owner and the Commission. Interior work for a tenant goes through building management.
  • Building approvals and insurance. Managers of office buildings and larger complexes usually ask for a certificate of insurance naming them before any work. Ours is free and goes out with the confirmation.
  • Electrical work. New outlets for a ceiling Amp location or a back-bar equipment shelf are line-voltage work for a licensed electrician. We stay on the low-voltage side and say so in writing.
  • Wiring in plenum ceilings. Where a dropped ceiling is used as an air return, cable has to be rated for that space; we specify it in the quote rather than discover it on the day.
Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in St. George: Questions Answered

These are the questions we expect from this neighbourhood that are not answered elsewhere on our Sonos pages: about landmark rules, regulated and amenity buildings, the commute and the businesses around the terminal.

Inside the historic district

The Landmarks rules say interior work needs the Commission's approval when the Buildings Department requires a permit. Does low-voltage speaker wire count as one of those jobs?

Not in our reading of the rules, and that is the point that makes most district projects straightforward. The Commission's permit guidebook lists interior work as needing an LPC permit when the project requires a permit from the Department of Buildings. Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 says no electrical permit is required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signalling, communication, alarm and data circuits, done by a qualified person. Speaker cable from an Amp and Ethernet to a Sonos product fall in that low-voltage group. Two caveats: this is our reading, not legal advice; and the moment a job needs a new outlet, a moved wall or anything outside, the answer changes and we tell you.

Can AESS file the Landmarks application for speakers under our porch roof, or does it have to come from us?

It has to come from the owner. The guidebook is explicit that the property owner, or an authorised representative such as a co-op or condominium officer, a managing agent or someone with power of attorney, signs the owner attestation, and that contractors and expediters may not sign it as owner. What we do is prepare everything that goes with it: the product's cut sheet, photos of the porch ceiling and the proposed positions, the cable route, and a note on how the speakers are fixed and how they would be removed. You upload those to Portico, and we book the porch once the permit arrives.

Our half of an 1860s double house shares an open attic with the other half. Can speaker cable cross it?

Only through your side of it. An attic that spans both halves of a double house is common in the older pairs, but the half beyond the line of the party wall belongs to your neighbour, and we will not run cable, step or store anything there without their written agreement. Within your half, the attic is often the best route between upstairs rooms. We keep cable clear of any insulation that looks loose or unusual, and if there is a fire separation at the party line, we do not pierce it.

Rented and amenity buildings

We rent in a Mitchell-Lama building. Who has to say yes before anything is fixed to a wall?

Management. In a Mitchell-Lama development the tenant's lease and the building's house rules, set by the owner and its managing agent under the programme's supervising agency, govern what tenants may attach to walls. We do not know of a city form a tenant has to file for a speaker that plugs in, so the usual answer is simple: anything freestanding is fine; anything drilled needs management's written permission. Bring us that letter, or choose the no-drill plan: a soundbar on the console, rears on stands, the Sub on the floor and everything tuned on the day.

Our new waterfront building has a shared outdoor deck with grills. Can we bring our own Sonos speaker out there?

That depends on the building's rules for the shared deck, not on Sonos. Where a building allows personal speakers in a common area, a portable Sonos is the tidy choice: the Roam 2 is rated IP67 and runs on its battery, and it can join your home system over Wi-Fi or play from your phone by Bluetooth. Where a building plays its own music out there, or restricts amplified sound, a portable stays in the apartment. We cannot install anything on a shared deck without written permission from management.

We are on the 7 a.m. boat and home after dark. Can the music be playing when we walk in from the terminal?

Yes, on a schedule. Sonos alarms can start a chosen room or group with a set playlist or station at a set time and volume on chosen days, and you can switch a weekday alarm off for a holiday in the app. What the Sonos app does not do on its own is start music because your phone has arrived home; for that you need a smart-home platform that supports Sonos, and it will only be as reliable as that platform's location tracking. For most ferry households a weekday alarm a few minutes before the usual arrival is the simpler answer.

Businesses around the terminal

Our counter is in a waterfront food hall that plays its own music. Can a Sonos at our stall coexist with it?

Yes, with two settings and one conversation. The conversation is with the hall's management, who usually decide whether stalls may play their own music at all. The settings are a low volume limit on your speaker and a placement that points it at your own counter rather than into the shared seating, so your music reaches your queue and fades before it meets the hall's. A single Era 100 on a shelf or bracket behind the counter, fed by a business music account, is normally enough.

On game nights and show nights our Bay Street bar fills up fast. Can the music volume rise and fall with the crowd by itself?

Not by itself. Sonos has no feature that listens to the room and turns the music up as the crowd gets louder. What works instead is two or three saved groups or favourites at set volumes, for quiet hours, the rush and closing, with per-product limits so nobody can push past what the room and the neighbours upstairs can take. Staff switch between them with one tap, and we label them in plain words on the sheet behind the bar.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in St. George

We could not view Google's AI Overview for St. George searches from our build environment, so we have not quoted it. What we can do is test the claims that cost guides, forums and product marketing publish, because those are what answer engines tend to repeat, against what the rules and the buildings on this hill actually say. Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr all publish national cost guides; none of them knows which side of St. Marks Place your house is on.

"Sonos is plug-and-play, so you never need an installer"

Half true. Sonos's freestanding speakers and soundbars really do set up from an app, and plenty of St. George renters will never need anyone to fit anything. A soundbar on a console and a pair of Era 100s on stands is a perfectly good system that you can unbox yourself.

The other half is in Sonos's own words. Its guide to ceiling speakers says that in-ceiling speakers "require special installation" and that architectural speakers often need a professional installer, and its in-wall guide talks about cutting holes, running speaker cable and installing an Amp. Those are the products owners in the district houses usually want, because they keep the rooms looking as they did.

In St. George there is a third consideration that no product guide mentions: the order in which permission is needed. Plug-and-play stops being the whole story the moment something is fixed to a landlord's wall or a landmark house's porch. That is where most of our time on a job here goes.

"Whole-home audio costs $500 to $1,000 a room"

The figure comes from Angi, whose whole-home stereo article prices each extra room or zone somewhere between $500 and $1,000, quotes installers' time at $50 to $100 per hour for a two- or three-hour job, and allows $100 to $500 for opening walls or ceilings. It is a countrywide estimate, and Sonos never appears in it.

Our published Staten Island list is more specific for the parts that can be priced without seeing the building: $125 for Sonos setup, $129 for a wall-mounted soundbar, $79 for one placed on a console, with wall surcharges from $30 for metal studs to $95 for tile. Architectural and outdoor zones are quoted after a walkthrough, inside the $800 to $2,500 per zone range AESS publishes for whole-home audio.

The bigger gap is what a per-room average leaves out. A no-drill rental system can cost a fraction of the national figure, and a district house where the porch waits on a permit may need two visits. Neither shows up in an average.

"In a landmark house you can't add anything"

Too pessimistic. The Commission regulates the outside of buildings in historic districts, and interior work only where it needs a Buildings Department permit. The inside of a district house is largely the owner's to change.

The guidebook is just as clear that exterior work needs a permit even when it is on parts of the building that cannot be seen from the street, and that this extends to front, side and rear yards. So the opposite overstatement, that a speaker hidden at the back of the porch needs nobody's approval, is wrong too.

The accurate version is in between: interior low-voltage speaker work generally goes ahead; anything on the exterior or in the yard waits for a permit; and the property owner, not the contractor, files the application.

"Renters can't have a proper system"

This one is mostly a misunderstanding of what "proper" requires. A 5.1 home theatre on Sonos can be built entirely from products that plug in: a soundbar, a Sub and two rear speakers, joined wirelessly.

What renters give up is concealment. Without permission to drill, the TV sits on a console, the rears sit on stands and power cables run along the skirting. That is a matter of appearance, not of sound.

With 70.2 percent of St. George homes rented, according to the 2020 census figures summarised on Wikipedia, this is the system that suits the largest number of households here, and it moves with them.

"Add a range extender to reach the far rooms"

Buy a booster and plug it in halfway: that is the stock answer to a weak room. It fails here because Sonos publishes a list of network setups it will not work with, and wireless range extenders sit on it next to guest networks and any Wi-Fi that makes you log in through a web page.

In the larger houses of the district, where the router often sits in a basement or front parlour and the music is wanted on the top floor, the reliable fixes are a Sonos product wired to the router, a wired access point, or a supported mesh system with Sonos wired to its main unit.

In buildings with managed Wi-Fi, an extender would not help at all, because the problem is the type of network rather than the signal strength. That is a case for your own router.

"Trueplay tuning works from any phone"

For the bulk of the range it needs an iPhone or iPad. Sonos's support page on Trueplay states that most of its speakers can only be tuned from iOS; a quicker tuning mode that also runs on Android exists for a handful of recent models, Arc Ultra and the Era 100 and Era 300 among them.

It also asks for a quiet room while tuning runs. In a front room facing Richmond Terrace or the harbour, that means windows shut and a quiet few minutes, which we arrange on the day.

If your household only has Android phones, we tune from our own iPhone during the visit and note it on the handover sheet, so you know to call us if a speaker is moved and needs retuning.

"Any speaker wiring needs a city permit"

Not for the low-voltage part. The Administrative Code exempts wiring under 50 volts for signalling, communication, alarm and data circuits from electrical permits when a qualified person does the work, which covers speaker cable and network cable.

The line-voltage part is different. A new outlet behind the TV for a soundbar, or a dedicated circuit for an equipment shelf, is electrical work for a licensed electrician and is not something we do.

And in the historic district the permit question has a second layer, the Commission's, which depends on whether the work touches the outside. Answer engines tend to flatten all of this into one sentence. On this hill it is three separate questions.

If your building has a loading zone, a garage or a service entrance with rules about deliveries, tell us when you book and we will plan the arrival around it.

We are bringing our Sonos speakers over from a Manhattan apartment. Will they simply reconnect once we unpack in St. George?
+

They will not find the new network on their own, because every Sonos product remembers the Wi-Fi name and password of the old home. The usual fix is to update the network details from the Sonos app on a phone connected to the new router, or to wire one product to the router so the rest can follow. Products moved between different Sonos apps or very old S1-only units are the exceptions we check first. If the move also changes which rooms things live in, a $125 setup visit renames the rooms, regroups them and reruns Trueplay where it is supported.

Our living room faces the harbor and the windows are open half the year. Does outside noise spoil Trueplay tuning?
+

It can, which is why we tune with the windows shut. Sonos describes Trueplay as measuring how sound reflects off the walls, furnishings and other surfaces of the room, and asks for the room to be as quiet as possible while it runs, which takes about three minutes per room. A truck on Richmond Terrace or a conversation on the stoop mid-sweep can skew the result, so we watch for a quiet stretch and simply rerun a sweep that was interrupted. Once tuned, opening the windows does not undo anything; retune only if you move the speaker or rearrange the room.
