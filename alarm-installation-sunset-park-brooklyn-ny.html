The Neighborhood

Why Sunset Park Properties Need Alarm Installation

Sunset Park takes its name from a 24.5-acre park on top of a hill between Fifth and Seventh Avenues and 41st and 44th Streets, and the neighborhood spreads downhill from it to the harbor. Wikipedia’s article on the neighborhood describes it as bounded by Park Slope and Green-Wood Cemetery to the north, Borough Park to the east, Bay Ridge to the south and New York Harbor to the west, and notes that the area north of 36th Street is also called Greenwood Heights. Community Board 7, which covers Sunset Park and Windsor Terrace, describes Sunset Park as cut in two by the Gowanus Expressway, with a large industrial area mainly west of Third Avenue along the waterfront and commercial strips along Fifth and Seventh Avenues. For an alarm installer that one description already sets out three different jobs: houses and apartments on the hill, shops on the avenues, and workshops and warehouses by the water.

The houses are the part outsiders picture. Wikipedia describes them as mostly two-story-above-basement bayed row houses built mainly between 1892 and 1910, most originally two-family, mostly faced with brick, though many have since been clad in vinyl siding and Formstone. Brownstoner’s architecture writer Suzanne Spellen, describing a row of 29 houses on 44th Street between Sixth and Seventh Avenues built in 1903, puts the arrangement plainly: two-family homes, generally with a duplex for the owner and a rental apartment on the top floor. Writing about four 1903 brownstones on Sixth Avenue between 54th and 55th Streets, she says the same of the neighborhood as a whole: most row houses were two-family, with the lower duplex owner-occupied and the upper apartment rented. That arrangement is the starting point for almost every residential alarm we design here. A Sunset Park row house is rarely one household behind one front door. It is two or more households sharing a street door, a vestibule and a stair, each with its own door off that stair.

The households are also unusually full. The Fifth Avenue Committee’s 2020 Sunset Park Housing Report, a community development nonprofit’s study released in September 2020, says Sunset Park “has become the 4th most overcrowded neighborhood in New York City,” that 9.2 percent of units were severely overcrowded in 2018, up from 8.2 percent in 2010, and that the homeownership rate in 2018 was 28.1 percent. It adds that over half of the 45,903 housing units in Community Board 7 were built before 1939, and that the neighborhood has seen an increase in illegal basement apartments. Community Board 7’s own page speaks of “the second oldest housing stock in NYC.” Old buildings, divided among many people on different schedules, need more than one shared code.

The burglary reports point the same way. In the police reports and news items we could find for the neighborhood, residential break-ins were almost all at an apartment’s own door, in daylight, while the household was out. In February 2020 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that two men got into a building at 34th Street and Fourth Avenue through an unlocked door at about 3 p.m., then forced open an apartment door with a tool and took cash and jewelry worth more than $1,500. In March 2020 two men broke through the front door of a home near Fifth Avenue and 59th Street at about 8:30 a.m. and took a purse with cash. In July 2019 someone broke down the front door of an apartment at 50th Street and Seventh Avenue at about 11 a.m. and left with $1,200. In June 2026, according to a News 12 report published in July, a man forced his way through the front door of a home at 53rd Street and Eighth Avenue, took $10,000 in cash and fled east on 53rd Street.

The business reports keep a different clock. In June 2017 CBS New York reported that police wanted a man and a woman for a three-week spree in which the man “bust open doors and gates to businesses closed for the night” while the woman kept lookout, every break-in between 2:30 and 5 a.m.: a seafood store’s gate on 59th Street, a hot pot restaurant’s gate on 53rd Street where the register was cleaned out of about $8,000, and the kitchen door of a grill on Eighth Avenue, with attempts at a business on 51st Street and at an adult day care and a grocery on Seventh Avenue that failed. Patch reported a man breaking the rear window of a seafood restaurant on Eighth Avenue at about 5:10 a.m. on Thanksgiving 2018. CBS New York reported a man getting into a restaurant on Eighth Avenue through a back door at about 8:40 a.m. in August 2020 and taking $600 from the register and a locked drawer. The Brooklyn Paper reported two men cutting the gate locks of a supermarket on Sixth Avenue near 51st Street with bolt cutters at about 4 a.m. and prying the front door with a crowbar.

None of this makes Sunset Park unusual among Brooklyn neighborhoods, and the long view is reassuring. Wikipedia records that crime of all kinds in the 72nd Precinct fell 79.1 percent between 1990 and 2018, and a Brooklyn Paper report on a meeting of the Sunset Park Business Improvement District quoted city data showing break-ins in the precinct falling from 1,645 in 1990 to 212 in the year before the meeting. The point is not how many burglaries there are, but when and where they happen: apartment doors in the working day, shops from the back or the gate before the first worker.

That is the design idea behind every Sunset Park system on this page. We start by drawing what we call the empty-hours map: each household’s or business’s own door, the hours that space is actually empty, and who needs to know when that door is touched. The owner’s duplex, the top-floor rental, the restaurant downstairs and the stockroom in the cellar can each have their own area, their own codes, their own schedule and their own list of people to notify. Shared doors such as the street door and the stair are handled with care, so that one household’s alarm never depends on another remembering to lock up. And at the back of every shop, where the documented break-ins came in, protection stays armed until the first person who belongs there arrives.

In each case the way in was a shared or forgotten opening: a cellar window, a storage area, a hallway door. A Sunset Park design always asks who is responsible for those, and makes sure no household’s protection depends on them.

Most Sunset Park households that add an apartment alarm pair it with a small camera on their own door, and shop owners usually add one at the back door.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Sunset Park’s Architecture

Four building types account for most Sunset Park homes, joined by mixed-use buildings on the avenues and the industrial lofts by the water. Each changes where devices go and who controls them.

Bayed two-family row houses

Two stories over a basement, with a bay at the front (Wikipedia calls them bayed row houses), usually one stair, a cellar and a yard at the back. The owner usually has the lower duplex and a tenant the top floor. We set the system up in two areas on one panel, and treat the bay as one unit of glass with its own detection rather than three separate windows.

Brownstone-fronted rows

The 50th Street rows and the Sixth Avenue houses Brownstoner describes have brownstone fronts. Contacts go on the inner doors and sashes that actually lock; wiring follows the interior trim, never the facade, and outdoor devices stay off the front altogether.

Houses re-clad in Formstone or siding

Many houses were re-fronted decades ago. Formstone is a cement coating over the brick; siding sits on furring over the original wall. Both change how anything outside is fixed. In practice we keep sirens and wiring inside, and where an outdoor device is wanted we fix it through a mounting block sealed against water and against cracking.

Limestone and brick flats

The early-1900s apartment houses of 44th Street and similar buildings elsewhere have several apartments off a common stair. An apartment alarm here is a quiet, self-contained system on your own door and your own windows, with a keypad inside, and nothing on the hallway walls without the owner’s consent.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

On Fourth, Fifth and Eighth Avenues the ground floor is a shop and the floors above are apartments, often with the store owner living upstairs or nearby. The shop gets its own commercial panel and cellular line; the apartments get their own systems; the shared street door and stair belong to the building owner.

Waterfront lofts and warehouses

Industry City’s sixteen buildings and the Bush Terminal warehouses are large loft buildings divided into hundreds of units. A unit alarm covers your own doors to the corridor and to any loading area, your windows, and your office, reporting over its own cellular line rather than the building’s network.

The Design Idea

Two Clocks and Many Doors: The Empty-Hours Map

Most alarm advice assumes one household, one front door and one night. You arm when you go to bed and when you leave, and the system watches the whole house. That model fits a detached house in the suburbs. It fits very little in Sunset Park, where a single row house may hold an owner’s family, a tenant household on the top floor and sometimes a shop or an office on the ground floor, each with its own hours, its own door and its own idea of who should be told when something happens.

So the first thing we draw is not a floor plan but a timetable. Down one side go the spaces: the owner’s duplex, the top-floor apartment, the cellar, the shop, the shop’s kitchen or stockroom, the office where the cash is counted. Across the top go the hours of an ordinary week. Then we shade in, with the people who use each space, the hours when nobody who belongs there is inside. That shaded block is the space’s empty hours, and it is when that space’s alarm should be fully armed, whatever anyone else in the building is doing.

Drawn this way, the documented Sunset Park burglaries all land in shaded blocks. The apartment doors forced at 8:30 in the morning, at 11, at 3 in the afternoon were forced while those households were out, in buildings where other people may well have been at home. The restaurants and food shops entered at 2:30 to 5 a.m., at 5:10 and at 8:40 were entered after the last worker left and before the first one arrived. In no case did the burglar need the whole building to be empty. He needed one space to be empty and one door into it.

The map then turns into settings: each space becomes an area with its own codes, schedule and arming reminders, and its own notification list, so each household or business hears only about its own doors.

The shared parts of the building are decided last and on purpose. The street door, the vestibule and the stair belong to the building owner and are used by everyone at all hours. Putting them on one household’s alarm usually means either false alarms when the other household comes home or an alarm that is never armed. In most Sunset Park two-families we leave the shared parts off the alarm, or give the street door a chime-only contact that tells the owner it was left open, and put the real protection on each household’s own door, where the documented burglaries were forced.

Your Own Door

Every Household Its Own Door: Two-Family Row Houses and Walk-Ups

The Brooklyn Eagle’s February 2020 report from 34th Street and Fourth Avenue describes the pattern in two steps. The men got into the building through an unlocked door. Then they forced the apartment door with a tool. The first step cost them nothing because the street door was shared and someone had left it unlocked. The second step was the one that took effort and noise, and it is the one an alarm can watch.

In a two-family row house the owner’s duplex usually has its own door at the foot or the first landing of the stair, and the top-floor rental has its own door at the top. Those two doors are the front doors that matter. On each we fit a magnetic contact that reports the moment the door opens, and a shock sensor on the door or its frame that reports hard blows or prying before the door gives way. Inside each door goes that household’s keypad, out of sight of the stair, so nobody on the landing can watch a code being entered.

In walk-up apartment buildings, the limestone and brick flats of 44th Street and their relatives across the neighborhood, the same design is all there is: your own door, your own windows, nothing on the hallway walls. A renter can have a complete system without touching anything that belongs to the landlord except the inside of the apartment door, where a contact the size of a domino is fixed with screws or, where the lease is strict, with adhesive. When the lease ends the system comes down and goes with you.

The windows decide the rest. On a top floor the windows are usually out of reach from the street, but rear windows may be reachable from a lower roof or an extension, and a front bay or a window onto a fire escape needs to be on the system. On the owner’s lower floors every rear window over the yard is reachable, and so is the front bay on the parlor floor. In the cellar, windows at sidewalk level are the easiest way into the building. The Brooklyn Eagle’s December 2019 report of a man who got into a building at 41st Street and Seventh Avenue through an unlocked basement window and left with power tools is exactly this case.

Who protects the cellar is a decision the two households make together, and we write it down. In most two-families the owner takes it, with the cellar as a separate area so it can be armed at night while everyone is upstairs. Where the tenant stores belongings or bikes in the cellar, as the household at Third Avenue and 43rd Street apparently did, the tenant may want to be notified too. The panel allows either; the point is that someone is responsible for it.

Daylight

Doors Forced in Daylight: What the Panel Does in the First Minute

An apartment door forced at 11 in the morning is a different problem from a door forced at 3 in the morning. Nobody in the apartment will hear the siren, because nobody is home; neighbors on the stair may be out too, or may be asleep after a night shift. What matters is how fast the right person learns about it, and what they can do from wherever they are.

Here is how we set a Sunset Park apartment system to behave in the first minute after a door is forced while the household is out. The shock sensor on the door reports the blows themselves, before the door opens, and the panel sends a push notification and, if the household chooses monitoring, a signal to the central station. When the door opens, the entry delay starts. The delay is short, typically 20 to 30 seconds, because the keypad is right inside the door. When the delay runs out without a code, the indoor siren sounds and the full alarm goes out. If a camera covers the door, the clip arrives with the notification.

The notification list matters as much as the siren. In a household where the adults are all at work, the first person to call should be the one who can act soonest: perhaps a relative who lives on the next block, perhaps the owner downstairs if the tenant has agreed. Without monitoring, the app notifies everyone on the household’s list at once and anyone can open the clip and call 911. With monitoring, the central station calls down the list and dispatches police when it is confirmed. Either way, by the time the burglar is inside the apartment, the household knows.

Eighth Avenue

The Kitchen Door and the Rear Window: Eighth Avenue Restaurants

Eighth Avenue is the spine of Brooklyn’s first Chinatown. Wikipedia’s article on Brooklyn’s Chinatowns places its core along Eighth Avenue from 42nd to 68th Street and describes the 50th to 65th Street stretch as the original core; it records the first Cantonese-style seafood restaurant on the avenue, Canton House, opening in 1985 between 55th and 56th Streets, and calls the neighborhood New York City’s largest Fuzhou enclave. (Wikipedia’s article on Sunset Park itself gives the extent as roughly 44th to 68th Street.) The avenue is lined with restaurants, bakeries, groceries and fish markets.

The three restaurant break-ins on Eighth Avenue that we found in local reports came in from the back. In June 2017 a grill’s kitchen door was broken and $2,900, beer and an iPad were taken. On Thanksgiving 2018 a seafood restaurant’s rear window was broken at about 5:10 a.m. and $200 taken from the register. In August 2020 a restaurant was entered through a back door at about 8:40 a.m. and $600 taken from the register and a locked drawer. In each case the front of the restaurant faced a busy avenue and the back faced a yard, an alley or a neighbor’s wall.

A restaurant alarm here is therefore designed back to front. The kitchen door gets a heavy-duty contact and a shock sensor, because it is usually a steel or solid door that will be attacked with tools. Every rear window, including small kitchen and toilet windows and any window behind an exhaust opening, gets a contact or is covered by a glass-break detector. A motion detector watches the kitchen from a position that avoids the woks, the steamers and the exhaust hood, and a second watches the route from the kitchen to the register and the office.

Then the schedule. Many of these restaurants have prep cooks who arrive hours before the dining room opens, and owners who arrive later still. The 8:40 a.m. break-in happened in exactly that gap. We set the restaurant up in two or three areas: the kitchen, which the first cook disarms with a personal code when arriving through the back; the dining room and front door, which stay armed until opening; and the office, register or counting room, which stays armed until the owner or manager arrives. A person who comes in through the back at 8:40 without a code sets off the alarm even though the front of the restaurant looks closed and quiet.

Groceries and Markets

Groceries, Bakeries and Fish Markets on Fifth, Sixth and Seventh Avenues

The Sunset Park Business Improvement District represents Fifth Avenue from 38th to 64th Street, according to the Brooklyn Eagle, and the Brooklyn Paper reported that a BID board member wanted to ask all 385 businesses in the district to leave their roll-down gates up and their lights on at night, starting with a single night, to make the avenue look less forbidding. Whatever happens to that idea, Fifth Avenue and the parallel avenues carry many small food businesses: supermarkets, groceries, restaurants and seafood stores among them, as the reports below show.

The reports describe how these shops were attacked. In June 2017 the seafood store on 59th Street had its security gate broken and lost $2,000 and a case of beer. The hot pot restaurant on 53rd Street had its gate busted and its register cleaned out of about $8,000. A grocery on Seventh Avenue was tried and held. The Brooklyn Paper’s report from a supermarket on Sixth Avenue near 51st Street describes surveillance video of two men cutting the gate locks with bolt cutters at about 4 a.m., then prying the front door with a crowbar and taking about $3,000 in cash and $200 in lottery tickets. News 12 reported one man linked to a burglary at a supermarket on Fifth Avenue, where flowers and $20 were taken, and another at an eatery on Fourth Avenue five days later, where a cash register and $1,000 were taken.

For a food shop the alarm’s job at the front is to report the attack on the gate and the door while it is happening, not after the shop has been emptied. A contact on the gate tells the panel the gate has been raised. A shock sensor on the front door and frame reports the crowbar. Glass-break detection behind the front windows reports the glass. Each of these is instant once the shop is armed, because nobody who belongs there comes in through a forced gate at 4 a.m.

Inside, food shops have their own difficulties. Refrigerated cases, compressors and heaters run all night and move air. Fish markets and produce stores wash their floors down and keep the back room wet. We choose motion detectors that tolerate the moving air of refrigeration, mount them high and away from the cases, and keep keypads and panels out of the wet areas, in the office or a dry corner by the door the staff use. Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Sunset Park: Questions Answered

The row house and its people

Our 1903 row house has a three-sided bay on the parlor floor with three double-hung sashes facing the street. Does each sash need its own contact, or can one detector cover the whole bay?

Usually both, for different reasons. One acoustic glass-break detector, mounted on the wall or ceiling facing the bay, listens for the particular sound of breaking glass across all three sashes; that covers the glass, which is the weak point of a bay. Contacts are needed only on the sashes that actually open, because a contact reports a sash being raised and a glass-break detector does not. In many bays the two side sashes are painted shut or fixed and only the center one opens, so the answer is one glass-break detector and one contact. We check each sash on the survey rather than assuming.

Decades ago our row house front was covered in Formstone. Can an outdoor siren or a camera be fixed to it without cracking the coating, or should everything stay inside?

Everything for the alarm itself can stay inside, and in a two-family that is usually the better choice anyway: the indoor siren is what drives an intruder out, and it does not wake the block. If you also want a camera or a light on the facade, we fix it through a mounting block whose screws pass through the coating into the brick behind, with holes drilled slowly with a masonry bit and sealed, so water does not get behind the coating and the coating does not crack around the screws. Where the Formstone is already loose or hollow, we tell you and suggest a different position rather than fixing into it.

Eleven of us live in one row house across three generations, on different shifts. Is there a limit to how many people can have their own code, and is a code the easiest way for everyone?

The panels we install hold dozens of user codes and many hold far more, so eleven people is not a problem. Each adult should have a personal code, so the app records who armed and who disarmed, and a code can be removed when someone moves out without changing everyone else’s. Codes are not always the easiest way, though: for grandparents a key fob with one button to arm and one to disarm is often simpler, and for younger adults the phone app is quickest. We set up whichever mix the household needs, and write each person’s method on the handover sheet.

News 12 asked the public to help identify the man who forced a front door at 53rd Street and Eighth Avenue in June 2026. If our alarm comes with a camera, how do we get a clip to detectives without handing over our whole account?

Download the clip, not the account. In the app or on the recorder, the event clip for the alarm can be saved to your phone or to a USB stick and given to the detective, or shared as a single link that expires. Nobody needs your login, and nobody should be given your recorder’s password. We show every household how to export one clip during the handover, because the moment police ask is not the moment to learn it. If the camera records to a recorder in the cellar or the shop, we also make sure its clock is set correctly, since a clip with the wrong time is much less useful to an investigation.

The avenues before dawn

Police said the 2017 burglars tried an adult day care and a grocery on Seventh Avenue on the same night and got into neither. If someone works at our door and gives up, will we ever know?

With the right sensor, yes. A plain door contact reports only when the door opens, so a failed attempt leaves no record at all. A shock sensor on the door and its frame reports the blows and the prying themselves, and the panel logs the event and sends a notification even though the door held. We set shock sensors on armed shop doors to alarm, and on doors that are hit by deliveries or slammed during the day to a notification only. Either way, the morning after an attempt you know the door was attacked, when, and you can check the camera if there is one, instead of discovering scratches on the frame weeks later.

Our Eighth Avenue kitchen runs woks and steamers from early morning. Will grease and steam damage the detectors, or set them off?

They will if the detectors are put in the wrong places. Steam and cooking smoke set off smoke detectors, and grease slowly coats anything mounted near the hood. In the kitchen itself we use a heat detector rather than a smoke detector for fire reporting, mounted away from the hood and the steamers, and we place motion detectors on the far side of the room facing away from the cooking line, so neither heat shimmer nor steam reaches them. Keypads go by the staff door, not near the stoves. Kitchen detectors should be wiped and tested regularly, and we show the kitchen staff which ones they must never cover with plastic or foil.

The waterfront blocks

In June 2024 two men forced their way into a business on Second Avenue near 41st Street and took $8,000 from a desk. Can a single desk drawer or cash box be put on the alarm?

Yes. A small contact fitted inside a drawer or the lid of a cash box reports when it is opened, and it can be set to report even when the rest of the alarm is off, to the owner’s phone only. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Sunset Park

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, its related questions or Answer the Public from the environment where this page was built, so nothing below is quoted from them. The claims are the general advice that cost sites, kit makers and AI-written summaries tend to repeat about home and business alarms. Each is set against what the Sunset Park sources actually show.

“Burglars come at night, so arm the alarm when you go to bed”

Night arming matters for the shops on the avenues, where the reported break-ins fell between 2:30 and 5:10 in the morning. But every apartment burglary we found in Sunset Park reports happened in daylight or early evening: about 8:30 a.m. near Fifth Avenue and 59th Street, about 9:45 a.m. near Third Avenue and 43rd Street, about 11 a.m. at 50th Street and Seventh Avenue, about 3 p.m. at 34th Street and Fourth Avenue, about 5:45 p.m. at 41st Street and Seventh Avenue.

Those were the hours when those households were out, at work, at school or running errands, even if their buildings were not empty.

So for a Sunset Park apartment the most important arming is the one when the last person leaves in the morning, and the system should remind the household when that has not happened. Night mode is the second setting.

“Protect the front door first: that is where most burglars enter”

In a detached house, the front door is the house’s front door. In a Sunset Park two-family or walk-up, the front door to the street is shared, often unlocked, and not the door that was forced. In the 34th Street report the men walked in through an unlocked building door and then forced the apartment door. In the 61st Street report the hallway was unlocked.

The door that needs the alarm is each household’s own door, at the foot or the top of the stair, and the shop’s own back door.

We still look at the street door, and an owner may want a chime-only contact on it. But the first contact and shock sensor go on the door that the household alone controls.

“One alarm protects the whole house”

One alarm with one code protects one household. A row house with an owner’s duplex and a rented top floor has two households, and the Fifth Avenue Committee’s report suggests many Sunset Park apartments hold more people than they were built for.

If both households share one code and one siren, either the alarm goes off whenever the other household comes home, or nobody arms it.

A panel with separate areas, separate codes and separate notification lists gives each household its own alarm, while the shared parts of the building are handled by agreement.

“A security gate is enough for a small shop”

The reports describe gates being broken, busted and cut with bolt cutters, and in the 2017 spree the gate was simply the first obstacle on the way to the register. The Brooklyn Paper also reported the BID’s idea of asking Fifth Avenue merchants to raise their gates at night to make the avenue more welcoming.

A gate slows someone down. It does not tell anyone that it is being attacked, and it does nothing about the back door, which is where the Eighth Avenue restaurant break-ins came in.

An alarm reports the gate, the front door, the glass and the back of the shop the moment any of them is touched, gate or no gate.

“A basic alarm costs a few hundred dollars plus a monthly fee”

DIY kits do cost a few hundred dollars, and national cost guides quote broad averages. On our Brooklyn list, which is AESS’s base price for the whole company, a basic installed residential system starts at $600 and a wireless system typically runs $600 to $1,200.

Complete protection for a whole row house, with glass-break detection, smoke and CO reporting, sirens and the app, starts at $2,500. A small business alarm runs $1,500 to $3,000. Per-unit setups for landlords run $400 to $800 per unit.

Monitoring is optional: from $19.99 a month through our partners, month to month, and many households and shops use the app alone.

“Wireless sensors don’t work in old masonry buildings”

Brick party walls and plaster do weaken radio signals, and the large waterfront buildings weaken them more. But a Sunset Park row house is only about two rooms deep on each floor, and current wireless sensors cover those distances well when each one is tested from its final position.

Long waterfront units and cellars under the whole house are the harder cases, where a repeater may be needed.

We test every sensor’s signal before fixing it and note the reading on the zone list, so a weak sensor is found on installation day, not after a real alarm.

“You need a city alarm permit before installing”

Generic advice often mentions alarm permits and registration, which some cities require. We found no New York City permit or registration requirement for a private home or business burglar alarm.

What New York does have is the fifteen-minute siren limit in NYC Administrative Code § 24-238, the state license required of installers, and HPD’s rules on smoke and carbon monoxide detectors.

Landmarked facades and building contractor rules can add their own steps, which is one more reason we keep alarm equipment inside.

Many kitchen suppression systems have a set of switch contacts that change state when the system discharges, and a contact like that can be wired to a 24-hour zone on the alarm panel so the owner’s phone, and the central station if you have monitoring, are told at once. The suppression system itself belongs to its own service company and its own inspections; we connect only to the output it provides for this purpose, with that company’s agreement, and never alter the system.

If the siren goes off at 4 a.m. and will not stop, what can we do before a technician gets there?
+

First, enter your code at the keypad or press disarm in the app; that silences the siren in almost every case. If the panel will not respond, call (347) 934-8335 and we will talk you through it. Every siren we install is also programmed to stop by itself within fifteen minutes, as NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 requires, so even a fault cannot leave it sounding for hours over the apartments upstairs. A siren that keeps restarting usually means one zone is still open or faulty, and that is a repair visit, often the same day.

Can the panel sit in our part of the house when the tenant’s apartment is also on it, without the tenant being able to reach our controls?
+

Yes. The panel box itself holds no controls anyone uses day to day; it goes in a dry, hidden spot, usually in the owner’s part of the house or the cellar, and is locked or screwed shut. Each household controls only its own area from its own keypad or the app, with codes that work only for that area. The tenant never needs to reach the panel, and the owner’s code can be set so it does not disarm the tenant’s apartment either, unless both of you want that for emergencies.

We are planning to install a camera at the back door later. Should the alarm wait for it, or go in first?
+

Put the alarm in first. The alarm is what reports a forced back door, a broken rear window or a failed attempt; a camera adds the picture. We leave the back door contact, the shock sensor and the panel ready to pair with a camera later, choose a mounting position for it on the survey, and note it on the zone list, so adding the camera later is a short visit that changes nothing already working.
