The Neighborhood

Why Belmont Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Belmont packs a working food district, a student rental market and some of the Bronx's oldest apartment stock into a few dozen blocks, and almost every one of those buildings was finished decades before data cable existed. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as bounded by Fordham Road, Bronx Park, East 181st Street and Third Avenue, dominated by five- and six-story walk-up and elevator apartment buildings, with row houses and Art Deco and Tudor apartment houses mixed in, and with most of its architecture dating from before 1939. City lot data from our earlier Belmont research points the same way: in ZIP 10458, which Belmont shares with Fordham and Bedford Park, 386 of 662 lots with three or more apartments were built between 1901 and 1929, and another 114 in the 1930s. A cable plan here is almost always a retrofit, and the building decides the route long before the customer decides how many jacks they want.

The commercial side of Belmont is unusually dense for its size. The American Planning Association, naming Arthur Avenue one of its Great Streets in 2016, noted that all of the buildings on the strip are mixed use, with residences above and shops below. The Belmont Business Improvement District's 2025 fact sheet highlights forty merchants on its own list alone: bakeries, butchers, cafés and pastry shops, delicatessens, fish markets, pizzerias, restaurants and specialty stores, several of them a century old. Egidio Pastry Shop dates to 1912, Madonia Bakery to 1918 and Mario's to 1919. Businesses that old have added card terminals, online ordering tablets, kitchen printers and cameras one at a time, usually on whatever wire the last vendor left behind, and the result is often a network nobody can draw from memory.

The internet itself is rarely the problem. Provider data compiled from the FCC's October 2026 update by bestneighborhood.org shows fiber from Optimum reaching 88.7 percent of Belmont and fiber from Verizon and EarthLink reaching 85.9 percent each, with eight residential providers covering about 91 percent of the neighborhood. The same page cautions that some homes and apartments do not have access to those speeds. In our experience that gap is usually inside the building: the service arrives at a box in the cellar or on the ground floor, and nothing dependable carries it up four flights of stairs, across a restaurant ceiling or into the back office behind the walk-in. Structured cabling is the part that closes that gap.

Turnover adds another layer. Wikipedia notes that Belmont is home to a large population of Fordham University students living in off-campus housing and private apartment buildings, and The Fordham Ram's April 2025 housing guide lists six local realty companies managing student apartments around Crescent Avenue, Cambreleng Avenue, East 187th Street and Arthur Avenue, with one including Wi-Fi in the rent. The same guide observes that when students leave, they all leave at the same time. For an owner who supplies the internet as part of the lease, a building-wide network that can be reset between tenants, with access points cabled back to one locked cabinet, is a far easier thing to manage than a router in every apartment.

We also want to be clear about what we could not find. None of Belmont is a designated historic district; the Historic Districts Council lists the Retail Market, Aquinas High School and Our Lady of Mount Carmel as sites worth considering for designation, which is a proposal, not a rule. Where this page describes cellars, stair halls and ceilings, it draws on the building ages and types in the public record and on what we routinely find in buildings of those kinds, not on inspections of particular addresses.

Getting Up the Building

From the Cellar Box to the Fifth Floor: Vertical Pathways in a Belmont Walk-Up

The hardest single piece of cabling in most Belmont walk-ups is not the jack in the apartment. It is the vertical path. Providers generally bring service into the cellar or the ground floor, and a building put up in the 1910s or 1920s was given no shaft, no sleeve and no empty conduit for anyone to follow upward. Telephone wiring, when it went in, was usually stapled up the stair hall or fished through whatever gap a previous installer found. A good riser decision at the start of a job saves holes, saves money and saves the next provider from drilling its own way up.

Start where the provider stops

Every vertical plan starts at the demarcation point, the place where the provider's equipment ends and the building's wiring begins. In a Belmont mixed-use building that is usually a box or a row of boxes on a cellar wall, sometimes shared by two or three providers and the store downstairs. We do not open, move or cut the provider's equipment. We mount a small locked cabinet or backboard nearby, run the riser cable from there, and leave the provider's box exactly where its technician expects to find it. That separation matters later, when the provider comes for a repair and can see at a glance where its work ends.

Floors are fire separations, and we treat them that way

Every hole we make through a floor or a fire-rated wall is sealed the same day with a listed firestop product, and each seal is photographed for the owner's records. In older buildings with wood framing and wood floors, that is not a formality. A neat bundle of cables through an unsealed hole is an open path for smoke between apartments. When a building owner shows us an earlier installer's unsealed penetrations, we include sealing them as an optional line in the quote, even when our own cable does not pass through them.

Food-Business Networks

Ovens, Counters and Cold Rooms: Wiring an Arthur Avenue Food Business

A bakery, deli, pizzeria or trattoria on Arthur Avenue or East 187th Street runs far more of its day over the network than its owners usually realize: the card terminal, the register, the ticket printers on the line, a kitchen display, the tablet that takes delivery orders, the office computer, a camera recorder and the Wi-Fi guests ask for. Point-of-sale companies are explicit about what that means for wiring. Toast's site-readiness guide says most of its devices need an Ethernet cable run from the switch, that a single Cat5e or Cat6 cable should run to each device location, that each run should stay under 300 feet, and that Toast does not run the cables itself; the owner or a licensed low-voltage contractor has to finish, terminate and test them before installation day.

The line is the hardest room in the building

Kitchens punish cable. Heat rises off ovens and fryers, grease coats everything above the line, mops and hoses reach the floor, and walk-in coolers have insulated panels nobody wants punctured. We keep cable overhead and away from the hood, bring it down inside the wall or in a metal raceway where it must reach a printer, place jacks high enough to stay out of splash and away from sinks, and never route data cable across the top of the hood or along a gas line. Toast's guide also warns against plugging terminals into outlets shared with microwaves, refrigerators or blenders; that is electrical work, so we note it on the plan and leave the outlet to a licensed electrician.

Install outside service, not during it

Help New York's April 2026 spotlight on Arthur Avenue notes that weekday mornings are the quietest time on the street and weekend afternoons the busiest. We schedule food-business work accordingly: pulling and drilling before opening or on a closed day, terminating and testing in the hours before the first delivery, and leaving the kitchen exactly as we found it. Where a POS vendor has an installation date, we work backwards from it, so every cable is certified and labeled before their technician arrives, the way their guide asks.

Street dining and festival days stretch the network outdoors

Arthur Avenue regularly closes to cars for outdoor dining and for events such as Ferragosto, which in 2026 ran along Arthur Avenue between East 187th Street and Crescent Avenue on September 13. A restaurant that takes orders on handhelds at sidewalk or street tables needs Wi-Fi that reaches past its front door. The answer is usually not a stronger router behind the counter; it is a cabled access point mounted near the front of the dining room, powered over its own cable, placed so its signal covers the tables outside as well as the room. That keeps orders flowing on the busiest days without hanging equipment outside the building.

Rental Building Networks

When the Lease Includes Wi-Fi: Building-Wide Networks for Belmont Rentals

Some Belmont owners now provide internet the way they provide heat. The Fordham Ram's 2025 guide notes that one of the realty companies near campus includes Wi-Fi in the rent, and owners who rent to students, or who simply want one bill instead of five, are asking the same question: is it better to put a router in every apartment or to build one network for the whole building? For a walk-up of ten to thirty apartments, a properly cabled building network is usually easier to run, easier to secure and much easier to reset between tenants.

What a building network looks like

The provider's service comes into the cellar. A locked cabinet beside it holds the router, a managed switch that can power devices over their cables, the patch panel and a battery backup. From there the riser carries a cable to each apartment, or to an access point in each apartment or hallway, depending on the building's walls. Each apartment gets its own private network so neighbors cannot see each other's devices. The owner, or a manager the owner chooses, can see which access point is offline, change passwords and restart equipment from one place.

Access points in the apartment, not the hallway, when the walls are thick

Hallway access points look tidy on a drawing, but in a pre-war walk-up with heavy plaster and masonry walls between the stair hall and the rooms, a signal from the hallway often reaches the front room and little else. Where the walls are dense, we usually put a small access point inside each apartment, mounted on the ceiling or on a wall plate, cabled back to the switch. In newer or gut-rehabbed buildings with lighter partitions, fewer hallway units can sometimes cover more apartments. We decide during the walk-through, after measuring how the walls actually behave, rather than promising a number of units in advance.

Resetting the network when everyone moves out at once

The Fordham Ram observed that when students leave, they leave at the same time. For a building network that is a convenience rather than a problem: the owner can change every apartment's network name and password in one sitting, confirm each access point is online, and hand each new tenant fresh credentials at move-in. We leave a written procedure for that turnover in the cabinet, alongside the port map, so the owner does not need to call anyone to do it.

Who owns what

On a Verizon community forum a renter asked whether the provider's equipment could stay in the building's data closet with an Ethernet cable run to the apartment; the reply was that it should work, but that it becomes a question between the tenant and the landlord about who owns that cable. We answer that question in writing at the start. Cable inside the walls and the cabinet in the cellar belong to the building. Devices a tenant buys belong to the tenant. Our plan says which is which, and we only work inside an apartment with the owner's authorization and the tenant's agreement on the time.

Open-Wall Work

Rebuilds, Gut Rehabs and New Floors: Cable Before the Walls Close

Belmont's housing has been rebuilt in waves. Wikipedia describes losses to arson and demolition in the 1970s and 1980s, followed by a restoration and construction boom from the mid-1990s on. Belartes Court, at Arthur Avenue and Belmont Avenue, is described by its manager as a 73-unit, three-building, five-story elevator gut rehab. New buildings at 2189 and 2276 Arthur Avenue add 25 and 41 apartments. And in April 2026 the fire at 660 East 187th Street, which ABC7 reported spread from the first floor to the fifth in a roughly century-old building, left a building that will have to be largely rebuilt inside. Each of those projects shares one moment that matters for cabling: the walls and ceilings are open.

The cheapest cable is the one pulled with the framing exposed

With open framing, a riser can go into a dedicated sleeve stacked floor over floor, every apartment can get a cable to a central closet, access point locations can be set in the ceilings before the drywall goes up, and the cellar can be given a proper backboard and cabinet location with power planned by the electrician. None of that requires guessing. The same work done after the walls close means fishing, surface raceway and compromises in every room.

Coordinate with the electrician and the general contractor

We work alongside the electrician rather than in place of one. The electrician installs outlets and circuits for the cabinet, kitchen screens and any device that needs line power; we install the low-voltage pathways, cable, jacks, cabinet, switch and access points. On a rebuild we ask for a copy of the electrical and architectural drawings, mark our outlet locations on them, and agree the order of work with the general contractor so our cable is in and labeled before insulation and drywall crews arrive.

Firestopping is part of the cable job, not someone else's

Every floor and rated wall a cable passes through is a fire separation. In a rebuild we prefer sleeves at each floor with a listed firestop system, so future cables can be added and resealed without breaking open a floor. After a fire in particular, owners and tenants deserve to know that new penetrations are sealed and documented. We photograph each one and include the photos in the handover file.

Early walk-ups: work with the plaster, not against it

Plaster on wood or metal lath cracks if it is attacked carelessly, and in a century-old building a crack does not always stop where it started. We scan each spot, drill small and slow, and prefer routes that avoid opening plaster at all: closets, the space behind baseboards and door casings, and the interiors of built-ins. Where a wall must stay untouched, a slim paintable raceway along the baseboard is honest and tidy. We also expect to find generations of telephone and cable television wire stapled along hallways and window frames; we identify what is live before anything is removed.

Apartments over shops: two networks, one cellar

In a mixed-use building the store and the apartments often share the cellar where every provider enters. We keep the store's equipment in its own locked cabinet, give the building's riser its own space beside it, and label every cable so the shop's printer line can never be mistaken for the third-floor apartment. When the store renovates its ceiling, the building's cable should not be in the way; we route it along a wall or in conduit the store's contractor can see and avoid.

1930s apartment houses: watch the distance

Larger buildings with long corridors and courtyard wings can push a cable run past the length a single switch can serve well. Where that happens, we place a small cabinet with a switch on an upper floor, fed by one riser cable or fiber from the cellar, and run each apartment's cable from there. It keeps every run within limits and gives the building a sensible second point to work from.

Two- and three-family houses: owner first, tenants separate

In a house where the owner lives on one floor and rents the others, the cleanest design gives each unit its own run from a single basement cabinet, as one Brownstoner forum commenter recommended for a two-family renovation: home runs to one central box, conduit from outside for providers, and cable to closets for access points. Each unit can then have its own service or its own network on a shared one, and nobody's equipment sits in someone else's apartment.

Restaurants and pastry shops: a network that cannot go down on Saturday

For a restaurant, the network is part of service. If the kitchen printer stops, tickets stop; if the terminal drops, the line at the counter grows. We design for the busiest hour: every printer and screen on its own certified cable, the switch and router on a battery backup sized to ride through a short outage, the cabinet in a cool, locked spot rather than over the dishwasher, and a printed port map so a manager can tell a vendor on the phone exactly which jack is which.

Butchers, fish markets and delis: cold, wet and busy

Shops with display cases, walk-ins and wash-downs need cable that stays out of the wet zone. Scales and label printers that connect to a network get jacks mounted above counter height with sleeved drops; cable stays overhead or inside walls, and nothing runs along the floor where it will be mopped and stepped on every day. We never pass cable through a cooler panel without the owner's and the refrigeration contractor's agreement, and if a scanner or tablet must work inside a walk-in, we talk through whether an access point outside the door is enough before touching the box.

Market stalls: the landlord's building, the vendor's network

A vendor in the Arthur Avenue Retail Market works inside a city-owned building where, as the Bronx Times reported, the electrical work did not meet current requirements in 2018, and where a 2026 DOB permit covers rooftop mechanical replacement. Any cable route there, from the service point to a stall, has to be agreed with the building's management first. Once approved, we give each stall its own labeled run and lockable equipment space, keep it separate from neighboring vendors, and leave a record management can keep on file. The same approach applies to any shared space where several tenants depend on one landlord's ceiling.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Belmont: Questions Answered

Getting service up a walk-up

Our walk-up's fiber service stops at a box in the cellar. Should every apartment get its own cable down to the cellar, or should a switch go on an upper floor?

For most Belmont walk-ups of five or six floors, a separate cable from each apartment to the cellar cabinet is the better design. Every apartment can then be tested, disconnected or given its own service independently, and there is only one place to troubleshoot. An upper-floor switch makes sense when the runs grow too long, as in larger 1930s buildings with courtyard wings, or when there is room for only one or two cables in the riser. In that case one cable or a fiber strand climbs to a small locked cabinet on an upper floor, and the apartments on the upper floors connect there.

Our walk-up has thick plaster and brick between the stair hall and the rooms. Will access points in the hallway cover the apartments?

Usually only partly. Dense plaster and masonry absorb Wi-Fi, so a hallway unit may serve the room nearest the door and little beyond it. In buildings like that we normally place one small access point inside each apartment, cabled back to the switch and powered over the same cable. We test signal through the actual walls during the walk-through before committing to either design, because two buildings of the same age on the same block can behave quite differently.

Counters, kitchens and cold cases

The ticket printer on our line sits a few feet from the pizza oven. Will the heat ruin the cable or the jack?

Not if the cable is routed sensibly. The cable should come down to the printer from above or inside the wall on the cooler side, never across the oven's face or over its top, and the jack should sit where radiant heat and grease do not reach it, with a short patch cord to the printer. Where the drop is exposed, we run it in a metal raceway or sleeve. If the printer itself has to sit closer to the oven than its maker recommends, that is a printer placement problem, and we will say so rather than wire it anyway.

We put tables in the street when Arthur Avenue closes to cars. Can our handheld order tablets stay connected out there?

Often, yes, with the right access point in the right place. A ceiling or high wall access point near the front of the dining room, cabled back to the switch, can usually cover sidewalk and street tables through the storefront glass and the open door. We check coverage at the tables during the walk-through. We do not mount equipment on the outside of the building without the owner's approval, and we keep the outdoor coverage on the staff network so the tablets never compete with guests.

Our butcher counter and floor are hosed down every night. How do you keep the network outlets dry?

By keeping them high and keeping cable off the floor. Jacks for scales, label printers and the register go above counter height, in boxes with covers where spray reaches, and cable runs overhead or inside the wall rather than along a baseboard that sits in water every night. Drops that must be exposed run in sealed raceway that sheds water downward. Patch cords from jack to device are short and replaceable, so if one gets soaked, it is swapped in seconds rather than the run inside the wall.

Our point-of-sale vendor's checklist says every cable must be labeled and tested before their technician arrives. What should the test results show?

Each run should show a pass on a certification test that measures the wiring order, length and signal performance of the whole link, along with a label that matches the jack and the patch panel port. Toast's own guide asks for each cable to be labeled with a matching identifier on both ends and to stay under 300 feet. We give you a printed list of every run, its label, its length and its pass result, so the vendor's technician can plug in and go instead of tracing cables on install day.

The router, the modem and a camera recorder sit on a shelf above the dishwasher in our back room. Is that a bad place for them?

It is one of the worst. Steam and heat shorten the life of network equipment, a leak from above or a splash can kill it outright, and a shelf in a busy prep area means cords get pulled. We move the equipment into a small locked, ventilated wall cabinet on a dry wall, ideally in an office or a corner of the cellar that stays cool, and bring the provider's line and the device cables to it. A battery backup goes in the same cabinet, off the floor.

Rental buildings and student apartments

We include Wi-Fi in the rent. How do we stop one apartment's heavy use from slowing down the whole building?

On a properly built building network, each apartment has its own network, and the router or controller can cap or balance how much of the shared connection any one apartment uses at a time. Cabled access points also help, because each apartment's traffic travels over its own wire to the switch instead of being relayed through neighbors' units. We set fair limits during setup, show the owner where to adjust them, and leave a note in the cabinet explaining the settings.

A student tenant asked to plug a gaming router into the building's network. Will that cause problems for everyone else?

It can. A second router plugged into an apartment jack can hand out addresses that confuse other devices, broadcast a competing Wi-Fi network on the same channels, or bypass the limits the owner set. On networks we build, each apartment's port can be configured to accept only normal devices, or the tenant's router can be allowed in a mode that does not interfere. The owner decides the rule; we set the network up to enforce it and explain how to change it.

Fordham has turned some nearby apartment buildings into dorms. Does a building full of students need a different network design than one with families?

It usually needs more capacity in the evening. Students tend to use many devices at once and at the same hours, so the design leans toward an access point in every apartment rather than one per floor, a switch with headroom, and a connection sized for simultaneous use. The cabling itself is the same; what changes is how many access points it feeds and how the network is managed. Help New York's 2026 spotlight notes the conversions near Fordham Road; this page does not describe those particular buildings, and we would plan any of them from a walk-through.

We rent a stall in the Arthur Avenue Retail Market. Who has to approve a new cable to our counter?

The building's management, before any work starts. A list of every floor and wall penetration, the firestop product used at each one, and a photo of each seal. That is part of our handover on every job. What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Belmont

These are claims that come up often in quick online answers about building networks, tested against the public record for Belmont and against how we work. Marketplaces and cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are useful for a general sense of the trade, but they describe national averages, not a 1912 walk-up on a Belmont side street.

"Belmont has fiber, so its buildings are already wired for fast internet."

Fiber availability describes the street, not the building. The FCC-based figures for Belmont show Optimum fiber at 88.7 percent and Verizon fiber at 85.9 percent coverage, which is good news for anyone ordering service.

But service typically arrives at a box in the cellar or on the ground floor. In a building from the 1910s or 1920s, nothing dependable carries that service up to the fourth floor or back to the office behind the kitchen. The same directory notes that some homes and apartments do not get the advertised speeds.

The fix for that gap is inside the walls: a riser, a cabinet and certified runs. A faster plan from the provider will not climb the stairs on its own.

"Restaurant point-of-sale systems run on Wi-Fi, so cabling a kitchen is optional."

Handheld tablets do run on Wi-Fi. Much of the rest does not. Toast's site-readiness guide says most of its devices, including printers, kitchen screens and ceiling access points, need an Ethernet cable from the switch.

Even the wireless pieces depend on cable: the access points that serve the handhelds connect to the switch by wire. A kitchen full of steel, steam and running motors is a hard place for wireless printing to stay reliable through a full service.

For an Arthur Avenue restaurant, the practical answer is wire to everything that does not move and cabled access points for everything that does, finished and tested before the POS company's installation day.

"Since the permit change, anyone can pull data cable through an apartment building."

NYC Local Law 128 of 2024 did remove the electrical permit requirement for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. The same provision, as a hiring guide summarizes it, still expects the work to be done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person.

No permit does not mean no rules. Fire alarm wiring is outside the exemption, new outlets remain electrical work, and every hole through a floor in a multi-family building is a fire separation that has to be sealed properly.

In a Belmont walk-up, the owner also decides who works in the cellar and the stair hall. A qualified installer with insurance, a written plan and photographed firestopping protects the owner far better than a cable pushed through whatever gap is handy.

"Cabling cost is a simple per-drop number you can look up."

Online cost guides often present a single range per outlet. That works for a new office with open ceilings. It does not describe Belmont's buildings, where one drop might be ten feet across a shop ceiling and the next might climb five floors through plaster.

One New York City cabling guide notes that a pre-war building with long, difficult runs and after-hours labor can exceed the top of its range. That matches what we see: the route, the vertical path and the hours available drive cost more than the cable does.

That is why we price Belmont jobs drop by drop after a walk-through, with each route and penetration written down, rather than printing a per-drop figure that would mislead most readers.

"Belmont's old buildings are landmarked, so no conduit can go on the outside."

The list of designated historic districts in the Bronx includes areas such as Mott Haven, Clay Avenue, Morris Avenue and Fieldston, but no Belmont district. The Historic Districts Council has proposed the Retail Market, Aquinas High School and Our Lady of Mount Carmel for consideration, which is not the same as designation.

So Landmarks approval is not currently a step for cabling in Belmont. The building owner's approval still is, and so is good judgment about appearance.

We keep exterior work to a minimum regardless: a neat conduit on a rear wall where nothing inside works, never a tangle of cable on a storefront, and every exterior hole sealed against water.

"Building-wide Wi-Fi just means a few access points in the hallways."

In some newer buildings with light partitions, hallway units can do a reasonable job. In a pre-war Belmont walk-up with plaster, lath and brick between the hall and the rooms, they often reach only the first room past each door.

The difference shows up at night, when every tenant is home and the back bedrooms have one bar of signal. Owners who include Wi-Fi in the rent, as one realty company near Fordham does, hear about it quickly.

The more dependable design puts a small cabled access point in each apartment, managed from the cellar cabinet. We decide which design fits by measuring the walls during the walk-through, not by counting doors on a floor plan.

"Gigabit internet needs Cat6A or fiber run to every room."

Properly installed Cat6 handles gigabit connections comfortably over normal building distances, and it is easier to route through tight closets and old walls than thicker Cat6A.

Cat6A and fiber earn their cost on specific links: a riser feeding an upper-floor switch, a backbone between two cabinets, or a building where the owner wants headroom for much faster service later.

What matters more than the category on the box is the installation: one continuous run, correct termination, no crushed jackets, and a certification result for every link. A badly installed Cat6A run performs worse than a well-installed Cat6 one.

Belmont Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Can one cabinet serve both our shop and the apartments upstairs if we own the whole building?
+

Yes, if it is set up so the two never mix. One locked cabinet in the cellar can hold the router, a switch and the patch panel for the whole building, with the shop's registers, printers and office on their own network and each apartment on its own. Many owners prefer that, because there is one place to look when something stops working. If the shop is leased to someone else, we usually give the shop its own lockable section or a separate small cabinet beside the building's, so the tenant never needs access to the apartments' equipment and the owner never needs the store's keys.

Will you coordinate with the point-of-sale company's technician on their installation day?
+

Yes. We ask for the vendor's site checklist and installation date at the start, plan every drop to match it, and finish, label and certify the cabling before their visit. We give the restaurant a printed list of every cable, its label and its test result to hand to the vendor's technician. If the vendor wants someone from our side on the day, we can arrange it, but the goal is that their technician plugs devices in and goes, with nothing left to trace or fix.

Can the guest Wi-Fi in our dining room be set to shut off after closing time?
+

On most business-grade access points and controllers, yes. The guest network can be scheduled to switch off outside business hours while the staff and payments networks stay on, which stops people outside using the connection overnight. It can also be limited in speed so guests never crowd out the registers. We set the schedule during setup and show the manager where to change it when hours change for summer, holidays or street events.

Is a wired jack still worth having in a student apartment when the building already supplies Wi-Fi?
+

Often it is. A desk computer, a game console or a television on a wire takes load off the apartment's wireless network and gets a steadier connection than anything over the air, especially in the evening when every apartment in the building is busy. If an access point in the apartment is already cabled, a wall-plate model with a couple of extra ports can add wired connections without a second cable through the wall. We price that as an option when we plan the building.

What is the difference between a drop and a run on a Belmont cabling quote?
+

A run is the cable itself, one continuous piece from the patch panel in the cabinet to a single outlet. A drop is the finished connection at the device end: the run plus its jack, faceplate or box, its label and its test result. On our quotes each drop is its own line, with the route its run takes, any wall or floor it passes through and how that hole is sealed. Riser cables that feed an upper-floor switch are listed separately, because they serve several drops at once.

Will you ask our landlord for approval, or do we have to arrange it ourselves?
+

The approval has to come from the landlord to you, but we make it easy. We provide the written plan showing every route and penetration, our certificate of insurance and our license number, and we are happy to talk the plan through with the landlord or managing agent on the phone. Many landlords mostly want to know that holes will be sealed, nothing will be left on the facade and the building's shared spaces will be left clean, and the plan answers those questions in writing.

Our shop's internet comes in through a cellar the landlord controls and another tenant uses for storage. Can our cabinet still go down there?
+

It can, with the landlord's agreement and a locked cabinet. We mount it on a wall away from the other tenant's stock, high enough that boxes cannot be stacked against it, and run one protected cable up to your shop. If the cellar is damp or hard for your staff to reach, the better option is often to bring the provider's line up into your back room once and put the cabinet there, so you never need access to someone else's space to restart your router.

Will the access point you install work with the tablets the delivery apps gave us?
+

Yes. Delivery and ordering tablets connect to ordinary Wi-Fi, so they work with any business access point we install. We put them on the staff network rather than the guest network, so they are not slowed by customers' phones, and we place the access point so the counter where the tablets sit has strong signal. If a tablet must sit somewhere coverage is weak, such as a back room behind a cooler, a short drop and a small access point there usually solves it.

If we add drops in a few years, how do the new labels fit with the old port map?
+

Our labels follow a simple pattern tied to the cabinet, the patch panel port and the location, and the port map leaves room for growth. New drops take the next numbers on the panel, get the same kind of label at both ends and are added to the map, whether we install them or another qualified contractor does. Because the original test results are on file, any new work can also be checked against them to confirm nothing existing was disturbed.

Do you label the wall jacks in the dining room and kitchen, or only the patch panel?
+

Both ends, every time. Each faceplate or surface box carries the same identifier as its patch panel port, and in kitchens we use labels made to stand up to heat and cleaning. That way a manager standing at the line can read the label on a printer's jack and tell a vendor on the phone exactly which port to check, without anyone going down to the cellar to guess.
