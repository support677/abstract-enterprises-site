The Neighborhood

Why Bensonhurst Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Walk any side street between 18th Avenue and Bay Parkway and the reason buzzer work here is different becomes obvious. The houses are mostly attached and semi-detached brick, with stucco, stone or aluminum siding on some fronts, and a great many of them are two-family homes built so the owner could live on one floor and rent the other. That layout produces a front entrance that is almost never just one door. There is the house door at the top of a short stoop, an aluminum storm door in front of it on many blocks, a low fence and gate at the sidewalk, and on houses with a rentable basement, a separate door down a few steps or around the side. A buzzer system for a Bensonhurst two-family has to answer a simple question for each of those openings: does a visitor stop here, and if so, who lets them through?

The apartment stock adds a second kind of job. Along 86th Street, Bay Parkway, New Utrecht Avenue and the numbered streets near them stand four- to six-story buildings from the late 1920s and 1930s, the same era when Brooklyn builders put up hundreds of apartment houses with courtyards and lobbies sized to the building. Two examples on the record show the scale: a rent-stabilized building at 8210 19th Avenue listed with 53 apartments over six floors, built in 1939, and a four-story building at 2430 63rd Street with 23 apartments, built in 1928. Bell and buzzer systems in buildings of that age have usually been patched for decades. When they fail, they rarely fail all at once. They fail one apartment at a time, and tenants live with it. A former resident of the 19th Avenue building wrote in a review that the "door bell is not working since April." Another wrote: "The packages get stolen by outsiders who come in when you hold the door open."

Those two complaints are the whole case for doing an installation properly. A bell that works in only some apartments pushes tenants to wait in the lobby or prop the door for a delivery. A door that strangers can follow residents through turns the lobby into the easiest place on the block to take a package. A working system fixes the first problem directly, and the right choices at installation, such as video at the door, a release on the correct door and a closer that pulls the door shut every time, make the second one harder. Neither problem is solved by a new panel alone, which is why we start every Bensonhurst job with the door rather than the electronics.

The newer housing brings its own version of the same issue. Bensonhurst saw a burst of construction in the early 2000s, with about 1,200 new units approved between 2002 and 2005, and new buildings are still going up; Tower 85 at 2066 85th Street, between 20th and 21st Avenues, is an eleven-apartment building completed in 2022 and opened in 2025, listed with an intercom among its features. Buildings like these arrive with whatever system the developer chose, and in buildings of eight or more apartments built after 1968, that intercom is a legal requirement rather than an amenity. Owners and condo boards in those buildings usually call us when the developer's system turns out to be audio-only, when the directory was never finished, or when nobody can find the admin login.

Finally, Bensonhurst's houses keep changing shape. Researchers reported years ago that many newer immigrant families here lived in houses divided into small apartments, and the city's basement legalization program now gives owners in Community District 11 a legal route for basement and cellar units, provided the unit has at least one door leading outside, a ceiling at least seven feet high and no immediate safety risk, and sits outside the highest-risk flood zones. Every one of those conversions, legal or planned, adds a door and a household to an entry system that was designed for fewer of both. The practical result is that a great deal of buzzer work in Bensonhurst is really entrance planning: deciding where each household's visitors arrive, and making sure each household can answer for itself.

Need Repair in Bensonhurst? Bells Dead in Some Apartments, a Release That Hums but Never Opens, or a Door That Swings Free

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Bensonhurst's Architecture

Nearly every Bensonhurst buzzer job happens in one of six kinds of building. What separates them is not the brand of panel but three practical things: how many households the system has to call, how many doors those households use, and which way a cable can travel from the entrance to each apartment without opening finished rooms.

The semi-detached two-family

One side of a pair of brick houses, often with a shared stoop line or a shared driveway, an owner's floor and a rental floor, and a front door set back behind a storm door.

The attached brick row two-family

A house in a continuous row, with a stoop, a small yard behind a fence at the sidewalk, and two apartments stacked over a cellar or a finished basement.

The legal three- and four-family

Larger houses with two front entrances or a front entrance and a side entrance, plus a basement door, where buttons have to be split between doors.

The house with a basement unit

A two-family with a basement apartment, existing or planned under the legalization program, whose separate outside door needs its own way for visitors to call.

The prewar apartment building

Four- to six-story buildings from the late 1920s and 1930s with dozens of apartments, a lobby, an outer and inner door and a riser that has been patched many times.

The newer condo or rental

Buildings from the early 2000s and the 2020s, often with eight or more apartments, where the intercom is required by law and owners want video or app answering.

The semi-detached two-family: one wall shared, one wall free, and a stoop on the property line

A semi-detached house is attached to its twin on one side and open on the other, usually with a driveway or a side path along the open side. That geography helps a buzzer installation more than people expect. The open side gives a cable route that never touches a finished room: from the cellar, up the outside of the side wall under a neat cover, and into the hall at the top of the stoop. The shared side is the one to leave alone. The party wall belongs to both owners, and the stoop on many pairs is built right up to the line, so a door station fixed to the shared wall or the neighbor's railing is a dispute waiting to happen. We mount on your own door frame, your own reveal or your own side of the stoop wall, and if there is no good spot, on a short post or plate on your property.

Inside, the usual layout is the owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, sharing one street door and one hallway. The system needs two buttons, two answering points and one release, plus a decision about the storm door, which we cover in its own section further down. When the owner's household spans two levels, such as a first-floor apartment with a finished basement, it often makes sense to put an answering station on each of the owner's levels so the bell is heard wherever the family is. That is a second station on the same button, not a second button, and it adds only a little to the job.

The attached brick row two-family: a fence, a stoop and two floors over the cellar

Houses in an attached row share both side walls, so the open-side route of a semi-detached house is not available. The cable has to go up inside. In Bensonhurst row houses the better paths tend to be a closet that stacks from the first floor to the second, the space beside the stair, or the back of the house where a rear wall can carry a cable up to the second-floor kitchen without anyone seeing it from the street. The old bell wire, if any survives, usually runs along the stair hall, and it is worth testing before anyone opens a wall, because two-conductor bell wire in good condition can carry more than people think for a two-apartment audio system.

Many of these houses also have a low fence and gate across the front yard. Most owners leave the gate on a simple latch and put the buzzer on the house door, which is the cheapest arrangement and works well. Owners who keep the gate locked, often because the yard holds bins, bicycles or a parked car, need the door station at the gate instead, a release on the gate itself and a cable under the yard paving. That is a bigger job, and it changes the price, so we ask about the gate in the first call rather than finding it at the walkthrough.

The legal three- and four-family: buttons that follow the doors

Larger Bensonhurst houses often have more than one front entrance. The four-family at 8418 17th Avenue, built in 1929, was described in its sales listing as having two residential entrances in front plus its own steps down to a separate basement entrance. In a house like that, each front door typically leads to its own stair and its own pair of apartments. A single four-button panel at one door would send half the visitors to the wrong stair. The right plan is a panel at each entrance with only the buttons for the apartments behind that door, and a release on each door wired so that apartment 3 can open the door its guests are standing at and nothing else.

The power supply and the head-end equipment can still be shared, which keeps the cost down, and a single wiring plan in the cellar can feed all three doors. What we avoid is the shortcut of putting one panel at the busiest door and telling everyone else's visitors to walk around. That arrangement produces exactly the propped doors and missed deliveries a buzzer is supposed to end. For houses of three or four apartments, a two-door or three-door system still sits in the small-building ranges on our list, with the extra doors reflected inside the range.

The house with a basement unit: a separate door that needs its own call

Basement apartments are part of the housing story in this part of Brooklyn, and Community District 11 is now one of the fifteen districts where the city's legalization program applies. The program's basic conditions, as reported when the district list was published, include at least one door leading outside, a ceiling of at least seven feet and a location outside the highest-risk flood zones, and HPD's proposed rules from November 2025 add work such as sprinklers and flood water sensors, with owners applying before April 20, 2029. None of the program materials we reviewed list an intercom or buzzer among the requirements. Visitors still have to reach whoever lives downstairs, though, and a basement tenant whose guests ring the owner's bell is a source of friction in any house.

The installation answer is a button at the basement's own outside door that calls only the basement unit, with either a release on that door or simply a doorbell and a peephole, depending on what the tenant and owner want. If the basement door opens into the same hallway as the rest of the house, the owner also has to think about whether a release on it lets visitors into shared space. We wire the basement station into the same power supply and system as the rest of the house, so it is one system to maintain, and we leave a spare pair in the cable for the day the arrangement changes again.

The prewar apartment building: a riser that has seen every decade

Bensonhurst's late-1920s and 1930s apartment buildings were built in the period when Brooklyn's builders put up hundreds of apartment houses, many of them six stories, with lobbies scaled to the size of the building and rooms facing a courtyard or the street. Their entry systems were added and replaced over the following decades, so a typical lobby today has a panel from one era, handsets from two or three others and a riser cable that has been spliced on every floor. The tenant complaints recorded for the 19th Avenue building, a bell dead since April and packages taken by outsiders who follow residents in, are the two symptoms we see most often in buildings like it.

Replacing a system in a building of 23 or 53 apartments is planned like a small construction job. We test the existing riser pair by pair, decide whether a two-wire system can reuse it or whether new cable is needed, and set a schedule that keeps the front door locked and working every night of the job. Where an owner cannot replace everything at once, the work can be split: the lobby panel, power supply and release first, then the apartment stations floor by floor, provided the new panel can talk to the old handsets in the meantime or the old and new systems can run side by side. We tell you plainly whether your building's equipment allows that before you commit to a phased plan.

The newer condo or rental: a legal requirement with a developer's choices built in

New York's Multiple Dwelling Law requires an intercommunication system with voice contact and remote release at the self-locking main door of Class A buildings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968. Most of Bensonhurst's newer buildings fall on that side of the line, which means the intercom is not optional and the owner, or the condo board, has to keep it working. The developer's system is often adequate. Sometimes it is an audio panel in a building whose owners all expected video, a cloud system whose subscription was in the sponsor's name, or a directory that still lists the sales office.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Counting the Doors on a Bensonhurst Front Before Quoting

These notes set out how our technicians approach the houses and buildings typical of Bensonhurst. They describe our standard practice, not an account of any particular address.

Count every opening from the sidewalk in. The technician starts at the sidewalk and walks the path a visitor takes: the yard gate, the storm door, the house door, any inner door, and then any other outside door, at the side, at the back or down at basement level. Each one gets a note: does it latch, who uses it, and does anyone want it released from inside. A Bensonhurst two-family can easily have four openings between the sidewalk and the hallway, and the price depends on which of them the system has to handle.

Test the storm door before the main door. If an aluminum storm door has a working latch or lock, a visitor buzzed through the house door is still stuck outside it. The technician checks whether the storm door's latch is used, whether the owner would be happy leaving it on a closer without a lock, and whether the house door's frame can take a strike with the storm door closed in front of it.

Read the front material. Brick, stucco, stone and aluminum siding each need a different mounting method and a different way of bringing cable through. Brick takes sleeves and anchors well. Stucco cracks if it is drilled carelessly. Siding hides a wall that may be anything underneath. The technician notes which surface the panel would sit on and whether a frame-mounted station would avoid the issue altogether.

Fronts, Gates and Storm Doors

Storm Doors, Yard Gates, Siding and Stucco: Fitting a Buzzer to a Bensonhurst Front

The release is the part of a buzzer system people forget, and in Bensonhurst it is the part that most often decides the plan. The house door on a two-family here is rarely the only thing between a visitor and the hallway. Fronts were updated over the decades with aluminum storm doors, iron or aluminum fences, new siding and new stucco, and every one of those changes affects where a panel can go and what the buzzer can open. Here is how we handle each.

Aluminum storm doors. A storm door in front of the house door is the single most common complication on a Bensonhurst two-family. If the storm door has a latch that catches, a visitor buzzed through the inner door still cannot get in. There are three workable answers. The simplest is to leave the storm door on its closer with the latch disengaged or removed, so it pulls shut against weather but opens with a pull; the house door stays the locked, released door. The second is to replace the storm door's latch with one that never locks. The third, for owners who want both doors locked, is to move the release to the storm door itself, which works only on heavier storm doors with a frame solid enough to carry a strike, and usually means the door station goes outside the storm door where the weather reaches it. We talk through which one suits how you use the front before quoting, because each has a different price and a different feel.

Yard gates. Many Bensonhurst houses have a fence at the sidewalk with a gate on a latch. If the gate is never locked, the buzzer belongs at the house door and the gate is irrelevant. If it is locked, the door station has to move to the gate, the gate needs its own release, which on a light metal gate is usually a gate-rated electric strike or lock with a weatherproof housing, and a cable has to reach the gate post under the paving or along the base of the fence. Some owners want both: the gate released for deliveries during the day and the house door released for guests. That is two releases on one system, and the apartment station can be set up so one button opens the gate and another opens the door.

Aluminum and vinyl siding. Siding hides the wall. Behind it may be the original brick, wood sheathing over framing, or a layer of insulation board, and the only way to know is to look at an edge or a utility penetration. Siding is also the easiest surface to run a hidden cable behind on the outside of a house, because a cable can travel in the space between the siding and the wall and come out at the door frame, provided it is protected where it passes through. What we avoid is screwing a panel straight through the siding, which lets water in and leaves the panel loose. Panels on sided fronts go on a mounting block fixed through to the wall behind, or on the door frame itself.

Stucco. Stucco fronts are common on the side streets here, and stucco cracks when it is drilled with the wrong bit or anchored too close to an edge. We drill stucco with a masonry bit at low speed, keep anchors well back from corners and window returns, and seal every penetration, and where the door frame is wide enough, we mount the station on the frame and avoid the stucco entirely. If a stucco front has already cracked around an old bell button, we tell you before we work near it, so the old damage is not mistaken for ours.

Electric strikes on Bensonhurst doors. Most house doors here are wood or metal-clad doors in wood or steel frames, with a cylinder lock or a lockset and a deadbolt above it. The usual release is an electric strike fitted in the frame where the latch catches, which leaves the lock unchanged: the key works from outside and the handle turns from inside. Fail-secure strikes stay locked if power is lost, and as hardware maker Allegion explains, with an electric strike you can always exit by turning the lever, whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. A deadbolt is the one thing a strike will not release, so if the household throws the deadbolt during the day, the buzzer will not open the door until it is unlocked. We point that out at the walkthrough, because it is the most common reason a new buzzer seems not to work.

Lobbies and Packages

Lobby Doors, Riser Cable and the Package Problem in Bensonhurst Apartment Buildings

In a building with dozens of apartments, a buzzer installation succeeds or fails at the lobby door. The panel can be the best on the market, but if the door behind it drifts open, if residents routinely hold it for anyone walking up, or if a courier who is buzzed in for one apartment can reach every mailbox and package on the floor, tenants will keep losing deliveries. The former resident of the 19th Avenue building who wrote that packages were taken by outsiders who come in when someone holds the door was describing a door problem and a habit problem, not a broken buzzer. A new system can help with both, but only if the installation is planned around them.

Start with the closer. HPD's rules on self-closing doors are strict: a public-hall door that does not close and latch on its own is a Class C immediately hazardous condition with 14 days to correct, and HPD says it does not wait for a complaint before inspecting for it. Acting HPD Commissioner Ahmed Tigani put the point simply when the agency announced its enforcement figures in November 2025: "the last thing you should have to worry about is whether your door will close behind you." A lobby door that closes slowly, or bounces off the latch, gives anyone behind a resident a few seconds to walk in. Before we wire any release to a lobby door, the closer is adjusted or replaced and the latch is set so the door locks every time it swings shut.

Separate the package path from the apartment path. Where the lobby layout allows it, the best defense against package theft is to give couriers a place to leave deliveries that is not open to everyone who follows a resident inside. In some buildings that is a locked package room on a code that changes, in others a vestibule shelf between the outer and inner doors, and in newer buildings a package locker. The entry system can be set up so couriers reach that space and nothing more. We are honest that none of this stops a resident from holding the door for a stranger; it just makes the building less convenient for the stranger.

Test the riser before promising anything. The riser is the cable that climbs from the lobby to every floor. In Bensonhurst's prewar buildings it has often been spliced at every floor, extended for added apartments and cut during renovations. A two-wire system can reuse a riser in good condition, which saves a great deal of labor, but a riser with bad splices produces misrouted calls and dead stations. Industry reporting has described a co-op that skipped rewiring to save money, got misrouted calls and failed audio and video, and ended up rewiring the whole building anyway at a higher cost. We test every pair end to end and put the result in writing before we price reuse.

Phones, Apps and the Law

Answering the Door From a Phone in Bensonhurst: Family Houses, Absent Owners and the City's Rules

Phone and app answering suits Bensonhurst in several ways. Owners of two-family houses who spend long stretches away from home can answer their own door from wherever they are. Tenants who work long shifts can let a delivery into the vestibule. A building where opening walls in every apartment would be disruptive can get a system that needs cable only at the door and the power supply. And a household where grandparents use a wired handset and grandchildren use an app can have both on the same button.

For multiple dwellings, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, also applies. HPD describes it as covering owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access, or keyless, systems. Owners must obtain express consent in writing or through a mobile application before collecting data; may collect only the minimum authentication and reference data, such as a name, a unit number and a preferred contact method; must destroy authentication data no later than 90 days after it is collected unless it is kept in anonymized form; and may not require a tenant to use a smart access system to enter their own apartment. A two-family house is not a multiple dwelling under the state's definition, which starts at three families living independently, but a legal three- or four-family house is.

In practice that means an owner of a Bensonhurst four-family who adds app entry should keep keys working on every apartment door, collect only what the system needs, and have a written privacy policy ready for tenants. When we install one of these systems, we set it to collect the minimum and give the owner the system's data and retention settings in writing. The policy itself is the owner's to write, and for a larger building it is worth having a lawyer look it over.

Language matters more here than in most of the borough. With a large share of residents born abroad and families who speak Chinese, Russian, Italian, Arabic or Spanish at home, a panel full of English instructions is a barrier. Our approach is practical: directories by unit number or letter, large clear buttons, a short picture-based instruction card at each answering station, and, for app systems, checking before purchase which languages the maker's app actually offers, rather than assuming.

The second request is from upstairs businesses: offices, clinics, tutoring centers and studios one flight up, whose clients come through a street door that is shared with apartments or that must not be left unlocked. Business doors work harder than house doors, and the hardware has to match. A door that is opened dozens of times a day needs a heavier strike, a closer adjusted to pull it shut every time, and a station rated for constant use. Rear doors also need thought about exit. Any locked door that people may leave through must let them out freely without a key or special knowledge, which shapes whether a strike or a magnetic lock is used and whether a push-to-exit button is needed.

Back doors are also where break-ins happen. The Brooklyn District Attorney's office described a burglary series between August 2022 and January 2023 that hit a grocery on 17th Avenue, a grocery on New Utrecht Avenue and a vape shop on 18th Avenue, along with homes on 76th Street and later 72nd Street; detectives from the 62nd Precinct used phone location data and enhanced video, and the defendant was sentenced to ten years in May 2024. A buzzer does not stop a determined burglar, but a rear door that stays locked because staff no longer prop it for deliveries is a smaller target, and a door station with a camera adds a record of who was let in. If you want a separate camera on the rear door, it goes in on the same visit, and combining the two saves most customers $200 to $400 in labor.

Compliance points for Bensonhurst entry installations

  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments, erected or converted after January 1, 1968, must have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system with voice contact and remote release. Older buildings must install them if tenants in a majority of apartments request or consent in writing, with work started within 90 days and finished within six months.
  • Multiple dwelling definition. Under the Multiple Dwelling Law, a multiple dwelling is a building occupied by three or more families living independently, so a legal three- or four-family house is covered by multiple-dwelling rules that a two-family is not.
  • Self-closing doors. HPD classes a failed self-closing door as an immediately hazardous Class C condition with 14 days to correct, and says it inspects for self-closing doors without waiting for a complaint. Every release we add leaves the door closing and latching on its own.
  • Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021). Keyless entry in multiple dwellings needs express consent, collects only minimal listed data, deletes authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and cannot be required for entry to a tenant's own apartment.
  • Egress and electrified locks. Under the building code, electrically locked egress doors with sensor release need an inside sensor, a manual push-to-exit device, release on power loss and release on fire alarm or sprinkler activation; fire-rated doors take fail-secure strikes.
  • Accessible reach. The 2010 ADA Standards set a high forward reach of 48 inches maximum and a low forward reach of 15 inches minimum for operable parts. Business entrances and new panels are mounted within that range wherever the front allows.
  • Low-voltage wiring and permits. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 exempts most signaling and communication wiring under 50 volts from the electrical permit requirement when done by a licensed electrician or qualified person, with exceptions for life-safety systems. A new 120-volt circuit or outlet for the power supply is electrical work for a licensed electrician.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Bensonhurst: Questions Answered

These are the questions Bensonhurst owners, tenants, boards and shopkeepers raise when a new entry system is being planned: houses with more doors than apartments, fronts that resist drilling, basements that are becoming legal units, big prewar buildings and the businesses on the avenues. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Two-family and multi-family houses

Our semi-detached two-family shares its stoop wall with the house next door. Can the door station be mounted on that shared wall?

We would rather not, and in most cases we do not need to. The party wall and any stoop wall or railing built on the property line belong to both owners, so fixing a panel or running cable through them without your neighbor's agreement invites a dispute, and if the neighbor later rebuilds their side, your panel goes with it. On a semi-detached pair there is almost always a better spot on your own side: the door frame, the reveal beside the door inside the storm door, or the open side wall where your driveway or path runs. That open side is also the easiest cable route from the cellar, up the outside wall under a neat cover and in at the hall, so the installation is usually simpler, not harder, for staying off the shared wall. If both owners of a pair want buzzers, we are happy to do both houses on the same visit, each on its own side.

The owner's apartment in our two-family covers the first floor and the finished basement. Can one doorbell button ring a station on both levels?

Yes. One button can call more than one answering station, so the owner's button at the stoop can ring a handset or monitor on the first floor and a second one in the basement family room, while the tenant's button rings only upstairs. Most audio and video systems support several stations per apartment, and phone answering can add every family member's phone to the same call. The extra station needs its own short cable run, usually down through the floor near the stair or along the cellar ceiling, and on our Brooklyn list it moves a two-family job up a little within the $400 to $900 audio range or the $700 to $1,800 video range rather than into a different one. We ask where the family actually spends its evenings before choosing where each station goes.

We own a legal four-family with two front entrances, each leading to two apartments, and a separate basement door. How should the buttons be divided?

Put the buttons where the visitors for those apartments will actually stand. Each front entrance gets a panel listing only the two apartments behind that door, with a release on that door. The basement door, if it serves a unit, gets its own button calling only that unit. All three can share one power supply and one wiring plan in the cellar, so it remains one system to maintain. A house of this type was listed for sale at 8418 17th Avenue, a 1929 legal four-family with two residential entrances in front plus a basement entrance down its own steps, and the layout is common enough here that we plan for it from the first call. What we steer owners away from is a single panel for every apartment at one door, which sends half the visitors to the wrong stair and ends with doors being propped.

We are applying to legalize our basement apartment through the city's program. Does the basement unit have to get its own buzzer?

Nothing we have found in the program says so. Community District 11 is one of the districts the program covers, and the conditions reported for it include at least one door leading outside, a ceiling of at least seven feet, no immediate safety risk and a location outside the highest-risk flood zones; HPD's proposed rules add work such as sprinklers and flood water sensors and an application deadline of April 20, 2029. An intercom is not listed in any of that material. The state's eight-apartment intercom rule will not usually reach a two-family with a basement unit either. Your architect or filing representative has the final word on what your plans need. Practically, though, a basement tenant needs a way to be called, and the cheapest time to add a button at the basement door is while walls and ceilings are already open for the legalization work, so tell us the schedule and we will fit our cable in with it.

Fronts, gates and storm doors

There is an aluminum storm door in front of our house door. If the buzzer releases the house door, how does a visitor get past the storm door?

Only if the storm door is not latched. The cheapest and most common answer is to leave the storm door on its closer and disengage or remove its latch, so it pulls shut against wind and rain but opens with a pull, while the house door behind it stays the locked door the buzzer releases. Some owners replace the storm door's handle with a non-locking one for the same effect. If you want both doors locked, the release has to move to the storm door, which only works on a heavy storm door with a frame strong enough for a strike, and the door station then has to sit outside the storm door in the weather. We check the storm door at the walkthrough and price the option you choose, so you are not surprised on installation day by a visitor stuck between two doors.

Our front-yard gate stays locked because of the bins and the car. Can the buzzer open the gate as well as the front door?

Yes, as two releases on one system. The door station moves to the gate, where visitors arrive, and the gate gets a release rated for outdoor use, usually a weatherproof electric strike or lock sized for a light metal gate. A cable runs from the house to the gate post, either under the yard paving in a protected sleeve or along the base of the fence. Inside, the answering station can open the gate alone, for a courier leaving a parcel in the yard, or the gate and then the house door, for a guest. Because a locked gate means the house door is no longer the first barrier, some owners then leave the house door on a key and release only the gate. The gate work adds cable, trenching or channeling and a second release, so a gate job usually sits higher in its range on our Brooklyn list, and we price it at the walkthrough once we see the gate and its hinge side.

Our two-family is covered in aluminum siding. Can the buzzer cable run outside, behind the siding, instead of through the rooms?

Often yes, and it is one of the tidier ways to wire a Bensonhurst house. The space between the siding and the wall behind it can carry a low-voltage cable from the cellar up to the door frame, or up to a second-floor station, without touching a finished room. The cable has to be rated for the conditions, protected where it enters and leaves the wall, and kept away from any electrical service entrance, and the siding panels have to come off and go back cleanly, which works on most aluminum and vinyl siding in good condition. What we will not do is screw a door station straight through the siding, because that lets water in and leaves the panel loose; the panel goes on a mounting block fixed to the wall behind or on the door frame. If the siding is brittle or patched, we tell you before we touch it.

Apartment buildings

Our 1930s building has more than fifty apartments. Can the entry system be replaced in stages instead of all at once?

Sometimes, and we will tell you up front whether yours is one of those times. A phased replacement usually starts with the lobby: the door station, the power supply, the release and the closer, which fixes the part every resident uses. The apartment stations then follow floor by floor. That works only if the new panel can still call the old handsets during the changeover, or if the old and new systems can run side by side on separate cable until the last floor is done. Some two-wire systems allow that; some older systems simply cannot talk to anything new. The riser test decides it. Large buildings start around $2,500 on our Brooklyn list and can pass $10,000 for full video or cloud, and a phased plan spreads that over time without changing the total much. The one thing we never phase is the front door's locking, which has to work every night.

People who do not live here walk in behind residents, and packages disappear from our lobby. Is there anything the installation itself can do?

It can make the lobby harder to use as a pickup point, though it cannot stop a resident from holding the door. A former tenant of a 53-apartment building on 19th Avenue put the problem plainly in a review: "The packages get stolen by outsiders who come in when you hold the door open." Three installation choices help. First, a closer adjusted so the door shuts and latches quickly, leaving no gap for someone trailing behind. Second, video at the panel, so residents see who they are releasing, and ideally a recorded image of each call. Third, a separate place for deliveries, such as a locked package room on a code or a shelf between the outer and inner doors, so couriers do not need to reach the inner lobby at all. A short notice to tenants about not holding the door finishes the job.

Con Edison cut voltage across southern Brooklyn during feeder repairs in 2020. Is a battery backup on a new buzzer power supply worth paying for here?

For most buildings, yes. In July 2020 Con Edison asked residents of southern Brooklyn neighborhoods including Bensonhurst to cut their power use during repairs and reduced voltage by 8 percent, with more than 96,000 residents affected. Low-voltage entry systems draw little power, and a supply with a battery can keep the panel, the apartment stations and a fail-secure strike working through a short outage or a dip, so residents can still answer and release the door. Without a battery, a fail-secure strike stays locked when power fails, which keeps the building secure, while residents still get in with keys and can always walk out by turning the handle. The battery adds little to the cost of a system and is standard in our multi-apartment installs. For a two-family, it is a choice we offer rather than insist on.

Businesses

Our bakery on 18th Avenue takes deliveries at a side door before we open. Can the driver be buzzed in from the kitchen?

Yes. A door station at the side door, a release on that door and a monitor or phone call in the kitchen let the person on shift see the driver and open the door without walking away from the ovens. The door locks again as soon as it closes, so it does not sit propped while the driver makes three trips. Business doors open many times a day, so we fit a heavier strike, adjust or replace the closer, and choose a station rated for constant use. Because people may also leave through that door, it must always open freely from inside, which rules out any hardware that would trap staff. If you would like a camera on the side door as well, it is installed on the same visit, which saves most customers $200 to $400 in labor, and the job is priced on the same Brooklyn list as residential work.

Yes. Steel doors in steel frames, common on building lobbies and shop rear doors, take electric strikes made for mortise locks, and the frame is cut and reinforced to suit. Some older mortise locks need an adjustment first so the latch meets the strike cleanly.

Can the door station be chosen in a finish that suits our door and brick?
+

Usually. Door stations come in several finishes, commonly black, silver, bronze or stainless, and in flush or surface versions. We bring finish options to the walkthrough so the panel suits the door, the frame and the brick or stucco around it.

Our house has a stucco front. Will drilling for the panel crack it?
+

Not when it is done carefully. We drill stucco with a masonry bit at low speed, keep anchors back from edges and corners, and seal every hole. Where the door frame is wide enough, the station goes on the frame and the stucco is not touched at all. Existing cracks are pointed out before work starts.

Do I have to stay home for the entire installation?
+

Someone with keys should be there at the start and at the end, and every apartment has to be reachable for a few minutes so its station can be fitted and tested. In between, the crew works at the door, in the cellar and along the cable route, so you do not need to stand by.

Can the door camera be watched on a television?
+

On some systems. Certain video door stations can show their picture on a smart TV through the maker's app or a network video feed, though answering and releasing are still done from a monitor or phone. We check what the chosen model supports before you buy, rather than promising it.

Should a cloud panel share the building's internet or get its own connection?
+

A dedicated connection, or a cellular module, is more reliable, because a shared line can be switched off or changed by whoever pays for it. In a small house sharing the owner's internet is usually fine, as long as the owner knows the panel depends on it.

Can the buzzer release work with our burglar alarm so it does not set off the siren?
+

It can be planned that way. The door contact on a released door can be placed on an entry delay, or a code or card at the door can disarm the alarm when it releases. We install alarms as well, so we coordinate both systems on one visit where the owner wants them linked.

Will you adjust or replace the door closer when the buzzer goes in?
+

Yes. A release is only useful on a door that pulls itself shut and latches every time, so we adjust the existing closer or fit a new one as part of the job, and tell you in the quote if a new closer is included.

Can our old doorbell chime stay connected alongside the new system?
+

Often it can, if the chime is sound and the new system offers an output for it, which many audio systems do. Some households like keeping the familiar chime in the hall as well as the new station. We test the old chime before promising to reuse it.
