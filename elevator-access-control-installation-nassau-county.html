What Elevator Access Control Means for a Nassau County Board, Landlord or Facilities Manager

Elevator access control in Nassau County usually begins with a specific complaint rather than a plan. A board member in a Long Beach tower notices that people who do not live in the building are riding to the top floor to look at the ocean from the hallway window. A property manager at a new rental near a Long Island Rail Road station finds that the amenity floor is being used after hours by friends of a friend of a tenant. A medical practice on an upper floor of a Garden City office building wants patients to stop wandering onto the floor where the billing staff sit. A housing provider running a five-story senior residence wants to know who is in the building at two in the morning. In every one of these cases the front door is doing its job some of the time, and the elevator is the part of the building that never asks a single question.

An elevator with no access control will carry anyone to any floor it serves. That is how it was designed, and for decades it was acceptable in most of Nassau, because the county's elevator buildings were relatively few and most of them had a doorman, a resident super or a staffed lobby. That has changed. Staffing has been trimmed, package volume has exploded, garages and side doors have multiplied, and new buildings around the train stations are going up with more cars, more floors and more shared space than the county has seen before. The elevator is now the one route to every apartment, office and roof that a stranger can use without a key.

Elevator access control closes that route. A reader is added to the elevator, either inside the car or beside the call button at a landing. A resident, tenant or employee presents a credential: a key fob, a card, a code, a phone, or in some buildings a face or a fingerprint. The reader sends that credential to an access control panel, the panel checks whether this person is allowed and, if so, closes a relay that tells the elevator controller the request is permitted. If the person is not allowed, the buttons stay dead and the attempt is logged. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems installs that whole low-voltage side: the elevator card reader, the elevator keypad or video station, the elevator key fob credentials, the panel, the relays, the cable and the programming. The building's licensed elevator contractor makes the final connection inside the controller, because in New York State that is elevator work.

For a first-time buyer in Nassau the most useful thing to understand is that this is not one product with one price. It is a short ladder, and each building climbs only as far as it needs to:

  • Level 1, a reader in the car. The car ignores floor buttons until a valid credential is shown. Once it is, the rider can choose any floor the car serves. This is the right answer for a great many Nassau co-ops and small rentals, where the only question is whether someone belongs in the elevator at all.
  • Level 2, biometrics. The same control, but the credential is a face, a fingerprint or an iris. It suits buildings with a serious lost-fob or shared-fob problem and staff who change often.
  • Level 3, floor-by-floor control and the extras. Each rider reaches only the floors they are allowed on. Readers can be added at garage and lobby call buttons, floors can open and close on a timetable, and the intercom can send a guest to one floor only. Station-area rentals, multi-tenant medical buildings and office buildings usually live here.

Two more points save a lot of confusion. First, your elevator does not get replaced or rebuilt. Its motor, doors, brakes and safety circuits are untouched, and your elevator maintenance company keeps maintaining them. Second, every honest proposal for elevator access control in Nassau has two parties on it: the low-voltage security contractor and the licensed elevator contractor. We plan both halves together, and we explain later on this page exactly where the line between them falls. If you are still deciding whether the work belongs on the front door, the garage door or the elevator, our Long Island elevator access control overview covers the regional picture, and this page stays with Nassau.

The County

Where Nassau County's Elevator Buildings Are: Ocean Towers, Village Downtowns and Station Rentals

Nassau is a county of houses, and anyone planning elevator security here should start from that fact. Most residents live in single-family homes, and most commercial space is low and spread out. Elevator buildings exist, but they cluster in a handful of places, and each cluster has its own character. Knowing which cluster a building belongs to tells us a lot before we ever walk in: how old the elevator controller is likely to be, whether there is a garage stop, who manages the property, and what kind of people are riding the car.

The county's own description of itself explains why the work feels so local. Nassau is divided among three towns, Hempstead, North Hempstead and Oyster Bay, plus two cities, Long Beach and Glen Cove, and inside the towns sit 64 incorporated villages and more than a hundred unincorporated hamlets. Many of those villages run their own building departments. A tower in Long Beach, a co-op in Great Neck Plaza and a new rental in Mineola are three different jurisdictions, even though they are a short drive apart.

The clusters we see most often are these. Long Beach has the county's densest group of tall residential buildings along Shore Road and the boardwalk, most of them built in the 1960s. The north shore villages around Great Neck have prewar and postwar elevator co-ops near the station. The county seat area, Mineola and Garden City, has medical and office buildings along Franklin Avenue and Old Country Road and, more recently, a wave of new apartment buildings across from the train. Westbury, Hempstead Village and other downtowns are rezoning for multifamily housing around their stations. Glen Cove's waterfront is being rebuilt with condominiums and rentals over parking. And scattered across all of it are senior and supportive housing buildings, usually four to six stories with one or two cars.

How It Works

How a Nassau Elevator Card Reader Talks to the Car, From Tap to Floor

The mechanics of an elevator access system are simpler than most people expect, and knowing them helps a board or a manager read a proposal critically. There are four pieces of hardware and one connection that matters.

The first piece is the reader. In most Nassau buildings it goes inside the car, mounted on or next to the button panel, because that is where the decision about floors is made. A reader can also go at a landing, beside the hall call button in the lobby or the garage, so the car will not even come unless a credential is shown. The reader might be a simple elevator RFID card and fob reader, a keypad, a combined keypad and reader, a phone-compatible reader, or a video station with a camera and a screen.

The second piece is the access control panel. It is a small powered enclosure, usually in the elevator machine room or in a communications closet nearby, that holds the list of approved credentials and the rules for each one. It has a battery so it keeps working through a short outage, and in most systems it connects to the building's network so the manager can add and remove people from an office or a phone.

The third piece is the relay board. A relay is an electrically operated switch. In a Level 1 system there may be only one relay per car: when the panel approves a credential, the relay closes for a few seconds and the controller accepts floor calls. In a floor-by-floor system there is a relay for each controlled floor in each car, and the panel closes only the relays for floors that person is allowed to reach.

The fourth piece is the wiring. The reader in the car needs power and data, and the only path into a moving car is the traveling cable, the flat bundle of conductors that hangs from the car to the hoistway wall. Many elevators have spare conductors in that cable that the elevator contractor can assign to the reader. Some have none, or have conductors that have been used up by earlier additions such as cameras, phones or card readers from a previous system. When there are no spares, the options are a new traveling cable, which is elevator work, or a wireless bridge between the car and the hoistway, which we plan with the elevator contractor.

The connection that matters is the one between our relays and the elevator controller. That is where the elevator learns whether a floor call is allowed. On newer controllers it is often a dedicated security input or a set of terminals the manufacturer designed for exactly this purpose. On older controllers it may involve the elevator technician wiring our relay contacts in series with the floor button circuits. In both cases it is the elevator contractor's job, and in both cases the access system must step out of the way whenever the elevator is in firefighters' service, which we cover in detail below.

Put together, a ride works like this: a resident steps in, taps a fob, the reader sends the fob number down the traveling cable, the panel looks it up, the panel closes the right relay or relays, the controller lights the allowed buttons for a few seconds, the resident presses their floor and the car goes. The whole exchange takes well under a second. A stranger who follows the resident in can press any button they like after the timer runs out, and the car will not move for them. Every tap, accepted or refused, is written into an audit trail with the time, the car and the credential.

Design Choice

Car Reader or Floor-by-Floor Restriction: Picking the Right Starting Point in Nassau

The most important design decision on any Nassau job is whether the building needs to control access to the elevator, or access to particular floors. They sound similar. They are not, and they lead to very different amounts of equipment and coordination.

A reader that simply enables the car answers one question: does this person belong in our elevator? Once they tap in, they can reach any floor that car serves. For a residential co-op where every shareholder has a reason to be on every hallway, because the laundry is on one floor, the storage on another and friends live throughout the building, that is usually all the building needs. It stops the stranger who slips in behind a delivery from roaming the halls, and it keeps the roof and the top-floor corridor from becoming a hangout. It is also the least disruptive to install: one reader, one relay and one controller input per car.

Floor-by-floor control answers a different question: which floors may this person reach? Each controlled floor in each car gets its own relay, and each credential carries a list of floors. A tenant on the fourth floor of a medical building sees only the lobby and the fourth floor light up. A resident of a rental tower sees their own floor, the lobby, the garage and the amenity floor, but not anyone else's corridor. That is a stronger kind of security, and in some buildings it is essential, but it multiplies the relays, the wiring at the controller and the programming. In a building with two cars serving eight floors and a garage, it can mean eighteen controlled stops instead of two.

Our honest advice to most Nassau residential boards is to start with the simplest version that solves the real problem, and to build it so it can grow. A Level 1 system installed with a panel that has room for more relays, cable sized for more conductors, and credentials that will work in a floor-by-floor system later can be upgraded without throwing anything away. We would rather install a car reader your co-op will actually use than a floor-restriction scheme that annoys shareholders until someone props the stairwell door open. Where a building really does need floors locked, such as a rental with a paid amenity floor, a professional building with separate practices, or any building with a mechanical penthouse or roof that must stay staff-only, we design that from the beginning.

There is one more variant worth knowing about: landing control. Instead of, or in addition to, a reader inside the car, a reader is placed at the call station on a specific floor, typically the garage or the lobby. The car will not answer a call from that landing until someone presents a credential. In Nassau buildings where the garage is the weak point, because residents drive in, gates stay open, and anyone who walks down the ramp can summon the elevator, a single reader at the garage call button can do more good than readers in every car.

Floor Control

Elevator Floor Restriction System Installation for Nassau Buildings With Mixed Floors

When a building does need elevator floor restriction, the design is a matter of counting and then deciding. We count the cars, the floors each car serves, the call stations at each landing and the doors that open onto each floor from the car. Then we decide, floor by floor, who should reach it and when.

In Nassau, the buildings that genuinely need floor-level control fall into a few groups:

  • Station-area rentals with amenity floors. Fitness rooms, lounges, co-working rooms and roof decks are a selling point, and management wants them reachable by residents only, sometimes only during posted hours. Floor restriction with a time-based schedule does exactly that.
  • Multi-tenant medical and professional buildings. Along Franklin Avenue in Garden City and around the Mineola hospital campus, buildings hold several practices on different floors, each with its own hours. Patients need to reach their doctor; they do not need to reach every floor.
  • Office buildings with mixed tenancy. A law firm on one floor, an accountant on another and a vacant suite on a third each want their floor protected after hours, while the lobby and common floors stay open during the day.
  • Buildings with staff-only floors. Mechanical penthouses, roof access, storage floors and management offices are better locked to everyone except staff, even in a building that otherwise runs on Level 1.
  • Garages. A garage level is often the one floor that needs control in both directions: residents should be able to ride down to their cars, and nobody coming up from the garage should be able to call the car without a credential.

Floor restriction also brings a few questions every Nassau manager should settle before installation, because they decide how the system behaves on its worst day. Which floors are always open, such as the lobby? Which floors open on a schedule, such as an office floor from eight to six on weekdays? What happens on holidays? Who can override the schedule, and how? Can a resident with mobility needs be given a credential that lights every floor they visit, including a neighbor's? And what is the plan for a contractor who needs to work on several floors for a week? We write the answers into the programming worksheet that goes with the proposal so nothing is decided by accident on install day.

The relay count is the number to watch. A simple rule of thumb is that each controlled floor in each car needs its own output, and each controlled landing call station needs its own as well. Two cars serving a garage, a lobby and eight upper floors, with every upper floor controlled and the garage landing controlled, can need twenty or more outputs. That is perfectly normal for a modern rental, but it is the reason a floor-by-floor quote looks so different from a single car reader, and it is why we never give a floor-restriction price over the phone.

Building Types

Nassau Building Types and the Elevator Access Control Each One Usually Needs

The best way to plan an elevator system in Nassau is to start from the building type. The same reader, panel and relays can be arranged a dozen ways, and the right arrangement depends on who rides, how the building is managed and what has gone wrong before. These are the types we see most across the county, with the design that usually fits each one.

Long Beach oceanfront co-ops and condos

Long Beach holds most of the county's tall residential buildings. Of the nine Nassau co-op developments of seven stories or more in a recent compilation of MLS records, eight are in Long Beach, with names residents will recognize: Long Beach Terrace and Neptune Towers at nine stories, Sea Pointe Towers at ten, and Lafayette Terrace, Oakwood House, Sherwood House, Surfside and Watersedge at seven. Most were built between 1965 and 1968. That tells us a lot. Controllers in these buildings have usually been modernized at least once, but the hoistways, machine rooms and traveling cables often date from the original construction or a modernization decades ago. Many have two cars in a bank. Residents skew older and many have lived in the building for years, while summer brings visitors, renters and guests who do not know the rules.

For these buildings we usually recommend Level 1 readers in every car of the bank, keyed to the same fob used at the front and boardwalk-side doors, with a code or video station option for residents who struggle with small fobs. Where the building has a garage or a beach-level storage floor, a landing reader at that call station often does more than anything else. And because these buildings sit on a barrier island that flooded badly in 2012, the location of the panel and its power supply is a design question in its own right, which we cover in the flood section below.

Prewar and postwar elevator co-ops on the North Shore

Great Neck Plaza, Great Neck and the surrounding villages have a different kind of elevator building: smaller, older, often closer to the train and often handsome. The Wychwood in Great Neck Plaza dates to 1929 and stands seven stories. Buildings like it have narrow hoistways, machine rooms on the roof, and traveling cables that may have been replaced once in their lives. Spare conductors cannot be assumed. Boards here tend to care deeply about how a reader looks in a paneled car, and about not disturbing residents with weeks of noisy work. A slim reader matched to the car finish, a careful survey of the cable with the elevator contractor, and a short, well-announced installation window are what these boards value.

New rentals across from the train in Mineola, Westbury and other downtowns

The fastest-growing category of elevator building in Nassau is the transit-oriented rental. Mineola has led the way: an eight-story, 119-apartment building planned across from its station; The Bridge at Mineola with 101 units across from the station; a 266-unit building at 199 2nd Street with underground parking; and a 106-unit condominium planned at 530 West Broadway in two buildings joined above a mezzanine. Westbury rewrote its downtown zoning in 2019, approved Alpine Residential's 187-unit building across from the station, and in 2026 approved the MTA's first transit-oriented development on Long Island on a former commuter lot. These buildings have multiple cars, garages, amenity floors, package rooms and a leasing office that wants everything managed from one screen.

For station rentals the right design is usually Level 3 from the start: readers in every car tied to the same platform that runs the entry doors, floor-by-floor control so each resident reaches their floor plus the shared floors, a landing reader at the garage, scheduled hours for the amenity floor, and intercom floor release so a guest who is buzzed in can ride only to the resident who let them in. If the building is still under construction, the single most valuable thing an owner can do is bring elevator access into the design before the elevator contractor finalizes the controller order, so the security inputs and traveling cable conductors are specified rather than improvised later.

Glen Cove's rebuilt waterfront

Glen Cove's Garvies Point redevelopment brings a mix of condominiums and rentals to the harbor, including plans for a large condominium building of nine residential stories over two stories of parking. Parking podiums are a recurring theme: the garage levels are where residents arrive, where guests and contractors park, and where an open gate turns into an open elevator. Readers in the cars plus a controlled garage landing are the core of the design, with the condominium board or the rental management deciding how far floor restriction should go.

Senior and supportive housing

Nassau has a steady stock of senior and supportive buildings, and they have their own needs. The Allan and Geraldine Rosenberg Residence in Freeport is five stories with 44 one-bedroom apartments and an apartment for an on-site manager. Estella Housing in the Village of Hempstead is a new five-story building with 95 affordable supportive apartments. The Homestead Senior Apartments run by the North Hempstead Housing Authority date to 1979, with elevator access to every level across 98 apartments. In buildings like these the priority is a system residents can use without thinking: large readers, credentials that work on a lanyard or wristband, codes for aides, and a clear record of who entered and when, without turning the elevator into an obstacle for people who depend on it.

Medical and professional buildings along Franklin Avenue and Old Country Road

The county seat area is Nassau's medical district. NYU Langone Hospital—Long Island, a 591-bed medical center, anchors Mineola, and Franklin Avenue in Garden City is lined with multi-suite medical buildings where practices occupy lower-level, mid-level and upper-level suites. We mention these as landmarks, not as customers. Buildings of this kind usually need floor-by-floor control with office-hour schedules, a way for cleaning crews to reach every floor at night without carrying a master fob all day, and a policy for patients who arrive after the front desk closes. The landlord, the practices and the cleaning contractor each need their own credential groups.

Office buildings and corporate parks

Nassau's office stock is mostly low-rise, but the buildings with elevators tend to be large: multi-tenant buildings in Garden City and Mineola near the courts and county offices, and office parks along the Northern State and Long Island Expressway corridors. Tenants often bring their own badge systems. The elevator design question is whether the building can read each tenant's badges, or whether every tenant needs a second credential just for the elevator. Where the readers support several card formats, we program the building's system to accept them; where they do not, a mobile credential is often the cleanest common denominator.

Mixed-use buildings in village downtowns

From Rockville Centre to Freeport to Great Neck, many downtowns have three- to five-story buildings with stores on the ground floor and apartments or offices above, sometimes served by one small elevator. The problem here is usually customers of the ground-floor business finding their way upstairs. A single Level 1 reader in the car, with the ground floor always open and everything above it credential-only, solves that for very little equipment.

Two Contractors

Who Does What: Our Low-Voltage Crew and Your Licensed Elevator Contractor in Nassau

The most common misunderstanding about elevator access control is who is allowed to do which part of it. In New York State, the business and the workers who install, maintain or repair elevators must be licensed under Article 33 of the Labor Law. Wiring a reader into a car, landing a relay output on a controller terminal and changing how the controller responds to floor calls are elevator work. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems holds New York State low-voltage license #12000287431 and does the access control side; we do not touch the elevator's controller, safety circuits, door operators or hoistway equipment, and we do not claim to.

In practice the two crews work as one job. We survey first, often with the elevator contractor's technician present or on the phone, and write a proposal that names both scopes. The building orders the elevator contractor's part directly from its maintenance company, because that company already holds the maintenance contract, knows the controller and carries the responsibility for the elevator's safe operation. We then schedule our installation days around the elevator technician's visits so the car is out of service as briefly as possible.

Approvals

Permits in a County of 64 Villages: Who Signs Off on Elevator Access Work in Nassau

Building owners new to Nassau are sometimes surprised that there is no single county building department to call. The county's own permit page says it plainly: building permits come from your city, town or village. Long Beach and Glen Cove issue their own. The towns of Hempstead, North Hempstead and Oyster Bay issue permits in their unincorporated areas. And most of the 64 incorporated villages, from Garden City and Mineola to Great Neck Plaza, Rockville Centre, Freeport and Westbury, run their own building departments with their own inspectors. Village building superintendents work under the New York State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code; the village code of Floral Park, for example, requires its superintendent of buildings to hold the qualifications of a code enforcement officer under the Uniform Code.

What does that mean for an elevator access project? Three things.

  • The elevator side follows the elevator code. New York State's codes reference ASME A17.1, the safety code for elevators, and alterations to elevator equipment are the licensed elevator contractor's responsibility, including any filing or inspection their work requires in your jurisdiction. We plan the schedule around that, not around it.
  • The low-voltage side is usually light. Readers, panels and low-voltage cable are often treated as minor work, but practice varies by municipality. Safety

Firefighters' Service, Storm Surge and Blackouts in Nassau Elevator Buildings

An elevator security system is judged on its worst day. A board does not lose sleep over a fob that works on a sunny Tuesday; it loses sleep over what the elevator will do during a fire, a hurricane or a summer blackout. These are the three scenarios we design for on every Nassau job.

Fire: the elevator stops listening to the access system

Modern elevators have firefighters' emergency operation. When a smoke detector in an elevator lobby or machine room trips, or when a firefighter turns the recall key in the lobby, the cars return to a designated floor and open their doors. That is Phase I. A firefighter can then take a car under manual control from inside, which is Phase II. The safety code for elevators requires that devices added to the car or the hall stations, such as card readers, must not interfere with that operation and must become inoperative when it begins. In plain terms, the moment the elevator enters fire service, our relays no longer matter. The elevator contractor wires the interface so that this happens automatically, and we test it with them before the system goes live. No Nassau building should accept an elevator access installation that has not had that test witnessed and written down.

Storm surge: put the brains of the system above the water

Nassau's south shore knows what salt water does to building systems. In the days after Hurricane Sandy in 2012, a property manager described Long Beach buildings that lost their ground floors, lobbies and heating plants; at one building on Neptune Boulevard the boiler room sat under fifteen feet of water, and at an oceanfront condominium the first floor, gym and lobby were destroyed. Elevator machine rooms on the roof generally survived; anything in a basement, cellar or ground-floor closet did not. We apply that lesson directly. In Long Beach, Oceanside and other flood-exposed parts of the south shore, we recommend placing the access control panel, its power supply and battery in the machine room or on an upper floor, never in a cellar, and running the lobby and garage reader cable so that a flooded ground floor means replacing readers rather than replacing the system. Where a garage reader must sit at a flood-prone landing, we choose a reader that can be swapped in minutes and keep a spare on site.

Power failure: decide the answer in advance

Every access panel we install has a battery, so short interruptions pass unnoticed. A long outage raises a policy question the board must answer before it happens: when the panel's battery runs down, should the controlled elevators fail open, meaning anyone can ride anywhere, or fail secure, meaning only staff with a key switch can operate them? In most residential buildings, and in any building where people depend on the elevator to get home, failing open is the safer choice for people even if it is the weaker choice for security. Buildings with an emergency generator that powers the elevators should also power the access panel from the same emergency circuit, or the elevators will run on the generator while the readers sit dark. We ask whether your building has a generator, what it carries and who tests it.

Manual override and accessibility

Every system we install has a way for staff to bypass the readers: a key switch in the car or the lobby, a button in the management software, or both. That override exists for move-in days, emergencies, inspections and the morning a resident's fob fails while they are holding groceries. We also design around residents with disabilities. Readers are mounted where a seated rider can reach them, credentials can be wristbands or larger cards, and a resident who cannot tap at all can be given a code or a phone credential instead.

Privacy

Privacy Outside the City Line: Fob Logs, Biometrics and Staff Fingerprints in Nassau

An elevator access system keeps a record of every ride: which credential, which car, which floor and when. That audit trail is one of its main benefits, because it lets a manager answer real questions, such as whether a former tenant is still getting in, or whether the roof door alarm at midnight lines up with someone riding up. It is also personal information, and a Nassau board or landlord should treat it that way.

As noted above, New York City's tenant data privacy law does not reach Nassau. New York State has, at the time of writing, no general biometric privacy statute in force for residential buildings. A statewide Biometric Identifier Privacy Act has been introduced in the State Senate as S1422A, but it is a bill, not a law, and we do not describe it as anything else. There is one state rule that does apply today, and it matters for staff rather than residents: New York Labor Law section 201-a bars employers from requiring employees to be fingerprinted as a condition of employment. A management company or building that wants staff to use fingerprint readers in the elevator should make enrollment voluntary and offer a card or fob alternative. This is general information, not legal advice; your building's attorney should review any biometric program.

Beyond the legal minimum, these are the practices we recommend for every Nassau elevator system, whether or not anyone requires them:

  • Tell residents and tenants in writing what the system records, who can see it and how long it is kept.
  • Keep logs only as long as you need them for security, and delete the records of people who have moved out.
  • Give each staff member their own administrator login so changes can be traced, and remove logins when people leave.
  • For face or fingerprint readers, store templates rather than photos where the platform allows it, and offer a non-biometric option to anyone who asks.
  • Never use elevator logs to monitor residents for reasons unrelated to building security.
Credentials

Fobs, Cards, Codes, Phones and Faces: Choosing Credentials for Nassau Riders

The credential is the part of the system people touch every day, so it deserves more thought than the brand of the panel. The right choice depends on who rides your elevator.

  • Key fobs. Small, durable, cheap to replace and familiar. Most Nassau co-ops start here, often using the same fob already issued for the front door. Their weakness is that they are easy to lend and easy to copy if the building uses an old, unencrypted format.
  • Cards. Better for offices and medical buildings, where staff already carry badges and photos can be printed on them. Many readers accept several card formats, which matters in multi-tenant buildings.
  • Codes. A keypad or combination reader lets a resident who forgot a fob still ride, and lets a home aide or a contractor use a temporary code that expires. Codes can be shared, so they work best alongside fobs rather than instead of them.
  • Phones. Elevator access control with smartphone credentials is increasingly popular in station rentals, where residents expect to manage everything in an app and management wants to issue and revoke access without handing out plastic. Phones run out of battery, so we keep a fob or code as a backup.
  • Video stations. A video keypad station in the car or at the garage landing can combine a code, a card reader and a camera, and some can call the management office. They suit buildings that want a person to be able to help a rider remotely.
  • Biometrics. Face, fingerprint and iris readers end the problem of lent and lost fobs, and they suit buildings with high turnover or strict staff areas. They cost more, they carry the privacy considerations described above, and they need good lighting and placement to work well in a car.

If your building already uses fobs or cards at the entry doors, the first question we answer at the survey is whether the elevator readers can read the same ones. If they can, residents carry one credential for everything. If the existing format is old and insecure, a building may use the elevator project as the moment to re-badge everyone onto a modern encrypted credential, which we can phase in over a few weeks so nobody is locked out.

People

Visitors, Home Aides, Package Carriers and Garage Arrivals in Nassau Buildings

Residents are the easy part. Every elevator system succeeds or fails on how it treats everyone else, and Nassau buildings have a particular mix of non-residents who need a rule before the system goes live.

  • Guests who drive in. In much of Nassau, visitors arrive by car and walk in from a lot or a garage rather than through the front lobby. If the intercom is at the front door and the guest is in the garage, someone has to decide how they get up. The usual answers are a garage intercom that can release the elevator to the resident's floor, or a guest code the resident issues for the visit.
  • Home health aides and caregivers. Many older Nassau buildings have residents who rely on daily visits from aides. A schedule-limited code or credential, issued by the resident or the office and expiring automatically, keeps them moving without handing out permanent fobs.
  • Package and food deliveries. Most buildings now want carriers to reach the package room and no further. A delivery code that works only for the lobby or the package-room floor, or a package room on the ground floor so carriers never need the elevator, is the cleanest design.
  • Summer and seasonal visitors. Oceanfront buildings in Long Beach and along the south shore see guests and seasonal renters who stay a week or a month. Time-limited credentials that end on the checkout date are far better than fobs that walk away.
  • Contractors and movers. A move or a renovation can need the elevator for days. A credential tied to one floor and one week, plus the key switch override for the moving pads, keeps the job going without leaving the system wide open.
  • Emergency responders. Police and EMS need a way in that does not depend on someone finding a fob. The fire service override covers firefighters; for other responders most buildings use a lock box or an arrangement with the local police and fire department, which the board should confirm.

Each of these rules is written into the programming worksheet with a name next to it, so the super, the managing agent and the board all know who issues guest codes, who can extend a contractor's access and who gets the phone call when something does not work.

Myths

Common Misconceptions About Elevator Security in Nassau County

Search results and AI summaries about elevator access control are mostly written for Manhattan office towers. Applied to Nassau buildings, several of the most repeated claims are wrong or incomplete.

  • "Elevator access control means replacing the elevator." It does not. The elevator keeps its motor, controller, doors and safety systems. Our equipment is added beside it and connected through a small number of inputs.
  • "Any security company can wire a reader into the car." Not in New York State. The connection inside the elevator controller is elevator work under Article 33 of the Labor Law and belongs to a licensed elevator contractor. A security company that offers to do it alone is offering to do something it should not.
  • "The readers will trap people during a fire." A correctly installed system is bypassed the instant the elevator enters firefighters' service, which is a requirement of the elevator safety code. That is why the fire service test is part of every handover.
  • "New York City's tenant privacy law applies to every New York building." Local Law 63 is a New York City law. Nassau buildings are not covered by it, though sensible privacy practices are still worth following.
  • "Floor restriction is always better." Floor restriction is stronger, but for many Nassau co-ops a car reader alone solves the actual problem with a fraction of the equipment and inconvenience.
  • "One permit covers the county." Nassau has two cities, three towns and dozens of villages with their own building departments. The right office depends on the address.
  • "Phones have made fobs obsolete." Phones are convenient, but residents' batteries die and some residents do not want an app. Most buildings run phones and fobs side by side.
