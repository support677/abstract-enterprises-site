The Neighborhood

Why Cobble Hill Homes Need Sonos Installation

Cobble Hill is one of the smallest neighborhoods in Brooklyn, and almost all of it is protected. Wikipedia puts the Cobble Hill Historic District at about 60 acres, roughly bounded by Atlantic Avenue, Court Street, Degraw Street and Hicks Street, and the National Register listing counts 796 contributing buildings put up between the 1830s and the 1920s. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the district in December 1969 and extended it in 1988. The Cobble Hill Association, whose research supported the designation, credits the landmark law together with low-scale zoning — "including a 50-foot height limitation" — for keeping "the nineteenth-century scale, feeling, and quality of the buildings and streets." For anyone planning music in a house here, scale is the word that matters.

The houses are Greek Revival from the 1830s and 1840s, then Gothic Revival, Italianate and early Romanesque Revival from the 1850s and 1860s, mostly brick and brownstone, built singly or in short rows rather than whole blocks at a time. The Association's history dates the oldest surviving house in the district, 122 Pacific Street, to 1832. Widths vary more than people expect. A Kane Street Greek Revival offered for sale in 2019 was described by Brownstoner as 25 feet wide with 11-foot ceilings, an "elaborate foliate cornice," six mantels, a pier mirror and scenic wallpaper in the dining room. Around the corner, the cottages Alfred Tredway White built in 1878–1879 along what is now Warren Place are two storeys high, 11½ feet wide and 32 feet deep. The same Sonos shopping list cannot be right for both.

That is the problem we are usually called to solve. Sonos makes it easy to keep buying: another Era for the kitchen, a Sub because the soundbar seemed thin, a pair of rears because the box suggested it. In a small Cobble Hill room the result is often too much bass for a plaster ceiling and a party wall, rear speakers with nowhere to stand, and a network straining under products that never needed to be on it. A good installation here frequently means fewer products, chosen and placed carefully, with Trueplay doing the work an extra speaker was meant to do.

The second thing that shapes the job is how close everybody lives. Party walls are shared on both sides of almost every rowhouse. The Tower and Home Buildings on Hicks Street — now Cobble Hill Towers — were designed around internal courtyards, with exterior staircases and open balconies, so neighbours look across at one another. On the western edge the Brooklyn–Queens Expressway runs in its trench below Hicks Street; one Reddit user who had lived on Hicks in Cobble Hill wrote that they "found the consistent traffic sounds soothing," while others in the same thread recommended air purifiers. Inside the quiet streets, though, sound carries. When the railings on a new 15-storey condominium at 347 Henry Street started whistling in the wind in 2021, Brownstoner reported residents comparing it to "fingernails on a blackboard" and saying "it wakes us up." A neighbourhood that notices a railing will notice a Sub.

The third thing is that Cobble Hill is no longer only rowhouses. The former Long Island College Hospital campus has become River Park, which the Brooklyn Heights Association describes as a renovated Polhemus Building, eight new townhouses on Amity Street and five new residential towers, the tallest of them 36 storeys at 2 River Park. Minard Lafever's 1851–52 Strong Place Baptist Church became 23 condominiums in 2010, according to CityRealty. So a Cobble Hill Sonos visit can mean an 1840s plaster ceiling in the morning, a concrete slab in a brand-new tower after lunch and a vaulted former sanctuary the next day. We write the plan for the building you actually live in, and this page explains how each one changes it.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Cobble Hill's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up almost every Cobble Hill call. They differ in width, ceiling, wall and who else has a say, and each one changes the Sonos plan in a specific way.

Greek Revival Rowhouses

The district's oldest stock, from the 1830s and 1840s: flat brick fronts, simple cornices, high parlor ceilings and double parlors divided by an arch or doors. Ceiling speakers are possible on the upper floors; in the parlor, original plaster detail usually argues for free-standing or wall-mounted Sonos instead.

Italianate and Brownstone Rows

The 1850s and 1860s houses with brownstone fronts, deeper cornices and heavier interior mouldings. Floors are often split between owner and tenant, which turns one Sonos system into two and makes the network plan the first conversation.

Warren Place Cottages

Thirty-odd two-storey houses, 11½ feet wide, facing each other across a shared garden walk between Warren and Baltic Streets. Rooms are small, walls are close, and the right system is usually two or three speakers for the whole house.

Cobble Hill Towers

Alfred Tredway White's six-storey Tower and Home Buildings on Hicks Street, 188 apartments around two courtyards, being converted to condominium ownership under a non-eviction plan. Ceilings are 8 feet 3 inches, walls are masonry, and the neighbours are across the yard as well as through the wall.

Church and Institutional Conversions

Homes carved out of buildings that were never meant to be homes: the 23 condominiums inside the former Strong Place Baptist Church, and the renovated Polhemus Building at River Park. Expect arched windows, exposed structure, tall volumes and a condominium board.

River Park Towers and Townhouses

New construction on the former hospital site: concrete floors, steel-stud partitions, developer-installed data wiring and large windows. Here the wiring is easier and the questions are about the building's rules and the room's glass.

Two methods matter more in Cobble Hill than in most of the borough. The first is how we approach old plaster. Before any ceiling cut, we locate joists and services by small pilot holes and probing, not by a scanner alone, because century-old lath confuses stud finders. The Sonos in-ceiling manual tells installers to perform an obstruction survey and to probe through a pilot hole when unsure, and that is exactly what we do. A cut is made slowly with the template, the edge supported so the plaster doesn't let go of the lath, and dust is vacuumed at the blade. We tell you before we start that old plaster can crack despite care, and we cut nothing until you say yes.

The second is how we approach a party wall. Brick party walls carry sound well and wiring badly. We avoid recessing speakers into them, avoid mounting a Sub against them, and route cable along the inside face rather than chasing into the masonry. If a soundbar has to hang on brick, it goes up on masonry anchors with a controlled drill depth, and we tell you which line on the price list that adds before we begin.

Rowhouse owners who are opening a floor for Sonos often add a camera or an intercom handset while the cable route is open. Our Cobble Hill intercom installers and AESS security cameras in Cobble Hill work from the same office and can share the visit.

Sized to the House

How Many Sonos Speakers a Cobble Hill House Really Needs

Most Sonos plans start from a wish list. Ours start from a tape measure. A room's width, its ceiling height, what is on the far side of each wall and how the household actually moves between rooms decide how many speakers it can use. In Cobble Hill, where rooms are often narrower and taller than in newer housing, the honest count is frequently lower than the one on the shopping list — and the system sounds better for it.

Sound in a small room behaves differently from sound in a big one. Bass builds up between close, parallel walls, so a Sub that is pleasant in a 20-foot-wide family room can boom in an 11½-foot cottage parlor and travel straight through the party wall. Treble reflects quickly off plaster and glass, so a second speaker a few feet from the first can blur the sound rather than widen it. Trueplay, which Sonos says "measures how sound reflects off walls, furnishings and other surfaces," corrects much of this for a supported product in a given position, but it cannot make a room larger. The fix is choosing the right products and putting them in the right places.

Why fewer products often means a steadier system

Every Sonos product on a network is something else that has to find the router, stay grouped and survive the next app update. Sonos's own system requirements cap a household at 32 products and list wireless range extenders, Ethernet-over-power adapters and guest networks among the setups it does not support. In a narrow house the more common failure is simpler: five or six speakers competing for the same crowded 2.4 GHz air that every neighbour's router is also using through the party walls. Sonos's interference guidance rates plaster as a high obstruction and asks for at least 18 inches between a Sonos product and other electronics. Fewer, better-placed products, with one of them wired, removes most of that strain.

Commercial Sonos

Business Sonos Installation in Cobble Hill

Cobble Hill's shops and restaurants sit along three streets and on the ground floors of buildings where people live upstairs. That arrangement shapes every business system we install here: the music has to make the room feel right without reaching the apartment above or spilling onto the sidewalk.

Atlantic Avenue

The neighbourhood's northern edge and its oldest shopping street. The Atlantic Avenue Business Improvement District represents more than 300 businesses from Fourth Avenue to the waterfront across Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill and Cobble Hill, and the Historic Districts Council notes the Middle Eastern shops and restaurants concentrated between Court and Clinton Streets.

Court Street

The eastern boundary of the historic district, lined with restaurants, bakeries, services and the Cobble Hill Cinemas at 265 Court Street, which opened in 1926 as the Lido and was split into more screens from 1982 onward. The Cobble Hill Association has worked with the Court Street Merchants Association on neighbourhood projects.

Smith Street

A block east of Court, a run of independent boutiques, bars and restaurants that NYC Tourism describes, with Court Street, as lined with "indie boutiques and locally owned specialty" shops. Narrow storefronts, deep rooms and back gardens are the norm.

Henry and Clinton Corners

Small ground-floor businesses on residential blocks: cafés, studios and offices under apartments, where the 42 dBA limit for commercial music measured inside a home is the number that decides the volume.

Professional Offices

Therapists, architects, dentists and small firms in converted rowhouse floors who want quiet background sound in a waiting room and nothing at all leaking into the treatment or meeting rooms.

River Park Ground Floors

Commercial and medical space in the new buildings on the former hospital site, with modern ceilings that take in-ceiling speakers easily and building managers who want paperwork first.

Compliance notes for business music in Cobble Hill

  • The apartment upstairs sets the limit. The city's noise code fact sheet states that commercial music in a store, restaurant or bar "shall not exceed 42 dBA as measured in any dwelling unit receiving the noise." In a building with homes above, we set Sonos volume limits per room and check the result with you before we leave.
  • No speakers aimed at the street. New York City Administrative Code §24-244(b) prohibits using a sound reproduction device for commercial advertising or to attract attention outside, or through an opening onto a public street. We mount and angle shop speakers into the room.
  • Licensed music. A personal streaming account is meant for personal use; businesses should use a service licensed for commercial playback. We set up whichever service you choose on the Sonos system but do not sell music licences.
  • Landmarked storefronts. Most of the shopping streets lie inside or beside the historic district. Speakers and wiring inside the shop do not change the facade; anything that would be visible on the exterior is a question for the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and we leave the facade alone.
  • Line voltage is not our trade. A new outlet for an Amp shelf, a dedicated circuit or a change to the panel goes to a licensed electrician; we coordinate the location with them.

Music that suits a small shop

A narrow storefront 15 or 20 feet wide rarely needs more than a pair of ceiling speakers or two Era speakers on brackets, plus perhaps one in the back room. What it does need is consistency: the same level every morning, no one turning it up at closing because the room emptied out, and a playlist that starts and stops with the shop. Sonos alarms can start music at a set time, room and volume; volume limits stop the level creeping up. We set both and show your staff the two things they are allowed to change.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Cobble Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions Cobble Hill households ask that are particular to the houses here. Broader Sonos questions — dropouts, building Wi-Fi, board approvals, outdoor speakers — are answered on our Brooklyn Sonos page and the New York City Sonos hub.

Narrow houses, small rooms

What is the smallest set of Sonos products that covers a two-storey Warren Place cottage?

Usually three: a soundbar on the TV downstairs, one fixed speaker upstairs, and a portable that moves between the two and out to the garden walk. A Warren Place cottage is 11½ feet wide and 32 feet deep over two storeys, so each floor is effectively one or two rooms joined by open doorways or a short stair. A Beam or Ray covers the living space below, an Era 100 covers a bedroom above, and the portable fills whatever gap the household actually notices — the kitchen while cooking, the bathroom in the morning, the garden in summer.

That set also keeps the network simple: three products, one of them near the router, in a house small enough that Wi-Fi reaches every corner. If a fourth product is ever added, it should be because the portable keeps ending up in the same room every day, which tells you that room wants a speaker of its own.

Are rear surround speakers worth it in a living room that narrow?

Often not. Rear speakers need somewhere to stand behind or beside the seating, a little way from the side walls, and in a narrow room the sofa is usually against the back wall with the door at one end. Squeezed into the corners behind the sofa, rears end up so close to the listener's ears that they draw attention to themselves rather than widening the sound. A soundbar alone, set properly and tuned with Trueplay, usually gives a better result in a parlor under 12 feet wide.

If you watch a lot of films and the room has space at the sides of the seating, Era 100s on slim stands or wall brackets can work well. We try both before you buy: we can set the soundbar up first, and if you still want surround after a week, the rears are added on a short setup-only visit.

Can one portable Sonos follow us between the cottage's two floors and the garden instead of buying three speakers?

Yes, and in a small house that is often the best value. A Roam 2 or Move 2 joins the home Sonos system over Wi-Fi like any fixed speaker, so it can be grouped with the soundbar downstairs, carried upstairs at bedtime and taken out to the garden walk in summer. The Move 2 is rated IP56 and its charging base is for indoor use; the Roam 2 is smaller and lighter and rated IP67.

The trade-off is that a portable only plays where it is. If you want music upstairs and downstairs at once, or a speaker that is always in the kitchen, a second fixed product makes sense. We usually suggest starting with the soundbar and one portable, and adding a fixed speaker in the one room where you find yourself carrying the portable every day.

Original parlors and old fittings

We still burn wood in the parlor fireplace — is the mantel shelf a safe place for Sonos speakers?

Not while the fire is lit, and preferably not at all. A working fireplace sends heat up the face of the chimney breast and across the mantel shelf, and fine soot and ash settle there too. Sonos gives an operating range of 32–104°F for products such as the Amp — well below what a mantel can reach with a fire burning — and its guidance for products such as the Port is to keep them away from radiators and other heat sources and leave space around them for ventilation. A speaker cooking on a mantel won't fail at once; it will age faster and its plastics and grille will discolour.

The better spots in a Cobble Hill double parlor are usually the built-in shelving beside the chimney breast, a bookcase between the windows, or slim stands at the sides of the room. If the mantel is the only place that works visually, we keep the speaker toward the end of the shelf, away from the firebox, and you move it on the evenings you light a fire. Many households here use the parlor fireplace a handful of times a winter, and that arrangement works.

Will an Era 100 tucked into a bookcase shelf sound muffled?

It can, if the shelf is deep and the speaker is pushed to the back. A speaker inside a cavity excites the box around it, so the bass gets heavier and boomier and the detail softens. The fix is placement within the shelf: bring the speaker forward so its front is flush with the shelf edge, leave a few inches of air on each side, and keep books from leaning against it. Then run Trueplay with the speaker in that exact position, because the tuning measures the shelf as part of the room.

Built-in parlor bookcases in Cobble Hill rowhouses are often shallow enough to work well; deep, enclosed cabinets are less forgiving. We test the sound in the shelf you have in mind before deciding, and if it can't be made to sound right we say so and suggest the next spot.

Our dining room has hand-painted scenic wallpaper — how does music reach it without a single hole in the paper?

By keeping everything wireless and free-standing in that room. Scenic paper is irreplaceable in a way plaster is not; even a carefully patched hole shows. For a dining room like the one described in the 2019 Brownstoner listing on Kane Street, we use a free-standing Era 100 or a pair on a sideboard or in a corner, powered from an existing outlet, with the cord run along the baseboard behind furniture. Nothing is fixed to the papered walls, and nothing is cut into the ceiling directly above them.

If you want hidden sound, the ceiling may still be an option where the paper stops short of it, but we treat that as a separate decision with its own risk discussion. In most houses the free-standing answer sounds good enough that the question never comes up again.

The parlor still has its original two-prong outlets — can Sonos products plug into them?

This is a question for an electrician before it is one for us. Many older Cobble Hill houses still have ungrounded two-prong receptacles in some rooms. We don't alter line-voltage wiring, and we won't use three-to-two "cheater" adapters to force a grounded plug into an ungrounded outlet. If the products you have chosen need a grounded outlet where there isn't one, we mark the exact positions so a licensed electrician can install suitable receptacles, and we come back to finish once they are in.

It is worth asking anyway, because the answer helps the whole house: if the parlor still has original outlets, the electrician's visit is a good moment to check them. We often schedule the Sonos work for the week after so nothing waits on anyone.

Apartments, courtyards and new buildings

With 8-foot-3 ceilings in our Cobble Hill Towers apartment, will in-ceiling speakers feel too close to our heads?

They can. At that height a seated listener's ears are only four or five feet from the ceiling, so a speaker directly overhead sounds like it is in the room with you rather than filling it, and it is obvious where it is. The Cobble Hill Association's history of the Tower Buildings records ceiling heights of 8 feet 3 inches as built, which is low by rowhouse standards. Ceiling speakers in rooms like these work best when they are kept well away from the seating, toward the walls, and in pairs rather than singly.

There is also the question of what the building allows above your ceiling and how much of the masonry structure is in the way. For most Towers apartments we recommend free-standing or wall-mounted Sonos instead: a soundbar in the sitting room, an Era 100 in the bedroom, both on volume limits suited to the courtyard. It sounds better at that ceiling height and leaves nothing to patch.

The only wall for the TV in our condo is bare brick — will the soundbar sound harsh bouncing off it?

Bare brick is a hard, reflective surface, but in practice the harshness people hear usually comes from the whole room — brick on one side, large windows on another, hard floors and little soft furnishing — rather than the wall behind the bar. The Arc Ultra detects when it is wall-mounted and adjusts its EQ for the wall, and Trueplay measures the room as it is. Between them, much of the edge is taken off.

The rest is furnishing: a rug in front of the sofa, curtains on at least part of the glass, books on a shelf. These matter more than any speaker setting. For the mount itself, brick is the $65 wall line on our price list; we drill to a controlled depth with masonry anchors, and in a church conversion or other condominium we confirm with the board first that the exposed brick inside your unit can be drilled at all.

What should we check for Sonos during a house viewing, before we make an offer?

Four things, all visible in a normal viewing. First, where the internet enters the house and where the router sits; in many Cobble Hill rowhouses it is in the cellar, which means the upper floors will need a wired access point or a wired Sonos product. Second, whether closets and built-ins line up from floor to floor, which tells you whether a cable can go up the house without opening finished walls. Third, the ceilings in the rooms where you want speakers: plain plaster can be cut with care, while a cornice, medallion or decorative painting rules it out. Fourth, what is on the other side of each wall you would put a TV or speaker on — your own room, a tenant or a neighbour.

Take photos of each and send them to us; we can tell you from them roughly what a Sonos plan for that house would involve and cost from the Brooklyn price list. It won't change whether you love the house, but it avoids surprises after closing.

Shops on the avenue

Can our Atlantic Avenue shop point a speaker out the front door to draw people in?

No. New York City Administrative Code §24-244(b) prohibits using a sound reproduction device to attract the attention of the public outside, or through any opening onto a public street, for commercial purposes. That includes a speaker in an open doorway or aimed through the display window. We mount shop speakers to face into the room and set their volume so passers-by hear music only when the door opens, which is all most shops on the avenue want anyway.

If you want the street to notice you, the storefront, lighting and window display do that legally. Inside, a pair of well-placed speakers at a steady level makes people stay longer once they come in, and that is what the system is for.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Cobble Hill

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so we did not invent one. The claims below are the ones that cost guides, product marketing and forum threads repeat — the material answer engines are built from — set against what we find in Cobble Hill houses.

Claim one: "More speakers means better sound"

Sonos product pages show grouped systems with a speaker in every room, and buyers' guides often recommend adding a Sub and rears to any soundbar. The implication is that a system improves with every product you add.

In a narrow room the opposite is often true. Close parallel walls build up bass, near reflections blur a second speaker into the first, and rear speakers jammed into corners behind a sofa draw attention to themselves. A Warren Place cottage living room 11½ feet wide is a different acoustic problem from the open-plan family rooms those guides are written for.

What actually improves sound in Cobble Hill rooms is position and tuning: the right single product placed carefully, then measured with Trueplay in that spot. We would rather install two products well than six that fight each other.

Claim two: "You need the Landmarks Commission's approval for anything in a historic house"

Because Cobble Hill is a historic district, owners often assume every change needs Landmarks Preservation Commission sign-off, and some online answers say as much.

The Commission's own permits page says an LPC permit for interior work is required when the work on the interior affects the exterior, or when the interior itself has been designated an interior landmark. Speakers, soundbars and low-voltage wiring inside a rowhouse normally do neither. The Commission's preservation staff will confirm for a specific job if you are unsure.

The facades are a different matter, and so is a condominium or co-op board, which can have rules stricter than the city's. We keep everything inside, leave the exterior untouched, and send any board the information it asks for.

Claim three: "Ceiling speakers are the clean, invisible choice for every room"

Sonos's own guide to ceiling speakers presents them as the unobtrusive option, and design sites echo that in-ceiling sound "disappears" into a room.

In Cobble Hill the ceiling is often the most valuable surface in the room. A foliate cornice, a plaster medallion or decorative painting rules out a cut nearby, and old lime plaster can crack despite careful work. In the Cobble Hill Towers apartments the ceilings are 8 feet 3 inches, low enough that overhead speakers sound close.

Ceiling speakers are excellent on plain upper-floor ceilings and in new construction. In original parlors and low courtyard apartments we usually recommend free-standing or wall-mounted products instead.

Claim four: "Professional Sonos installation costs about $500 to $1,000 per room"

National cost guides quote whole-home audio by the room, and figures in that range are widely repeated. One Reddit user wrote that a local company quoted "about $500 (all-in)" for a complete Sonos setup.

A Cobble Hill job priced per room is usually overpriced, because many of the rooms need nothing fixed at all. Setting up Sonos you already own is $125 on our Brooklyn price list; hanging a soundbar is $129 plus the wall line; ceiling speakers on an Amp are quoted after a walkthrough because the plaster and the route decide the labour.

Ask for itemised lines. If a quote can't tell you what each room costs and why, it is guessing.

Claim five: "A mesh Wi-Fi kit fixes Sonos in any old house"

Mesh kits are sold as the answer to any dead spot, and many forum replies suggest one for Sonos dropouts.

Sonos says that in a mesh network with a wired Sonos setup, the Sonos product must be wired to the primary node, and it lists wireless range extenders and Ethernet-over-power among unsupported setups. Its interference guidance rates plaster as a high obstruction. A rowhouse with the router in the cellar and four plastered floors above is close to the worst case for a mesh that relies on wireless backhaul.

In a cottage or a single-floor apartment, a good router alone is often enough. In a four-storey rowhouse, one cable up the house to an access point or a wired Sonos product does more than any wireless kit.

Claim six: "A bigger soundbar is always the upgrade"

Reviews rank soundbars by size and channel count, and the flagship naturally comes out on top.

The Arc Ultra is a little over 46 inches long. Many Cobble Hill TV walls sit between two original windows or beside a chimney breast, and the screen that fits there is often 55 inches or smaller. A bar wider than the TV looks wrong and can block the window trim. The Beam, at about 25½ inches, is frequently the better match.

We measure the wall and the TV first, then recommend the bar. The flagship is the right choice for a wide parlor or a River Park living room, not by default.

Claim seven: "Sonos speakers sound the same wherever you put them"

Marketing for smart speakers stresses automatic tuning, which can suggest placement no longer matters.

Sonos's Trueplay measures how sound reflects off walls, furnishings and other surfaces and adjusts for the room, and Sonos advises re-tuning after a speaker is moved. That is the point: the tuning is for one position. A speaker pushed into a bookcase cavity, pinned in a corner or perched on a warm mantel needs a better spot, not just a re-run of Trueplay.

We place first, tune second, and show you how to re-run Trueplay yourself if you move things around later.

Quiet Blocks

Keeping the Music on Your Side of the Wall

Cobble Hill's streets are quiet, and the people who live on them notice sound. That is not a reason to avoid a good system; it is a reason to set one up with the neighbours in mind from the start. Almost every complaint we hear about home audio in this neighbourhood comes from the same few causes, and each has a practical fix.

Bass is what travels

Voices and treble are stopped well by brick and plaster. Low frequencies are not: they move the structure itself, so a Sub on a timber floor or against a party wall is heard as a hum two floors away even when the music is at a sensible level in the room. In a rowhouse split between owner and tenant, or a Towers apartment with neighbours on four sides, we place a Sub away from shared walls, off the floor on a dense pad where the floor below is someone else's, and set its level by ear with you in the room. Often the right answer is no Sub at all; a Beam or Arc Ultra set properly produces enough low end for a small room without one.

Courtyards and garden blocks carry sound sideways

The Tower and Home Buildings were built around courtyards so that every apartment had light and air, and the rowhouse blocks enclose a ring of back gardens. Both arrangements reflect sound between facing walls. On a summer evening with the windows open, music from one apartment can be clearer across the yard than in the next room. We set volume limits on rooms whose windows face a courtyard or the gardens, and show you how to raise them when the windows are shut and lower them again in summer.

The BQE side of the neighbourhood

Households along Hicks Street live with the constant sound of the expressway in its trench; some residents on Reddit describe it as soothing, others as something to filter. Either way it raises the background level in rooms facing west, and people tend to turn the music up to match. The result is a room that is comfortable for you and loud for the neighbour on the quiet side. We set a volume limit that suits the quieter rooms and tune the west-facing ones so speech stays clear without extra level.

Quiet hours, used on purpose

Sonos lets you limit the maximum volume for each product in the app, and the soundbars have night and speech settings that keep dialogue clear while holding back sudden peaks. We set these up as part of every Cobble Hill handover, explain what each does, and leave the controls with you. The city's noise rules, the managing agent's house rules and your own neighbours all point the same way: a level that is right in the room, and quiet beyond it.

Floor by Floor

A Cobble Hill Rowhouse, Floor by Floor

Not every house here follows the same plan, but most four-storey rowhouses in the district share a pattern, and each level asks something different of a Sonos system.

The cellar

Where the internet usually arrives and the router often sits. Plaster and brick above it weaken Wi-Fi floor by floor, so the cellar is where the network plan starts: a good router, and either a wired Sonos product on the floor above or a cable up the house to an access point. An Amp for upper-floor ceiling speakers can live here if the cable route allows, on a ventilated shelf away from the boiler.

The garden floor

Often the kitchen and family room, sometimes a separate rental apartment. Ceilings are lower than the parlor's, surfaces are hard, and the back windows face the gardens. One Era 100 usually covers a kitchen; a family room with a TV takes a Beam or Ray. If this floor is a tenant's home, it is a separate system on a separate network, and the parlor Sub, if any, moves away from the floor above it.

The parlor floor

The tallest ceilings and the most original detail: cornices, a medallion perhaps, mantels, pier mirrors, pocket doors or an arch between front and back rooms. Free-standing and wall-mounted products are the rule here. The TV and soundbar go on whichever wall the household agrees, clear of the mantel and the mirror; a pair of Era speakers or the bookcases beside the chimney breast handle music. Ceiling cuts in the parlor are rare and always discussed first.

The upper floors

Bedrooms, a study, sometimes a top-floor rental. Ceilings are plainer and lower, and this is where in-ceiling speakers on an Amp most often make sense if you want them. Otherwise an Era 100 per bedroom, with alarms and volume limits set per room, is enough. Wi-Fi is weakest here in a house with the router in the cellar, which is why the network plan comes first.

FAQ

Cobble Hill Sonos Installation FAQ

Can an Era 100 go high on the wall of a child's bedroom, out of reach of small hands?
+

Yes. Sonos sells a Sanus wall mount made for the Era 100, and fixed high on the wall it keeps the speaker's buttons and cable away from toddlers. In an old rowhouse bedroom we fix the mount into a stud or use anchors chosen for the plaster, run the power lead down in a slim painted channel so there is nothing to pull, and set a volume limit for the room in the app. The mount and fixing are itemised in your written quote before the visit.

If we rearrange the parlor furniture later, do we need you back, or can we re-tune the speakers ourselves?
+

You can usually do it yourselves. Sonos advises re-running Trueplay after a speaker is moved, and on supported products it takes a few minutes per room from the app; most products need an iPhone or iPad for the full tuning, while some newer ones offer a quick tuning on Android too. At handover we show you where the setting is and walk through it once. If a move means taking a soundbar off one wall and hanging it on another, that is a new mount and we come back for it.

We're staging the house for sale. Can a Sonos setup go in for showings and leave with the furniture?
+

Yes. For a staged house we keep everything free-standing: the soundbar on the console at the $79 placement line, Era speakers on shelves or stands, and the system set up for $125 so the agent can play music during viewings from a phone. Nothing is fixed to walls, so the rooms photograph as the buyers will inherit them, and the whole setup leaves with your furniture. If the buyers want it, it can also stay.

Do we have to tell the neighbours before you drill into the party wall?
+

We recommend it, as a courtesy. Drilling for a soundbar bracket takes a few minutes and is loud on both sides of a brick wall, and the people next door will hear it clearly. A note the day before with the time of the visit avoids surprise. If your building has house rules about working hours or notice, or a condominium board that expects to be told, follow those; we can provide a certificate of insurance free of charge if anyone asks for one.

Will the power have to be shut off in the house while you work?
+

No. Sonos installation is low-voltage work: speaker wire, network cable and products that plug into existing outlets. We do not open electrical panels or switch off circuits. The only time power becomes part of the job is when a new outlet is needed for an Amp, a soundbar or an in-wall power kit, and that is done by a licensed electrician, either before our visit or on a schedule we agree with them.

Can the rooms in the Sonos app be named the way we talk about the house, like front parlor or garden floor?
+

Yes, and we do it as part of setup. Room names in the Sonos app are free text, so a rowhouse can have Front Parlor, Back Parlor, Garden Kitchen and Top Floor rather than Living Room 1 and 2. Clear names make grouping easier for everyone in the house, and for voice control if you use it. We agree the names with you before we start so the list in the app matches the way the household already speaks.
