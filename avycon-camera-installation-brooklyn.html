The Borough

Why Brooklyn Owners Pick a Wired AVYCON System

Most people who call us about cameras in Brooklyn have already tried something else. A doorbell camera that misses half the people on the stoop because the router is three floors up behind two brick party walls. A pair of battery cameras in the yard that needed charging every few weeks and went dark in the first cold snap. A cheap kit from a big-box store with a recorder that nobody can log into since the person who set it up moved out. The reason they end up asking about AVYCON is simple: they want a system that records all the time, keeps the footage in the house, and does not depend on a Wi-Fi signal getting through a wall that was built to hold up the house next door.

That is exactly what an AVYCON recorder and wired cameras do. On the IP side, each camera gets one Cat6 cable. That single cable carries power and video, so there is no outlet needed at the camera and nothing to recharge. The other end lands on the recorder, which writes everything to a hard drive in your cellar, your hall closet or your back office. When the drive fills, AVYCON's own documentation says the system automatically overwrites the oldest video, so it never simply stops. You watch it from your phone through the AVYCON ONE app, from a computer with AVYCON's CMSONE software, or from a monitor plugged straight into the recorder, which is how a lot of Brooklyn shop owners keep an eye on the floor from behind the counter.

The brick problem is not something we made up to sell cable. On Brownstoner's long-running forum, the owner of a four-story brownstone asked whether to go wired or wireless, and the experienced answer was that even the best wireless systems are spotty in older brick buildings. The same thread recommended either PoE or the older coax-and-power approach, warned against the quick setups that open ports on your router, and suggested keeping a copy of the footage locally rather than relying only on a cloud. That advice is a decade old and it is still right. AVYCON gives us both cable routes. Its IP cameras run on PoE, and its HD analog cameras run over coax, which matters in Brooklyn because many houses here already have coax snaking through the cellar from a camera system or a cable TV install from years ago.

Then there is the reason most people start looking in the first place: things go missing from stoops and lobbies. News 12 Brooklyn reported in December 2024 that residents of Mill Basin were sending their deliveries to a shipping store instead of their homes because of porch theft, and one of them said the thieves he had on video would walk up, look at the camera and put their heads down. In August 2025 the same station showed building video from 240 Crown Street in Crown Heights, where a man with a child in a stroller got a resident to open the front door, checked that the lobby was empty and took two packages from the pile on the floor. Both stories make the same point about camera placement. A camera that only looks down from high above the door gets the top of a hood. A camera at the right height inside the vestibule, aimed at the face of someone coming through the inner door, gets something the police can use. Planning those angles is most of what a good install is.

Finally, AVYCON fits the buyers who need paperwork. The company says its compliant products are made and substantially transformed in the United States, South Korea or Vietnam, use approved chipsets, and contain no system-on-chip from the vendors named in Section 889 of the National Defense Authorization Act, which include Hikvision and Dahua. If you run a daycare, a medical office, a nonprofit with a grant, a contractor's yard that works for a public agency, or a co-op whose insurer has started asking questions, that matters. We check each model against AVYCON's current listing before it goes on a quote and we keep the spec sheets with your paperwork.

Repairs and Rescues

Brooklyn AVYCON Trouble: A Dead Drive, a Blank App, or a Recorder Nobody Can Log Into

Not every call is a new system. A good share of the AVYCON work we do in Brooklyn is on recorders someone else installed, sometimes years ago, sometimes for a previous owner. The problems repeat, and most of them are fixable without replacing cameras that still work.

The app says the device is offline

The phone app reaches the recorder over your internet connection, so the first suspects are the boring ones: a new router from the internet provider, a changed Wi-Fi name, a recorder that was moved and plugged into a different port, or a modem swap after an outage. AVYCON's documentation adds the recorder to the app by scanning a QR code from the recorder's system information screen, and when a router changes, that link frequently has to be made again. We check the recorder's network settings from a monitor and mouse, confirm it can reach the internet, re-add it to every phone that needs it, and write down what we did so the next router swap does not leave you blind again.

Nobody knows the password

This one comes up on almost every house sale in Brooklyn. The seller's installer set the admin password, the seller never wrote it down, and the buyer has a recorder full of footage they cannot open. AVYCON's FAQ is clear that its cameras and recorders ship with no default password at all, which is good security but means there is no factory login to fall back on. AVYCON publishes separate reset procedures for recorders and cameras, and a factory reset on the recorder clears its settings. We do resets only for the owner of the equipment, we set a new strong password with you in the room, and we put it on the handover sheet that stays with the house.

Recording stopped weeks ago

A recorder that is powered on and showing live video can still have a dead hard drive. People find out when they need footage and the playback search comes up empty. Drives wear out, especially in a cellar that gets damp in August or a closet where the recorder shares a shelf with the boiler controls. We check the drive status, replace the drive if it has failed, size the new one so you keep at least two weeks of recording as on our price list, and move the recorder somewhere cooler if heat is what killed it.

A camera went dark after a storm or a repair

On a PoE system one cable is both power and video, so a single black screen almost always means that cable, its connector, or the port it lands on. In Brooklyn the usual culprits are a roofer who moved a cable to get at the flashing, a contractor who cut through a cellar ceiling during a renovation, water in a connector on the back of the house, or a squirrel. We test the run, re-terminate or replace the bad section, and if the cable route itself is the weak point, we move it.

An old analog system on coax

Plenty of Brooklyn houses and stores still have an analog camera system from fifteen or twenty years ago with coax running to each camera. You do not always need to rip that out. AVYCON's HD analog cameras work over coax, and the four-channel DVR on our Brooklyn list takes them along with two IP cameras. If the old coax tests good, we can often reuse it, which saves labor and keeps holes out of finished walls. If it does not, we tell you, and we price new Cat6 instead.

Building Specialists

AVYCON Cameras for Brooklyn's Building Types

Seven kinds of building make up nearly every AVYCON call we take in Brooklyn. They differ in what the walls are made of, where a recorder can live, how cable gets from the inside to the outside, and who besides you gets a say. Knowing which one you have tells us most of what the job will involve before we arrive.

The brownstone or brick rowhouse

Three or four stories on a cellar, party walls on both sides, a stoop at the front and a yard at the back. Found across Park Slope, Fort Greene, Clinton Hill, Bedford-Stuyvesant, Cobble Hill and Boerum Hill.

The limestone row

Two- and three-story limestone fronts on the side streets of Crown Heights, Bed-Stuy and Bay Ridge, with carved bays and very little plain surface to mount on.

The frame Victorian

Freestanding wood houses with porches and driveways in Flatbush, Ditmas Park, Prospect Park South and Midwood, where cameras can reach all four sides.

The two- or three-family

Brick or siding houses in Bensonhurst, Dyker Heights, Canarsie, East Flatbush and Sheepshead Bay, often with an owner downstairs and tenants above.

The walk-up apartment building

Four- to six-story buildings on avenues like Ocean Avenue, Eastern Parkway's side streets and Bushwick's grid, with a vestibule, a mail area and a single stair.

The co-op or condo

Prewar elevator co-ops and newer condos where a board or managing agent controls the lobby, the roof and the cable routes between them.

The storefront, loft or yard

Shops under apartments on the commercial avenues, converted factories in Williamsburg, Greenpoint and Gowanus, and the yards and warehouses of Red Hook, East New York and Sunset Park.

The brownstone: the cellar ceiling is the cable highway

In a brownstone, the place to put the AVYCON recorder is almost always the cellar or the garden level, near where the internet service comes in. From there, the front camera cable runs along the cellar ceiling to the front wall and comes out under the stoop or through the areaway, where it can climb up to a camera mounted under the stoop's landing or beside the parlor-floor door. The back camera cable runs to the back wall and out to the yard. Neither route needs to touch a finished room upstairs. Where a camera has to go higher, for example to cover the sidewalk from the second floor, we look for a path inside a closet or along an old dumbwaiter or chimney chase before we ever consider an exterior run. Because brownstone fronts are sandstone over brick, we drill through mortar joints rather than the stone itself, which is also what the Landmarks Preservation Commission asks for in a historic district.

The limestone row: small cameras, few spots

Limestone fronts are beautiful and difficult. The carved surfaces, bowed bays and rusticated bases leave few flat places that are not decorative, and a camera bolted into carving looks wrong and damages stone that cannot be replaced. On these houses we usually put the front camera inside the vestibule, looking out through the glass of the outer door, or tucked under the stoop, and we choose a small turret in a color close to the stone. AVYCON's 2.8 millimeter turrets give a wide view from a short distance, which is what a narrow Brooklyn stoop needs.

The frame Victorian: four sides and a driveway

Flatbush and Ditmas Park houses are the one Brooklyn building type where you can see a camera's whole circle. Porches, side driveways, detached garages and deep backyards give a lot to cover. Wood framing also makes cable easier, since we can often fish through the attic and down a wall cavity. The trade-off is distance: a driveway camera might need to read a plate forty or fifty feet away, and an AVYCON camera with a narrower lens may make more sense there than a wide-angle turret, because a wide view spreads the same pixels over far more ground. These neighborhoods are also where Brooklyn's newest historic districts are. Brownstoner reported that the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Beverley Square West and Ditmas Park West historic districts in November 2025, together covering 245 houses, which brought the Flatbush area to five historic districts. If your house is in one, the front-facade rules below apply.

The two- or three-family: whose camera is it

When an owner lives on the ground floor and rents the floors above, the camera system is the owner's, but the front door and the hallway are shared. We usually put the recorder in the owner's space, cover the shared front door, the mailboxes and the back gate, and keep cameras pointed at the common entry rather than at tenants' apartment doors. If a tenant wants to see who is at the front door, we look at whether your recorder model can give a separate login limited to that one view, and we confirm it on the recorder itself before promising it, so the person upstairs sees the stoop and nothing else.

The walk-up: one stair, many doors

A walk-up has no elevator shaft and often no spare conduit, and the owner rarely wants surface wiring in the hallways. The trick is usually the stair itself: a cable can rise inside the stair hall's corner, behind a molding, or through a closet on each landing, and a single PoE camera on each floor's landing covers the corridor. In the vestibule, the camera goes where it sees the face of someone opening the inner door, which is exactly the moment the Crown Street video captured.

The co-op or condo: the board writes the rules

In a co-op or condo, the lobby, the hallways, the roof and the cellar belong to the building, and so do the decisions about cameras in them. Boards and managing agents typically want a written scope, a certificate of insurance, the license number, product sheets, and a plan for who can see the footage. We provide all of that up front. Inside your own unit, the same building rules apply to drilling, so we confirm what your alteration agreement allows before the visit.

The storefront, loft or yard: business first

A shop under apartments on Fifth Avenue in Bay Ridge, Flatbush Avenue, Kings Highway, Nostrand Avenue or Knickerbocker Avenue has a different set of needs: a clear view of the register and the door, a camera in the stockroom, and something watching the sidewalk gate after the shutter comes down. Lofts and warehouses add long runs and high ceilings. Yards add distance and weather. For all of them, we put the recorder somewhere locked and out of reach, because the first thing a thief does when he sees cameras is look for the box.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Half Hour at a Brooklyn AVYCON Job

People sometimes expect the technician to start drilling as soon as the ladder comes off the van. On a well-run camera job, the first half hour has no drilling at all. Here is what our crews actually do when they arrive at a Brooklyn address, and why each step saves time later.

They stand across the street. Before going in, the technician looks at the front from the opposite sidewalk, the way a stranger would. Where would someone stand to read the house numbers? Where does a delivery driver leave a box? Which side of the stoop is in shadow at night? Is there a street tree that will fill with leaves by June and block a camera that sees perfectly in November? On a lot of Brooklyn blocks the London plane trees decide where the front camera can go.

They go down to the cellar. In a rowhouse or two-family, the cellar tells us how the house was built and how hard the job will be. Exposed joists mean easy cable runs. A finished basement ceiling means we look for access panels, a soffit or a closet. Old knob-and-tube wiring, active leaks or a boiler sharing the only free wall change where the recorder can safely live. We also look for coax from an older system, because good coax may save you money.

They find the internet. The recorder needs a cable to your router, and the router is rarely where it should be. In many Brooklyn houses the internet comes in at the front of the cellar and the router sits on the parlor floor, so we plan a single Cat6 between them or put the recorder upstairs and bring the camera cables to it.

They hold a camera up. Before we commit to a mounting spot, we hold a camera or a phone with a similar lens at the spot and look at the picture with you. That two-minute check catches most of the problems people find only after an install: a view that is mostly sky, a door frame that blocks half the stoop, a streetlight that will blind the lens at night.

They count watts. Every PoE camera draws a little power from the recorder or switch. AVYCON's spec sheets put an 8MP turret like the AVC-NDE81F28 at a maximum of 7.1 watts on PoE and the 180-degree AVC-NPE81F180 at a maximum of 12 watts. Most recorders have a total PoE budget, and cameras with white-light deterrence, speakers or heaters draw more. We add it up before choosing the recorder so nothing browns out on a cold night.

They agree the route out loud. Finally, the technician walks you through every cable: from here, along there, out through that joint, up to this camera. If something on the route surprises you, a molding you love or a closet you did not want opened, this is when we change it. Then the drilling starts.

Cable and Power

Cat6, Coax and Watts: How an AVYCON System Gets Through Brooklyn Masonry

Every AVYCON system is two things: the cameras you see and the cable you do not. The cameras are the easy part. The cable is where the money, the time and the quality of the job really sit, and in Brooklyn the cable has to get through masonry that was laid a century or more ago.

PoE on Cat6: one cable per camera

AVYCON's IP cameras are powered over Ethernet. One Cat6 cable runs from the recorder's PoE port, or from a PoE switch beside it, to each camera. AVYCON's own setup guide says a camera plugged into a PoE port on the back of its NVR shows up on screen after about a minute. Our Brooklyn price for each camera includes a Cat6 run up to 100 feet, exposed, through an attic or through a basement. That covers most brownstones and two-families end to end. Longer runs, for a detached garage at the back of a Flatbush lot or the far end of a warehouse, need a switch partway or a different cable plan, and we price those individually.

HD analog on coax: reuse what is already there

AVYCON also makes HD analog cameras that run on coax with separate 12-volt power, and a four-channel DVR on our list that accepts those cameras plus two IP cameras. This is the right choice when a building already has coax from an old system that tests good. In a walk-up where the owner does not want new holes in the hallway plaster, the old coax route can be the difference between a clean job and a messy one. Our Brooklyn list shows four-camera HD analog packages starting at $2,188 installed, below the starting price of the IP packages, partly for that reason.

Getting out through the wall

Where a cable has to leave the building, we drill through a mortar joint, never through brownstone, limestone, terracotta or a decorative brick course. The hole is sized for the cable, sloped so water runs out rather than in, and sealed. On the back of a house, where the rules are looser and the brick is usually common brick, we often bring the cable out directly behind the camera so nothing is visible at all. On the front, we try first to keep the camera inside the vestibule or under the stoop so no hole is needed.

Hidden in-wall wiring

If you want a cable hidden inside a finished wall rather than run through a cellar, closet or attic, our Brooklyn list charges $200 more per camera for hidden wiring in hollow drywall, up to 100 feet. Plaster, brick and exterior walls are checked on site, because they cannot be fished the same way, and some Brooklyn walls that look like drywall turn out to be plaster on metal lath once a hole is opened.

Counting the power

The numbers on AVYCON's spec sheets matter more than most people think. An 8MP turret such as the AVC-NDE81F28 is rated at a maximum of 7.1 watts on 802.3af PoE. An 8MP dome such as the AVC-VHN81FLT/2.8 is rated at 9 watts. The 180-degree AVC-NPE81F180 needs the higher-power 802.3at standard and up to 12 watts. A four- or eight-channel recorder can feed a typical house of turrets with room to spare, but add white-light deterrence, a panoramic camera and a long cold night and the budget gets tight. We match the recorder or add a PoE switch so every camera has headroom.

Keeping the recorder safe

A recorder in a Brooklyn cellar faces heat from the boiler, damp in summer, and the occasional flood. We put it on a shelf off the floor, away from the boiler and the water heater, with some air around it, and we recommend a small battery backup so a brief power blip does not reboot the system right when you need it. In stores and multi-family buildings, the recorder goes in a locked cabinet or room. A camera system whose recorder can be carried out the back door is only half a system.

Rules and Permissions

Landmarks, Landlords, Boards and Neighbors: Who Gets a Say on a Brooklyn Camera

Putting a camera on your own property is generally your call, but in Brooklyn several other people may have a say about where it goes and how it is wired. None of them is a reason not to install cameras. All of them are easier to deal with before the job than after.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission

Brooklyn has many historic districts, from Brooklyn Heights, the city's first, to the Flatbush districts added in 2025. If your building is in one, work on its exterior, including cameras, needs a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The good news is that cameras that meet the Commission's published criteria can be approved at staff level, without a public hearing. The LPC's Permit Guidebook sets out those criteria. On small residential and commercial buildings, no more than two security cameras may be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage; large buildings may have three. Cameras cannot go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no other option, and when they must, they go in a discreet spot that does not detract from significant architectural features. Equipment must be as small as possible and finished to blend with the material around it. Conduit must be concealed by running through plain masonry or cladding, with only about ten inches of visible conduit allowed where concealment is impossible, painted to match. And equipment must attach at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork or concrete, never through cast iron or a decorative surface.

For a typical 20-foot-wide Brooklyn rowhouse, that means at most two cameras on the front, small, color-matched, preferably not on the face of the building at all. A camera inside the vestibule looking out, or under the stoop, often avoids the facade question entirely. We plan to those rules from the start, prepare the product sheets and photos the application needs, and do not drill a historic front until the permit is in hand.

Your landlord

If you rent, a wired AVYCON system means holes, cable and equipment attached to someone else's building. Get the landlord's written consent first. We are happy to send the landlord the scope, the camera locations and our insurance certificate, and to plan the job so everything can come out cleanly when you leave.

The co-op board or condo association

Boards control common areas and usually require an alteration or work agreement for anything inside a unit that involves drilling. For building-wide systems they will want to know who can view footage, how long it is kept, and who holds the passwords. We put that in writing and we set the recorder up with separate logins so the managing agent, the super and the board each see only what they should.

Tenants and residents

In a building with tenants, hallway cameras should show the corridor and the doors, not the inside of apartments when a door opens. Cameras go at the ends of corridors and on landings, aimed along the hall. Audio is off unless there is a clear reason and clear notice. And if the building uses fobs, apps or other smart entry, New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what the owner can do with the access data, so we keep the camera system's role simple: watching doors and common areas.

The neighbors

Straight Answers

AVYCON in Brooklyn: The Questions We Hear at the Door

Our router is on the top floor and the cellar has no internet. Where does the recorder go?

Either place can work. The recorder needs one cable to the router and one cable to each camera. If the cameras are all low, at the stoop and the yard, the cellar is usually best and we run a single Cat6 up to the router. If the router cannot move and a run up through the house is hard, we can put the recorder near the router and bring the camera cables up instead, usually inside a closet stack. We choose whichever route opens fewer walls.

The block has a lot of street trees. Will leaves set off the alerts all summer?

They will if alerts are based on simple motion. AVYCON's AI analytics classify people and vehicles, and its spec sheets list human and vehicle detection, intrusion zones and line crossing on the models we install. We draw the zones on the sidewalk, the stoop and the gate, not on the tree canopy, and set alerts to people only. Recording continues on everything; only the alerts are filtered.

We are selling the house. What happens to the system?

The system stays with the house unless your contract says otherwise. Before closing, have the system reset or have the admin password changed and handed to the buyer, and remove your phones from the app. We do this as a short visit, and we leave the buyer a handover sheet with the camera map, the recorder model and the new login so they are not the next person calling us with a recorder nobody can open.

How do we find a specific moment, say a delivery on Tuesday afternoon?

On the recorder's monitor or in the app, open playback, choose the camera and the date, and use the time bar. AVYCON's playback can also search by AI events, so you can ask for every time a person crossed the stoop zone between 2 and 5 p.m. and skip the empty hours. We show everyone who will use the system how to do this at handover.

Our upload speed is not great. Is that a problem?

Recording does not use your internet at all; it goes straight to the drive. Upload only matters when you watch from outside the house. AVYCON cameras record a high-quality main stream to the drive and a lighter sub stream for viewing several cameras at once in the app, so a modest connection can still show the cameras. The Brownstoner thread suggested at least about 1 megabit per camera of upload for comfortable remote viewing, which is a fair rule of thumb.

A streetlight shines right at our stoop. Will the front camera wash out at night?

It can, if the camera faces the light. Bright point sources at night are the most common reason a front camera disappoints. We mount the camera so the streetlight is behind it or off to the side, and we set wide dynamic range and exposure for the real light on your block during the evening visit or from photos you take after dark. Where the stoop is in deep shadow instead, AVYCON's IR reaches 82 feet on the AVC-NDE81F28 and 98 feet on the AVC-VHN81FLT/2.8 dome, far more than a Brooklyn stoop needs, and the WarmLight models can show color at night when someone approaches.

AI Overview Reality Check

What Generic Answers Get Wrong About AVYCON Cameras in Brooklyn

These are claims that show up in quick online answers and national how-to guides, paraphrased, each followed by what actually happens in Brooklyn buildings.

"Wireless cameras are just as good and easier"

Generic answers recommend Wi-Fi cameras because they skip the cable work.

In a brick rowhouse, a signal that has to pass through two masonry walls and a floor is weak and unreliable, which is exactly what Brownstoner's brownstone thread found. Wireless cameras also need power, and battery models need charging. A wired AVYCON camera takes power and data on one cable and keeps recording when the Wi-Fi is down.

"Mount the camera as high as you can"

The usual advice is to put cameras high so nobody can reach them.

High cameras see the tops of heads. The Mill Basin resident on News 12 described thieves who look at the camera and put their heads down. A camera inside the vestibule at face height, or a dome within reach but rated IK10, gets the face. We often use one high camera for the overview and one lower camera for identification.

"Any camera brand is fine for any building"

Many guides treat all brands as interchangeable.

For a home, that is close to true. For a daycare, a contractor that works for a public agency, a grant-funded nonprofit or a co-op whose insurer asks about the supply chain, the origin of the chips matters. AVYCON publishes an NDAA Section 889 statement and identifies compliant products; we check the specific model against AVYCON's listing before it goes on a quote.

"You can put cameras anywhere on your own house"

Ownership seems to settle it.

In a Brooklyn historic district, the front of your own house is regulated. The Landmarks Preservation Commission limits a small building to two cameras per 25 feet of frontage and wants them small, finished to match, and off the primary facade where possible. We plan to those rules so the permit is a formality.

"The cloud is safer than a recorder"

Some answers say a recorder in the house can be stolen, so cloud storage is better.

A recorder in a locked cellar cabinet, out of sight, with a small battery backup, is very hard to steal in the minutes a burglar has. It also keeps recording when the internet is out and has no monthly fee. If you want an off-site copy as well, AVYCON documents a way to back up clips to cloud storage, and you can combine the two.

Coverage Planning

A Camera-by-Camera Plan for a Typical Brooklyn Property

The most useful thing we can do before quoting is tell you what each camera is for. A system where every camera has one clear job records better footage and costs less than a system where cameras are scattered wherever there was a convenient spot. Here is how we think through the positions on the properties we see most in Brooklyn.

Camera one: the identification camera at the door

Its only job is to record the face of every person who comes to the door, clearly enough to recognize. That means it sits close, at or a little above head height, aimed at the spot where people stop: the top step of the stoop, the vestibule, the gate. In a brownstone, that is usually under the stoop landing looking up at the door, or inside the vestibule looking at the outer door. A wide 2.8 millimeter lens at a short distance puts plenty of pixels on a face. This is the camera that would have told the Mill Basin resident exactly who took his packages, and it is the one we never leave out.

Camera two: the overview of the front

The second front camera, if the building allows a second one, shows context: the sidewalk, the curb, which direction someone came from and where they went, whether a car pulled up. It can sit higher and wider. On a historic facade, this may be the camera that does not get installed, in which case we lean on the identification camera and on a neighbor's or the building's view of the street.

Camera three: the back door or garden door

Brooklyn burglaries often come through the back, where a rear yard, a fire escape or a garden door is hidden from the street. A camera over the back door, aimed so it sees the door itself and the path to it, covers that. In a rowhouse the cable for this camera usually comes straight through the back wall from the cellar ceiling, which makes it one of the easiest cameras to wire.

Camera four: the yard, the driveway or the side alley

The fourth camera covers the open space: the whole back garden from the far corner, the driveway of a Flatbush or Bay Ridge house, the side alley of a two-family, the gate of a yard. This is where a panoramic or longer-range AVYCON camera earns its place, and where AI line crossing is most useful, because you can draw a line at the fence or the gate and get an alert only when a person crosses it.

Inside cameras, if you want them

Many Brooklyn owners add a camera inside the parlor-floor entry or the garden-level hallway, aimed at the door from the inside. It records anyone who gets past the door and, with notifications set up, tells you when the house is entered while you are away. We avoid bedrooms and bathrooms entirely and talk through who lives in the house before placing any indoor camera, because a camera that records family members or tenants in private moments is a problem, not a feature.

For multi-family buildings: one camera per decision point

In a walk-up or co-op, we place cameras at the points where someone decides to go somewhere: the outer door, the inner door, the mailboxes or package area, the bottom of the stair, the cellar door, the roof door and the back door to the yard or the trash area. Each of those gets a camera aimed along the path, not at apartment doors. Counted that way, the camera total on a five- or six-story walk-up adds up quickly, so we choose a recorder with spare channels to leave room to grow.

For stores: register, door, stock, back, gate

For a shop, the order of priority is the register, the front door from inside, the stockroom, the back door and the gate or shutter outside. Anything beyond that, a camera on each aisle, a camera on the sidewalk display, is added based on where losses have actually happened. Ask yourself where the last three problems occurred; that is where the next camera goes.

Once each camera has a job, the rest follows: the lens for each spot, the cable route for each camera, the recorder size, the drive size and the price. That is why our quotes start with photos and a short conversation about what you want to see, not with a box of cameras.

FAQ

Brooklyn AVYCON Camera Installation FAQ

Our brownstone cellar has the boiler and the water meter. Is it still the right place for the AVYCON recorder?
+

Usually yes, as long as the recorder sits on a shelf off the floor, a few feet from the boiler and the water heater, with air around it. The cellar is where the internet usually comes in and where camera cables run most easily, so it saves the most work. If the cellar floods or gets very hot in summer, we move the recorder to a closet on the garden or parlor floor instead.

How many cameras can go on the front of a rowhouse in a Brooklyn historic district?
+

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook allows no more than two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage on a small residential or commercial building, so a typical rowhouse front gets two at most. They should be small, finished to match the facade and kept off the primary facade where another spot works, which is why we often mount the front camera under the stoop or inside the vestibule.

Someone got into our lobby by following a resident through the door. Where should the camera go to catch that?
+

Put one camera inside the vestibule, at about head height, aimed at the inner door so it records the face of everyone who comes through it, and a second wider camera over the lobby or mail area. A camera high above the outer door mostly records the tops of heads and hoods. On a walk-up we often add a vandal-rated dome because it can sit lower without being knocked off.

Does an AVYCON recorder come with a factory password we need to change?
+

No. AVYCON states that its cameras and recorders ship with no default password at all. The password is created when the system is first set up. We set a strong admin password with you during the install, put it on the handover sheet, and create separate logins for anyone else who needs to view the cameras.

When the hard drive fills up, does the AVYCON system stop recording?
+

No. AVYCON recorders automatically overwrite the oldest footage when the drive is full, so recording continues. On our Brooklyn list every recorder is sized with a drive that keeps at least two weeks of recording. If you need footage kept longer, for a business or an insurer, we fit a bigger drive.

We have coax in the walls from a camera system the last owner put in. Do we have to replace it?
+

Not always. AVYCON makes HD analog cameras that run over coax, and the AVR-DSV504H recorder on our list takes four of them plus two IP cameras. We test the old coax first. If it tests good, reusing it keeps holes out of finished walls and lowers the price; if it does not, we quote new Cat6.

Can the shop owner watch the AVYCON cameras on a computer at the counter as well as on a phone?
+

Yes. AVYCON's CMSONE software runs on a computer and adds the recorder as a device, and the recorder can also drive a monitor directly, which many Brooklyn stores mount behind the register. The phone app, AVYCON ONE, works alongside both, so the owner can check in from home.

Is there funding that helps a small Brooklyn store pay for security cameras?

Possibly. In October 2026 the City Council announced security technology funding for ground-floor retailers with 50 or fewer employees, handled in Brooklyn through the Brooklyn Chamber of Commerce, and New York State has offered a retail security tax credit that counts security cameras. For a typical yard about 20 feet wide, a wide 2.8 millimeter turret mounted on the back wall usually sees the whole space from fence to fence. Open playback on the recorder or in the app, pick the camera and time, or search by AI event, and export the clip to a USB stick or your phone. We show you how at handover. Sharing a specific clip is different from giving police a live feed, which is a separate, voluntary decision for you to make.
