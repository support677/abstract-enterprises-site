The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need Alarm Installation

Flatbush is one of the oldest place names in Brooklyn and one of the hardest to draw on a map. It began as the Dutch town of Midwout, chartered around 1652, and Wikipedia notes that the city has never fixed official borders for the modern neighborhood. Definitions have wandered: the Flatbush Development Corporation’s 1980s version ran from Parkside Avenue south to Avenue H and from Coney Island Avenue east to Bedford Avenue, while the New York Times later pushed the eastern edge to New York Avenue and the western edge to Ocean Avenue. Prospect Park sits to the north, East Flatbush to the east, Midwood to the south, and Kensington and Parkville to the west. The planned Victorian enclaves that once counted as Flatbush, from Prospect Park South to Ditmas Park and Fiske Terrace, now carry names of their own. What is left in the core is a dense grid of apartment buildings, commercial avenues and a few blocks of row houses, all of it inside Brooklyn Community District 14 and the ZIP codes 11203, 11210, 11225 and 11226.

The buildings tell the story of one boom. After the First World War the city exempted new housing from property tax until 1932, and Wikipedia describes the result in Flatbush: older suburban houses converted or demolished, and six-story Art Deco and Colonial Revival elevator apartment houses rising along Ocean Avenue and the streets around it, with walk-ups of the same style beside them. Many follow the light-court plans that state housing law demanded at the time: wings arranged around open courts, so that every room got a window. A good example just north of the neighborhood line is 135 Ocean Avenue, a six-story Art Deco house of 1928 that the 6 to Celebrate guide describes as an X-shaped plan with five wings around light courts. Inside, these buildings share a pattern that matters for security: one street door, one lobby, an elevator and a stair, long double-loaded corridors, a roof door at the top and a basement with laundry and storage at the bottom.

Most of the people living in them rent. The Furman Center’s 2010 profile of Community District 14 put rent-regulated units at 82.5 percent of rental apartments, and its current profile gives a homeownership rate of 24.4 percent in 2024. That arithmetic shapes every alarm conversation we have here. A tenant can protect their apartment, but the building door, the intercom, the lobby, the package shelf and the basement belong to the owner, and the super holds a key to almost every unit. Some buildings went co-op during what the Times in 1989 called the co-op waves washing over Flatbush, which adds a board and an alteration agreement for owners while leaving long-term rent-stabilized tenants living beside them. The alarm has to work inside that arrangement rather than pretend the apartment is a house.

The break-ins that make local news fit that shape. In January 2026 BK Reader and News 12 reported a Flatbush tenant who walked into her own kitchen on a Monday night and found a stranger already standing in it; she had not heard the door open. Neighbors told News 12 the building’s front door had been flimsy for nearly eight years, and that the property managers acted only after the intrusion. On the r/Flatbush thread where the story was shared, a commenter’s first reaction was exactly that: eight years of complaints about one door. Package rooms and lobby shelves are another flashpoint. In November 2025 NBC New York reported that a superintendent died after confronting a suspected package thief in the lobby of an Ocean Avenue apartment building. Online, Flatbush renters asking about particular buildings regularly single out the package room as the weak spot. None of this is solved by a siren in the lobby. It is solved by knowing, the second it happens, that your own door opened when it should not have, and by giving tenant groups ways to watch shared doors without anyone having to step in.

The avenues have their own history with theft. Wikipedia records that stores on Flatbush and Church Avenues were looted during the 1977 blackout, and that the Flatbush Avenue Business Improvement District, founded in 1990, had 195 merchants between Parkside Avenue and Cortelyou Road by 1996. In 2012 CBS New York reported the arrest of a man accused of entering a church on Snyder Avenue twice, a store on Flatbush Avenue, a nail salon and a clothing boutique, each time through a skylight. When the city’s Department of Small Business Services surveyed the corridors in 2021 and 2022, it counted 1,662 storefronts along Flatbush, Church, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues, found that 53 percent of merchants wanted safety changes to bring in more shoppers, and reported shoplifting as a concern for many of them. A store alarm here has to deal with roofs and skylights, with apartments directly above the sales floor, and with losses that happen during business hours as much as after them.

Many Flatbush tenants and shop owners end up pairing the alarm with a small camera, a video door viewer or a better intercom, because seeing who is at the door is half of feeling safe in an apartment building. Camera work is described on our Flatbush security camera installation page, and both can be scheduled for the same visit.

  • Two precincts: the 70th Precinct at 154 Lawrence Avenue and the 67th Precinct at 2820 Snyder Avenue. For 2018 Wikipedia lists 173 burglaries and 527 grand larcenies in the 70th, and 225 burglaries and 586 grand larcenies in the 67th.

Keypad Chirping in the Foyer, a Door Contact Gone Quiet or a Store That Will Not Arm: Same-Day Alarm Repair in Flatbush

Repair calls from Flatbush apartments tend to follow the building. A door contact stops reporting because the super replaced the door closer or rehung the door, and the magnet now sits a quarter inch from where it used to. A panel left behind by a previous tenant chirps every night because its backup battery died years ago and nobody has the installer code. An older system goes offline because its communicator depended on a phone line or a 3G radio, both long gone. In stores, the most common complaint is a back-door zone that never closes properly, so the owner bypasses it every night until the alarm protects nothing but the front glass.

Phone (347) 934-8335 and read us the maker’s name from the keypad along with whatever it is displaying. A siren that will not stop usually falls silent once its programmed bell time ends; in any case New York City’s Administrative Code says a building burglar alarm must be able to shut itself off within fifteen minutes, so a sounder still going after that is a same-day job, if only for the people on the other side of your walls. Systems put in by other companies are fine with us, and when one is not worth repairing you hear that before the meter starts.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Flatbush

Each item is quoted on its own line. A typical rented one- or two-bedroom in an Ocean Avenue elevator building uses the first three; a store on one of the avenues usually uses the last four.

Apartment-door protection

A contact on the apartment entrance door that does not interfere with its self-closer, an entry delay long enough to reach the keypad from the door, and an alert to your phone whenever the door opens while the system is armed, or at hours you choose even when it is not.

Removable, no-wiring systems

Battery-powered contacts and detectors mounted with removable adhesive, a panel that plugs into an ordinary outlet, and a cellular communicator, so nothing connects to the building’s phone, network or intercom wiring and nothing stays behind when you move.

Window and light-court coverage

Contacts or glass-break detectors on windows that open onto fire escapes, light courts, setbacks and low roofs, with the street-facing windows on upper floors usually left to a motion detector rather than a sensor on every sash.

Shared-door alerts for tenant groups

A single sensor on a package room, storage area or roof door that texts several tenants at once, set up only with the owner’s written permission, so a door left open or forced is noticed without anyone having to confront a stranger.

Store alarms under apartments

Front-door and back-door contacts, roof hatch and skylight protection, interior motion and glass-break detection, a siren inside the store set to cut off on time, and opening and closing reports, all planned so the tenants above are not woken by every test.

After-hours reports for owners

A text when the store is opened in the morning and armed at night, with the name or code of the person who did it, and an alert if closing time passes and nobody has armed the system.

Old-panel replacement

Where a previous tenant or an old store alarm left working contacts in place, a new panel and cellular communicator can reuse them; where the wiring is unknown or damaged, we replace the system with wireless parts rather than open walls.

Monitoring and app control

Watch it yourself from the app for nothing a month, or pay a partner central station from $19.99 monthly with no term to sign. Where a camera is part of the job, its clip arrives with the alert.

Repairs and false-alarm fixes

When the super rehangs a door, the magnet ends up in the wrong place; radiators confuse badly aimed detectors; old sirens ring without end; batteries swell and die; and communicators built for landlines or 3G simply stopped talking. These are the faults we fix most.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Flatbush’s Architecture

Flatbush’s building stock is mostly the product of a few decades between the wars, with older and newer layers on either side. These are the types we work in most often, and the parts of each that decide an alarm design.

Six-story elevator apartment houses

The Art Deco and Colonial Revival buildings along Ocean Avenue and its side streets: brick, with a single lobby, an elevator, a main stair and sometimes a service stair, and wings around light courts. Apartment doors open onto long interior corridors; the windows that matter are the ones on fire escapes and courts, not the ones on the avenue.

Pre-war walk-ups

Four- and five-story buildings without elevators, often on the side streets between the avenues. Fire escapes on the front or rear, a roof door that opens from inside without a key, and a vestibule door that is only as good as its lock. Top-floor and rear apartments need more attention than the ones over the street.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Stores at street level with two to four floors of apartments above, along Flatbush, Church, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues. The store usually has its own back door to a yard or alley and sometimes a cellar; the apartments share a separate street door beside the shop window. The two alarms should never be one.

Co-op and condo conversions

Buildings that went co-op from the 1980s on, where some apartments are owned and others are still rented from the sponsor or a successor. Owners need the board’s approval for alterations; renters next door are governed by their leases. The same corridor can hold both.

Row houses on landmarked blocks

Pockets such as the East 25th Street Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in 2020. These are houses, with basements and back yards, and are alarmed more like the rest of Brooklyn’s row houses than like Flatbush’s apartments.

New construction

Newer rental and condo buildings, including the large residential project planned around the landmarked 1932 Sears building at Bedford Avenue and Beverley Road. These usually come with a building intercom and fob system already, so a tenant alarm is about the apartment alone.

Method notes for each type

  • Elevator buildings. A contact on the apartment door, mounted on the hinge-side corner of the frame or the top of the door where it will not catch on the closer arm; a pet-tolerant motion detector in the foyer or living room facing away from radiators; window contacts only on fire-escape and court windows. The panel goes on a shelf or wall inside the apartment, never in the public hallway.
  • Walk-ups. The same apartment-door approach, plus glass-break detection in rooms with fire-escape windows, because those windows are often left partly open in summer. On top floors, a second detector covers the room nearest the roof door and stair bulkhead.
  • Mixed-use buildings. The store gets its own panel with its own communicator, mounted inside the store. Back doors and roof hatches get heavy-duty contacts; skylights get a contact on the hatch frame or a glass-break detector beneath. Sirens go inside the store, and the bell time is set well under the city’s fifteen-minute limit.
  • Co-ops and condos. We give the managing agent the parts list, the mounting method and our certificate of insurance before the visit. Most boards accept an all-wireless system inside the unit without further review, but the board decides, not us.
  • Row houses and new buildings. Row houses are wired through the basement where possible. In new buildings we stay off the building’s intercom and network, and mount everything with removable hardware unless the owner says otherwise.

Where Your Building Ends and Your Apartment Begins

The first thing we do in a Flatbush apartment is draw a line, sometimes literally on the floor plan we sketch. On one side is everything the landlord or the co-op owns and is responsible for: the street door and its lock, the intercom, the lobby, the mailboxes, the elevator, the stairs and hallways, the roof door, the basement, the boiler room, the fire escape itself and the outside walls. On the other side is what the tenant controls: the inside of the apartment and, with some limits, the apartment’s own entrance door. An alarm built for a Flatbush renter lives on the tenant’s side of that line. It is not a building system and should never be wired like one.

State law already marks parts of the line. Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law requires automatic self-closing and self-locking street doors in apartment buildings, and an intercom at the lobby door in buildings with eight or more apartments; for buildings put up or converted before 1968, the doors and intercom became required once a majority of tenants requested them in writing. The same section says a roof door must close on its own but must not lock on its own, so it can always be opened from inside without a key. Those are owner obligations. If the street door in your building does not latch, the remedy is a complaint to the owner and, if nothing happens, to 311. An apartment alarm cannot fix a broken building door, but it can make sure that a stranger who gets through it is noticed at yours.

Section 51-c gives tenants one specific right at their own door: to install and keep their own lock in the apartment entrance door, in addition to the owner’s lock, provided it is no more than three inches in circumference, and with a duplicate key to the owner on request. Any lease clause charging extra for it is void. The section does not mention alarms, and we do not stretch it to cover them. What it does show is that the door belongs, in a practical sense, partly to the tenant, which is why we place the alarm contact on the inside face of that door and frame and leave the outside face alone. HPD adds a third rule: in buildings with three or more apartments, apartment entrance doors must close and latch on their own, and tenants must not disable or prop the closer. A door contact has to be mounted so the door still swings shut and latches every time, and we test that before we leave.

The practical result is an alarm with four parts. A contact on the apartment door. A motion detector in the room you would have to cross to get anywhere from that door. Contacts or glass-break detectors on the windows a person could reach from outside, which in most elevator buildings means the fire-escape windows and nothing else. And a panel with a cellular communicator and a backup battery, sitting on a shelf or a wall inside the apartment where only you can reach it. It reports to your phone, and if you choose, to a central station. Nothing is connected to the building, and when you move, it goes with you.

How a Stranger Moves Through a Six-Story Pre-war Building

Think of the building as a vertical street. A person who gets through the lobby door, by following a resident in, by pressing buttons until someone buzzes, or because the latch has not caught properly for years, has the whole interior to themselves. The elevator takes them to any floor in seconds. The main stair and, in larger buildings, a service stair let them move between floors without being seen in the lobby again. Corridors are long and usually empty in the middle of a weekday. At the top is a roof door that, by law, must open from the inside without a key, and from the roof a determined person can reach the fire escapes on the court side of the building.

That geography suggests where the risk sits in a given apartment. Units at the far end of a corridor, out of sight of the elevator, give someone more time at the door. Units beside the stair can be reached and left without passing anyone. Top-floor apartments sit next to the roof bulkhead. Ground-floor and second-floor apartments facing a court or a rear yard have windows that can be reached from below or from the fire escape. And any apartment whose fire-escape window is left open a few inches in summer has an entrance that a door contact will never see.

So we design for the route, not the floor plan alone. Apartments at the end of a corridor get the shortest practical entry delay and an alert the instant the door opens while armed, so you know within seconds rather than when you get home. Apartments on fire escapes get a sensor on the fire-escape window and a glass-break detector in that room. Top-floor units get a detector covering the room nearest the roof. And every system gets a stay mode that protects the door and the windows while you are home, which matters in a building where, as the January 2026 Flatbush case showed, a stranger can be inside the apartment before the person living there hears anything.

Lobbies, Package Rooms and Basements: What a Tenant Group Can Watch

The shared parts of a Flatbush building are where most of the friction is, and where an individual tenant has the least say. Packages pile up on lobby shelves or in a package room that may or may not lock. Basement storage cages sit behind a door that is propped for the laundry. The roof door is supposed to close behind people but often does not. These are the owner’s spaces, and the owner decides what goes on their doors. What we can offer is a way for a tenant association, a co-op board or a small-building owner to know when one of those doors is opened, forced or left open, without wiring anything into the building’s systems.

The arrangement is simple. A battery sensor on the door, a small cellular hub in a closet or a tenant’s apartment nearby, and a group of phone numbers that receive a text when the door opens outside set hours or stays open longer than a few minutes. On r/Brooklyn, one renter in a building without a doorman described adding a keypad deadbolt to the package-room door; a sensor alert is the natural companion to that, telling people that the door was opened and when. It is not a siren, and it should not be. The November 2025 Ocean Avenue case is a reminder of how badly a confrontation in a lobby can end. The point of an alert is to let people check a camera, call the super or call the police, not to send anyone downstairs to deal with a stranger.

We set up shared-door alerts only with the owner’s written permission, and we tell tenant groups plainly when the better answer is a complaint about a broken lock rather than a sensor that will report the same open door every night. Where an owner wants more than an alert, cameras over the lobby and package room, a new intercom or fob-controlled doors, those are building systems and belong on our Flatbush access control and Flatbush intercom pages.

Keys You Did Not Cut: Codes, Supers and Visitors in a Flatbush Apartment

A house owner usually knows every key that exists. A Flatbush tenant rarely does. The super has one, and so, often, does the managing agent. A previous tenant may have kept a copy, and the lock cylinder may not have been changed when you moved in. Relatives who visit on weekends have spares, a home aide may need to come in on weekday mornings, and a dog walker or cleaner may have a key for a few hours a week. An alarm cannot take keys back, but it can turn every one of them into a named, dated entry instead of an anonymous one.

The method is simple and it is the part of a Flatbush job we spend the most time explaining. Everyone who comes in regularly gets their own code or fob, never a shared one. Codes for people who work on a schedule, such as an aide or a cleaner, are limited to their days and hours, so a code used at midnight is itself an alert. The super is given a code only if you choose to, and only for the dates you are away. The app shows who disarmed the system and when, and a code can be deleted from your phone the same hour someone stops coming. If a door opens and nobody enters a code, that is the event worth an immediate call.

This also answers a question many tenants are shy to ask: what if the alarm goes off while the super is fixing a leak or the exterminator is visiting? The answer is that it should, unless you planned for it. Agree with the super that he will text you before any non-emergency entry, put the visit on the calendar, and give a time-limited code for that morning. Our technicians walk through these settings with you at handover and leave them written down, because the panel is only as good as the habits around it.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Flatbush

Flatbush’s commercial life runs along four avenues and one junction. The city’s Avenue NYC assessment, carried out with the Church Avenue BID, the Flatbush Nostrand Junction BID and local merchant groups, counted 1,662 storefronts on Flatbush, Church, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues and listed the most common businesses as pharmacies and optical and health stores, restaurants, salons and barbershops, clothing and general merchandise stores, delis, houses of worship and medical offices. Nearly every one of them sits under apartments.

Flatbush Avenue

From Parkside Avenue past the Kings Theatre and Erasmus Hall to Cortelyou Road and on to the Junction. Wikipedia dates the Flatbush Avenue BID to 1990; a later Brooklyn Paper report on its proposed merger with the Church Avenue BID counted 477 storefronts and 450 businesses between the two districts.

Church Avenue

The old entertainment and shopping crossroads at Flatbush Avenue, and the spine of Little Caribbean, designated in 2017; the avenue is co-named Bob Marley Boulevard. The Church Avenue BID covers the stretch between Coney Island Avenue and Flatbush Avenue, and the higher of the assessment’s two pedestrian counts was taken on it, east of Nostrand Avenue.

Nostrand Avenue

From Parkside Avenue south to the Junction at Avenue H and on toward Avenue J, served by the 2 and 5 trains, which end at Flatbush Avenue–Brooklyn College. Shops, restaurants, salons and offices, most of them in low buildings with apartments above.

Rogers Avenue

The shorter stretch from Clarkson Avenue to Church Avenue in the assessment, with the firehouse of Engine 255 and Ladder 157 at number 1367. Smaller storefronts, many of them family businesses with the owner living nearby or upstairs.

The Junction

Where Flatbush and Nostrand Avenues meet Avenue H, beside Brooklyn College. Wikipedia notes that the area was redeveloped with Target’s largest store; the assessment reported higher vacancy here than elsewhere, which leaves occupied shops beside empty ones.

Cortelyou Road and Coney Island Avenue

On the western side, restaurant and shop strips that serve both Flatbush and the Victorian blocks around them. Engine 281 and Ladder 147 are at 1210 Cortelyou Road.

The store alarm problem on these avenues has four parts. First, the roof: a row of two- and three-story buildings with flat roofs, skylights and hatches, which is how the 2012 Snyder Avenue and Flatbush Avenue burglaries were committed. Second, the back: rear doors onto yards, alleys and garbage areas, often propped during deliveries and badly aligned after years of slamming. Third, the people upstairs: a siren that runs for an hour at three in the morning is a quality-of-life complaint for twenty households, and the city limits building alarms to fifteen minutes. Fourth, the open hours: merchants told the assessment team that shoplifting was a pressing concern, and an intrusion alarm does nothing about losses while the doors are open. We design for all four, and we say plainly which of them an alarm can solve and which need a camera, a layout change or staff.

Compliance notes for Flatbush businesses

  • Fifteen-minute ceiling. Section 24-238 of the city’s Administrative Code says an audible burglar alarm on a building has to be able to stop, and must stop, no later than fifteen minutes after it starts. Our store sirens are programmed for a few minutes and the cutoff is demonstrated before we leave.
  • Check the installer’s license. The New York Department of State licenses alarm installers; a company that puts in, services or maintains burglar or fire alarms for paying customers must hold that license. Ask to see the number. AESS works under #12000287431.
  • Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm is not a code fire alarm. Where a mixed-use building has a fire alarm system, it is the owner’s, and we do not tie a store’s intrusion panel into it; code fire work is covered on our Flatbush fire alarm installation page.
  • Exits stay usable. Contacts and locks on rear doors must never stop the door from being opened from inside as an exit. We alarm exit doors; we do not lock people in.
  • Smoke and CO in apartments. HPD requires owners to provide smoke detectors in every apartment of a multiple dwelling, and carbon monoxide detectors where required; tenants maintain them. A store alarm does not change that obligation upstairs.

Roofs, Skylights and Delivery Doors: Alarming a Store on the Avenues

Walk along Church Avenue or Nostrand Avenue and look up: most storefronts sit in buildings of two to four stories, joined wall to wall, with flat roofs that run along the block at nearly the same height. From the street a store looks like glass and a roll-down gate. From above it looks like a hatch, a skylight or two, an exhaust fan and a parapet low enough to step over from the next building. The 2012 arrests that CBS New York reported, for burglaries through skylights on Snyder Avenue and Flatbush Avenue, came from that second view.

We protect the roof first because it is the route the front of the store cannot reveal. Roof hatches get a heavy-duty contact on the hatch frame; skylights get either a contact on an opening sash, a shock sensor on the frame that reacts to forcing, or a glass-break detector below, depending on what the skylight is made of and whether it opens. Interior motion detectors are aimed so that anyone who drops in from the ceiling crosses at least one of them before reaching the register or the stockroom. Where the roof is shared with the apartments above, we never put anything on the roof itself without the owner’s permission; everything mounts on the store’s side of the ceiling.

The back of the store is the second route. Rear doors on these avenues open onto yards, narrow alleys and garbage areas, and they are opened dozens of times a day for deliveries and trash. Years of that leave doors that drag, latches that do not catch and contacts knocked loose, which is how a store ends up bypassing its back-door zone every night. We mount a recessed or armored contact where a hand truck cannot hit it, set an alert if the door is left open after closing time, and make sure the door still opens freely from inside as an exit. The front glass and gate come last, because they are the part of the store that every passer-by is already watching.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Flatbush: Questions Answered

The apartment door and the rules around it

New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant add a lock to the apartment door and requires a duplicate key on request. Does that same rule cover an alarm contact on the door?

Not by its wording. Section 51-c speaks only of a lock no more than three inches in circumference, and it says nothing about alarms, so we never tell a tenant the statute protects their sensor. In practice an alarm contact is a far smaller thing than a lock: a small transmitter and magnet stuck to the inside of the door and frame, with no hole through the door and nothing on the hallway side. Your lease is what governs fixtures, so read its alterations clause; if it is silent, an adhesive-mounted contact that comes off cleanly is about as light a touch as any fixture gets.

HPD says apartment doors in buildings with three or more units must close and latch on their own. Will a door sensor get in the way of the self-closer?

It should not, and we check that it does not. The contact goes on the latch side or the top of the door, clear of the closer arm and its bracket, and the magnet is set so the door still swings fully shut and latches without help. Before we leave we open the door wide, let it go, and watch it latch; we do it again with the system armed so you see the door report. If your closer is weak or broken, that is the owner’s repair, and the alarm will not hide it.

Our super keeps a key to every apartment for emergencies. If he lets himself in for a leak while we are away, what does the alarm do, and who finds out?

The door contact reports the moment he opens the door, so you get a text, and if you pay for monitoring, the central station starts its call list. That is usually what tenants want: an emergency entry should never be a secret. The practical step is to leave the super a phone number and agree that if he ever has to enter, he calls or texts you first or right after, so you can cancel a dispatch from the app. Some tenants give the super a code that works only while they are traveling, which records his entry by name.

Our apartment is rent-stabilized. Does that change anything about how an alarm should be installed?

The stabilization itself does not change the equipment; your lease and your relationship with the owner do. Long-term stabilized tenants usually want two things: nothing the landlord could call damage when the apartment is next inspected, and nothing that would need the landlord’s cooperation to keep working. So we install wireless parts on removable mounts, plug the panel into an ordinary outlet, use cellular instead of the building’s phone or internet wiring, and leave a written list of every device and where it sits. That list is yours to share with the owner if you choose.

The lobby, the package room, the basement and the roof

The street door of our building has not latched properly for years. What does the law require of that door, and what can an alarm inside my apartment add?

Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a puts the street door on the owner: automatic self-closing and self-locking doors, and in buildings with eight or more apartments an intercom at the lobby door, with older buildings covered once a majority of tenants ask in writing. A door that will not latch is a repair to report to the owner and then to 311. Your alarm cannot secure the lobby. It can make sure that anyone who gets through the broken door and tries yours is reported instantly, and the panel’s history of every door opening is a record you can keep.

Our tenant association wants to know when the package-room door is opened late at night. Can one sensor alert several of us at once?

Yes, with the owner’s written permission, since the room is the owner’s space. One battery contact on the door and a small cellular hub nearby can text a list of numbers when the door opens during hours you choose, or when it is left open longer than a few minutes. There is no siren, deliberately. The alert is a cue to check a camera, phone the super or call the police, not a reason for anyone to go down and confront someone in the lobby.

Every apartment has a storage cage in the basement next to the laundry room. Is there any way to know if someone gets into ours?

Often there is. A small battery contact on the cage door, or a vibration sensor on a stored item such as a bicycle frame, can report through a long-range wireless hub in your apartment if the signal reaches; basements under concrete floors sometimes need the hub placed lower, which requires the owner’s agreement. We test the signal from the cage before promising anything. A metal cage with a padlock remains the first line; the sensor only tells you when it has been beaten.

We live on the top floor, a few steps from the door to the roof. Should a top-floor apartment be set up differently?

A little. The law requires the roof door to close by itself but not lock by itself, so it can always be opened from inside, which means the roof is a route between buildings and down the fire escapes. On a top floor we put a contact on every window that faces the roof or a bulkhead, add glass-break detection in rooms with fire-escape windows, and aim the motion detector to cover the room nearest the stair. Your front door is protected the same way as anyone else’s.

Windows and rooms in a pre-war apartment

Our bedroom windows face a light court between the wings of the building, across from other apartments. Do court windows need sensors?

It depends on what is outside them. A court window ten feet above the court floor with no ledge, no fire escape and no setback roof below it is hard to reach, and a motion detector inside covers it well enough. A court window beside a fire escape, over a one-story extension or at the level of a neighboring roof gets a contact. We go to each window and look down and sideways before deciding; that two-minute check usually saves the cost of several sensors.

Our living-room window holds a through-the-window air conditioner all summer. How is that window protected while the unit is in it?

The window cannot close on its contact while the unit sits in it, so a contact on that sash will read open all season. We fit a glass-break detector in the room and make sure the motion detector sees that window, then program the window contact to be skipped while the air conditioner is installed. The accordion side panels are the weak point, so it is worth having the unit braced with a proper bracket and the side panels screwed in place.

We are home most days, reading or working at the back of the apartment. Can the alarm guard the front door while we are awake and moving around inside?

Yes. Stay mode arms the door and window sensors and ignores the motion detectors, so you can move freely while the door is watched. You can set the door to sound instantly in stay mode, with no countdown, and add a voice announcement from the panel or a chime on a plug-in speaker in the back room whenever the door opens, armed or not. That way a door opening quietly at the far end of a long foyer is never a surprise.

Stores on Flatbush, Church, Nostrand and Rogers

Most of what our store loses is shoplifting during business hours, not break-ins at night. Is a burglar alarm even the right purchase?

Partly. An intrusion alarm protects the store when it is closed and nothing else; it will not stop a person walking out with stock at four in the afternoon. For daytime losses the useful tools are cameras over the aisles and the register, a monitor near the door that shoppers can see, a layout that keeps high-value items in view, and a hold-up or panic button at the counter for the times things turn threatening. We often install the alarm and two or three cameras together, and we are honest about which problem each solves.

Two businesses share one storefront behind a single roll-down gate. Can each have its own alarm code and its own reports?

Yes. One panel can be split into two areas, each with its own codes, its own sensors and its own opening and closing reports, while the shared front door and gate belong to both areas so neither business can leave the entrance unprotected. If the two businesses keep different hours, the system can tell the second one that the front is already armed. Alternatively, each business has its own small panel; that costs more but keeps everything separate if one tenant moves out.

The building owner already has a fire alarm panel and a monitoring company for the whole building. Will our store’s burglar alarm interfere with it?

No, as long as the two stay apart, and we keep them apart. Your burglar alarm has its own panel, its own power supply and its own cellular communicator, and reports to your phone or your monitoring plan. We do not connect anything to the building’s fire alarm panel or share its phone lines. If you want smoke detection reported from your store, that is a fire alarm question for the owner and a licensed fire alarm contractor working within the building’s system.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Flatbush

Google’s AI Overview would not load where this page was written. What follows, then, is a set of broad claims that automated answers and contractor-listing sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to repeat about home alarms, put in our own words and held up against conditions in Flatbush apartment houses and storefronts.

“Protect every door and ground-floor window”

The advice assumes a house. A Flatbush tenant on the fourth floor of an elevator building has one door and perhaps two windows that anyone could reach, and most of the walls around them belong to neighbors. Spreading sensors across every opening wastes money and still misses the real routes in.

What matters is the apartment door, the fire-escape windows and the room you would have to cross from either. A door contact, a fire-escape window contact, a glass-break detector in that room and one motion detector cover most pre-war apartments completely.

Ground-floor and court-facing apartments are the exception, and we treat them that way. The point is to count the openings a person can actually reach, not the openings a generic checklist lists.

“Renters should stick to cameras”

This one comes from the fear of drilling and lease trouble. It underestimates what a wireless alarm can do without leaving a mark: nothing is drilled, nothing joins the building’s wiring, and the whole system leaves with the tenant.

A camera records; an alarm reports and sounds. In the January 2026 Flatbush case, the tenant was home and did not hear her door open. A door contact with an instant chime in stay mode would have announced it.

Many tenants end up with both, a door contact and a small indoor camera, because together they answer the two questions that matter: did someone come in, and who was it.

“Ask your landlord to add the alarm to the building system”

Building systems in Flatbush are the street door, the intercom and, in some buildings, a fire alarm. None of them is an apartment burglar alarm, and few owners want tenants’ alarms connected to their equipment.

Owners do have duties at the building level. Multiple Dwelling Law § 50-a covers self-locking street doors and lobby intercoms, and HPD enforces self-closing apartment doors. Those are worth insisting on.

The apartment alarm is the tenant’s own, separate, and portable. Keeping it that way avoids disputes about who maintains what, and means a change of management or a new owner cannot switch it off.

“Monitoring requires a long contract”

National dealers have long financed equipment through multi-year monitoring agreements, and residents on Reddit regularly describe being locked into them. That is a sales model, not a technical requirement.

Our systems work with the app alone, at no monthly charge, and a central station plan through our partners starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract.

For a Flatbush renter who may move in two years, that flexibility matters more than any feature. The equipment is yours from the day it is installed.

“An alarm will keep thieves out of a store”

An alarm detects; it does not lock. On Flatbush’s avenues the routes in after hours include skylights and roof hatches, which is how the 2012 burglaries on Snyder Avenue and Flatbush Avenue were carried out, and rear doors onto alleys.

A well-designed store alarm sees those routes and reports within seconds, but what happens next depends on the response: your phone, a monitoring center, a camera clip that confirms the break-in.

And during business hours an alarm does nothing at all about shoplifting. Merchants in the city’s Flatbush survey named it as a real concern; it calls for cameras and staff, not sensors.

“Sirens should be as loud and as long as possible”

In a store with twenty apartments above it, a siren that runs until someone arrives is a nuisance and, past fifteen minutes, against city law: Administrative Code § 24-238 requires building burglar alarms to terminate within that time.

Loudness belongs inside the protected space, where it ends a search quickly. We mount sirens inside the store or apartment and set bell times of a few minutes.

The real alarm is the notification. A text and a call within seconds beat a siren that neighbors learn to ignore.

“Anyone handy can install a burglar alarm”

Anyone can install a kit in their own home. But New York State requires a Department of State Security or Fire Alarm Installer license for a business that installs, services or maintains alarm systems for others.

Before letting anyone into your apartment or store to install an alarm, ask for the license number and a certificate of insurance; co-op boards and managing agents often ask for both anyway.

Field Notes: Come Up the Way a Stranger Would

This is how we survey in Flatbush, not the story of a particular building. The technician does not start in the apartment. He starts at the street door, and notices whether it latched behind him or whether he could have walked in without anyone buzzing. He looks at the lobby: where packages are left, whether the mailboxes are in view of the door, whether there is a camera. He takes the stair one flight, then the elevator, and counts how long the corridor is from the elevator to your door and whether your door can be seen from anywhere. Only then does he come in. He checks that the apartment door closes and latches on its own before he touches it, because a contact on a door that does not latch reports nothing useful. By the time he sits down to write the quote, the sensor list is usually shorter than the customer expected, and every item on it answers something he saw on the way up.

+

Not the way we mount it. Apartment entrance doors in multiple dwellings are often fire-rated assemblies, so we do not cut, drill or recess anything into the door or frame. A surface-mounted contact and magnet on the inside face, held by adhesive or small screws into the frame trim only where permitted, leaves the door, its hardware and its self-closer as they were.

Can the alarm’s history help if we need to show the landlord or HPD that the building door keeps failing?
+

It can support what you report. The app keeps a dated log of every time your apartment door opened and every alert, and a camera can add clips of the hallway or lobby where that is allowed. It cannot record the street door itself unless a sensor is on it with the owner’s permission. For the building door, the formal route is a complaint to the owner and then to 311; your own records are useful alongside it.
