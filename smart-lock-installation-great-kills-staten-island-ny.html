The Neighborhood

Why Great Kills Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Built Around the Household

Great Kills is where the South Shore starts. Local geographers usually count it as the northernmost South Shore community, with Richmondtown to the north, Bay Terrace to the east, Eltingville to the west and Great Kills Harbor along its southern edge. It covers about 3.25 square miles in Community District 3, and its main streets are the ones residents use every day: Amboy Road as the long commercial spine, Hylan Boulevard and Arthur Kill Road as the other shopping streets, Giffords Lane leading to the library and the railway station, and Nelson Avenue with the post office at number 1 and the firehouse of Engine 162, Ladder 82 and Battalion 23 at number 256.

The neighbourhood's shape comes from two very different eras. Until the 1950s it was largely rural, with resorts along the shore; one local history describes early twentieth-century bungalows, colonials and craftsman-style houses on streets such as Hillside Terrace, Giffords Lane and Greaves Avenue. Then the Verrazzano–Narrows Bridge opened the island to Brooklyn, and decades of building followed. For the Great Kills South area, NeighborhoodScout's housing profile describes mostly small to medium single-family homes and townhomes, with 33.2 percent of homes classed as rowhouses or other attached homes — a share it says only 2.8 percent of American neighbourhoods exceed — and many homes built between 1970 and 1999. That is the stock we see most: attached, multi-level townhouses in short rows, alongside detached houses on the older streets.

Those townhouses change how a lock gets used. A 1987 Great Kills townhouse on the market in recent years is a fair example of the layout: a one-car garage and an entry foyer on the first level, the kitchen and living room upstairs, the bedrooms on the top floor, a lower level of extra living space and sliding doors from the family room to the yard. The front door is a few steps from the garage door, and the people who use it most are a floor or two away when someone knocks. Next door is a mirror-image house whose front door may be only a few feet from yours. Locks, keypads, alerts and codes all have to make sense in that arrangement.

The people behind the doors matter just as much. The 2020 Census counted 54,699 residents in Great Kills in 19,994 households, and 22.9 percent of occupied homes were rented — so roughly 77 percent are lived in by their owners, which means the person asking for the lock is usually the person who decides about the door. Nearly three households in ten have children, 72.9 percent include at least one family, and 29 percent of residents are aged 45 to 64. Commuters leave early for the Staten Island Railway at Giffords Lane or for the express buses to Manhattan. Others leave early for the water: Great Kills Harbor holds marinas, two yacht clubs that administer the moorings in its centre, and Nichols Marina on the western side of Great Kills Park.

Crime, by city standards, is low. A 2010 study ranked the 122nd and 123rd Precincts as the two safest of 69 by per-capita crime, and Great Kills has been in the 122nd since the precinct maps were redrawn in July 2013. The precinct still recorded 91 burglaries in 2022, and the island has had its warnings: in February 2026 Borough President Vito Fossella and police told residents about a group using crowbars to force windows and steal cars in Todt Hill and Dongan Hills. In Great Kills itself, a May 2012 home invasion on Elverton Avenue began with a knock: a woman in scrubs said she was a home health aide, and when the resident opened the door an accomplice pushed in. No lock stops a door that is opened by hand, which is why we talk about seeing the visitor first as often as we talk about bolts.

So the question in Great Kills is rarely “is this door strong enough?” on its own. It is “who in this household needs this door, when, and what should happen when they stop needing it?” We answer that on paper first, then choose the lock.

Rows, Harbour and Household

Three Things That Decide a Great Kills Lock Plan

The row: a shared wall, a mirror image and a door at garage level

In an attached townhouse the front door is part of a pattern. Each house in the row is often a mirror of the next, so your door may be hinged on the left while your neighbour's is hinged on the right, and the two entrances can sit side by side under a shared roofline or a pair of matching porches. That affects more than looks. It decides which way the lock's interior unit has to face, how close the keypad is to someone else's front step, and whether anything you mount by the door ends up pointing at the neighbour's entrance. When we survey a row we look at the house next door as well as yours, because a keypad that lights up a neighbour's window at midnight, or a camera that records their visitors, is the kind of thing that sours a row quickly.

The garage-level entry also changes the routine. Many owners in these rows rarely use the front door themselves; they drive into the garage and climb the inside stair. The front door becomes the visitors' door, the delivery door and the children's door after school. That is the reverse of what most lock advice assumes, and it means the most important features are often the ones that let you manage the front door from upstairs: knowing whether it is locked, letting a known visitor in, and setting codes that only work at the times they should.

The harbour: households that are out on the water for the day

Great Kills Harbor has been a boating harbour for a long time; one neighbourhood history says private marinas and yacht clubs were on its shores by the 1920s. Boating households tend to leave early, come back late and leave the house empty for whole weekends in season. Their doors also face a different set of temptations: club gate codes that get reused at home, marine gear in the garage or on a trailer in the driveway, and keys for the boat, car and house all hanging on the same float. A good lock plan keeps the house's credentials separate from everything else and makes it easy to check the house from the dock.

The household: children, grandparents, pets and the people who come to help

With roughly three households in ten raising children and many more with older parents nearby, the real list of people at a Great Kills front door is long. Children coming home from P.S. 8, the Gifford School or I.S. 24; a parent who shares custody; grandparents who come for the week at Christmas; a dog walker; the cleaner; a babysitter on Friday nights. Each one should have their own code or credential, with a schedule if that helps, and each should be removable without changing anyone else's. Equally important is what the lock must never do: trap a toddler or a visitor inside, or leave a household member who cannot hear the doorbell unaware that someone is at the door.

None of this needs a complicated system. It needs a plan written down before the box is opened, so that the lock is set up for the house you actually live in rather than the one in the advertisement.

Door details that change from house to house

  • Pre-hung steel and fibreglass doors on townhouses: usually bored for a standard deadbolt already, which makes a smart deadbolt a straightforward swap, but the factory strike is often held by short screws into a thin jamb. We replace those with long screws into the framing before trusting the bolt.
  • Handing on mirror-image doors: most smart deadbolts are reversible and learn their handing at setup; some keypad levers and mortise units are handed and must be ordered for the door. We record the handing for each house in a row before ordering anything.
  • Foyer clearance: the inside unit of a smart deadbolt is bulky. In a narrow garage-level foyer, check that the door can still open fully past a coat hook, a light switch or the first stair tread.
  • Older wood doors: thicker or thinner than modern locks are listed for, old bores to fill, softened wood around the latch. Measured first, filled and reinforced where needed.
  • Doors facing the harbour: wind and salt air on one face, so a finish and gasket suited to exterior exposure, and stainless or coated screws on the outside trim.
  • Rear sliders: sliding doors in townhouses usually take a secondary bar and a contact sensor rather than a smart deadbolt; we tell you honestly where a smart lock is not the right part.

Compliance callouts for Great Kills business doors

  • Nobody needs a code to leave. Under section 1010.1.9 of the city's 2022 Building Code, people inside must be able to open an exit door with no key and no special knowledge or effort. So the code pad goes on the street side only, and staff walk out using a lever, a push bar or a button.
  • Pick fail-safe or fail-secure up front. A powered strike or maglock has to be told what to do in a blackout: hold the door shut or let it swing. Exit doors and stockrooms want different answers, so that is agreed before parts are ordered.
  • Ask the landlord before the frame is cut. Most shops on Amboy Road and Hylan Boulevard rent their premises, and the frame, glass and facade normally stay the owner's property. A short written yes from the landlord comes before any strike or cable run.
  • Apartments above shops follow housing rules. A tenant's right to enter comes from housing law and the lease, not the shop's security plan. Shop codes never open the residential door unless the owner and tenants agree in writing.
  • Clubs and marinas set their own rules. We only change a gate, dock or clubhouse door when the club or operator engages us, never at a member's request alone.
Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Great Kills: Questions Answered

These are the questions Great Kills owners actually put to us, grouped by where they come from — the townhouse rows, the boating households, the family house — and answered the way we would answer them standing at the door.

Living in a townhouse row

Three households in our townhouse row want smart locks — can one visit cover the whole row while each house keeps its own account?

Yes, and it is one of the more efficient ways to do it. We survey each house in turn on the same day, because mirror-image doors are rarely identical in practice: one may have been replaced, one may have a storm door, one may have a warped jamb. Each household gets its own written quote and pays for its own door, so nobody is subsidising a neighbour's extra work. On installation day we work through the row in one visit where the parts allow.

What we do not do is put the houses on one shared account. Each household creates its own app account, with two of its own administrators, and its own codes. If neighbours want to exchange emergency codes, that is their choice, and we would suggest a temporary code each side can delete rather than permanent access. We also note each door's handing, the lock model and the battery type on a sheet for each house, which makes later service quicker for everyone.

A whole-row booking also helps with appearance. If the row looks better with matching finishes, we can supply the same model in the same finish to each house, while still keeping the accounts completely separate.

When the townhouse association's painters redo every front door, should the smart lock come off first, and who puts it back on?

Yes, it should come off. Painters working around a smart lock tend to tape over the keypad, paint up to the edge of the escutcheon or get paint into the bolt opening, and the inside unit's cable can be damaged if someone tries to loosen it without knowing how it connects. Paint built up on the door edge or the strike also makes a motorised bolt work harder, which shortens battery life and can cause jam alerts.

Before the painters arrive, either take the lock off yourself following the manufacturer's instructions or book a short visit for us to remove it, bag the parts and label the screws. Leave the deadbolt hole and latch bore clean and masked. Once the paint has cured, the lock goes back on, the bolt and strike are checked for alignment, and the codes are tested. Most locks keep their codes in memory without batteries; a few need re-enrolling, and we will tell you which yours is.

If your association is organising the painting, it is worth asking whether it can schedule the doors in a block so that one visit removes and refits the locks for several houses at once.

We pressure-wash the front of the townhouse and the driveway every spring — can the spray damage the keypad?

It can. Exterior smart locks are built to cope with rain, but a pressure washer drives water at a force and angle no weather does, and it can push water past the keypad gasket, into the key cylinder or behind the outside escutcheon. The result may not show at once; corrosion on the contacts or a keypad that fails a few weeks later is more typical.

Keep the nozzle well away from the door hardware and use a low-pressure rinse around the door itself. Cover the keypad with a plastic bag and painter's tape while the rest of the front is washed, and remove it afterwards so trapped moisture can dry. If the door surround must be cleaned close up, use a cloth and a bucket. The same goes for anyone you hire to do the job: tell them the lock is electronic.

If water does get in and the keypad misbehaves, take the batteries out, let the lock dry for a day and try again before assuming it has failed. If it still does not respond, call us before you order a replacement.

Households that live by the harbour

Our yacht club gives members a gate code — is it safe to use the same number for the front door at home?

We advise against it. A club gate code is, by its nature, known to many people: other members, staff, and anyone who has watched it being entered at the gate. It is usually changed only when the club decides to, and you have no say over who learns it. Using the same digits at home turns a widely shared number into the key to your house.

The same principle applies to any code you use elsewhere: the garage-door keypad, an alarm panel, a gym locker or a bank card PIN. Give the front door its own code, at least six digits long, and do not use a date or a sequence someone could guess. Most locks let each household member have a different code, which is better still, because the entry history then shows who came in.

We also never install, change or programme a club or marina gate on a member's request. Those systems belong to the club or the operator, and any change goes through them.

Can a smart padlock secure the boat trailer or the gear shed out back, and should it be on the same app as the house?

Smart padlocks, opened by a phone over Bluetooth or by a code on the body, work well on sheds, gear lockers and storage boxes, and some are made for outdoor use. A padlock on a trailer hitch takes more punishment from road spray and salt, and is a better job for a heavy, purpose-made hitch lock; we will tell you where the smart version makes sense and where a plain, solid lock is better.

Keeping the padlock in the same app as the house is convenient, but keep its credentials separate. Do not give the shed or the trailer the same code as the front door, and if a friend or a mechanic needs the shed for a weekend, give them a padlock-only code or a temporary phone key that cannot open the house.

Bear in mind that a padlock is only as strong as the hasp and the shed door it is fixed to. If a shed hasp is held on with short wood screws, the padlock is the strongest part of a weak arrangement, and we will suggest carriage bolts through the door.

The family house

We share custody, and the children's other parent collects them at our door — how should that parent's access be set up?

Start by deciding together whether the other parent needs to come inside at all. In many families the handover happens at the door, and the other parent needs no code. If they do need entry — to collect a child from a nap, or to wait inside on cold days — give them their own code, not a shared family one, and set it to work only on their days and hours. Most locks support recurring schedules, so a Wednesday-and-alternate-weekends pattern can be built in once.

Their code should not come with administrator rights, and they should not be the second administrator on the account. Keep the entry history in mind as well: it records when each code is used, which can be helpful but which both parents should know about in advance. If a court order or a written agreement deals with access to the home, follow it; a lock setting is not a substitute for that agreement.

Children's own codes should be separate from either parent's. If the arrangement changes, the other parent's code can be suspended or deleted from the app in seconds without anyone else's code changing.

Our toddler has worked out how to turn the thumb turn — how do we stop a small child walking out without trapping anyone in a fire?

This is one of the hardest balances at any front door, because the feature that lets a two-year-old out is the same one that lets everyone else out quickly in a fire. The city's building code expects exit doors to open from the inside without a key or special knowledge, and we will not fit a lock that needs a code, key or app to leave. So the answer is not to lock people in.

What works is a combination of measures that slow a small child but not an adult. A door-open alert or chime that sounds through the house the moment the door opens tells you at once; a door contact paired with the lock's app can do the same on your phone. A childproof cover over the thumb turn, or a secondary latch fitted high up out of a child's reach, adds a step that an adult can undo with one hand. Auto-lock also helps: if the door shuts behind a child, it relocks, and the history tells you exactly when it opened.

Talk to your paediatrician or a child-safety specialist about your particular child, and remove any high latch once the need has passed. Whatever you add, every adult in the house should be able to open the door in the dark in one or two movements.

Someone in our household is deaf — how will they know when the door has been unlocked or someone is standing at it?

A lock and a doorbell camera can both send events to places other than a ringing speaker. Most apps send notifications to a phone or a smartwatch, which vibrate, and a smartwatch on the wrist is often the most reliable signal in the house. Many smart-home systems can also flash a lamp or a coloured light when the doorbell is pressed, when the door unlocks or when it is left open, and a light in each main room means the signal is seen wherever the person is.

When we plan these, we check which events matter: a visitor at the door, a family member arriving, the door left unlocked at night, a wrong code entered repeatedly. Too many alerts and people stop noticing them; we set up the few that count. A video doorbell lets the person see who is there before deciding whether to open, which matters to everyone in a neighbourhood where a 2012 home invasion began with a knock and a false story.

If a household member uses a dedicated alerting system already, tell us at the walkthrough. Some can take a signal from a doorbell or smart-home hub directly, and we will check compatibility before recommending a lock.

Our dog has learned to open lever handles — will a smart lock stop him letting himself out?

Usually, yes, as long as the deadbolt is thrown. On most front doors the lever or handleset works only the latch, while the deadbolt is separate. A dog that pulls the lever retracts the latch, but if the deadbolt is out, the door stays shut. Setting auto-lock so the bolt throws a short time after the door closes means the deadbolt is out far more often than when people had to remember to turn a key.

There are two catches. Some handlesets and mortise locks have the deadbolt and latch linked, so pulling the inside lever retracts both — a safety feature for people that also lets a clever dog out. We check which type your door has. And a dog that learns to paw at the thumb turn can sometimes turn it, so we look at the shape of the inside unit as well.

Where a dog regularly tries the door, a round knob or a lever turned upward on an inside door can help, but on the main exit door we keep a hardware arrangement that every person in the house can open at once.

Is it worth it here?

Great Kills sits in one of the city's quietest precincts — is a smart lock still worth the money here?

Low crime is real here. A 2010 study ranked the 122nd and 123rd Precincts the two safest of 69 on per-capita crime, and crime across all categories in the 122nd fell 88 percent between 1990 and 2022. But the same precinct still recorded 91 burglaries in 2022, and the island-wide warnings in February 2026 show that patterns move from one neighbourhood to the next.

For most Great Kills households, though, the case for a smart lock is not mainly about burglars. It is about keys: the copies given to babysitters and relatives over the years that nobody can account for, the teenager who loses one every school year, the dog walker who moved away. A code can be deleted in seconds; a key can only be replaced by changing the lock. Auto-lock deals with the door left unlocked when the family rushes out for the express bus. The entry history settles arguments about who came home when.

If none of that applies — one or two people, keys always accounted for, no visitors who need to get in — a good mechanical deadbolt with a reinforced strike may be all you need, and we will say so. The lock should solve a problem you actually have.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Great Kills

We could not capture Google's AI Overview wording while preparing this page, so below are the claims it typically draws on, as published by cost guides, shops and reviewers. Each one is right for some door in America. Here is how they look from a townhouse foyer or a family hallway in Great Kills.

“Installing a smart lock costs $250 to $400”

The number most often repeated online is $250 to $400, average $300, from Fixr's national guide (January 2025 update). It is based on one Wi-Fi lock plus an accessory going onto an ordinary door, and for that job across the whole country it is fair enough.

The figure does not include what makes a Great Kills job take longer: a strike held by short screws in a townhouse jamb, a mirror-image door that needs a differently handed lever, a code plan for five people and a dog walker, or a doorbell and a door contact planned alongside the lock. Nor is it an AESS price.

We quote house locks as one flat figure after the free walkthrough, with the door work listed separately, and we publish our island-wide figures for commercial keypad and strike work in the pricing section below. Where we mention the national figure, we label it as national and third-party.

“Any smart lock fits any door”

Retail listings tend to promise a fit for “most standard doors”. For a modern pre-hung door bored for a standard deadbolt, that is usually right, and many townhouse doors fall into that category.

The exceptions are common here, though. The older houses on streets such as Hillside Terrace and Greaves Avenue can have thicker or thinner doors, odd backsets and old bores. Some keypad levers are handed and will not simply flip for the mirror-image house next door. Some townhouse foyers are too tight for a bulky inside unit.

So we measure every door: thickness, backset, bore size, handing and the clear space inside. Only then do we recommend a model, and if a lock you already own will not fit properly, we tell you before drilling rather than after.

“A smart lock means you'll never be locked out again”

Smart-lock marketing leans on the end of lost keys. Codes, fingerprints and phones do make lockouts rarer, because there is nothing to leave on the kitchen counter.

But lockouts still happen, just for different reasons: batteries ignored after weeks of low-battery warnings, a phone that is the only credential and has gone flat, an app account whose only administrator moved out or forgot the password, a keypad damaged by water from a hose or a pressure washer.

That is why every exterior lock we fit has a mechanical key kept somewhere sensible, why we set up two administrators, and why each household member has at least one way in that does not depend on a phone. The lock reduces lockouts; planning removes most of the rest.

“One code for the family keeps it simple”

Quick-start guides often show a single code being set at the door. For a person who lives alone, one code is fine.

For a Great Kills family it throws away most of what a smart lock is good at. With one shared code, the history cannot tell you which child came home, a babysitter who leaves cannot be removed without changing everyone's code, and a code that has spread to friends and neighbours cannot be traced.

We set one credential per person, add schedules where they help, and give visitors temporary codes. It takes a little longer at installation, and it is the reason the lock is still useful two years later.

“Auto-unlock opens the door as you arrive”

Some locks offer an auto-unlock feature that uses the phone's location to unlock the door as you approach. Lock makers promote it as a hands-free convenience for arriving with full arms, and on a detached house with a long driveway it can work well.

In a townhouse row it needs more care. The houses are close together, and a phone that thinks it has arrived home may unlock your door while you are standing at a neighbour's front step, or while you are unloading the car in the shared parking area with your back to the house.

We show owners how the feature behaves on their own door, suggest a tighter trigger where the app allows, and leave it switched off for anyone who would rather unlock with a deliberate tap or code. Convenience is worth having only if the door stays shut when you are not at it.

“Smart locks are easy to hack”

Opinion pieces regularly warn that connected locks can be hacked. Some older models had genuine flaws, and it would be wrong to say a connected device has no risk.

In practice the weak points we see are human rather than technical: a code reused from a club gate or a bank card, a shared administrator password, old codes never deleted, a phone with no screen lock that holds the house key. These are far easier to exploit than the radio protocol.

We use reputable brands that issue firmware updates, turn on automatic updates, set unique codes and two administrators with strong passwords, and show you how to remove codes. A plain mechanical lock can be picked or bumped too; the question is which risks you can manage.

“A better lock makes the house secure”

Product pages, naturally, are about the product. The incidents that reach the news on Staten Island are mostly about something else: in February 2026, officials warned of crowbars used to force windows and steal cars in Todt Hill and Dongan Hills; in May 2012, a Great Kills resident on Elverton Avenue opened his door to a stranger with a false story.

Against a window, the door lock does nothing. Against a door opened by hand, the lock does nothing either. Against a pry bar, the strike plate and the screws matter more than the electronics. What a smart lock does very well is control and record who has a way in.

So every Great Kills quote we write covers the door and frame alongside the lock, and we suggest a video doorbell or viewer wherever someone in the house answers the door to strangers. We will not pretend a lock covers what it does not.

The Townhouse Rows

Living With a Shared Wall: Smart Locks in Great Kills Townhouse Rows

The attached rows that went up across Great Kills from the 1970s into the 1990s were built for families who drove. The garage is the front of the house, the entry foyer is squeezed in beside it, and the stairs take you straight up to the kitchen. Thirty or forty years on, those houses are being bought by second and third owners who want the same things from a front door as anyone else, but who have to fit them into a layout designed around the car.

Which door gets the smart lock first

In most rows there are three ways in: the front door at garage level, the overhead garage door itself and the inside door from the garage into the house, plus a rear slider or door from the lower level to the yard. The front door is usually the right first choice, even though the family rarely uses it, because it is the door that visitors, children, helpers and deliveries use. It is also the one where losing track of keys matters most. The inside door from the garage comes next for households that leave the overhead door open while they unload or work in the garage.

The rear slider is the awkward one. Sliding patio doors do not take a normal deadbolt, so for most rows we recommend a secondary security bar or pin lock and a contact sensor that reports when the slider opens, rather than an electronic lock. That keeps the yard door secure and visible in the app without pretending it is something it is not.

Signal through floors

Townhouse routers are usually on the living floor or in a bedroom, one or two storeys above the front door, with a concrete slab or a garage between them. A lock that connects directly by Wi-Fi may hold on, or may drop out every evening when the house is busy. Locks that use a bridge or a Thread border router need that bridge within a sensible distance of the door. We test the signal at the door during the walkthrough with the household's own network, and if it is weak we place the bridge in the foyer, on the stair or in the garage where the door can reach it.

The neighbour's side of the wall

Screws for a reinforced strike go into the framing around the door, and in an end-of-row or mid-row house that framing is near the party wall. We use screws long enough to reach solid framing but check what is behind the jamb before driving them, so nothing breaks through into the neighbour's foyer. We also think about what the neighbour sees and hears: a keypad's backlight at night, an entry chime that sounds outside, a doorbell camera that records their path. Most can be adjusted, and a two-minute conversation over the fence avoids a lot of trouble.

Association rules and appearance

Some townhouse developments have a homeowners' or condominium association that sets rules about the outside of each house, and some do not. If yours does, check its rules on door hardware, colours and anything mounted on the facade before choosing a model. A retrofit unit that changes only the inside of the door can avoid the question altogether. Where the association is happy with keypads, matching the finish of the row's existing handlesets usually keeps everyone content.

End-of-row and mid-row houses

An end-of-row townhouse often has a side wall open to a path or a driveway, and sometimes a side window close to the front door. A mid-row house has neighbours on both sides and only its front and back exposed. That changes where a keypad is visible from, which windows a passer-by could reach through, and where a doorbell camera should point. On an end-of-row house we look at the side as well as the front before deciding where everything goes.

Selling a townhouse with a smart lock

When a house in a row changes hands, the lock should be handed over with it, not with the seller's account still attached. Before closing, the seller removes their codes and transfers or deletes the admin account; after closing, the buyer resets the lock to factory settings, sets up their own account and changes the mechanical key cylinder or has it rekeyed. We can do both halves at the setup-only rate, and leave a short written record for each side.

Harbour Season

Boat Days and Empty Houses: Planning Locks for Harbour Households

From spring launch to autumn haul-out, a good share of Great Kills spends its weekends on the water. Some households keep a boat on a mooring administered by one of the two yacht clubs; some use a marina slip; some trailer a small boat down to the ramp at Nichols Marina on the western side of Great Kills Park. All of them leave home early, often before light, and come back hours later with sunburn, wet gear and no free hands. That routine shapes what they need from a door.

Leaving before dawn

The most useful feature for a boating household is often the simplest: auto-lock that throws the bolt a short time after the door closes, and a notification if it does not. When the car is loaded in the dark and the last person out is carrying a cooler, nobody checks the front door. The lock does, and the app confirms it from the dock. We test auto-lock with the door slightly ajar so that the lock reports a failed throw rather than silently showing “locked”.

Coming home with full hands

The return trip is the opposite problem. Fingerprints may not read through wet, salty fingers, and a phone in a dry bag is no help at the door. A keypad code is usually the fastest way in for a returning boater, and we position the keypad so it can be reached without putting gear down on the step. Where the garage is the usual way in, the garage's own keypad and the inside door's lock should work together so the house is not left open while the boat gear is unloaded.

Keys on the float

Many boaters keep the boat key, car key and house key together on a floating keychain, which means that a keychain lost overboard, or left in the car at the marina car park, takes the house key with it. Once the house is keyless for daily use, the mechanical override key can come off that ring and live somewhere safer: with a trusted neighbour, or in a key safe whose code you change regularly. Car key fobs are worth keeping well away from the front door inside the house, too; island-wide warnings in early 2026 were about thieves after cars.

Gear, sheds and trailers

Outboard motors, fishing gear, life jackets and tools are expensive, portable and often stored in the garage, a shed or on a trailer in the driveway. They are also the things a casual thief would take. Smart padlocks and keypad padlocks let a household give the shed its own codes, separate from the house, and see when it was opened. We look at the hasp and the shed door as well as the lock, because the cheapest part of the arrangement is usually what fails.

Club and marina doors are not ours to change

Gates, docks and clubhouse doors at the clubs and marinas belong to their operators, who set their own access rules for members and staff. We will gladly talk to a club or marina about keypads or readers if it asks, but we do not change those doors for individual members. If your club's gate code is the same as your house code, change the house code, not the gate.

Through the Year

A Year at a Great Kills Front Door

Locks are bought on one day and lived with for years. In Great Kills the year has a rhythm — the boating season, the beach, the school calendar, the holidays — and each part of it asks something slightly different of the front door. Here is what we suggest households keep an eye on, season by season.

Spring: launch, paint and wash

Spring is when boats go back in the water and when houses get their outside jobs done. If the association or a contractor is painting front doors, take the lock off first, as described above, and put it back once the paint has cured. If the front of the house or the driveway is being pressure-washed, cover the keypad and keep the spray away from the door. It is also a good moment to look at the strike: winter can shift a door slightly, and a bolt that scrapes in April will jam in August.

Boating households should check the codes on any shed or trailer padlock before the gear goes back out, delete codes given to a mechanic over the winter, and make sure the override key for the house is not still on the boat keychain from last season.

Summer: beach days, humidity and visitors

Great Kills Park's lifeguarded beach is open from Memorial Day to Labor Day, and summer brings children in and out of the house all day, often with sand on their hands and feet. Keypads cope with that better than fingerprint sensors, so make sure every child old enough to come home alone has a code as well as a fingerprint. Wipe the keypad with a damp cloth now and then; grit in the gap around the buttons wears them faster.

Humid weeks make wood and some fibreglass doors swell. If the bolt starts to drag or the lock reports a jam, do not keep forcing it: the motor works harder, batteries drain and eventually the lock gives up. A quarter-turn adjustment of the strike usually fixes it, and it is a quick visit if you would rather we did it. Summer is also the season of house guests, so set temporary codes with an end date rather than handing out the family code.

Autumn: school, haul-out and the clocks

September brings the school year, and with it new routines. Children who have moved up from P.S. 8 or the Gifford School to I.S. 24 may start coming home alone for the first time. Set their codes, decide whether you want an arrival notification, and walk through what they should do if the keypad does not respond: go to the neighbour with the override key, not try the back door. Babysitters and after-school helpers change in the autumn, too, so tidy the code list.

Autumn is also haul-out, when boating gear comes back to the garage or the shed. Check that the shed's padlock and hasp are up to the job before the outboard goes in. When the clocks go back in November, do the twice-yearly review: delete old codes, change batteries if they are low, and confirm both administrators can still log in.

Winter: cold batteries and holiday visitors

Cold weather shortens battery life, especially on a door that faces open ground or the harbour wind. Replace batteries at the first low-battery warning rather than waiting for the last one, and use the type the manufacturer recommends; cheap batteries tend to fail first in the cold. If a door is exposed to driving rain or snow, check that the keypad's gasket is seated properly after any battery change.

The holidays bring grandparents for a week, cousins for a night and a long list of deliveries. Give visiting relatives their own temporary code or enrol a fingerprint for the length of their stay, and remove it when they leave. If you travel over the holidays, switch off helpers' codes you will not need, leave the override key with a neighbour, and check from your phone that the bolt has thrown after you leave.

Any time: when something changes in the household

Some events do not follow the calendar: a new baby, a grandparent moving in, a separation, a teenager learning to drive, a household member losing their hearing, a new dog. Each of these is a reason to look at the lock plan again. Most changes take minutes in the app; a few, such as visual alerts or a child-safety latch, are worth a short visit. The point of a smart lock is that it can change as the household does, but only if someone remembers to change it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check at a Great Kills Front Door

General working practice from our installers — not a story about any particular customer or address.

At a townhouse we stand in the foyer first and open the front door as far as it will go. If the door hits a coat hook, a radiator or the first stair, we know a bulky inside unit may not fit, and we pick a slimmer model before anything is drilled. Then we look at the house next door: which way its door swings, where its step is, where a keypad of ours would shine at night.

On every door we take the strike plate off. Factory screws three-quarters of an inch long into a thin jamb tell us more about the door's real strength than any rating on a box. We swap them for long screws into the framing, checking first what is behind the jamb when the party wall is close.

If the household includes a toddler, a dog that tries the handles or someone who cannot hear the bell, we note it on the plan and set up the chime, cover or light before testing anything else.

Before we leave, each person in the house tries their own code or fingerprint at the door, the two administrators log in on their own phones, and someone who was not part of the setup opens the door from inside in the dark with one hand. If that last test is not easy, the job is not finished.

FAQ

Great Kills Smart Lock Installation FAQ

Can the smoke alarm unlock the front door automatically when it goes off?
+

Some smart-home systems can link a connected smoke alarm to the lock so the door unlocks when the alarm sounds, which can help firefighters or a neighbour get in. It also means the door unlocks on every false alarm from the toaster, so we only set it up when the household understands that trade-off. Either way, the inside thumb turn already lets everyone out with one movement, and that does not depend on any automation.

The lock's history shows entries at the wrong times — how is its clock put right?
+

Most locks take their time from the app or the hub when they connect, so wrong times usually mean the lock has been offline, the time zone in the account is wrong, or the batteries were changed and the clock reset. Open the app beside the door to let it resync, and check the account's time zone. If times drift again, the connection to the bridge or router is the real problem, and we can look at that on a setup-only visit.

Can a child under 13 have their own login in the lock's app, or only a code?
+

That depends on the brand. Many lock apps require account holders to be adults or teenagers above a minimum age set in their terms, so younger children usually get a code or a fingerprint rather than an app login. A code also suits children better: there is no phone to lose or let run flat. We check the terms of the brand you choose and set each child up in the way it allows.

Can the lock require a code and a fingerprint together, say overnight?
+

A few models offer a two-step mode that asks for two credentials, but most consumer smart deadbolts accept any one valid credential. Where extra protection at night matters, a schedule that switches off helpers' codes after a set hour, plus an alert for any entry overnight, gives most of the benefit without making the door harder for the family. We will tell you which approach your chosen lock supports.

Will you come out to fit just one lock on one door?
+

Yes. A single front door is one of the most common jobs we do in Great Kills, and it gets the same free walkthrough, strike check and code setup as a whole-house job. If you think you may add a second door or a padlock later, mention it, so we can choose a lock family that keeps everything in one app.

Can we keep the swing-bar guard on the inside of the door once the smart lock is on?
+

Usually, yes. A swing-bar or chain guard on the inside of the door is separate from the deadbolt, and most smart locks fit around it. We check that the inside unit of the new lock does not collide with the guard's arm or its catch, and move the guard a little if needed. Remember that a guard only works when someone is home to set it, and it must be released before anyone can come in with a code.

Can the outside keypad be switched off while we're at home?
+

Many smart locks have a privacy or vacation mode, or a setting that disables the keypad or particular codes for a period, usually from the inside unit or the app. It is useful at night or when the house is full and nobody else needs to come in. We show you where the setting is and how to switch it back, so a helper is not left outside the next morning.

Do we get a written record of the lock model, its settings and who holds access?
+

Yes. Each installation ends with a short written sheet for the house: the lock model and finish, its handing, the battery type, where the override key is kept, which two people are administrators and which credentials exist, without the codes themselves. It makes later service quicker and helps if you sell the house or hand the property to a relative.
