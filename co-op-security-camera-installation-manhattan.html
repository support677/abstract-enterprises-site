The Neighborhood

Why a Manhattan Co-op Needs a Camera Plan Built for Shareholders

In a cooperative, nobody owns an apartment in the usual sense. A corporation owns the whole building, and each resident owner holds shares in that corporation plus a proprietary lease for one unit. The New York State Attorney General's guide for co-op residents puts it plainly: owners of co-op apartments "are both shareholders in and tenants of the same corporation" (NYS Attorney General, co-op board guide). That one fact shapes everything about a camera project. The lobby, the elevator, the laundry room and the boiler-room corridor belong to the corporation, the board of directors acts for the corporation, and every shareholder is both a part-owner who pays for the cameras and a resident who walks past them twice a day.

Manhattan is still co-op country. Brokers' fourth-quarter 2025 data showed roughly 1,500 co-op sales in Manhattan in that quarter against about 1,200 condo sales, with a co-op median of $825,000 compared with $1.7 million for condos (The Real Deal, Jan. 6, 2026, citing Miller Samuel and Douglas Elliman). Citywide, the Comptroller's office counts 450,000 occupied apartments in cooperative buildings (NYC Comptroller, March 2024). Each sale brings a new household, new movers, new contractors for a renovation, and a new set of people who need to be recognized at the door.

The buildings themselves are older on average than the borough's condos. A typical Manhattan coop is a prewar elevator building on West End Avenue or a side street off Park, a white-brick postwar tower in the East 60s, a six-story walk-up in the West Village, a converted rowhouse in Hamilton Heights, or a courtyard building in Hudson Heights with two or three entrances off one shared yard. Plaster walls, terrazzo floors, ornamental lobby ceilings and riser shafts packed with decades of abandoned telephone and cable wire are the normal working conditions. A camera plan that ignores those details ends up with surface conduit across a landmark-quality lobby, or with cameras the doorman can't see because the old riser can't carry the signal.

The problems boards call about are familiar. Package theft from lobbies is a steady Manhattan story: Patch reported a thief who took packages from the same West 74th Street lobby twice in a few days in July 2023 (Patch, July 10, 2023), and a West 89th Street building saw a man pose as a courier, carry in empty boxes and leave with residents' deliveries; one tenant counted at least ten thefts in a year even though both entrance doors were "always locked" (I Love the Upper West Side, Aug. 16, 2022). Down in the Village, CBS New York covered a man with a garbage bag lifting packages from a Jones Street lobby at 7:30 in the morning (CBS New York, May 22, 2018). In each case, the building's own cameras, where they existed, decided whether there was anything useful to hand to the precinct.

Co-ops also carry a legal shape that rental buildings don't. A co-op board's decisions about common areas get unusual deference from New York courts: in the leading case, the Court of Appeals held that so long as a board "acts for the purposes of the cooperative, within the scope of its authority and in good faith," courts will not substitute their judgment for the board's (Levandusky v. One Fifth Ave. Apt. Corp., 75 N.Y.2d 530 (1990)). That protection rewards a board that follows its bylaws, records a resolution and treats shareholders evenly. Our job is to give the board a written camera plan clear enough to support exactly that kind of decision.

Manhattan facts that shape a co-op camera job

  • Who owns what: the corporation owns the building; shareholders hold shares and a proprietary lease, and are tenants of the corporation (NYS Attorney General).
  • Market activity: about 1,500 Manhattan co-op sales in Q4 2025, ahead of condo sales for the first time in more than a year (The Real Deal).
  • Landmarks: by the end of Fiscal 2026 the Landmarks Preservation Commission had designated 38,550 buildings and sites citywide, with 159 historic districts and extensions (Mayor's Management Report, FY2026), and large parts of the Upper West Side, Upper East Side and the Village fall inside them.
  • Facade cameras: LPC's guidance asks that equipment not go on a primary facade unless no other option exists and that cameras be "well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance" (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11).
  • Wiring permits: low-voltage signaling and data wiring under 50 volts is generally exempt from an electrical permit in the city (NYC Admin. Code §28-105.4.7); camera cabling is low-voltage work, and we do no electrical work.
  • Sidewalk sheds: the city reported taking down roughly 1,200 sheds between January and October 2026 (NYC Mayor's Office, Oct. 19, 2026), which matters to any co-op whose entrance camera has been staring at plywood during facade work.

Lobby and vestibule cameras

Courtyards, roofs and rear yards

Building Types

Camera Plans for Manhattan's Co-op Building Types

The same four-camera starting system behaves very differently in a 15-story prewar building and in a five-story walk-up. Before we price anything, we place the building in one of these groups, because the group tells us where the cable can travel, where the recorder can live and which views the board will care about most.

Prewar doorman elevator buildings

Upper East Side and Upper West Side buildings with a staffed lobby, a passenger and a service elevator, and thick masonry walls. Cable rides the service elevator shaft wall or an existing riser; lobby cameras are chosen to suit plaster ceilings and decorative moldings.

Village and Chelsea walk-ups

Five- and six-story buildings with a single stair, a tight vestibule and no staff on site. A PoE video doorbell or a small turret at the street door, plus stair-landing cameras on the lower floors, often gives the board most of what it needs.

Harlem rowhouse and HDFC co-ops

Converted brownstones and limited-equity buildings where budgets are tight and the shareholders often run the building themselves. We phase the work and keep the recorder somewhere a volunteer officer can reach.

Washington Heights and Inwood courtyard buildings

Art Deco and prewar courtyard complexes with several entrances, long hallways and big basements. The courtyard gate, every entrance and the laundry room usually come first, with cable through the basement to each stairway.

Postwar white-brick towers

East Side buildings from the 1950s to the 1970s with drop ceilings in the basement and garage below. Easier wire paths, but more cameras: garage ramp, package room, two elevators and a long service corridor.

Large limited-equity complexes

Multi-building co-ops such as Penn South, with 2,820 apartments in ten 22-story buildings between Eighth and Ninth Avenues (Penn South), need one design applied building by building and a recorder layout that the central office can manage.

Mixed-use co-ops with stores

Avenue buildings with ground-floor commercial tenants. The residential cameras stay the corporation's; a store's own cameras stay the store's. We keep the two systems apart unless the board and the tenant sign off on sharing a view.

Approvals

The Co-op Paperwork That Comes Before the First Camera

A camera job in a Manhattan co-op moves at the pace of its approvals, not its drill. None of these steps is hard, but skipping one is how a good system ends up with a shareholder dispute attached. Here is the order we see work best, with the parts we handle and the parts that belong to the board, the managing agent and the corporation's lawyer. This is practical guidance from installers, not legal advice; your counsel has the last word on your documents.

1. The board decides, on the record

Common-area cameras are a corporate decision, usually made by the board under its bylaws. We give the board a written proposal with a numbered camera schedule so the resolution can refer to a specific plan instead of "cameras in the building." When the scope is a capital project, some boards also take it to the annual meeting or a special meeting; which route your building needs is in your bylaws and proprietary lease, and the managing agent will know.

2. Shareholders hear about it before go-live

Courts give co-op boards room to decide, but shareholders who learn about a camera by spotting it on the ceiling tend to object to the process before they ever object to the camera. A one-page notice from the board or managing agent, listing the areas covered, the fact that audio is off, the retention period and who may view footage, heads off most of that. We supply a plain-language list of camera locations for the notice.

3. The managing agent handles insurance and access

Most Manhattan managing agents require a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the agent before any contractor works in the building. We provide the certificate on award, once the corporation has accepted the proposal, with the additional insureds and wording the agent sends us. The agent also sets the work hours, freight or service elevator reservations and which shareholders need notice of work near their doors.

4. Alteration agreements are for shareholder work, not common areas

An alteration agreement governs work inside a shareholder's apartment. It becomes relevant to cameras when a shareholder wants a video doorbell, a peephole camera or a camera on a private terrace, or when our cable path for a common-area camera has to pass through an apartment closet. In those cases the board's usual alteration process applies, and we work under whatever agreement the corporation requires.

5. Landmark filings where the facade is protected

If a camera must go on the street front of a landmarked building or one inside a historic district, the corporation applies to the Landmarks Preservation Commission. LPC's guidebook expects cameras to be small, painted to blend, attached at mortar joints or plain masonry rather than through decorative surfaces, and limited in number per stretch of frontage (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11). We supply the manufacturer cut sheets and mounting details the filing asks for, and we design the plan so most coverage comes from inside the vestibule, where no filing is needed.

6. The footage policy is adopted with the system

The best time to decide who sees recordings is before there is a recording anyone wants. We hand the board a draft access list (managing agent, resident manager or super, one named officer) and set the recorder's user accounts to match whatever the board adopts.

Your Questions Answered

Manhattan Co-op Camera Questions, Answered on Site

These come up on our walkthroughs with Manhattan co-op boards, house committees and supers. The answers describe how we handle each situation. Where your proprietary lease, bylaws or house rules control the answer, or where a lawyer should weigh in, we say so.

Shared rooms and shared money

Can the co-op keep a camera running in the community room when a shareholder books it for a private party?

A co-op lawyer who answered that exact question in CooperatorNews said yes: the room "remains the property of the corporation," the shareholder is "merely a licensee to utilize the space for a finite period," and a camera there is not an intrusion on privacy. He suggested cameras that do not record voices and a sign "conspicuously placed" so guests know the room is under surveillance (CooperatorNews, Nov. 2012). We install community-room cameras with audio disabled and mount the sign at the door at the same visit, and your board should confirm the policy with its own counsel.

The ground-floor store wants to share our cameras and split the bill. Should we agree?

It can work, but write it down first. We prefer two separate recorders: the corporation's cameras cover residential entrances and common areas, and the store covers its own selling floor and storefront. If the board agrees to give the store a view of a shared sidewalk or loading door, we set that up as a single read-only camera feed under a short written agreement the managing agent drafts. What we avoid is one recorder with mixed ownership, because the first insurance claim turns into an argument about who controls the footage.

The proprietary lease makes shareholders responsible for their own doors. Who pays for a hallway camera aimed at one apartment?

It depends on why the camera is there. A corridor camera the board installs to cover a common hallway is a corporate expense like any other common-area camera. A camera a shareholder asks for because of a problem at their own door is a different case, and some boards approve it on condition that the shareholder pays, the way they handle other requests for work outside an apartment. Your lease and house rules decide. Either way, we price that one camera on its own line so the board can allocate the cost however its documents require, and the camera still records to the corporation's recorder under the board's footage policy.

Prewar buildings and their quirks

Can a camera go on an ornamental plaster lobby ceiling without damaging it?

Usually we avoid the ornament altogether. Most prewar lobbies have a plain field between the decorative bands, a cornice shadow line or an existing light fixture box, and a small turret or fisheye mounted there, with cable fished from the floor above or from a service closet, leaves the plaster work untouched. When there is no plain surface at all, we look at a wall position above a doorway instead. The proposal shows each lobby mounting point in a photo so the board can approve it before anyone drills.

Our old recorder sits in the super's basement apartment. Should it move?

In most cases, yes. A recorder inside a staff apartment means nobody else can reach it when the super is off, on vacation or between jobs, and it mixes the corporation's equipment with someone's home. We move the recorder to a locked common closet near the building's internet service, put it on a battery backup, and give the super the same access through a personal login. If the cable all lands in his apartment today, we extend it to the new location rather than rewiring the cameras.

A neighboring building's camera looks straight into our courtyard. What can the board do?

We can't adjust another building's equipment, and whether its owner has to move the camera is a question for the corporation's attorney. What usually works first is a polite letter from your managing agent to the neighbor's agent, since most building owners will re-aim a camera once asked. On your side, we can make sure your own courtyard cameras are masked away from the neighbor's windows, so the conversation starts from a fair position.

Two buildings, one theft, one morning

Our corporation owns two adjoining buildings. Can one camera system serve both?

Yes, and in Manhattan it is common for one co-op corporation to own twin buildings or a building plus an attached walk-up. We run a link between the two basements, or a short network connection where the buildings share a wall, and put both sets of cameras on one recorder or on two recorders viewed in a single app. Each lobby desk can be set to show its own building first. The board gets one system, one policy and one set of logins.

What should the super do the morning after a package theft?

Write down the time window from the resident, find the moment on the recorder's timeline, and export the clip to a USB drive before the recorder overwrites it. Then hand it to the managing agent, who decides with the board how to share it with the precinct. What the super should not do is post the video to a building group chat or social media; that decision belongs to the board and its counsel. We show the super these steps at handover, and they take about five minutes.

Equipment

Co-op Surveillance Cameras We Install in Manhattan

There is no single best camera for a co-op. A Village vestibule, a Park Avenue lobby ceiling and a Washington Heights courtyard each need a different body, lens and mount. These are the pieces we choose from, in the house system by default and in partner brands when a board asks.

Turret cameras

Our everyday choice for corridors, stair landings and service doors. No dome bubble to reflect the night illuminators, simple to re-aim, and quiet-looking on a painted ceiling.

Vandal-resistant domes

Impact-rated housings for laundry rooms, bike rooms and any spot within reach. Harder to tilt away or smash with a broom handle.

360-degree fisheye cameras

One ceiling unit for a small lobby, mailroom or elevator landing. Fewer cameras, fewer cable runs through plaster, and a full view of the room.

Bullet cameras

Long, narrow views down a basement trunk corridor, along a courtyard path or across a garage ramp, where a dome would waste its field of view on the ceiling.

Dual-lens panoramic cameras

A wide stitched view from one mount for broad lobbies or long courtyards, useful where LPC limits how many devices can go on a frontage.

PoE video doorbells

For walk-up co-ops with a single street door, a network-powered doorbell camera gives a face-height view of every visitor and runs on one cable.

Elevator cab cameras

Small corner cameras paired with a wireless bridge to bring video out of a moving car, installed with your elevator contractor.

Network video recorders

The box that stores everything. Its disks match how many cameras feed it, how sharp they are and how many days the policy keeps; it lives behind a locked, vented door with a UPS beneath it to ride out flickers.

PoE switches and cabling

Power over Ethernet means a single wire to each IP camera. We tag both ends of every wire and record it in the packet the corporation keeps.

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Co-op Cameras in Manhattan

Board members now often arrive at a meeting with a printout from a chatbot. Those answers tend to assume a suburban house or a generic apartment complex. Six claims we hear in Manhattan co-op meetings, and what we find in the buildings themselves:

"The board can put cameras anywhere in the building it owns."

The corporation does own the common areas, and that gives the board wide room. It does not reach restrooms or changing areas, where state law makes hidden or improper recording a crime (Penal Law §250.45), and it does not reach into apartments through an open door. Camera placement inside a co-op is broad, but it has edges, and we draw them on the plan.

"Just buy wireless cameras; no wiring needed."

Battery and Wi-Fi cameras are built for a single house. In a masonry co-op, Wi-Fi drops between floors, batteries die in cold vestibules, and each camera ends up on someone's personal app account. Wired cameras on a recorder the corporation owns are what survive a change of super or managing agent.

"Any handyman can install building cameras."

New York licenses the installation of security systems, and the state's licensing rules cover closed-circuit television and video surveillance used to detect or monitor intrusion (NYS Department of State, security and fire alarm installer guidance). Ask any bidder for its NYS license number; ours is #12000287431. Most managing agents will also ask for insurance before work starts.

"Landmark buildings can't have cameras."

They can. LPC staff routinely permit small, well-placed security cameras, and its guidebook spells out how: away from the primary facade where possible, as small as practical, painted to blend and spaced to avoid clutter (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11). Much of a landmarked co-op's coverage comes from inside the vestibule, where no filing is required.

"Recording sound makes the footage more useful."

Capturing people's talk when none of the speakers has agreed to it counts as eavesdropping under state law, a felony (Penal Law §250.05; definitions in §250.00). A lobby microphone records shareholders, guests and staff who never consented. We leave audio off.

"A four-camera kit is all a building needs."

Four cameras cover a small walk-up's front door, vestibule, stairs and cellar door. A prewar doorman co-op on the West Side typically needs ten to twenty-plus views once the service entrance, basement corridors, laundry, bike room, elevator and roof door are counted. That is why our Manhattan price starts at $1,099 for four and each added camera is priced after the walkthrough.

2

7

Light, glass and old plaster in Manhattan co-ops

Privacy and the Law

Where Co-op Cameras Can Point, and Where They Can't

Boards ask us about the law at almost every walkthrough. We are installers, not lawyers, so this section sticks to what published sources say and to how we apply them on the job. The corporation's counsel should review any policy before the board adopts it.

Common areas are corporate property

Lobbies, corridors, elevators, basements and community rooms belong to the co-op corporation. A New York co-op attorney writing in CooperatorNews noted that governing documents typically bar owners from changing common elements without board approval, and that "New York does not recognize a common law right of privacy in public or common areas" (CooperatorNews Q&A, Ricardo M. Vera, April 2025). That is why boards can generally place cameras in shared spaces, provided they follow their own documents.

Places a camera never goes

New York's unlawful surveillance statute covers recording in places such as a "bedroom, changing room, fitting room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom, shower" without knowledge or consent and for no legitimate purpose (Penal Law §250.45). Co-op buildings also employ staff, and FirstService Residential's guidance notes that state Labor Law §203-c bars employers from recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms or areas where they change clothes (FirstService Residential). In practice we keep cameras out of the porters' locker room, staff restrooms and the super's apartment, and we aim corridor cameras away from those doors.

Apartment doors and windows

Hallway cameras "must be carefully placed to avoid capturing footage that shows the inside of any residential unit," in FirstService's words. We follow that literally: corridor cameras are mounted at the ends of halls and aimed along them, so an open apartment door is seen edge-on, and any window that remains in an outdoor view is masked.

The board's rules have limits too

A board's authority comes from its documents. In a Habitat Q&A, attorney Andrew Lieb said that "a house rule that is unauthorized by these foundational documents is unenforceable" (Habitat, Aug. 28, 2023). The question there involved a condo, but the principle travels: a co-op camera policy should rest on the bylaws, the proprietary lease and a proper board resolution, which is exactly what Levandusky rewards.

Shareholders' own cameras

When a shareholder wants a doorbell camera or a peephole camera on their apartment door, the door's exterior and the hallway are usually common areas. Most boards handle these requests through the house rules or the alteration process. If the board approves, we install it with audio off and the view limited to the shareholder's own doorway; if the board says no, we don't install it.

This section summarizes published sources for general information. It is not legal advice. Ask the corporation's attorney how these rules apply to your building.

Footage Policy

A Footage Access Policy a Co-op Board Can Actually Run

A camera system without a written policy works fine right up to the first serious incident, and then every shareholder, board member and staff member has a different idea of who may watch what. A good co-op footage policy fits on one page and answers six questions. We set up the recorder to match whatever the board decides.

  1. Who may watch live video? Usually the doorman at the desk and the super, with view-only access.
  2. Who may search and export recordings? Usually the managing agent and the resident manager or super, with a named board officer as backup. FirstService suggests boards delegate this to the property manager and resident manager to "limit unnecessary access."
  3. What is the overwrite cycle? A month to three months is the norm FirstService describes ("for 30 to 90 days depending on the building size"). Whatever number the directors choose drives our hard-disk sizing.
  4. Who is told when footage is reviewed? The managing agent keeps "a log of requests and a list of anyone who accessed footage," again per FirstService. Our recorders keep their own user log as a second record.
  5. What happens with outside requests? Police, insurers and lawyers' requests go to the managing agent, who decides with counsel and records the request.
  6. When is footage preserved longer? When an incident may lead to a claim, the board asks its attorney whether to save the relevant clips beyond the normal cycle.

Christina Forbes of FirstService Residential's Manhattan condo and co-op division put the reason for limiting access this way: "Limiting access protects the privacy of residents and helps prevent potential misuse" (FirstService Residential, Sept. 23, 2024). In our experience the policy also protects the super, who otherwise gets asked by every shareholder with a grievance to "just pull up the tape."

Telling shareholders

Send the notice before the cameras go live. A short letter from the managing agent covers the areas with cameras, that sound is not recorded, how long footage is kept, who can see it and how to ask a question. Post the same summary in the laundry room and by the mailboxes. Shareholders who sublet should pass it to their subtenants; the managing agent can add a line about cameras to the sublet package.

Elevators & Service Doors

Elevator and Service-Entrance Coverage in Manhattan Co-ops

Ask a Manhattan super where trouble gets into a co-op and the answer is rarely the front door. The doorman watches the front door. The service entrance on the side street, the basement door at the bottom of the areaway stairs and the elevator, where people ride for a minute with no one watching, are where most of the incidents we are called about begin. A co-op camera plan that treats those spaces as afterthoughts leaves the board with excellent footage of the lobby and nothing from the places that mattered.

The service entrance

In a prewar doorman building, the service entrance handles deliveries, contractors working on renovations, movers, porters taking out trash and, in many buildings, residents with bikes or strollers. It is usually a steel door off a side street with a short corridor to the service elevator. We cover it from the inside, looking toward the door, high enough that the camera can't be reached and angled so the face of anyone stepping in is lit by the corridor fixtures rather than silhouetted against daylight. A second camera at the service elevator landing, facing the car doors, catches everyone who goes up from there. If the building uses a buzzer or intercom at that door, the camera goes where it complements the intercom's own view rather than duplicating it.

The basement door and areaway

Many Manhattan co-ops have a basement entrance below the stoop or behind an iron gate, used by the super, utility meter readers and trash pickup. That door is often the least watched and most propped open. An outdoor bullet camera inside the areaway, aimed up the steps toward the gate, gives the board a record of who comes down, and a turret inside the door covers the corridor beyond it. Where the areaway sits under apartment windows, the outdoor camera is masked so it shows the steps and the gate only.

Elevator landings and cabs

The lobby landing is easy: a camera across from the elevator doors records everyone waiting and everyone stepping out. The cab is harder and needs your elevator contractor, because power and mounting on a moving car are their territory. We bring the camera and a wireless bridge that sends video from the car top to a receiver in the hoistway or machine room, and we schedule the installation day around the contractor's technician. In older prewar cars with manual gates or wood paneling, we pick a small corner camera that can sit in an existing seam rather than through the paneling. Freight and service cars, which carry contractors and deliveries, are often a higher priority than the passenger car.

Stairs and roof doors

Fire stairs give access to every floor without passing the desk, which is why many boards want a camera at the bottom landing and inside the roof door. We mount stair cameras so they don't interfere with the stair's use or its signage, and we do not touch fire alarm devices, door hardware that is part of the building's egress, or anything that requires electrical work. If the roof door has an alarm or a sign, the camera simply records who opens it.

Landmarked Buildings

Cameras on Landmarked Co-op Facades in Manhattan

A large share of Manhattan's co-op buildings sit inside historic districts on the Upper West Side, the Upper East Side, in Greenwich Village, Chelsea and Hamilton Heights, or are individual landmarks. For those boards, the street facade is the one place a camera can't simply go up. It also rarely needs to. In most landmarked co-ops we cover the approach from inside the vestibule or the lobby door, and the facade camera, if any, becomes a short, separate decision for the board.

When a facade camera does make sense, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook sets the expectations. Equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists. Cameras should be as small as possible and painted to blend. They must be "well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance." The guidebook limits the number of cameras per stretch of frontage, with tighter numbers for small buildings than for large residential ones, asks for conduit to be concealed, and directs that hardware attach at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork rather than through cast iron or decorative surfaces (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11). Applications include the manufacturer's cut sheets for the camera.

Our part is the design and the paperwork that supports the corporation's application: the camera model and its cut sheet, a photo of the proposed location marked up with the mounting point, the cable path and how it is hidden, and the color the housing will be painted. The corporation, usually through the managing agent or the building's architect or expediter, submits the application. We don't install a facade camera on a landmarked building until the permit is in hand, and the proposal lists that camera separately so the rest of the system isn't held up waiting for it.

A practical tip for boards: if a sidewalk shed is up for facade work, the best time to plan an exterior camera is before the shed comes down, because the exterior work is already on the managing agent's agenda and the camera's mounting point and cable path can be settled while the facade is being repaired.

Walk-Up Co-ops

Walk-Up Co-ops: Making Recorded Video Do the Work

A five-story walk-up in the Village or a Harlem rowhouse co-op has no doorman, often no live-in super and a board made up of volunteers who also work full-time jobs. In those buildings nobody is watching a screen, so the cameras are about two things: discouraging strangers who slip in behind residents, and having a clear recording when something does happen. That changes the design.

  • The street door gets the best camera in the building. A 4K turret in the vestibule or a PoE video doorbell at the door itself, so a face is identifiable, not just a figure.
  • Mail and packages get their own view. In walk-ups, packages sit in the vestibule or on the first landing, and that is where thefts happen.
  • The cellar door and the rear yard come next, since they are the quiet ways in and out.
  • Storage sized generously, because a theft in a walk-up is often noticed days later, when a resident gets home from a trip.
  • Phone access for two or three named people, such as the board president, the treasurer and the managing agent, each with a personal login the board can switch off.
  • Motion alerts used sparingly, on the cellar door at night for example, so that volunteers aren't buried in notifications every time a neighbor comes home.

Most walk-up co-ops can start with our four-camera Starter system at $1,099 on the Manhattan price list and add a camera or two as the budget allows. Because there is no staff to train, the handover is aimed at the board members who will hold the logins: how to find an event on the timeline, how to save a clip, and how to remove a user.

Network Security

Keeping the Co-op's Camera Network Private

A camera system is a small computer network inside the building, and the weakest version of it is one that shares a router with the super's laptop and still uses the password printed on the box. In a co-op, where staff, vendors and managing agents change over the years, the network has to outlast every one of them.

  • Cameras on their own network. Network cameras plug into the recorder's built-in PoE ports or a dedicated switch, so they never sit on the building's guest Wi-Fi or an office network.
  • No default passwords. Every camera and the recorder get unique passwords at installation, recorded in the handover packet the corporation keeps.
  • Remote viewing through the recorder's app, with each viewer on a personal account the board can switch off, rather than a shared login passed around by text message.
  • Firmware updated at handover, with instructions for the managing agent on checking for updates.
  • The recorder in a locked, ventilated closet on a battery backup, so the system can't be unplugged by someone walking by and rides through brief power dips.
  • A written account list so that when the super changes, the board removes one user rather than resetting the whole system.

Remote viewing raises a fair question at board meetings: who else could be looking? With the setup above, only the people holding a login the corporation issued, and the recorder logs each sign-in. If the board prefers that nobody view off site at all, we can leave remote access switched off and the system records locally only; the trade-off is that the managing agent then has to come to the building, or ask the super, to pull a clip.

None of this needs a monthly service contract. It needs a careful setup and a corporation that holds its own keys, which is how every co-op system we install is handed over.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Look at First in a Manhattan Co-op

After years of walking cooperative buildings across the borough, our technicians check the same things in roughly the same order. If you are a super getting ready for our visit, this is what we will ask to see.

  1. The existing recorder, and whether anyone can log in. In older co-ops the answer is often no; the person who set it up left years ago. If nobody has the password, the recorder usually has to be reset or replaced, and we say so up front.
  2. Where the cable can travel. Service elevator shaft walls, old dumbwaiter shafts, abandoned telephone risers and basement ceilings are the usual highways. Lobby plaster is the last resort.
  3. Every door to the outside, including the ones nobody thinks of: the coal chute turned storage door, the areaway gate under the stoop, the roof bulkhead and the door from the neighboring building's yard.
  4. Where light comes from at night, because a camera facing a bare bulb or a glass door at dusk records silhouettes.
  5. The staff rooms, so we can mark them on the plan as places cameras must not see.
  6. The elevator machine room, if a cab camera is in scope, and the elevator contractor's name.
  7. Landmark status, which we check on the city's map before the proposal goes out.
  8. Where the building's internet comes in, if the board wants remote viewing.

One pattern repeats in Manhattan co-ops more than anywhere else: an older system recording on a schedule nobody remembers setting, or overwriting itself every few days because the drive is tiny. Boards discover it the morning after an incident. On every walkthrough we check how many days of video the current recorder actually holds, so the board knows before it needs to know.
