The Neighborhood

Why Windsor Terrace Properties Need Access Control

Windsor Terrace is small and oddly shaped. Wikipedia describes it as a curved, somewhat comma-like strip of about half a square mile, held between Prospect Park on the east and Green-Wood Cemetery on the southwest, with Park Slope across Prospect Park West, Kensington below Caton Avenue and Borough Park beyond McDonald Avenue. The Prospect Expressway runs through the middle of it in a trench, and the two halves are stitched back together by bridges. The Landmarks Preservation Commission, in the report it wrote when it designated the neighborhood’s firehouse in 2013, calls it “a small residential enclave” set between the park and the cemetery, on the southeastern slope of the terminal moraine that runs from northern Queens to Bay Ridge.

It began as a speculation that failed. According to the same Landmarks report, a developer bought the old Vanderbilt farm here in 1849 and named the site Windsor Terrace; Edward Belknap bought it in 1851, opened Seeley Street, Vanderbilt Street, Adams Street (now Reeve Place) and Short Street (now Prospect Avenue), and divided the land into 49 building lots he called “Pleasant Cottage” sites. Belknap and his partners lost their interest in 1856. By 1860 there were only about a dozen houses and 30 people. After the Civil War, Irish immigrants, mostly from County Longford and County Cavan, settled here; Holy Name Church went up on Ninth Avenue, now Prospect Park West, in 1878, and by 1890 there were about 150 houses, most of them small frame buildings.

The houses most callers live in came next. The Landmarks report says trolley lines on Ninth Avenue and 15th Street brought row houses in the early 1900s, “many erected by developer William M. Calder who built and sold 700 houses in Windsor Terrace between 1902 and 1919.” Brownstoner, quoting a 1919 Brooklyn Daily Eagle profile, says he built so many that the area was known as Calderville. Calder was a prolific builder and later a United States senator, and his houses were sold by advertisement: a 1909 ad called his Sherman Street two-families “honestly constructed,” and 1915 ads for his row on Windsor Place promised parquet floors, hardwood trim, built-in iceboxes, basement laundry, ornamental parapets and open front porches.

Those porches are where the door story starts. Brownstoner’s 2026 histories of two houses in that Windsor Place row, designed by the architects Eisenla and Carlson from plans filed in 1914, note that tax photos from around 1940 already show many of the porches enclosed. On those houses the porch became a sunroom, a mudroom or a small extra room, and the street door moved forward to the front of the porch. Other rows kept theirs: Brownstoner points to a row of Doric-columned porches on Fuller Place that has stayed largely unchanged since the 1910s. So a Windsor Terrace front entrance is one of two quite different things, an open porch with the original door at the back of it, or an enclosed porch with a lightweight outer door and the original door inside. The right lock, reader and station depend entirely on which one you have.

The second thing is how many households live behind that entrance. Calder sold two-families as well as one-family houses. Brownstoner’s 2020 history of a bow-fronted limestone house on Sherman Street, one of a row built around 1908, describes it still laid out as a two-family: an owner’s duplex on the garden and parlor floors and a two-bedroom floor-through apartment above. Wikipedia adds that many more single- and two-family houses went up in the 1920s, and that the 1933 arrival of the subway brought apartment buildings. Under New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law, a house occupied by not more than two families is a private dwelling; three or more families living independently make it a multiple dwelling. That line, between two households and three, decides which city rules apply to an electronic front door, and we plan around it.

The third is that a great many of these houses are changing hands. Time Out reported in January 2026 that Windsor Terrace placed third on StreetEasy’s list of the most in-demand New York City neighborhoods, with searches up almost 45 percent. Brownstoner listed one Calder row house on Windsor Place at $2.6 million in February 2026 and another on Sherman Street at $2.65 million in 2020. When a house that one family held for decades is sold, nobody can say how many keys are out there: with relatives, neighbors, a long-gone tenant, a contractor from the 1990s. New owners usually renovate as well, which means months of trades going in and out. Both moments, the closing and the renovation, are when a door system costs least to fit and does the most good.

None of this is a story about crime, and we do not sell it as one. We have no neighborhood crime figures newer than a 2020 news report, and we do not forecast crime. But the local record does show why a plan beats a gadget. In August 2020 Brooklyn Paper reported that one house on Kermit Place was burglarized twice in a month; the first time the intruder took jewelry and cash, and the second time, police believe the same man took the house’s battery-powered security camera. A camera that can be lifted off its mount is not much of a record. In 2017 Patch relayed a 72nd Precinct warning after a man was arrested near Windsor Place and Fuller Place for trying car door handles until one opened. What doors and cars have in common is that the unlocked one is the one that gets tried. A good door system makes locking automatic and keeps its record somewhere a thief cannot carry away.

Access Control for Windsor Terrace’s Architecture

Because so much of Windsor Terrace was put up by one builder in a few years, the same house repeats along whole blocks. That helps: once we have seen the cellar, the porch and the party walls of one house in a row, we know roughly what the neighbors look like. The types below cover nearly everything we are asked about between Prospect Park West and Caton Avenue.

Calder brick rows with enclosed porches

Two-story brick row houses from the 1910s, like the Windsor Place row by Eisenla and Carlson, whose open porches were closed in by around 1940. Two front doors: the porch door at the steps and the original door inside.

Rows that kept their open porches

Rows such as the Doric-columned porches on Fuller Place, where the original front door still faces the street from the back of the porch, under a roof but open to the weather.

Limestone bow-front two-families

Three-story bow-fronted houses like Calder’s Sherman Street row of about 1908, built as two-families: an owner’s duplex below and a floor-through apartment above, sharing a stoop and an entry hall.

Early frame houses and cottages

Small clapboard houses with attics from the neighborhood’s first decades, and the row of Victorian “jewel box” houses and cottages Forgotten New York describes on East 5th Street. Wood frames, wood doors, often altered many times.

1920s houses and apartment buildings

Single- and two-family houses and two large apartment buildings on Prospect Avenue that the Landmarks report attributes to Seeley Investors, plus the apartment buildings that followed the 1933 subway.

Newer and coming buildings

The 17 two-family houses built in 1983 on the old Pilgrim Laundry site, recent infill along Prospect Avenue, and the two ten-story buildings approved for 441 and 467 Prospect Avenue in 2025.

What the materials mean for the install

Brick party walls. An attached row house has solid brick walls shared with the neighbors on both sides. We never run cable inside a party wall or drill through one. Cable for a rear door travels along the cellar ceiling, front to back, and comes up inside the wall or trim next to the door it serves.

Porch enclosures. An enclosed porch is a newer, lighter structure bolted onto an old house. Its walls are often thin framing with a lot of glass, and its door may be an aluminum storm-and-entry door rather than a solid wood one. A strike needs solid wood or steel behind it; when the porch frame cannot give us that, we lock the original door instead and treat the porch as a lobby.

Limestone fronts. On the bow-front houses the stone around the entrance is the part people admire, and it is also the part that cracks if drilled carelessly. We mount stations on the wood door frame or inside the vestibule wherever we can, and if stone must be drilled we use the mortar joints, never the face of a carved block.

Cellars. The 1915 ads promised basement laundry, and most Calder cellars are still the room where the washer, the boiler and the electrical panel live. It is the natural place for the controller and its battery, provided we mount them high, away from the floor drain and the dryer vent.

Two Front Doors: What the Enclosed Porch Changes

Nearly every quote we write for an enclosed-porch row house turns on one decision: which of the two front doors becomes the controlled door. The porch door is the one visitors reach first, and the one most owners picture a keypad on. The original door is the one that actually separates the street from the house. Choosing well means looking at how the porch is used today, how it is built, and who needs to get into it without getting into the house.

Option one: lock the porch door, leave the inner door on its key

This works when the porch is part of the house: heated, furnished, used every day as a sitting room or an office, with nothing in it you would hand to a stranger. A keypad deadbolt on the porch door then behaves like a front-door keypad anywhere else. It suits porches with a solid door in a solid frame. It does not suit porches with a thin aluminum door, single-pane glass beside the lock, or no heat at all, because the door is only as strong as the glass next to it and battery locks in an unheated porch lose capacity fast in a January cold snap.

Option two: lock the original door, treat the porch as a lobby

This is our most common recommendation. The original door is usually a heavy wood door in a frame built with the house, the right place for an electric strike or a motorized deadbolt. The porch door stays on a simple lock, a keypad that is open during the day, or a closer and latch. Packages, coats, strollers and visitors wait in the porch; the house stays locked. The station goes at the porch door so callers can ring before they come in, and the indoor screen lets you see who is in the porch before you open the inner door.

Option three: both doors on one system

For two-family houses, home offices and houses with frequent deliveries, both doors go on the same controller. The porch door opens on a schedule or to a courier code; the inner door opens only to household credentials. The log then shows which door opened, so a box left in the porch at two in the afternoon and an inner door opened at two-fifteen are two separate entries rather than a single “front door” line.

Glass, weather and the open porch

An open porch protects the door from rain but not from wind-driven snow, and Windsor Terrace sits on a slope where weather comes straight off the park. Readers and stations on an open porch need to be rated for outdoor use, mounted under the porch roof, and wired so that water running down the cable cannot reach the connector. On an enclosed porch the opposite problem appears: in summer a south-facing glass porch can get much hotter than the street, and the inside of the porch door bakes. We pick hardware rated for the temperature range the porch actually reaches, not the range of a hallway.

Getting cable into a porch that was added later

The porch enclosure was built decades after the house, often on top of a brick or concrete porch slab with no basement underneath. There is rarely a hollow wall from the cellar up into the porch. Our usual route is up through the cellar to the inside of the original front wall, then out through the old door frame into the porch, where a short, painted raceway on the inside of the porch frame carries the cable to the porch door. On porches where even that is impractical, the porch door gets a battery lock that talks to a hub inside the house, and only the inner door is wired.

Two Households Behind One Stoop: Where the City’s Rules Begin

Calder built two-families on purpose, and many are still lived in that way: an owner below, a tenant or a relative above, one stoop and one street door between them. A door system in a house like that has two jobs. It has to let each household come and go and invite its own visitors without asking the other. And it has to be honest about what it records, because an entry log in a shared house is a record of the neighbors’ lives as well as your own.

The law draws a line here that owners rarely know about. Section 4 of New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law defines a private dwelling as a building occupied by not more than two families, and a multiple dwelling as one occupied by three or more families living independently of each other. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to “smart access” systems, meaning fobs, cards, phone apps or biometrics, in class A multiple dwellings. HPD’s summary of that law says owners must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, get express consent before collecting reference data, collect only a limited set of information for security purposes, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is kept anonymized, never use the data to harass or evict, and never require a smart access system to get into a tenant’s own apartment.

A two-family house sits below that threshold, so the city law does not, on its face, reach it. We set up two-family systems as if it did anyway, for a practical reason: houses change. A cellar apartment gets added, a house is converted, a two-family is sold to someone who rents both floors and lives elsewhere. When a third household arrives the building crosses into the multiple dwelling rules, and a system that already keeps logs short, collects nothing it does not need and leaves the apartment door on a key needs no rebuilding. We are not lawyers, and an owner considering a third unit should ask one; what we can do is make sure the hardware is not the obstacle.

What we set up in a Calder two-family

  • Two administrators, not one. Each household gets its own account on the system and adds or removes its own cleaners, sitters and guests. Neither sees the other’s list.
  • Logs kept short. The street door keeps entries for a limited period, set with the owner at installation, long enough to look into a problem and short enough not to become a diary.
  • Apartment doors on a key as well. The upstairs apartment door can have a keypad, but it also keeps a working mechanical key, because the tenant must never depend on an app to get home.
  • Separate bells. The porch or stoop station has one button per household, each ringing only its own floor and its own phones.
  • A written page for the tenant. We leave a one-page note describing what the street door records, who can see it and how long it is kept, which the owner can attach to the lease.

When a Calder House Changes Hands

The busiest moment for door work in Windsor Terrace is the first month after a closing. The sellers may have lived in the house for forty years; the keys they hand over are the ones they could find. A locksmith can change the cylinders in an afternoon, and that is often the right first step. But if a renovation is coming, as it usually is, the better plan is to put the doors on a system before the trades arrive, give each trade its own code with an end date, and finish the permanent hardware when the walls are closed.

The second reason to plan at the closing is the cellar. Most renovations of these houses open the cellar ceiling to replace old wiring and plumbing. For a few weeks the joists are exposed from the front wall to the back, and cable for a porch station, an inner-door strike and a rear-door reader can be laid in an hour that would cost a day once the ceiling is closed. We ask buyers to call us when the electrician is booked, not when the painter is.

What to do in the first month

  • Day of closing: change or rekey every exterior cylinder, including the cellar door, the porch door and any yard gate. Ask the sellers for alarm codes, garage remotes and the logins for any smart lock or doorbell camera, and remove their accounts.
  • First week: walk the house with us. We list every door, every old bell wire and intercom handset, and every device that runs on a battery or depends on someone else’s app.
  • Before the trades start: put the doors the crews will use on temporary keypad locks with one code per trade and an end date, so nobody needs a copy of the new key.
  • While the cellar ceiling is open: pull cable to the porch door, the original front door, the rear door and the place the indoor screens will go.
  • When the walls close: fit the permanent strike, station and readers, cancel the trade codes and hand the administrator account to you.

Old equipment deserves a second look before it goes in the skip. A working bell wire from the porch to the kitchen is a ready-made path for a new doorbell circuit. A wired chime transformer in the cellar can tell us where the old bell circuit was powered. Battery doorbell cameras are another matter: they are easy to install and easy to remove, which is the problem the Kermit Place burglary illustrates. We usually replace them with a wired station whose recordings are kept off the device.

Rules that apply to Windsor Terrace business doors

The New York City Building Code requires egress doors to open from the inside without a key or special knowledge. Two sections matter most for small shops. Section 1010.1.9.8 governs doors released by a motion sensor, with a manual push button marked PUSH TO EXIT between 40 and 48 inches high and within five feet of the door, a relock delay of at least 30 seconds and release on loss of power or fire alarm. Section 1010.1.9.9 governs electromagnetic locks, which need hardware on the door that releases them with one hand and must unlock when power is lost. We favor electric strikes and electrified levers on shop doors because the inside lever always works mechanically, whatever happens to the power.

A shop door is also often the only way out of the shop. That door stays on its key and timed lock; we control the rear staff or delivery door, which carries the daily traffic of employees and suppliers. The Americans with Disabilities Act asks for hardware that can be worked without tight grasping or twisting, which is another reason we fit levers rather than knobs. When a shop’s door ties into an alarm or a building fire system, that interface is designed with the alarm contractor, not improvised.

Access Control in Windsor Terrace: Questions Answered

These are the questions Windsor Terrace owners actually put to us, grouped by the part of the house they concern. Each is answered here in full; the FAQ further down covers different ground.

The enclosed porch

Decades ago someone glassed in our porch, so the house’s original door now sits inside it. Does the electronic lock belong on the outer porch door or on that original door?

In most Calder rows we put it on the original door. That door is solid wood in a frame built with the house, so an electric strike or motorized deadbolt has something strong to hold, and it is the door that actually separates the street from your rooms. The porch door then becomes the lobby door: on a plain lock, a daytime keypad or a latch and closer. We move the lock to the porch door only when the porch is heated, used daily as a room and has a solid door in a solid frame. If you want the convenience at both, both can go on one controller.

Couriers leave boxes inside our closed-in porch whenever the outer door is unlocked. Can the porch accept a delivery without anyone opening the house?

Yes, and this is one of the best uses of an enclosed porch. The porch door gets a keypad or reader with a courier code that works during delivery hours, or a station that lets you open it remotely when a driver rings. The original door stays locked to everything except household credentials. Each porch opening is logged, and an indoor screen or your phone shows the porch, so you can see a box arrive without walking down. Many carriers’ own in-home delivery programs are built for a whole house; this keeps them to the porch.

The porch walls are mostly glass in thin wood framing. Is that frame strong enough to hold an electric strike?

Often it is not, and we would rather say so than fit one. A strike works by holding the latch against a pull, and that force goes into the jamb. In a light porch frame the jamb can flex, and the glass beside the lock means the door is only as secure as the pane next to it. Where the frame is weak we either reinforce the latch side with a steel jamb plate during the install, fit a keypad deadbolt whose bolt goes into a reinforced strike box, or leave the porch door simple and control the original door behind it.

We work from the enclosed porch and clients visit it. Can the porch door stand unlocked during office hours while the house door stays shut?

That is exactly what a two-door setup is for. The porch door goes on a schedule, unlocked during the hours you see clients and locked automatically after, while the original door stays on household credentials only. A small station at the porch door lets late arrivals ring you. If the porch office has files or equipment you do not want visible from the street, we would also put a door contact on the porch door so you get an alert if it is left open after hours.

Our porch is unheated: freezing in January, an oven in July. Which locks and readers survive conditions like that?

Look at the rated operating temperature, not the marketing. Many indoor smart locks are rated for hallway conditions, and their batteries lose capacity in hard cold, which is why porch keypads tend to fail on the first frosty morning. For an unheated porch we use readers and stations rated for outdoor use, wired power instead of batteries where the cable can reach, and lithium cells where it cannot. On a south-facing glass porch we keep electronics out of direct sun and off the glass.

Party walls, cellars and back yards

Our row house shares brick walls with the houses on both sides. How does cable reach the kitchen door at the back without opening our finished rooms?

Through the cellar. In a Calder row the cellar runs the full depth of the house, and its ceiling joists span from party wall to party wall, so a cable can travel from the front of the cellar to the back along the joists without touching a finished room. At the back it comes up beside the kitchen door inside the door trim or a short wall cavity. We never run cable inside a shared party wall or drill through it, both because it is your neighbor’s wall too and because a brick party wall is often a fire separation.

Our cellar door is at the bottom of outside steps behind a low iron gate. Which should be controlled, the gate or the door?

The door. A low gate keeps out leaves and stray deliveries, not people, and a gate at the bottom of an areaway is hard to wire and takes a beating from weather. The cellar door is the real barrier, and it is usually the least watched door on the house. We fit a lock and a door contact on it so it reports when it is left unlocked or open; if you also want the gate to latch on its own, a self-closing hinge and a simple latch do that without electronics.

On an attached row the only way into our back yard is through the house. How can a gardener or contractor work out back without the run of the rooms?

Give them a route, not a house key. The cellar usually has a door to the yard or a rear areaway; put that door on a keypad and give the gardener a code that works on their day, between their hours, and only there. Inside, the door from the cellar to the parlor floor stays locked. If the route has to go through the house, a temporary code on the front door plus a lock on any rooms you want closed does the same job, and the log shows when they arrived and left.

Our kitchen opens to a rear deck several steps above the yard. Where does a reader go on a door that high up?

Beside the door on the deck, at a normal reach height from the deck surface, under an overhang if there is one. The deck makes the door easy to reach for you and for anyone who climbs the steps, so it deserves the same hardware as the front. The cable comes up inside the rear wall from the cellar rather than along the deck, which would expose it to weather and to anyone with snips. If the deck is to be rebuilt soon, we leave a sleeve in place for the cable.

The only sensible spot for the controller is the cellar laundry corner, beside the washer and dryer. Is that too damp for the equipment?

It is fine if mounted correctly, and it is where most Calder houses put their electrical panel anyway. We fix the controller and its battery high on the wall, away from the dryer vent and the floor drain, in a closed enclosure with the cable entering from below so water cannot run into it. If the cellar has ever flooded, we raise everything well above the high-water mark and tell you which parts would need replacing if it happened again.

Buying, renovating and the record

We close next month on a house the sellers owned for forty years. Who might still have keys, and what should change first?

Assume relatives, neighbors, former tenants, a cleaner and past contractors could all have copies, and that nobody kept a list. Rekey or replace every exterior cylinder on the day you get the keys, including the porch, cellar and yard doors. Then ask the sellers for any alarm codes and app logins and remove their accounts. That covers the gap. If a renovation is planned, the electronic system goes in when the cellar ceiling is open, so the rekey is a stopgap and the codes become the long-term answer.

The sellers left an old wired bell, two intercom handsets and a battery doorbell camera. What is worth keeping?

Usually the wires, rarely the devices. A working bell circuit gives us a route from the porch to the kitchen and a powered transformer we can reuse for a new wired doorbell or a small station. Old intercom handsets can sometimes be kept on a new door station through an adapter, but if they are from the middle of the last century, spare parts will be the problem. The battery camera is easy to remove, which is its weakness; we take its account off the sellers’ phones and replace it with a wired camera or station that records off the device.

We are gutting the parlor floor this winter but will not add a door system until next year. What should our contractor leave in the walls?

Ask for a few cables and boxes, not equipment. One Cat6 cable and one multi-conductor lock cable from the cellar to the latch side of the original front door; one Cat6 to the porch door or the spot beside it; one Cat6 to each place you might want an indoor screen; and a Cat6 to the rear door. Each should end in a labeled box with a few feet of slack, with the other end coiled and labeled in the cellar. We are glad to mark the positions on your plans before the walls close.

Couldn’t a burglar simply unscrew a battery doorbell camera from the porch and walk off with it?

Yes, and in 2020 Brooklyn Paper reported exactly that happening during a burglary on Kermit Place. A battery camera held by two screws, with its recordings on the device or tied to one phone, can disappear along with its evidence. A wired door station is fixed with tamper screws, takes its power from the cellar, and sends its video and its door events to a recorder or a cloud account elsewhere, so removing it ends the recording but not the record. That is why we put the recorder somewhere other than beside the front door.

Is any of Windsor Terrace landmarked, so that a station on our porch would need Landmarks approval?

Very little, as far as we could find. The Prospect Avenue firehouse of Engine 240 is an individual landmark, designated in 2013, and Patch reported that part of the large Park Slope historic district extension reaches into blocks some locals consider Windsor Terrace. We found no Windsor Terrace historic district. That generally gives owners more freedom than in Park Slope or Prospect Park South, but check your own address on the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s map before work starts; if it falls inside a district, we prepare the drawings for the staff-level permit with you.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Windsor Terrace

Google’s AI Overview could not be loaded for Windsor Terrace searches in our build environment, so nothing below is a quotation from it. These are the generalizations that recur in quick online answers, consumer how-to articles and national price guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, each set against what a house in this neighborhood actually needs.

“Put the smart lock on your front door.”

Nearly every quick guide assumes a house has one front door. On an enclosed-porch row house it has two, and the advice does not say which.

Put a smart lock on a thin porch door and the house behind it is still on its old key; put it on the inner door and the porch is open to anyone who tries the handle.

The answer depends on how the porch is built and used, which is why our quotes start with a photo of the porch and a photo of the door inside it.

“A video doorbell is access control.”

Doorbell cameras are often presented as the whole solution. They tell you someone is at the door; they do not decide who gets in or record which door opened.

A battery doorbell can also be taken with its recordings, as the Kermit Place burglary showed in 2020.

Access control means locks that respond to credentials, a log of each door, and a record stored away from the door itself. A camera is a useful part of that, not a substitute for it.

“Wireless is best for older homes.”

Wireless locks avoid cutting plaster, and in a finished room that is a real advantage. Consumer guides push them hard for that reason.

But a Calder row has brick party walls on both sides and a cellar running its full depth, and that cellar makes wiring easier than in many newer houses. Meanwhile a battery lock in an unheated porch loses capacity in the cold.

We wire the doors that matter through the cellar and use wireless only where the cable genuinely cannot go, such as some porch doors on slabs.

“Change your locks when you move in.”

This is sound advice as far as it goes, and we repeat it. It is also incomplete for a house that is about to be renovated.

New keys handed to a dozen trades are as hard to track as the old ones. A rekey on day one plus temporary codes for each trade, cancelled when the work ends, keeps the new keys in the family.

And the closing is also the moment to remove the sellers’ app accounts, alarm codes and garage remotes, which a locksmith does not touch.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of a battery smart lock and call it a door. That is the price of a part.

A controlled door with a strike, a reader, cable from the cellar and programmed fobs is on our Brooklyn list at $1,500 to $2,500, and a keypad on a service door at $1,500 to $2,000.

Windsor Terrace pays the Brooklyn base list, with no surcharge for the neighborhood or the age of the house.

“Landlords can monitor who enters their building.”

Generic answers treat any rental the same. In New York City, a system in a building with three or more apartments is covered by the Tenant Data Privacy Law, with consent, retention and use limits.

A two-family house sits below that line under the Multiple Dwelling Law, but the courtesy, and the practical sense, are the same: keep logs short and give each household its own account.

An owner who plans a third unit should take legal advice; we make sure the hardware does not stand in the way.

“Neighborhood crime rates tell you what to install.”

Some answers lead with crime statistics. The latest Windsor Terrace figure we found is a 2020 news report of burglaries in the 72nd Precinct, which covers far more than this neighborhood.

Statistics do not tell you which of your doors is left unlocked. The 2017 car break-ins near Windsor Place were about door handles that opened, not about the block.

We plan from the doors and the people who use them, and we leave forecasting to the precinct.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Row Before the House

This describes how we approach builder-repeated rows in general, not a particular customer. Before we knock, we walk the block. On a row put up by one builder in one season, the houses started identical, so the differences between them tell a story: which porches were enclosed and which were not, which ones were enclosed with wood and which with aluminum, which houses have a cellar door under the porch and which have had it bricked up. Ten minutes on the sidewalk shows us what the house we are about to enter probably looked like on the day it was sold, and what has been changed since.

Then we go to the cellar. In a row like this the cellar is the most honest room in the house: you can see where the porch slab meets the front wall, whether the joists are open from front to back, where old bell wires come down from the porch, and how far the electrical panel is from the front door. Only after that do we look at the porch door and the original door and talk about which one to control. Owners sometimes expect us to start with the keypad. We start with the joists, because they decide how much the keypad will cost.
