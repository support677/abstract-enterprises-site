The Neighborhood

Why Edenwald Properties Need Access Control

Edenwald is easy to miss on a map because it has never had firm edges. Bronx Community Board 12 lists it as one of eight neighborhoods in its district, alongside Baychester, Eastchester, Fishbay, Olinville, Wakefield, Williamsbridge and Woodlawn, and Wikipedia’s article on Eastchester treats Edenwald as a sub-neighborhood of Eastchester rather than a place of its own. Neighborhood guides draw it from roughly East 222nd Street north to the Westchester County line, with Baychester Avenue running through the middle and the tracks of the 5 train’s Dyre Avenue Line on the eastern side. Definitions vary, so we describe streets rather than lines on a map: if you live around Seton Falls Park, along Bussing, Schieffelin, Grenada or Laconia Avenues, or on the numbered streets from the 220s up to East 233rd Street, this page is written for you.

What makes the neighborhood different for access control is its mix of housing. Wikipedia describes the wider Eastchester area as “dominated by one- and two-family homes of various types, one large public housing project, and an industrial district.” Rob Stephenson’s profile of Edenwald in The Neighborhoods describes the same split from the street: one- and two-family homes and row houses on one side, and across Baychester Avenue the Edenwald Houses, the Bronx’s largest public housing development. The private side of that mix is mostly owner-occupied houses with their own driveways, side passages and yards, and the security question in a house like that is not one lobby door but five or six openings spread around a lot.

The numbers back that up. The NYU Furman Center’s profile of Williamsbridge/Baychester, the community district that takes in Edenwald, last updated May 6, 2026, puts the homeownership rate at 38.6 percent in 2024, above the 32.7 percent citywide and very different from the West Bronx, where single-digit ownership rates are common. The same profile counts 60,578 housing units in the district in 2024 and 2,731 public housing units in 2025. In other words, a large share of Edenwald’s private housing is run by the people who live in it, often with a rented apartment on another floor.

Those houses have more ways in than an apartment does, and some of them are out of sight. In June 2017 DNAinfo reported a daytime burglary at a house near Edson and Strang Avenues in Edenwald in which, police said, the burglar broke into the home’s basement and left with two pet cockatiels, cologne and $600 in cash. In February 2021 the Bronx Times reported that two men got into an apartment near East 227th Street and Barnes Avenue by pretending to be plumbers. One story is about a basement door nobody was watching; the other is about a door opened for someone who should not have been let in.

The landscape adds its own exposure. NYC Parks’ history of Seton Falls Park traces the land from the Prime and Seton families’ estate, called Cragdon, to the 1914 city purchase and the 1930 transfer to Parks, and Wikipedia describes about 35 acres of woods between East 233rd Street, Baychester Avenue, Crawford Avenue and Marolla Place, with private property along its eastern edge. Woods are a pleasure to live beside. They also mean that some Edenwald back yards end at a tree line rather than at another house’s yard, which changes where a sensible owner puts a lock, a light and a contact.

Finally, the neighborhood lives next to a very public lesson in what happens when doors are not maintained. The Edenwald Houses’ front doors have been in the news since at least 2012, a 2022 city audit found broken locks on a large share of public housing entrances across New York, and the development’s current renovation promises “enhanced security and access control.” We set out that public record further down. We do not work for the New York City Housing Authority or its property managers, but the lesson applies to every two-family house and six-unit building on the private side of Baychester Avenue: a lock is only as good as the closer, the frame and the person responsible for fixing it.

  • Parkland: Seton Falls Park, about 35 acres of woods with the only above-ground stretch of Rattlesnake Brook and an artificial waterfall (Wikipedia; NYC Parks).
Repairs

Gate Stuck Open or a Keypad Gone Dark in Edenwald?

A house with a failed entry device is either locked to the people who live there or open to everyone else, and neither can wait until next week. Ring (646) 490-0629 and tell us what the opening is doing: a driveway gate that stops halfway or reverses for no reason, a keypad that shows nothing when you press it, a strike that buzzes but does not release, a basement door that will not latch since the frost moved the frame. On houses the usual culprits are simple — a dead battery pack, a gate photo eye knocked out of line by a snow shovel, a power supply on a basement shelf that someone unplugged to run a heater, or a door that has dropped on its hinges so the bolt no longer meets the strike. Most single faults are put right within an hour or two of the technician arriving.

We work on equipment other installers put in, including gate operators, wireless keypad deadbolts and older wired readers. Tenants in a rented apartment should go to their landlord first; we can only work on a property with the owner’s authorization.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Edenwald’s Houses and Small Buildings

Neighborhood guides describe early twentieth-century two-story brick houses, small-lot houses first advertised around 1905 when the name “Edenwald” began appearing in real estate notices, later single- and two-family houses, and a scattering of small apartment buildings. Each type takes hardware differently.

Detached frame houses

Wood framing behind vinyl, aluminum or shingle siding, often with a porch, a side door to the driveway and a back door to the yard. Doors are usually wood or steel in wood frames, which take strikes well; the mounting surface outside is the part that needs care, because siding moves with heat and cold.

Brick two-family houses

An owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, a shared front door or two separate entrances, and a basement with its own door. Masonry takes a reader solidly; the planning question is whose code opens which door, and keeping the tenant’s entrance under the tenant’s control.

Attached and semi-detached houses

A shared wall on one or both sides, a front stoop or porch, and a driveway or alley that may be shared with the neighbor. Fewer doors than a detached house, but any cable that crosses a shared driveway or wall needs the neighbor’s agreement first.

Small apartment buildings

Four to eight units, a street door, sometimes an inner door, a cellar and a rear yard. Once a building holds three or more households it is a multiple dwelling under state law, and the entrance and data rules on this page apply in full.

Houses with garages at the rear

A detached garage at the back of the lot, reached by a driveway beside the house. The garage people door is often the least protected opening on the property, and it needs power and a link back to the house before it can take a reader.

The Whole Lot

The Lot, Not the Lobby: Mapping an Edenwald Perimeter

Apartment access control starts at one door and works inward. House access control in a place like Edenwald starts at the property line and works toward the house, because the house is approached from several directions at once. On a typical survey we walk the lot in a fixed order and sort every opening into one of three groups.

Group one: openings that get a credential

These are the doors and gates that people use every day and that need to open for the right person without a key: usually the front door, the side door the family actually uses, the driveway gate if there is one, and the people door of a garage. On most Edenwald houses this group is two to four openings, not one.

Group two: openings that get a contact, not a reader

These are openings that should almost never be used: a back door that leads only to the yard, a basement door at the bottom of an outside stairwell, a steel cellar hatch, a gate at the back fence. Putting a reader on them invites people to use them. Instead we fit a solid lock that stays locked, add a door contact that reports when the opening moves, and send that report to the owner’s phone. An alert from a door that is never used is worth more than a log from a door that is used all day.

Group three: openings that need no electronics

Some openings are better solved with hardware alone: a window well grate, a storm door, a side passage that only needs a self-closing gate with a lock the mail carrier cannot reach over.

Light, sight lines and the tree line

Behind houses that back onto woods, open lots or the rail cut, the rear approach is dark and private. We pair the group-two contacts there with lighting that comes on when the yard gate or back door is used, and, where the owner wants it, a camera that sees the approach rather than the door itself. Our floodlight camera installation in the Bronx covers the lighting side in more detail.

Where the brains of the system live

Everything in groups one and two reports to a controller or hub, usually on a basement shelf or a utility wall near the electrical panel, with a battery-backed power supply. On a detached house we look for a dry spot above any history of water, with a short route to the front of the house and a route out to the driveway for the gate and garage.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control on Boston Road, East 233rd Street and Baychester Avenue

Edenwald is mostly residential, but its edges carry traffic and trade. Wikipedia names Boston Road as the primary thoroughfare through the Eastchester area and East 233rd Street as a major one; Bee-Line buses run north from here to Mount Vernon, White Plains and Port Chester, and the Bx30 runs along Boston Road. Baychester Avenue runs through the middle of the neighborhood past the Edenwald Houses and Seton Falls Park. The businesses that use access control here are small: a storefront with an office in back, a clinic or day program in a converted house, a contractor who keeps tools in a garage, a church hall, a home-based child care program. We do not list or name local businesses; we describe what each type needs.

Storefronts with a back office

A keypad on the door between the sales floor and the office or stock room, with a separate code per employee and a schedule that matches opening hours. The front door keeps its mechanical lock and roll-down gate; the back door gets a contact that reports when it opens after closing.

Offices and clinics in converted houses

A reader on the entrance for staff, a video station so the front desk can release the door for patients, and an inner door that keeps records rooms closed. Converted houses often have two entrances; both need to be on the same plan.

Churches and community rooms in houses

A reader on the side door that works for volunteers at the times the room is in use, with the main door locked otherwise. Free exit from inside, always.

Home-based child care

Front-door hardware that keeps small children from slipping out without blocking adults’ exit in an emergency, codes for parents at drop-off and pick-up times, and a log of who collected whom. We cover the egress side of this in the questions below.

Rules That Apply

The Rules Behind an Edenwald Entry System

Most of the rules that govern access control in New York City were written for apartment buildings, so for an owner-occupied one-family house very little of the paperwork applies. The picture changes as soon as the house is rented, holds three or more households, or puts a moving gate across a driveway. These are the rules we build to, summarized in plain language; they are not legal advice.

  • When a house becomes a multiple dwelling. Under Multiple Dwelling Law §4, a building occupied by three or more families living independently is a multiple dwelling. A one- or two-family house is not, which is why a two-family owner has more freedom than the owner of a three-family next door.
  • Entrance doors and intercoms in multiple dwellings. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, an intercom with door release at the main entrance where there are eight or more apartments, and a path for tenants in older buildings to request the same in writing.
  • Apartment door locks. The Housing Maintenance Code (§27-2043) requires the owner to provide a key lock on each apartment entrance door and at least one key, with heavier hardware required in class A multiple dwellings.
  • Tenant data. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) governs fobs, cards, apps and biometrics in multiple dwellings: a written privacy policy, express consent, collection limited to what the system needs, authentication data destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, and no requirement that tenants use smart access to enter their own apartments.
  • Free exit. The NYC Building Code’s rules for electrically locked egress doors (sections 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9) require locks that release on loss of power and simple one-motion exit hardware where magnetic locks are used in the occupancies they cover. In every house we work on, we keep exit from inside to a single, obvious motion whatever the code strictly requires.
  • Motorized gates. Gate operators are built and installed to the UL 325 safety standard. DASMA’s technical data sheet on UL 325 explains that slide and swing gate operators need two separate means of entrapment protection in each direction of travel, that external sensors must be monitored, and that a separate pedestrian opening is required where people use the driveway.
  • Who may install. Security and access control installers in New York must hold a state license under General Business Law §69-m. Ours is #12000287431. Low-voltage wiring under 50 volts generally does not need a Department of Buildings electrical permit; the 120-volt circuit that feeds a gate operator or power supply is electrician’s work, and we say who will do it in the estimate.
The Public Record Next Door

What the Edenwald Houses’ Doors Teach Every Edenwald Owner

The Edenwald Houses are a New York City Housing Authority development, and the doors there are the responsibility of the Authority and, since the 2023 conversion, its private management partner. AESS does not work for NYCHA or for the development’s managers, and nothing on this page describes work we have done there. We mention the development only because it is the neighborhood’s largest building complex and because its door history is a matter of public record that every private owner nearby can learn from.

In May 2012 News 12 reported that Edenwald tenants were worried because broken front doors meant “anyone can walk through the building.” In May 2017 the station reported a burglar targeting maintenance rooms across the development and taking equipment worth hundreds of dollars. In September 2022 the New York City Comptroller’s auditors visited 262 developments citywide and found 40.1 percent of residential entrance door locks broken and 36.8 percent of entrance doors open; 57.9 percent of entrance doors were open, broken or both, up from 23.5 percent in a 2018 survey. The audit did not name Edenwald; its Bronx figures, as we read the report’s table, were worse than the city average.

In June 2023 the NYC Housing Development Corporation announced the closing of a $783 million PACT renovation covering 2,035 apartments in 40 buildings, with new roofs, façade repairs, lobby and common-area upgrades, “new entryways” and “enhanced security and access control,” with C+C Management’s security staff monitoring the cameras. The release projected about three years for the main building work and four years for apartments, in clusters of five or six buildings. Residents with questions about their own building’s doors should take them to the property manager, not to an outside contractor.

What the record shows is not that locks fail; it is that locks fail when nobody owns the repair. The Comptroller’s recommendations were about maintenance: repair or replace damaged locks, intercoms, release buttons and door closers, inspect exterior doors regularly, and make sure maintenance staff learn immediately when a door is obstructed or unsecured. That is why every system we install comes with a named person responsible for it, a monthly test, and a door contact that reports a door left open instead of waiting for someone to notice.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Edenwald: Questions Answered

These are the questions Edenwald owners raise when the property is a house on its own lot rather than an apartment behind a lobby. They are grouped by the part of the property they concern. Answers that depend on law are summaries, not legal advice.

The Yard, the Woods and the Back of the House

Our back yard ends at the woods of Seton Falls Park. What does the back of the house need that the front door does not?

Light, a report when anything opens, and locks that stay locked — rather than more readers. The front of an Edenwald house faces a street with neighbors and passing traffic; a back yard that ends at a tree line is approached in darkness, unseen, and from several angles at once. So the rear is planned around being noticed. The back door into the kitchen, the basement door and any gate in the back fence each get a contact, and the yard gets lighting that switches on when one of them moves.

For the back door we fit a deadbolt that actually throws into a reinforced strike box, a door contact wired to the hub, and an outside light triggered when the door or the yard gate moves. If the family uses that door every day, it can take a keypad deadbolt; if not, it stays on a key and the contact does the work. For the basement door we harden the lock and add a contact. The rear fence gate gets a lock the public cannot reach over and, where it is wired, a contact as well.

Burglars in this area have come in through basements. What should go on a basement door at the bottom of an outside stairwell that nobody can see from the street?

A strong lock that stays locked, a door contact that reports every opening, and nothing that depends on someone looking. DNAinfo’s 2017 report of a basement break-in near Edson and Strang Avenues is a reminder that below-grade doors are chosen precisely because they are out of view. A keypad on that door usually makes things worse, because it invites daily use of a door that is easier to attack.

Our standard approach is a steel or solid-core door in a frame that is anchored properly to the masonry, a grade-one deadbolt or a surface-mounted rim lock with a heavy strike, a hinge-side guard if the hinges are exposed, and a wired or wireless contact that sends an alert to the owner’s phone and, if there is an alarm panel, to the panel. Where a tenant uses the basement as a separate entrance, it stops being a rarely used door and becomes their front door, and the plan changes: the tenant gets a keypad they control and the owner keeps the contact.

There is a steel cellar hatch in our yard that opens onto the basement stairs. Can that be locked or watched electronically?

Yes. Steel bulkhead hatches can take an inside slide bolt or a hatch lock and a heavy-duty magnetic contact that tells you when either leaf lifts. They are usually secured from the inside only, with nothing on the outside to attack, which is the right arrangement for a house where the hatch is used a few times a year for deliveries or the boiler technician.

Driveways, Gates and Garages

We want to add a motor to our sliding driveway gate. What has to be in place so it can never close on a child or a car?

At least two separate forms of entrapment protection in each direction the gate moves, external sensors that the operator monitors, guarding on the gate itself, and a separate walk-through gate for people. That is the substance of UL 325 as DASMA’s technical data sheet explains it. In practice, on a sliding driveway gate, the operator’s own built-in sensing is one form; a photo eye across the opening and a contact edge on the leading edge of the gate provide the second and third. The operator checks the external sensors on every cycle and refuses to run if one is missing or faulty.

A sliding gate also needs screening from the bottom to six feet so a small hand cannot pass through the gate where it moves past the post, and the gap at the post where the gate retracts must be guarded. Because children and pedestrians use driveways, a separate pedestrian gate with its own keypad is part of every gate plan we write.

Our detached garage sits at the back of the lot. How does a reader out there get power and a connection to the house?

One of three ways: a cable run in conduit from the house, a wireless bridge between the house and the garage, or a battery-powered smart lock that needs neither. The right choice depends on what is already out there. If the garage has an electrical circuit, the reader can be powered locally and connected to the house by a small outdoor wireless bridge or by a buried or overhead data cable. If it has no power, a battery keypad deadbolt or smart lock on the people door is often the honest answer.

For a wired run we look for an existing route — along a fence, under the eaves of an attached shed, or through conduit that already carries the garage’s power — before suggesting new trenching. A wireless bridge is quicker and cheaper, but it needs a clear line between the house and the garage, which leafy yards sometimes do not have in summer. We test it with leaves on the trees before we promise it.

House Doors and Hardware

Our house has vinyl siding. Can a keypad or reader be mounted on it without leaks or rattling?

Yes, on a mounting block. Vinyl and aluminum siding are designed to move with temperature, so anything screwed tightly to a panel will either crack it or work loose, and the screw holes let water into the wall behind. A siding mounting block is a flat-faced plastic base that fits around the siding profile, screws through to the sheathing and framing, and gives the device a flat, sealed surface. The cable passes through the middle of the block into the wall cavity.

We pick the block color to match the siding, seal the top edge so rain runs over rather than behind it, and leave a small drip loop in the cable inside the wall. Where the reader goes beside a door, we mount it on the trim instead if the trim is wide enough and solid.

Can the side door unlock by itself when we walk up with our hands full of shopping?

It can, but we set it up carefully. Many smart locks and phone-credential readers offer “auto-unlock” or touch-to-open: the lock notices your phone arriving and unlocks when you get close or touch the lock. The convenience is real on a side door used for groceries, strollers and laundry. The risk is that a phone in a jacket by the door, or a phone that thinks you have just arrived when you are actually in the yard, unlocks the door when you did not mean it to.

Our preferred arrangement is touch-to-open rather than pure proximity, so the door unlocks only when the phone is near and someone touches the lock or reader, combined with auto-relock after a few seconds and a door contact so the app shows the door actually closed. We enable it only for the adults who want it, never for a whole household by default, and never on a rear door that faces the woods.

Family, Care and Changes of Ownership

My mother has dementia and sometimes tries to go out at night. Can the system warn us when the front door opens after bedtime?

Yes. A door contact on the front door, and on any other door she could use, can send an alert to one or more family phones whenever it opens during set hours, and a small chime inside can sound at the same time so whoever is awake hears it. The door still opens normally from inside; it is never locked in a way that would trap anyone in a fire. The alert simply makes sure somebody knows within seconds.

Families often add a second step: a keypad on the inside of the door for the overnight hours, set so that adults who know the code can leave freely while the alert sounds for anyone else. Whether that is appropriate depends on the person and on advice from her doctor, and on the household’s fire exit plan, so we set up the alerts first and the inside keypad only if the family decides it wants one.

Our late father’s house is empty while the estate is settled. How do we control who goes in and know when they do?

Replace the key lock on the main entrance with a keypad deadbolt or reader, issue a separate code to each family member, executor, real estate agent or contractor who needs access, and add contacts on the other doors so any opening is reported. Every code use is logged with the time, so the family can see who went in and when without anyone having to be there. Codes for a single visit can be deleted the same day.

An empty house also needs its rarely used doors checked: basement doors, back doors and garage doors that nobody has opened in years. We test that each one latches and locks, add contacts where they are missing, and set the alerts to go to the person handling the estate.

We run a family child care program in our house. How do we keep toddlers from walking out the front door without blocking a fire exit?

Use hardware that defeats small hands but not adults: a high-mounted secondary release or a two-motion exit device that an adult operates easily and a toddler cannot, plus a door contact and chime so staff know the moment the door opens. What you must not do is lock the exit door in a way that requires a key, code or special knowledge to leave. The New York State Office of Children and Family Services licenses family and group family child care in the city, and its fire safety and egress expectations take priority; check them before choosing hardware, and we will build to whatever the program’s inspector requires.

For the entrance side, a keypad or reader with a separate code for each parent, active only at drop-off and pick-up windows, means the door is not left on the latch while families come and go. The log of which code was used and when is a useful record for the program, and it is limited to that door.

We own houses on both sides of the Mount Vernon line. Can one app run the Bronx house and the Mount Vernon house?

Yes. Most current access control and smart-lock platforms manage several sites from one account, with each property shown separately and its own users, codes and schedules. A tenant in the Mount Vernon house never appears in the Bronx house’s list, and an owner can give a property manager rights to one site and not the other. We install the same platform at both addresses so there is one login and one set of habits to learn.

The two properties are in different jurisdictions. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law covers multiple dwellings in the city; the Westchester side follows its own local rules, and our Westchester County access control page covers that side of the line.

Small Buildings and the Neighborhood’s Largest Complex

Our six-family building’s front door keeps getting kicked and propped. What hardware actually survives that kind of abuse?

A heavy-duty closer with a hold-open that cannot be engaged, a continuous hinge, a steel frame anchored to the masonry, a strike rated for the door’s weight, a latch guard, and a door-held-open alarm that reports to the owner. A cheap reader on a weak door fails within a season; the reader is rarely what breaks. The door, the closer and the frame take the abuse, so they are where the money goes first.

We also look at why the door is propped. If tenants prop it because the buzzer does not work or deliveries cannot get in, fixing the intercom or adding a package solution removes the reason. If it is propped because the closer slams it on people’s heels, adjusting the closer helps more than any lock. A building of three or more apartments is a multiple dwelling, so the self-closing, self-locking entrance rules in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a apply, and the owner is the one responsible for keeping them working.

I live in the Edenwald Houses. Can AESS repair my building’s front door or put a reader on my apartment door?

No. The building doors and apartment doors in the Edenwald Houses belong to the development, and only its owner and property manager can authorize work on them. AESS does not work for NYCHA or the development’s managers. A broken building door, lock or intercom should be reported to the property management office or through the channel your management company provides, and tenants who cannot get a response can contact their elected officials or the city’s 311 line.

Inside your own apartment, the lease and the management’s rules decide what you may change. What we can help with is everything off the development: a relative’s house nearby, a small business, or a building you own elsewhere in the Bronx.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Edenwald

We could not see Google’s AI Overview answers for Edenwald while writing this page, so we have not quoted any. What follows corrects claims that turn up again and again in generic answers, cost guides and listicles about home access control, and checks them against the way houses here are actually built and used.

“A smart lock on the front door is all a house needs”

This is the most common simplification, and it comes from apartment thinking. In an apartment, the front door of the unit is the only door you control. An Edenwald house typically has a front door, a side door to the driveway, a back door to the yard, a basement door and often a garage, and in many cases a driveway gate as well. A smart lock on the front door alone leaves four or five other openings exactly as they were.

That does not mean every opening needs a smart lock. It means every opening needs a decision. The ones the family uses daily get credentials; the ones nobody should use get a solid lock and a contact; the rest get ordinary hardware in good repair. The decision is cheap. Skipping it is what leaves a house with an expensive front door and an unwatched basement.

National retailers and installers such as ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe sell good front-door products, and a homeowner can fit one in an afternoon. What a packaged product will not do is walk your lot and tell you that the back door onto the woods matters more than the front door onto a busy street. That walk is most of what a local installer is for.

“Access control for a home costs a few hundred dollars”

Cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr quote figures for installing a smart lock, and a single keypad deadbolt on a standard door is indeed a modest job. Those numbers describe one device on one door.

They do not describe a driveway gate operator with monitored safety sensors, cable to a garage at the back of the lot, or a reader with a strike and a battery-backed power supply.

Our published prices, at our Bronx rate, start at $1,500 to $2,500 for a single controlled door with a reader or keypad, strike, power supply and programming, and rise with the number of doors and gates. A plan that combines a keypad deadbolt on the front door with wired control at the gate and garage costs more than either guide figure and less than a full building system. Every quote we write is itemized so you can see which opening costs what.

“Any gate opener kit from a hardware store is safe to install yourself”

Gate operator kits are sold widely, and generic advice often treats a motorized gate like a garage door opener. The safety standard for gate operators, UL 325, sets out entrapment protection that has to be installed and tested correctly, including monitored external sensors and two separate means of protection in each direction of travel, as DASMA’s data sheet explains.

A kit installed without the photo eyes and edges in the right places, or with the sensors bypassed to stop nuisance reversals, can close on a child or a car.

On Edenwald driveways that are short, sloped or shared, the gaps and pinch points around the post and the hinge are exactly where the risk sits. We size the operator to the gate, add the sensors the standard calls for, screen a sliding gate where it passes the post, and install a separate pedestrian gate so nobody walks through the vehicle opening.

“Tenant privacy laws don’t apply to small landlords”

Half right. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to multiple dwellings, which under state law means buildings with three or more households, so the owner of a one- or two-family house is generally outside it.

The owner of a three-family house, or of the six-unit buildings found throughout the district, is inside it as soon as fobs, cards, apps or biometrics are used.

The obligations are not heavy, but they are specific: a written privacy and retention policy, express consent before collecting reference data, only the data the system needs, authentication data destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, and no requirement that tenants use smart access to enter their own apartments. Generic answers that tell a small landlord to “just install a smart lock” skip all of that.

“Wireless means no wiring and no installer”

Wireless locks still need power, a hub within range and, for remote access, a stable network. On a detached Edenwald house with a garage at the back, the hub in the living room may be fifty feet and two masonry walls from the garage door.

A wireless keypad that drops off the network is a keypad that cannot be updated or deleted remotely when a code needs to go.

We use wireless where it is reliable and wire where it is not. A wireless bridge to a garage, a hub placed centrally rather than beside the router, and a keypad lock that works locally by code when the internet is down are the usual compromises. The test is simple: everything must keep working at the door when the house Wi-Fi is off.

“Face recognition is the most secure option for a front door”

Face recognition is often promoted as the most advanced choice. For a family house it adds cost and complexity for little gain over a personal code or an encrypted fob, and lighting, hats and winter clothing affect it.

For a multiple dwelling, biometric identifiers are among the data the city’s tenant privacy law regulates closely, with consent and retention rules that make them a poor default.

The more useful camera at an Edenwald front door is the one in a video door station, which lets a person see who is there and decide whether to open. That addresses the problem in the Bronx Times’ 2021 report, where two men got into an apartment by claiming to be plumbers: the issue was not identifying residents, it was checking visitors.

“Once installed, access control looks after itself”

No electronic system looks after itself, and doors least of all. The Comptroller’s 2022 audit of public housing doors is a city-scale example: the recommendations were about inspection and repair, not about buying new hardware.

Batteries run down, closers lose tension, frames move with the seasons, and gate sensors get knocked by shovels and car bumpers.

We leave every Edenwald owner with a short monthly routine and a named person responsible. Test that each controlled door latches by itself, that a deleted code is refused, that the gate reverses when the photo eye is blocked, and that the battery indicators are green. Two minutes a month catches most failures before they become a break-in or a lockout.

5. Readers, locks and controller

Keypads, readers, strikes and contacts mounted; the controller or hub and the battery-backed supply installed; free exit checked at every door. We switch off the power to prove the battery carries the locks, and confirm every door and gate behaves correctly when it returns.

6. Codes for the household

Each person gets their own code, fob or phone key, with schedules for aides, tenants and trades. Old keys are retired on a set date, the garage remote and gate remotes are counted, and the alert recipients for back and basement doors are chosen with the family.

The administrator account in the owner's name, recovery details written down, the monthly check card left by the controller, and the household shown how to release the gate by hand and change a code. Our direct line goes on the card, and a follow-up check is booked for a month later.

After handover: the two-minute monthly check

Once a month, let each controlled door close by itself and watch it latch. Try a code you have deleted and confirm it is refused. If you have a gate, block the photo eye with a bag or bucket while the gate closes and confirm it reverses. Open the basement door and confirm the alert reaches your phone. Glance at the battery indicators on keypad locks and the power supply.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Doors Nobody Uses

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular house. When we walk a detached house with its owner, we ask a simple question at every opening: when did someone last use this? The front and side doors get an immediate answer. The back door usually does too. Then we reach the basement door at the foot of the outside steps, the hatch in the yard, the gate in the back fence, or the old door into a garage that now holds bicycles and paint cans, and the answer is often a shrug.

Those are the openings we pay most attention to. A door that nobody uses is a door nobody checks, and it is frequently the one with a rusted strike, a bolt that no longer reaches its keeper, or a frame softened by years of water. On many surveys the most useful thing we do is not electronic at all: we adjust or replace the lock on a forgotten door, fit a proper strike box with long screws into the framing, and then add a contact so the owner finds out if that door ever moves.

Only after the forgotten doors are sound do we talk about keypads for the doors people use.

FAQ

Edenwald Access Control FAQ

Is a video doorbell enough for an Edenwald house, or does it need a real access control system?
+

For a house where everyone uses one door and carries a key, a good video doorbell plus a sound deadbolt may be enough. A doorbell lets you see and talk to callers; it does not decide who can open the door, keep a list of codes, or watch the side, basement and garage doors. Once a house has a rental unit, a driveway gate, a home aide or relatives who come and go, a doorbell alone leaves those questions unanswered. The usual Edenwald plan keeps the doorbell or adds a video door station at the front, puts a keypad or reader on the one or two doors used every day, and adds contacts on the doors nobody should use.

Where should the keypad for a driveway gate go so a driver can reach it without getting out of the car?
+

On a gooseneck post on the driver's side, set back from the gate far enough that the car can stop clear of the moving leaf, at a height a driver can reach from a car window. Mounting the keypad on the gate post itself usually means the driver either stops too close to the gate or has to get out. The post needs a concrete footing and, on a busy or narrow drive, a bollard or curb in front of it so a car door or bumper does not take it out. The pedestrian keypad goes separately on the walk gate at hand height. Cable to the post runs in conduit under the drive, which is easiest to lay before new paving, so tell us if the driveway is about to be redone.

Will a sliding driveway gate keep working when snow and ice build up in the track?
+

Not reliably if the gate runs on a ground track. Snow, ice and road salt in a V-track stop the wheels, and the operator's obstruction sensing will reverse or stall the gate, which is the safe behavior but an inconvenient one. Where a driveway allows it, a cantilever gate that hangs from rollers on the post side and never touches the ground avoids the problem altogether. On an existing track gate, a heated track section or simply keeping the track clear after a storm keeps it moving, and the pedestrian gate beside it, with its own keypad, means nobody is stuck outside while the drive is cleared.

Can a keypad lock left by the previous owner be reset if nobody knows the master code?
+

Usually, yes. Most keypad deadbolts and smart locks have a factory reset that clears every code and pairing, carried out from the inside of the door with the lock's own reset button or by removing the battery and following the maker's sequence. Some require the original master code or the account that registered the lock; for those, the manufacturer's support line can often release it with proof of ownership. If neither works, replacing the keypad unit is cheaper than living with codes you do not control. After a reset we register the lock to your account, enter fresh codes for each household member and delete any old pairings.

Can porch or path lights switch on when someone uses the gate code?
+

Yes. A relay output on the access controller, or an automation in a smart-home hub, can turn on a porch light, path lights or a floodlight for a few minutes whenever a code or fob opens the gate or a door after dark. It works the other way too: a contact on the back door or rear gate can trigger the yard lights when that opening moves, which is what we usually recommend for houses whose yards back onto woods or open land. The lights themselves are wired by an electrician or are existing fixtures switched through a relay rated for them.

Can we get a notification when our children get home from school, without tracking anyone else?
+

Yes. Each child gets a personal code or fob, and the system can be set to send a notification to a parent's phone when that particular code is used, and only that code. Other household members' entries produce no message, and the notification says only that the door was opened with that credential at that time. Some families add a notification if the code has not been used by a set time on school days, which is a gentle prompt to call. The log is kept for as short a period as the family chooses.

Does an electric strike make a door easier to kick in than a deadbolt?
+

A cheap strike fitted badly can, which is why the details matter. The strength of a door against a kick comes from the bolt's throw, the strike box and the screws that hold it into the framing. A heavy-duty electric strike rated for exterior doors, installed with a reinforced box and long screws into the stud, holds as well as a good deadbolt keeper. Many installations keep a mechanical deadbolt on the same door for overnight, so the strike handles daytime convenience and the deadbolt handles security when the house is asleep. Where a door is weak, the fix is a better frame and hinge screws, not a different lock.

Will a keypad lock fit a door that already has a separate deadbolt above the knob?
+

Usually, yes. Most keypad deadbolts are made to replace a standard deadbolt in the same hole, so the knob below stays as it is, and the exterior keypad simply takes the place of the old cylinder. What we check is the backset, the door thickness and whether the old bolt hole lines up with the strike; older doors in particular may have been drilled to a non-standard size or have a mortise lock that needs a different product. If the door has a mortise lock or a storm door close in front of it, we choose a lock that fits rather than drilling new holes.

Our semi-detached house shares a driveway with the house next door. Do we need the neighbor's permission for a gate or a cable across it?
+

For anything that touches their side of the line, yes. A shared driveway is usually governed by the deeds or an easement, and a gate across it, or a cable buried or run along it, can affect your neighbor's right to use it. We ask owners to confirm where the property line runs and to get the neighbor's written agreement before we install anything on or across the shared part. Often the simplest plan keeps everything on your side: a pedestrian gate or keypad on your own side passage, a reader on your own side door, and no gate across the shared drive at all.

How many user codes can a keypad deadbolt hold, and is that enough for a large household?
+

Most residential keypad deadbolts hold somewhere between a couple of dozen and a few hundred user codes, depending on the model, and smart versions add phone keys on top. For a family house with relatives, a tenant, an aide and a couple of trades, that is plenty. The real limit is usually management rather than memory: who adds and removes codes, and whether each code has a name attached so you know which one to delete. Where a household needs schedules for many people, logs kept for longer, or the same codes working at a gate, a garage and two doors, a wired controller handles that better than separate locks.

Can the gate and front door stay open for a family party and then lock again on their own?
+

Yes. Most controllers and many smart locks have a hold-open or party mode that keeps a door or pedestrian gate unlocked for a set number of hours, or until a set time, and then relocks it automatically, with a notification if it fails to close. We recommend using it on the pedestrian gate and the front door only, never on the basement or back doors, and never leaving a motorized vehicle gate standing open in automatic mode with children around; for a gathering the vehicle gate should simply be closed and the walk gate held open. The schedule can be set from the app the morning of the event, so nobody has to remember to undo it at midnight.
