The Neighborhood

Why Port Morris Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Port Morris was built to make things and ship them, and almost every camera decision here starts from that. Gouverneur Morris Jr. established it as a seaport in 1842, when it was still part of Morrisania. By the end of the 1800s it was the center of piano making in the United States, and rail yards went in along the Bronx Kill in 1906. The Estey Piano Company Factory at Lincoln Avenue and what is now Bruckner Boulevard has stood there since 1886 and is the oldest known piano factory still standing in the Bronx. The area declined steadily from the 1940s; the Major Deegan Expressway in 1956 and the Bruckner Expressway in 1961 cut it off from the rest of the borough, and a wave of arson in the 1970s burned much of what was left. Then the city made Port Morris the home of its first special mixed-use district in 1997 and extended it in 2005, and in the 2000s old warehouses began turning into lofts. City Planning's summary of that rezoning describes large brick and concrete industrial buildings of one to six stories along Bruckner Boulevard, three- to five-story multifamily buildings on the same street, and two- and three-story row houses on East 133rd and East 134th Streets near Willow Avenue. Wikipedia credits the rezoning with the rehabilitation of several blocks of row houses between Bruckner Boulevard and East 134th Street. The newest layer is rising on the Harlem River: Bankside, more than 1,350 apartments straddling the Third Avenue Bridge, including Lincoln at Bankside's four 24-story towers at 101 Lincoln Avenue, and Maven, 27 stories and 200 apartments at 2413 Third Avenue. Where Port Morris ends and Mott Haven begins is argued over, and many listings put the Bruckner corridor and Bankside in Mott Haven. We go by your address, and the rate is the same on both sides of the line.

The theft story the neighborhood tells about itself is specific. The Bronx Times reported that since late 2019 the La Finca Del Sur community farm in Port Morris had been broken into 25 times and had lost more than ten thousand dollars' worth of generators, solar panels and other equipment. Residents and business owners told the paper that theft had been an ongoing problem that had grown in recent years, and some of them thought the neighborhood's closeness to the Bruckner, Major Deegan and Cross Bronx expressways made it easy for thieves to come and go. That describes the camera problem in Port Morris better than any crime table. The weak spots are often open ground rather than front doors: a fenced lot, a yard behind a warehouse, a loading door on a side street, a roof with equipment on it. The getaway is short. And the clip that helps afterwards is the one that shows a face or a vehicle clearly and then shows which way it went, so the next camera along the route can be checked. A wide shot of a dark yard does neither.

The question of who gets to put up a camera matters as much as where it points. NeighborhoodScout reports that many Port Morris homes were built in 1939 or earlier and that most are occupied by renters. In a nearby Mott Haven building, News 12 Bronx reported, management gave residents a deadline to take cameras off their apartment doors, and one tenant said she would have kept hers if she were not afraid of a violation or a fine. In another Mott Haven case News 12 covered, a tenant's Nest camera recorded the landlord entering a unit and taking out the electricity meter. Nearby, the Diego Beekman camera network covers a 12-block zone and records sidewalks, residential lobbies and the entrances of small businesses, and THE CITY noted that the city's facial-recognition disclosure law for businesses does not apply to residential buildings. On any given Port Morris block there may be a building owner's system, a tenant's own camera, a business's cameras and a private network, each controlled by someone different. Part of our job is working out which of those you control before anything is drilled.

The newest buildings change the picture again. Third at Bankside advertises keyless entry for every apartment, a 24-hour concierge in the lobby and valet parking with EV charging, which means the entrances, garage and halls already belong to a building-run system that a resident or a ground-floor tenant cannot simply add to. Bankside's waterfront park, which Welcome2TheBronx reported stays open as late as 1 a.m., sits right beside the towers, so a ground-floor camera there looks onto public space for much of the night. Even the way the towers were built carries a lesson: Hill West's description of 2401 Third Avenue notes that its landscaped podium roof sits two feet above the 100-year flood plain. Older buildings closer to the water were not built with that margin, and South Bronx Unite reports that parts of the waterfront were about three and a half feet under water after Hurricane Sandy, which is why we keep recorders up and out of cellars on those blocks. On the public side, PIX11 reported that the Bronx Borough President's office budgeted 1.2 million dollars for NYPD surveillance cameras and half a million dollars for Sanitation cameras aimed at illegal dumping. None of those public cameras is placed to cover your door, your dock or your yard.

Port Morris facts that shape where a camera goes

  • Theft of equipment, not just goods: 25 break-ins at La Finca Del Sur since late 2019, with generators and solar panels among the losses, according to the Bronx Times.
  • Fast routes out: residents and business owners named the Bruckner, Major Deegan and Cross Bronx expressways as part of why theft is easy here.
  • Old walls, mostly renters: many homes date from 1939 or earlier, and most are rented, so written owner approval comes before any outside work.
  • Two halves: the eastern side is mostly light industry and warehouses, while the western side along the Harlem River is filling with new residential buildings.
  • One landmark to plan around: the Estey Piano Company Factory, now the Clock Tower apartments at 112 Lincoln Avenue, is a designated city landmark, so its facade falls under Landmarks Preservation Commission rules.
  • The 40th Precinct: Wikipedia places Port Morris in the NYPD's 40th Precinct, which is where clips from Port Morris doors and yards usually end up.

What Port Morris owners tend to add next. Once the doors and the yard are on camera, loft and row-house owners often ask whether the lobby camera can feed a video intercom, or whether the freight door could report when it is left open. Warehouse owners ask about door readers that log who came in. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a second system is likely, a spare cable to the entrance, so a finished ceiling or a cored wall only has to be opened once.

Freight-Door Camera Blank, or a Yard Clip You Need Before It Loops? Fast Camera Help in Port Morris

Port Morris calls tend to arrive in one of two shapes. In the first, a camera that used to show the loading door, the yard or the back of a row house has gone dark, often after a power interruption, a new modem from the internet provider, a contractor working in the cellar or a truck that clipped the wall, and nobody can say how long it has been out. In the second, something has already happened: a fence was cut, a vehicle was entered, equipment walked off a lot or someone came through the lobby who should not have, and you need the recording before the drive fills and starts over. For a dead camera, write down which one it is and when it last showed a picture, check whether the recorder and any small switch beside it still have lights, and do not reset the recorder to factory settings, because that can erase the passwords and remote access along with the fault. For an incident, leave the recorder running, note the camera and the exact time, and if you know how, copy that stretch to a USB stick. The Way Out

Expressways and Bridges Minutes Away: Why a Port Morris Camera Has to Record the Way Out

Port Morris sits in a tight frame of roads and water. The Major Deegan and the Bruckner run along it, the Cross Bronx is close, and the Third Avenue, Willis Avenue and Robert F. Kennedy bridges are all near enough that NY YIMBY described the new logistics building at 307 Bruckner Boulevard as under a mile from all three. That is good for business and good for commuting, and it is the reason residents gave the Bronx Times for why thieves find the neighborhood easy. A camera plan here has to accept that most incidents will be short. Someone arrives, does something in a few minutes and is gone toward a ramp or a bridge. The recording that helps afterwards is rarely the one that proves something happened. It is the one that shows who did it and which way they went, so that the next recording along that route, from a business, a building or a public camera, can be found and lined up. We therefore plan most Port Morris entrances and gates as a pair of views: one close, for identity, and one long, for direction.

The close view: faces and fronts of vehicles

A camera mounted high on a warehouse or a loft facade sees everything and identifies almost nobody. From twenty or thirty feet up, a person becomes the top of a hat and a car becomes a roof. For identity, the camera has to come down to the level of the thing it is identifying. At a door, that means a camera at roughly head height, inside the vestibule or behind the glass where it cannot be knocked out of aim, looking at the face of whoever steps into the opening. At a gate, it means a camera at about bumper height on a post or the gatepost wall, aimed along the drive so the front of each vehicle passes through the frame head-on. We also slow nothing down in the picture: a fast shutter setting keeps a person walking briskly from smearing, which matters more after dark than any resolution figure printed on a box.

The long view: the stretch of street that tells the story

The second camera is the one most systems leave out. It sits on the corner of the building or high on the yard side and looks along the street rather than across it, so that a person leaving your door can be followed for a few seconds toward the next corner. In Port Morris the streets are long and the blocks are wide, so that stretch is often enough to show whether someone headed toward Bruckner Boulevard, toward Third Avenue or toward the water. It is framed on the sidewalk and the curb in front of your property and a little beyond, not into the windows across the street. Paired with the close view and a synced clock, it turns a single clip into a piece of a route that the next person investigating can pick up.

Night on a working block

Blocks given over to warehouses and light industry can be bright one hour and dark the next, depending on which businesses leave their lights on. A camera that relies on its own infrared light at night records in black and white, and on a wide street its light fades long before the curb. Where there is a working light over a door or a gate, we aim the camera to use it rather than stare into it, and we choose low-light color models for the long views so that the color of a van or a jacket survives. Where there is no light at all, we say so and suggest one, because a fixture over the gate does more for the night picture than any camera upgrade. Every camera gets checked after dark before we leave, not just in daylight.

Walk down a Port Morris block and you may pass four or five separate camera systems without noticing any of them: the building-wide system in a new tower, a loft building's lobby cameras run by its managing agent, a business's cameras on its own storefront, a tenant's camera inside an apartment door, and, a few blocks away, a large private network such as the one at Diego Beekman. Each is owned by someone, each answers to different rules, and none of them is placed to cover anyone else's property. Before we design anything, we work out which of these the person calling us actually controls. That single conversation saves more trouble than any choice of camera.

When the building already has a system

In the newer buildings along the Harlem River, the entry system, lobby cameras and garage cameras are part of the building and are maintained by whoever the owner hired. A resident who wants something more cannot simply bolt a camera to the corridor wall, and a retail tenant at the base of the building usually cannot tie into the building recorder without the owner's say-so. What a resident can do is cover the inside of their own apartment; what a retail tenant can do is cover its own space, its own door from inside and its own stock room, on its own recorder. When an owner or managing agent asks us to add to an existing system, we ask first for the make of the recorder and the name of the vendor, because adding to a system that someone else still supports is a conversation, not a surprise.

When tenants want cameras of their own

The Mott Haven building where management told residents to take down their door cameras is a reminder that a door camera in a rental is a lease question as much as a technical one. In a Port Morris loft building or row house with several units, we suggest owners and tenants settle it in writing: which spots the building covers, whether tenants may use a camera inside their own door, and who may see the building's footage. A tenant who wants a record of who comes and goes can usually get most of it from a small camera inside the apartment aimed at the inside of the door, which stays within the unit and leaves the hallway alone. An owner who would rather tenants did not put devices on the doors can make that easier to accept by giving each unit a view of the street door on its own login.

Large networks and face matching

Private networks on the scale of Diego Beekman's can record sidewalks, lobbies and shop entrances across a dozen blocks, and THE CITY reported that the city's facial-recognition disclosure law for businesses does not reach residential buildings. We do not install face matching on residential buildings in Port Morris, and we recommend that any business considering it read the city's disclosure rules before switching it on. For almost every job here, a clear picture of a face, a sensible number of days kept and a clock that matches everyone else's do the work people expect from more ambitious systems, without the questions they raise.

Factory lofts: thick walls, few hidden paths and a freight door that is now a lobby

Port Morris lofts were made from buildings designed for machines and pianos, not for wiring. The masonry walls are thick, the original floors are heavy, and there are rarely the stacked closets and dropped ceilings that make cable easy in a newer building. What the conversions did add, usually, is a corridor ceiling on each floor, a utility path from the cellar and a lobby built into what was once a freight entrance. We follow those. Cable for the entrance cameras travels along the cellar to the base of the existing chase, rises with the other low-voltage cable, and reaches each floor above the corridor ceiling. Where it has to cross a masonry wall, we core a single planned opening and carry several cables through it, rather than drilling a fresh hole for every camera. Outside, cameras go into mortar joints, not the faces of old brick, and as close as possible to the door frame so the cable can enter the building beside it. The freight entrance, if it still opens, gets its own close camera, because in many of these buildings it is the door contractors, movers and deliveries use, and the one the front-desk view never sees.

Row houses and tenements: one or two households, a stoop and a cellar

The row houses on East 133rd and East 134th Streets and the tenements on Bruckner Boulevard are the simplest Port Morris buildings to cable and the ones where framing matters most. Their fronts sit close to the sidewalk and to their neighbors, so a camera at the door hood or under the cornice can see the stoop clearly but can also see straight into the next house's door if it is not aimed with care. The cellar is usually the cable route: a run along the joists to the front wall, a sealed opening at the base of the stoop or beside the door, and a short climb to the camera. At the back, a yard camera on the rear wall watches the cellar door and the fence. On a tenement with a store below, we keep the building's cameras and the store's cameras on separate recorders, because the owner and the store tenant usually want different people looking at each. Every opening through brick is sloped slightly downward toward the outside and sealed, and in a multiple dwelling any opening through a rated floor or wall gets rated firestop.

Newer mid-rises and waterfront towers: the owner's system comes first

The six- and seven-story buildings that went up along Bruckner Boulevard after the rezoning, such as 101 Bruckner Boulevard and the two buildings at 25 Bruckner Boulevard, and the towers at Bankside and Maven, were built with corridors, ceilings and riser closets that make cable easy. They were also built with their own camera and entry systems, chosen by the developer. Work in these buildings almost always starts with the owner or the managing agent and the vendor already in place. When an owner asks us to add views, such as a bike room, a package room or a garage ramp, we look at the existing recorder before suggesting anything, and we add to it where it is sound rather than putting in a second system that staff then have to learn. For a retail tenant at the base of one of these buildings, we work inside the leased space, put the recorder in the tenant's own back room and keep the building's lobby out of the tenant's frame.

Warehouses and logistics buildings: height, steel and vehicles

The eastern side of Port Morris is mostly light industry and warehouses, and the new logistics building at 307 Bruckner Boulevard shows where it is heading: NY YIMBY describes covered fleet parking at ground level, a high-bay warehouse with 36-foot clear heights above, four loading docks and two drive-in doors. Cameras in buildings like these have to cope with height, steel and moving vehicles. High overviews of the floor need lenses chosen for the distance, not wide-angle cameras that turn the far end into a blur. Dock cameras go inside each door, out of the path of the trucks, and the long view of the street goes on the building face. Cable rides on supports along the structure, or in conduit wherever a forklift mast or a truck could strike it, and it never rests on sprinkler pipe. Fleet parking needs cameras at the entry and exit lanes at bumper height and overviews mounted where a van's roof will not block them.

Antique Row and the corridor shops: crowded floors and small valuables

A store full of furniture, lamps and small objects is one of the hardest places to cover with cameras, because every piece of stock is also something to hide behind. The core views are the same as in any shop: a camera just inside the door at head height that records one face per customer, a camera over the counter that shows hands and what crosses it, and a camera on the back door or stock room from inside. On top of those, an antiques floor needs overviews from at least two opposite corners, mounted high enough to look over tall pieces, and close views on any case that holds jewelry, silver or other small items. Varifocal lenses let us zoom each view so the case or the aisle fills the frame. When the floor is rearranged, which in an antique shop happens often, the cameras need a quick check; we show owners how to look at each view and nudge an alert zone without calling anyone. The recorder goes in a back room out of reach from the floor, on battery backup.

Restaurants, bars and taprooms: the register, the door and the back

The Bronx Brewery and the restaurants along Bruckner Boulevard and Alexander Avenue all share the same three camera needs. The register and the bar need a camera above and slightly in front, framed on hands, cash and cards rather than on customers' faces at the tables. The entrance needs a close camera at head height for identity. And the back of the house, where deliveries come in and staff go out with the trash, needs a camera of its own, because that door is often propped. A screen in the office or behind the bar, angled so customers cannot see it, lets a manager glance at all of them at once. Kitchens get housings that tolerate heat and grease, and cameras never cover restrooms, staff changing areas or lockers. Audio stays off. Staff accounts can be limited to live view, with playback and export kept to the owner and managers.

Warehouses, workshops and yards: docks, gates and the part nobody walks past

On the industrial blocks east of the Bruckner, much of what is worth stealing sits in a yard or behind a roll-up door, and most of the building is out of sight of anyone walking by. A warehouse plan starts at the gate, with a camera at bumper height on each lane and a high view over the gate to the street, and continues with a close camera inside each loading door. The floor gets overviews aimed along the aisles. The side and rear walls, which on these blocks often face a lot, a rail line or another business's yard, get cameras aimed along the wall so that anyone at a window, a vent or a pipe chase is recorded. Fleet parking gets cameras covering each row and the exits. Yard equipment such as generators, compressors or stored materials gets a camera aimed at it directly. Every camera records continuously, the clock is synced and the names say exactly which door, lane or wall each camera watches.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Port Morris

  • Private spaces stay unrecorded. Restrooms, changing areas, locker rooms and lactation or first-aid rooms are never covered, and we check each view for mirrors and glass that might reveal one.
  • Payment screens and keypads stay out of frame. Counter cameras are aimed at hands and items, not at card readers or monitors; anything that cannot be framed out is blacked out in the camera.
  • The landlord decides outside the lease line. Facades, sign bands, shared yards, roofs and cellars belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement before mounting there.
  • Landmarked facades go through the Commission. A business in the Clock Tower or another designated building needs the owner to clear exterior cameras with the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Public property is off limits. Nothing goes on lampposts, street signs, bus shelters or street trees on Bruckner Boulevard, Alexander Avenue or anywhere else.
  • Video only, no sound. Business cameras record pictures, not conversations.
  • Rated walls and floors stay rated. Any opening through a fire-rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.
  • Line-voltage power is an electrician's work. If a camera location needs a new outlet or circuit, a licensed electrician does that part, and the quote says so.
  • Proof on paper. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and we send proof to landlords, managing agents and yard operators ahead of the visit when asked.
Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Look for on the First Walk Through a Port Morris Building

The first walk through a Port Morris building is mostly about reading what it used to be. In a converted factory, we look for the old freight entrance and ask who still uses it, because that door is often the busiest one and the least watched. We look at the cellar ceiling to see whether it is open or boarded over, and at the corridors to see whether the conversion gave them a ceiling cable can ride above. We find every old camera, box and recorder, trace where each wire goes and write down which ones are dead. Outside, we stand at each entrance and look along the street both ways, because the second view we want to add depends on where the corner is and what lies beyond it. In a yard, we walk the fence and look for the places it has been patched, which tell us where it was cut before. And we ask the same questions everywhere: who owns this wall, who rents this space, and who is going to open the recorder when something happens. The answers decide the plan more than any camera spec does.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Port Morris: Questions Answered

Renters, owners and who records whom

Most homes in Port Morris are rented. If I rent a whole rehabilitated row house near Willow Avenue, whose permission do I need before cameras go on the front and the back?

The owner's, in writing, for anything fixed to the outside or wired through the walls. Renting the whole house gives you the use of it, not the right to drill its brick or run cable through its cellar. We ask for a short note from the owner or the managing agent that names the address, lists the camera spots and says cable may pass through the cellar, and we send the owner a plan with a photo of each view so there are no surprises. If the owner agrees, the cameras are installed as they would be for any owner, and the lease or the note should say whether they stay with the house or come down when you leave. If the owner says no, cameras inside the house aimed at the front and back doors and the ground-floor windows still give you a record, and they leave nothing on the outside to argue about.

A Mott Haven tenant's own camera recorded the landlord coming in and taking out the electricity meter. Is it legal for a renter to record whoever enters the apartment?

Inside your own apartment, a visible camera recording your own rooms and your own door is generally your call. The New York laws that matter here are aimed at recording people where they expect privacy, such as bathrooms and bedrooms, and at secretly recording conversations. So we set tenant cameras to record video only, aim them at the inside of the front door and the main room, and keep them away from bathrooms and from any room a roommate or a home attendant uses privately. The clip is stronger if it is ordinary: the camera has been there for weeks, the clock is right, and the recording is exported with the camera's own app rather than filmed off a phone screen. Keep the original, do not post it online, and give copies to whoever is handling the dispute. This is practical guidance, not legal advice, and a tenant with a serious dispute should speak to a tenant lawyer or advocate.

One installer's website says a city law makes owners post signs when common areas have cameras. Is that true?

We could not confirm it. One vendor's blog cites a "Local Law 50" posting rule for common-area cameras, and we found no source we could check that supports that. What we did find is a summary of New York Penal Law section 250.65, which exempts security systems from the state's unlawful surveillance law where notice is posted or the cameras are clearly visible. Because of that, and because a sign is cheap, we recommend that every Port Morris building with lobby, hall or yard cameras post a plain notice at the street door and keep the cameras themselves in view rather than hidden. It also tends to cut down on the arguments with tenants that a hidden camera invites.

Lofts, the landmark and the towers

We live in the Clock Tower, the landmarked former Estey piano factory on Lincoln Avenue. If the building wants street cameras, how many can go on the facade, and where?

That decision belongs to the building's owner and the Landmarks Preservation Commission, not to individual residents. The Commission's 2018 rules amendments cover equipment on facades, including security cameras, and require installations to minimize or avoid damage to significant architectural features. A secondary source summarizing those rules says the Commission limits cameras by street frontage, roughly two or three per 25 feet, and generally discourages mounting on a primary facade unless there is no other option. In practice that points to small, neutral-colored cameras fixed into mortar joints rather than brick faces, cable brought inside right at the mount, cameras on secondary elevations where they do the job, and cameras inside the entrance looking out through the door. The owner files for the work. A resident who wants a record of their own door can use an indoor camera within the unit.

Our converted factory has no hallways, closets or ceilings that were originally built for wiring. What route does camera cable actually take?

It follows whatever the conversion added, plus the cellar. Most loft conversions gave each floor a corridor with a ceiling, a utility path up from the cellar and a lobby with a dropped ceiling, and those are the first routes we look for. From the recorder, cable runs along the cellar to the base of the existing low-voltage path, rises with the other cable and travels above each corridor ceiling to its camera. Where it has to pass through a masonry wall, we core one planned opening for several cables instead of drilling many small holes. Cable never goes in an elevator shaft, and it never hangs from sprinkler pipe. Where there is truly no hidden path, a painted surface raceway in a hall or metal conduit in a cellar or a stair is the tidy answer, and the quote says which runs will be exposed so nobody is surprised.

Our tower advertises valet parking with EV charging. If a car is damaged or something goes missing from it, which camera shows who had the keys and when?

The views that settle that kind of question are at the handover, not in the parking rows. In a building like Third at Bankside, which advertises valet parking with EV charging, the garage cameras belong to the building or its parking operator, so a resident's first step is to ask management to save the footage while it still exists. For an owner or operator planning or upgrading such a garage, the key points are a camera over the key cabinet or key board that shows hands and tags, a camera at the handover counter that shows the driver and the attendant together, cameras at the entry and exit lanes at bumper height, and overviews of each charging row. All of them need a synced clock so the key camera and the lane camera tell the same story, and a retention period long enough that a complaint made a few days later still finds the footage.

Bankside's waterfront park stays open until 1 a.m. right next to the buildings. How does a ground-floor shop or a building door get watched without recording everyone in the park?

By framing and masking. Recording what anyone can see from a public place is generally lawful, but a camera aimed across a park records a lot of strangers and identifies none of them, and it makes the footage you actually need harder to find. We aim the door camera along the face of the building so it records people at the door and on the strip of sidewalk in front of it, set the motion zones so that people walking on the park paths do not trigger alerts, and black out any part of the frame that takes in the park's seating. For a shop, a camera inside the glass looking at the entrance does most of the work. Recording stays continuous; only the alerts are narrowed.

Yards, lots, shops and sets

Thieves have carried generators and solar panels off open lots like La Finca Del Sur. When the equipment itself sits outdoors with no building close by, where do the cameras go and how are they powered?

Aim one camera at the equipment, one at the gate or the stretch of fence where people get in, and one at the curb where a vehicle would wait, and mount all of them high on a pole or post so they are not the next thing taken. Power is the hard part. If a building is within reach, the best answer is a buried or overhead cable from that building, which carries power and picture together and keeps the recorder indoors. With no building nearby, a camera with its own solar panel, battery and cellular connection can work, but it can be stolen too, so it goes higher and is locked down, and its clips are copied off the site as they are recorded. Chain or anchor the generators, add a light, and expect an open lot to be harder to cover than a building; we will say plainly what a camera there can and cannot do.

Our antiques shop on Bruckner Boulevard has small pieces spread across a crowded floor. Where do cameras go so a piece that slips into a pocket shows up on the recording?

Start with the three views every shop needs: the door at head height, the counter from above and the back door from inside. Then add overviews from at least two opposite corners, high enough to look over armoires and tall shelving, and close views on any case or table that holds jewelry, silver, watches or other small pieces, zoomed so the case fills the frame and a hand reaching in is obvious. Varifocal lenses make that possible without crowding the floor with cameras. Keep the smallest, most valuable items in the cases those cameras watch, and after rearranging the floor, check each view on the screen so a new wardrobe is not blocking it. Record continuously, keep two weeks or more of footage, and the gap between a piece being picked up and leaving the shop will usually be on the record.

Our taproom fills up on weekends and the brewing floor sits behind it. Can staff watch the bar and the brew floor on one screen that customers cannot see?

Yes. The recorder can show every camera on one monitor in a tiled layout, and that monitor can sit in the office, behind the bar below counter height or on a wall facing away from the room. We set a weekend layout with the register, the bar, the entrance and the brew floor large and the back door smaller, and staff can switch layouts without touching anything else. The brew floor camera is an overview mounted where steam and washdown spray will not reach it. Staff logins can be limited to live view, so nobody can scroll back or export without the owner, and cameras stay out of changing areas and restrooms. Audio stays off throughout.

A production company wants to rent our warehouse as a set for a few weeks. Should our cameras keep recording while they shoot?

Settle it in writing before the first day. With Silvercup's North lot on Locust Avenue, the area is used to productions, and productions commonly ask that interior cameras that appear in shot be covered, turned or paused. That is reasonable, and the recorder can black out or pause specific cameras on a schedule. Perimeter cameras on the gates, loading doors and yard should keep recording, because a busy shoot brings many people, trucks and rented equipment onto the property, and if something goes missing both sides will want the footage. Agree who may view and export recordings during the rental, keep the owner as the only administrator, and check every camera's aim and settings once the production has left.

Licenses, Privacy and Permission

The Rules Behind a Port Morris Camera Job, Laid Out Plainly

Most Port Morris camera jobs involve no city filing at all, which is why the rules that do apply are easy to overlook. They come from five places: the state's installer licensing law, New York's privacy laws, the ownership chain of the building or lot, the Landmarks Preservation Commission for the one designated building in the neighborhood, and the city's rules about power and fire-rated construction. Here is how each one plays out in the lofts, row houses, towers, warehouses and shops here.

Who may install. New York General Business Law section 69-m, part of Article 6-D, says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. The sources disagree on where cameras fit. The Department of State's installer application says installing "passive," non-detecting security devices does not require a license, while other sources say no one may install video surveillance for pay without one. The same application treats owners installing systems on their own property as outside the business of installing, so an owner putting up their own cameras is not the problem. The question is a handyman or contractor who does it for pay. We settle the question by holding the license: AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, and we will send a copy to any owner, board, landlord or yard operator who asks. One installer's site claims the city requires a licensed electrician for every wired camera job; we could not verify that. New line-voltage power, such as an outlet for a gate camera or a circuit in a cellar, is electrician's work, and the quote lists it separately when a job needs it.

Where a camera may look. Recording what anyone can see from the street is generally lawful in New York. Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives residents a claim against a neighbor whose camera records them where they reasonably expect privacy, and the state's unlawful surveillance law covers recording people in places such as bathrooms and bedrooms. On close-set Port Morris blocks, where row-house fronts sit next to each other and loft windows face loft windows across the street, we frame every outdoor camera on your doors, your yard, your gate and your curb, and black out anything else that falls in the frame. Cameras record video, not sound.

Who decides. The owner of a building decides what goes on its walls and in its shared spaces. In a loft building, that may mean an owner, a managing agent or a board; in a tower, it is the owner and the vendor it has chosen; in a row house with a tenant, it is the owner, in writing. A business tenant decides what goes inside its own space and needs the landlord for anything outside it. A yard or lot operator may need the landowner's agreement if the lease says so. We ask who signs before the plan is drawn, not after the cameras are on the wall, and we put the handover of administrator logins in writing so the system does not end up belonging to whoever happened to set it up.

Landmarks. The Estey Piano Company Factory, now the Clock Tower apartments, is an individual city landmark, so its facade is subject to the Commission's rules on exterior equipment. The Mott Haven and Mott Haven East historic districts lie nearby but outside Port Morris. We found no other designated building inside Port Morris in the research for this page, and we check each address on the Commission's map before drilling anyway, because it takes a minute.

Permits. One installer states that low-voltage camera work in residential buildings typically does not need a Department of Buildings permit; that matches our experience on ordinary camera jobs, but we have not seen it in a city document, so we treat each job on its facts. Work that adds new power, touches fire-rated construction or ties into fire alarm or door-release hardware is handled with the trades and filings that work requires.

Walls, floors and water. Every opening through masonry is sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in a multiple dwelling, a loft building or a warehouse is sealed with rated firestop. On blocks near the water, recorders and switches go above the cellar floor, on a shelf or wall bracket, because the waterfront has flooded before and a recorder sitting in water loses exactly the footage you will want.

"Port Morris is an industrial area, so camera work there is business work."

The eastern side really is mostly light industry and warehouses, and businesses there are a large share of the camera work.

A large part of Port Morris camera work is therefore residential, and much of it involves a renter, an owner and a building system at once. The plan has to account for all three.

"A new tower with keyless entry and a concierge already has security covered."

Our Installation Process

Every Port Morris camera job, from a single indoor camera in a loft apartment to a row house, a warehouse with docks and a yard, or a shop on Antique Row, follows the same eight steps. Steps two and three carry the most weight here: settling who has the right to approve each camera, and walking each entrance and gate to find the long view that shows the way out.

1. Send photos and the address

The address, photos of the street door, lobby, loading door, yard, fence, counter or apartment door, who owns it, who rents it and who will watch the footage. Requests are booked within 2 hours.

2. Settle who signs off

The owner or managing agent, a board, a store's landlord, a yard's landowner, or the Landmarks Preservation Commission for the Clock Tower, in writing, before the plan is set.

3. Walk the building, yard and street

From the curb to each entrance, through the cellar and halls or along the fence to the gate, marking where faces and vehicles can be recorded and where the long view goes.

4. Give each camera one named job

Street door, freight door, yard gate, register: one task per camera, named in plain words an officer or a new manager could read.

5. Write the plan and the price

Each camera, mount, cable run, wall opening, recorder, drive, battery unit, viewing screen and setup task listed before anything is ordered.

6. Install through brick, concrete and steel

Cable through the cellar, existing chases, corridor ceilings or conduit, one planned core per masonry wall, every opening sealed, and the recorder locked away above the cellar floor.

7. Set the clock, test day and night

Recorder synced to a time server, each cable certified, every view checked in daylight and after dark, unique passwords and two-step sign-in on every account.

8. Hand over logins, a camera map and an export test

Each person gets only the views they need, the owner or manager locks and exports a test clip while we watch, and we leave a written map naming every camera and port.
