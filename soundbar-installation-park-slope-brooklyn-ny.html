The Neighborhood

Why Park Slope Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Park Slope is the one corner of Brooklyn where the room itself is very often the most valuable thing in it. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Park Slope Historic District in 1973; Wikipedia's entry on the district counts 1,802 contributing buildings put up between 1862 and about 1920, almost all of them two- and three-storey rowhouses on the blocks running down from Prospect Park. Two extensions followed, in 2012 and 2016 — the second alone added 292 buildings, mostly between St. Marks Avenue and Sterling Place, New York YIMBY reported — and the district is now described as the largest landmarked neighbourhood in New York by number of buildings. Landmark status governs facades, not living rooms, but it has helped keep a remarkable amount of interior detail alive behind those facades.

That detail is what makes a soundbar job here different. Read the property listings on Brownstoner for a season and the same inventory keeps appearing in Park Slope parlors: a pier mirror between the front windows, a marble or wood mantel with an overmantel mirror, plaster cornices and ceiling medallions, pocket doors between the front and back rooms, inlaid parquet. A listing for a house on Third Street describes a "grand pier mirror" that "dominates the front parlor"; one on Berkeley Place notes that the house "still has a fair number of its 1880s details in place, including mantels, wood floors, a pier mirror, and stained glass." Those are exactly the surfaces a TV and soundbar want to sit on or beside, and none of them can be bought new.

The second thing that sets the Slope apart is who is in the house. Rowhouses here were built for one family; today a great many are split into an owner's duplex with one or two rental floors, or converted into small condominiums and co-ops of three or four apartments. NYU's Furman Center puts the homeownership rate for the Park Slope/Carroll Gardens district at 37.1 percent in 2024, above the citywide 32.7 percent, which in practice means a lot of owners living directly above or below someone who pays them rent. When a family's TV room shares a floor with a tenant's bedroom, or the children's rooms are a flight above the sofa, the sound has to be set as carefully as the bracket. Threads such as "Sensitive neighbor situation (condo owners)" on Reddit's r/parkslope show how quickly that turns into a conversation between neighbours.

Not all of Park Slope is historic, though. The City Council's April 30, 2003 rezoning covered 110 blocks with two aims, in City Planning's own words: to "preserve the historic scale of the brownstone neighborhoods, and provide increased opportunities for residential and commercial development on Fourth Avenue." The brownstone mid-blocks were capped with contextual districts, while Fourth Avenue was mapped R8A, and it has since filled with mid-rise condominiums and rental buildings whose living rooms have metal-stud partitions, concrete columns and management offices. Along Prospect Park West the pre-war elevator co-ops — 9 and 35 Prospect Park West were both completed in 1929 — sit somewhere in between, with plaster walls, board rules and a super who wants to know who is coming up in the elevator.

Put together, a soundbar installation in Park Slope is rarely about the bar. It is about finding a fixing that won't crack an 1890s ceiling or catch a pocket-door track, routing cable without disturbing a mantel or an original surround, choosing which of two parlors the TV belongs in, and setting the subwoofer so the people downstairs and the toddler upstairs both get a quiet night. This page is written for both halves of the neighbourhood.

Good work here starts by asking what is on the wall and who is on the other side of it.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Park Slope's Architecture

Park Slope's housing falls into four groups, and the soundbar visit changes with each. The rowhouse blocks between Fifth Avenue and Prospect Park West were mostly built in the late nineteenth century in Romanesque Revival, Queen Anne and neo-Grec dress — brownstone fronts, but also limestone, brick and terra cotta, like the 1891 George P. Chappell Queen Anne rowhouse the Brooklyn Studio restored, or the 1892 Romanesque Revival houses on Montgomery Place that Brownstoner profiled. Behind the facades the construction is broadly the same: brick party walls, timber floors and lime plaster. The apartment houses and the new avenue buildings are another matter.

Rowhouse, Whole-House Owners

Single-family brownstones and limestones where one household uses every floor. The TV competes with the parlor's original features for wall space, and the main sound question is the bedrooms upstairs rather than a neighbour below.

Rowhouse Split Into Apartments

The most common arrangement: an owner's duplex over a garden rental, or three or four floor-throughs, sometimes now a small condo or co-op. Every TV wall and floor is shared with somebody, and the house may have a board of three.

Prospect Park West Elevator Co-ops

Pre-war buildings facing the park, such as 35 Prospect Park West, an Emery Roth design completed in 1929 at sixteen storeys. Plaster partitions, deep window reveals, parquet, and a managing agent with alteration rules and insurance requirements.

Walk-Ups Over the Avenues

Three- and four-storey buildings with shops at street level along Fifth and Seventh Avenues, zoned R6A in 2003. Railroad and floor-through flats, plaster or patched drywall over brick, steep stairs and a restaurant kitchen or bar underneath.

Fourth Avenue Buildings

Condominiums and rentals built since the 2003 R8A rezoning: metal studs, concrete columns and slabs, window walls, and sometimes a recessed TV box with conduit roughed in by the developer.

Garden and Basement Apartments

The lowest floor of the rowhouse, half below the sidewalk, with brick or block exterior walls, a lower ceiling and the boiler room nearby. Often a tenant's living room directly under the owner's parlor.

Garden apartments deserve a word of their own. Commercial Soundbar

Business Soundbar Installation in Park Slope

Park Slope's businesses sit almost entirely under somebody's apartment, which shapes every screen we put in for them. The commercial avenues are lined with three- and four-storey mixed buildings, so a café TV or a studio screen is one ceiling away from a bedroom. A soundbar suits that setting better than a pair of big speakers: it throws sound forward at the seats rather than up into the joists, and it can be held to a modest level with a remote that stays behind the counter.

Fifth Avenue

The neighbourhood's busiest strip, with 521 ground-floor businesses on the 62 block faces of the Park Slope 5th Avenue BID. Bars, restaurants and shops, many with one screen at the bar end. WXY's work for the BID notes stormwater that damaged more than 30 businesses in 2023, so we keep subwoofers and power strips off the floor in street-level rooms.

Seventh Avenue

The Slope's other main street, which one 2026 neighbourhood guide describes as skewing "toward upscale dining, boutique shopping, and specialty food stores." Smaller rooms, quieter screens, and very often a waiting-area or counter TV that wants dialogue, not volume.

Fourth Avenue

Ground-floor retail and community spaces in the new R8A buildings: higher ceilings, concrete, and landlord fit-out rules. Bars here are usually on metal-stud walls with the building's own requirements for insurance and work hours.

Flatbush Avenue Edge

The northern boundary toward Grand Army Plaza, with restaurants and shops facing heavy traffic. Rooms that need clear speech over street noise rather than more bass.

Studios, Clinics and Offices

Fitness and yoga studios, therapists, paediatric and dental practices and small offices on the avenues' upper floors. Screens for classes or waiting rooms, with a volume someone on staff can set and forget.

Compliance notes for business soundbars on the Slope

  • Commercial music limits: the city's Noise Code, as summarised by the Department of Environmental Protection, caps sound from commercial music at 42 dB inside a nearby residence and 7 dB over ambient at fifteen feet on the street, measured between 10 PM and 7 AM, with a separate limit on pervasive bass. With apartments directly overhead, those numbers matter more on Fifth Avenue than almost anywhere.
  • Protrusion near walkways: in a shop or clinic, a TV and bar beside an aisle or a stair shouldn't jut into the path where a person with low vision could walk into them. The federal accessibility standards set the depth that is allowed at those heights, and we place the bar with that in mind.
  • Landmark district storefronts: inside a business, a soundbar is ordinary low-voltage work. Anything that would go on a facade, an awning or a roof in the historic district is a different conversation with the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and we don't mount speakers outside.
  • Proof of cover for the building owner: a shop's lease on Fifth or Seventh Avenue may require the owner to be named on a contractor's insurance certificate. Tell us whose name goes on it and we'll send the certificate, at no charge, before the visit.
Original Detail

The Parlor Features We Work Around

This is the section most Park Slope owners read first, because it is the reason they hesitate to let anyone drill. The features below are the ones we meet most often in Slope parlors and front rooms. For each, here is what it is, why it matters to a soundbar, and what we do about it.

Pier mirrors

A tall mirror, usually in a carved or gilded frame, standing on a low marble shelf between the two front windows of the parlor. Brownstoner's Park Slope listings mention them constantly because so many survive. A pier mirror is heavy, old and held to the wall by cleats or hooks set into the masonry behind it, and the glass is often original. We never fix anything to the frame or the wall immediately around it, never lean ladders against it, and when it is the only wall facing the sofa, we look first at whether the TV belongs on another wall entirely. The question-and-answer section below goes through that choice.

Mantels and overmantels

Marble mantels in the parlor and wood or slate ones on the upper floors, sometimes with an overmantel mirror or carved cabinet above. The chimney breast behind is a reasonable place for a TV if the flue is respected, but the mantel itself is not a shelf for a heavy bar and its surround is not a place for cable. We keep fixings to solid brick on the breast, above or beside the overmantel, and run the lead down beside the breast rather than across the marble.

Cornices, medallions and picture mouldings

Run-in-place plaster cornices at the ceiling and medallions around the light fixture are fragile in a way flat plaster isn't: they are thick, heavy, and held mostly by their own keying into the lath. A ladder knock or a long drill bit angled upward can crack them. We plan cable routes that stay well below the cornice line and never fish wire through a ceiling with a medallion on it.

Pocket doors

Wide sliding doors between the front and back parlors that disappear into the partition. As the Brownstone Boys explain in their restoration guide, the door hardware "sits inside the wall," with rollers running on a track at the top of the pocket. That makes the wall on either side of the opening partly hollow and partly occupied. We map the pocket's extent before marking a single hole.

Inlaid parquet and original floors

Borders of contrasting wood, sometimes in intricate patterns, especially in Prospect Park West apartments and upper-floor parlors. We work on drop cloths and moving blankets, carry ladders rather than drag them, and put subwoofers on pads, not straight onto finish that can't be sanded again.

Front parlor or back parlor

In a double parlor, the TV most often belongs in the back room. The front parlor faces the street, with two or three tall windows, the pier mirror, and the most decorative mantel in the house, so it has the least spare wall, the most glare and the most traffic noise from the block. The back parlor usually looks over the garden, has a plainer chimney breast or a long side wall, and is quieter at night, which helps dialogue more than any setting. Splitting the rooms also suits the way many Slope households live: a formal front room left as it is, and a back room that is allowed to be a TV room. The case for the front is a family that watches with friends and wants the larger space, or a back parlor that has become the kitchen. Either way, we look at what is below each room. If the garden floor is a rental, the back parlor is often over the tenant's bedroom, which pushes the subwoofer decision toward a bar with built-in bass, or a sub kept low and well isolated. Tell us how the floors are used and we'll weigh the two rooms with you before anything is drilled.

What we will tell you not to do: fix to a mirror or overmantel frame, cut into a cornice, run cable across a marble surround, or drill a pocket-door wall blind. If the only way to get the TV where you want it involves one of these, we'll say so plainly and offer the nearest alternative — another wall, a stand-and-console layout, or a bracket that rides on the TV mount.
Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Park Slope: Questions Answered

These are the questions Park Slope households put to us that we don't hear the same way anywhere else in the city. They come from the houses themselves: their original features, their double parlors, their shared floors and their newer neighbours on Fourth Avenue. Each answer is the one we would give standing in your room.

Original parlor detail

The only wall our sofa faces holds the parlor's original pier mirror — can the TV and soundbar go there without disturbing it?

Sometimes, but rarely in front of it. A pier mirror sits between the two front windows, so the wall it occupies is mostly window, reveal and mirror; there is seldom a clear run of solid wall wide enough for a TV and a bar beneath it that doesn't touch the frame or the marble shelf. Mounting over the mirror means drilling through or right beside the cleats that hold it, which we won't do, and covering it with a screen hides the very thing many owners bought the house for. The arrangement that usually works is to swing the room around: put the TV on the facing wall or on the chimney breast, and let the sofa face away from the street. If the furniture really can't move, a TV on a slim floor stand in front of the pier, with the bar on a TV-attached bracket, keeps every screw off the old wall and lets the whole setup leave the room when you have guests. If the mirror ever has to come down, that is a job for a conservator or an antiques mover, not for us.

The pocket doors between our front and back parlors slide into the wall we want to use — where can the bracket go?

Not into the pocket. When the doors are open, each leaf sits inside the partition, running on rollers from a track at the top of the opening; the restoration writers at the Brownstone Boys put it simply: the hardware "sits inside the wall." A screw driven into that section can score the door, foul the track, or stop it closing. So we start by opening and closing the doors and measuring how far each pocket runs, usually a little more than the width of each leaf beyond the jamb. Past that point the partition is normally ordinary plaster on studs, and a bar of modest width can be fixed there with a backer if needed. If the TV has to be centred over the pocket itself, the bracket goes on the TV mount rather than the wall, or the TV moves to a stand. We also keep any cable out of the pocket; a lead dropped inside one is the classic cause of a door that suddenly sticks halfway.

Our fireplace hasn't worked in decades — can the subwoofer sit inside the old firebox opening?

It can, but it usually shouldn't stay there. The firebox looks like a perfect hidden cubby, and a sub fits, but three things go wrong. First, the opening is a brick box connected to a flue, and a subwoofer inside it tends to make the whole chimney resonate, which is louder for the neighbours who share the stack than for you. Second, even a sealed-up flue lets down soot, grit and cold air, which is hard on the sub's driver and electronics. Third, a sub tucked into a cavity loses much of the even bass it gives out in the open, so people turn it up to compensate. If the opening is properly blocked and lined, we'll try the sub there during the listening check and compare it with a spot on a pad a few feet out from the breast. Most households choose the open spot once they hear both. Never put a sub or any electronics in a fireplace that is still used, or a gas insert, even occasionally.

Protecting old plaster

Can a subwoofer's vibration crack old plaster ceilings or loosen a cornice, ours or the neighbour's?

At normal listening levels, a domestic subwoofer is very unlikely to crack sound plaster, but plaster in a Slope house is rarely all sound. Ceilings that have already separated from their lath, and cornices or medallions that were patched with the wrong material, can buzz, shed dust and loosen further under repeated low-frequency vibration, and the ceiling most exposed is the one under the floor the sub stands on, often somebody else's. Three things keep the risk down. Placement: the sub goes on a solid section of floor near a bearing wall, not mid-span between joists where the floor flexes most. Isolation: a dense pad or platform under it, so the cabinet doesn't drive the floorboards directly. Level: the sub set noticeably below the bar's default, with the crossover chosen so the bar does as much of the work as it can. If there's already a cracked ceiling below, or a medallion that has moved, tell us; we'd rather set the system around it than discover it by the light fixture swinging.

Wireless in a crowded house

Will a wireless soundbar and subwoofer interfere with the baby monitor?

It depends on which kind of monitor you have. Many audio and video monitors sold in the US use DECT 6.0, which runs in its own band around 1.9 GHz; as one monitor maker puts it, that is "a dedicated frequency band that does not interfere with WiFi." Those monitors and a soundbar generally leave each other alone. Monitors that stream over Wi-Fi, or older models that use the open 2.4 GHz band, share the same crowded air as your router, your neighbours' routers and, in some systems, the bar's link to its sub and rear speakers. In a rowhouse with a router on every floor and a dozen networks visible through the party walls, that can show up as a choppy monitor picture or a sub that drops out. The fixes are practical: wire the bar to the router with Ethernet where possible, move the bar and monitor to different bands or channels, and keep the sub's wireless path short and unobstructed. We test both together before we leave, with the monitor in the nursery and the TV playing.

Could the people next door connect to our soundbar over Bluetooth through the party wall?

Only if the bar is left discoverable. Soundbar manuals from Sony and Yamaha both give a pairing range of about 10 metres (33 feet), and a brick party wall cuts that down a lot, but in a rowhouse the next family's living room can be just a few feet away. A bar that sits in pairing mode, or that accepts any new device without a button press, can occasionally be grabbed by a phone next door or on the floor above — the classic sign is music you didn't choose coming out of your speakers. During setup we pair the phones and tablets you want, then switch off open pairing or set the bar to require a button press on the unit, depending on what the model allows. Where the bar supports it, we also turn off features you won't use, such as guest casting or an always-on Bluetooth input. If it ever happens afterwards, removing unknown devices from the bar's list and turning pairing off again solves it.

Equipment

Types of Soundbars and Mounts We Install

We install the bar you choose, from any maker; we don't sell soundbars. What we bring is the bracket, the fixings and the cable that suit your wall, and an honest view of whether the bar suits your room. These are the categories we fit most often in Park Slope, with the rooms they tend to suit.

Compact Bars

Short, light bars for bedrooms, studies and narrow floor-throughs. Sonos lists the Beam (Gen 2) at 6.35 lb with an HDMI eARC connection; What Hi-Fi measures the Ray at 1.95 kg, and it connects by optical rather than HDMI. Easy on a plaster wall, easy on the neighbours.

Full-Width Bars

Long bars with a dedicated centre channel and, on many models, up-firing drivers. Sized for parlors and larger TVs; a flagship such as the Sonos Arc Ultra is about 46 inches wide and about 5.9 kg, which wants solid fixings and a clear stretch of wall.

Bar, Subwoofer and Rears

A bar with a wireless sub and two small rear speakers. The fullest sound and the most to plan: three or four power outlets, a sub position that respects the floor below, and rears that can stand behind a sofa that floats in a long room.

Low-Profile Wall Brackets

Flat brackets that hold the bar a short distance below the TV. Either the one from the box, or a universal bracket we supply when the bar came without one or its own suits drywall but not brick and plaster.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Park Slope

Search summaries, chatbots and national cost guides answer soundbar questions in general terms. Most of that is fine for a suburban house with drywall. In a Park Slope parlor it misses things that decide whether the job goes well. Here are seven claims we hear repeated, and what a Slope room says back.

Claim one: "A brownstone wall is solid brick, so any masonry anchor will do"

The facade and the party walls of a rowhouse are masonry, but most of the interior walls are not. The partition between front and back parlors, the walls around the stair hall and the closets are typically wood studs with lime plaster on lath, and some later alterations are drywall on whatever framing was handy. A masonry anchor driven into lath and plaster has nothing to grip.

Even the brick varies. Park Slope's houses were built across roughly six decades by many different builders, with brownstone, limestone, brick and terra cotta fronts, and the common brick behind them ranges from hard to crumbly.

So the first job on any Slope wall is to find out what it is at each fixing point, with a small pilot hole, before choosing an anchor. A single wall can need timber screws at one end and masonry anchors at the other.

Claim two: "You need the city's permission to mount anything in a landmarked house"

This one worries Park Slope owners more than it should. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates exteriors and work that needs a Department of Buildings permit; the city's own guidance for landmarked properties says interior work that doesn't need a building permit, like ordinary repairs, doesn't need LPC review. A soundbar installation is low-voltage work that plugs into existing outlets, so the landmark designation on its own doesn't bring it under review.

Where permission really does come in is closer to home. A co-op or condo board in a converted brownstone or a Prospect Park West building can have its own alteration rules, and a lease can forbid holes. Those are the approvals that matter for a soundbar, and they are about the building's rules, not the city's.

The landmark rules do bite if the plan grows outward: speakers on a facade, a dish or antenna visible from the street, anything on the stoop or the roof. We don't install those, and they are a question for the Commission before anyone else.

Claim three: "Put the soundbar at seated ear height, about 42 inches off the floor"

Manufacturer placement guides suggest a bar close to the TV and aimed at ear level; Yamaha's guide puts that ear height at about 42.5 inches. It is a sound rule for a TV on a stand. In a Slope parlor with eleven- or twelve-foot ceilings, the TV is often hung higher, over a mantel or above a low console, and a bar left at 42 inches would sit a foot or more below the screen.

The bar belongs with the picture, directly under the TV, because voices should come from where the faces are. Dolby's own advice is to keep TV and bar as close together as practical. When the screen is high, we close the gap and, where the bracket allows, angle the bar slightly down toward the seats.

The better fix for a high TV is often the TV's height, not the bar's. If the screen is up because of the mantel, we'll say whether another wall in the room would bring both down to a comfortable level.

Claim four: "Wireless soundbars mean there are no cables to worry about"

Wireless means the sub and rear speakers talk to the bar without speaker wire. It doesn't mean they run without power. Each one needs an outlet where it stands, and the bar still needs its HDMI lead to the TV.

Rowhouse parlors were wired long after they were built, often with very few outlets, sometimes one per wall and rarely where a sofa now floats in the middle of a long room. Rear speakers behind that sofa can mean extension leads across the parquet unless an electrician adds outlets first.

We plan the wireless parts around the outlets you actually have, tell you where a new outlet would make the setup tidier, and leave the outlet work to a licensed electrician. We don't open electrical boxes.

Claim five: "National cost guides tell you what a soundbar install costs"

HomeWyse puts the national average for installing a soundbar at $58.43 to $231 per bar, and Thumbtack's national range for TV wall mounting is $121 to $234. Those are averages over mostly drywall houses with easy access.

A Slope job adds things those numbers don't price: plaster or brick in place of drywall, parlor ceilings high enough to need a taller ladder, a top-floor walk-up, a co-op's certificate of insurance, and the time it takes to plan around a pier mirror or a pocket door.

That is why our Brooklyn price list is built from lines: the mount, then the wall, then access. You can see the total before the visit, including any wall surcharge, instead of finding out on the day that "standard" meant drywall.

Claim six: "Bluetooth is the easiest way to hook a soundbar up to the TV"

It's easy, but it isn't the best. Bluetooth audio from a TV is compressed, can lag the picture enough to make lips and voices drift apart, and usually doesn't let the TV remote control the bar's volume.

The proper link is HDMI: the bar on the TV's ARC or eARC port with CEC enabled, which carries surround formats, keeps lip-sync tight, and lets one remote run everything. Optical is the fallback for TVs without HDMI audio return.

We use Bluetooth for what it's good at: music from a phone, once the TV connection is done properly. Then we lock pairing down so only your devices connect.

Claim seven: "A soundbar sounds the same wherever you put it"

Rooms change sound more than most bars do. A Slope parlor with tall ceilings, bare plaster, big windows and a wood floor reflects a lot of sound; voices can turn edgy and bass can boom in the corners. A small back room with a rug, books and curtains can sound dull by comparison.

Many bars include a room-calibration routine that uses a microphone in the bar or a phone to adjust for this, and running it from the main seat makes a real difference. Where a bar has no calibration, the treble, bass and dialogue settings do the same job by ear.

Placement is the other half: a bar centred under the screen and clear of the mantel's edge, a sub out of the corner, and soft furnishings where the first reflections land. We set all of it before leaving.

Quiet Hours

Setting Sound for a House That Sleeps in Shifts

In a lot of Slope households, somebody is always asleep. A baby naps after lunch, the older children go up at eight, the tenant downstairs starts work early, and the adults watch after everyone else is in bed. The bracket is the easy part. These are the settings and habits we leave a household with so the system fits that rhythm.

Bass is the part that travels

Voices and treble are blocked well by plaster, brick and doors; low bass is not, and it travels through floors and party walls as vibration as much as sound. That is why nearly every quiet-hours adjustment is about the subwoofer: where it stands, what it stands on, and how loud it is relative to the bar. We set the sub level noticeably below the factory default, choose a spot on solid floor near a bearing wall, and put it on a pad or platform, then show you the one setting that turns it down without touching the overall volume.

Night mode and dialogue settings, used on purpose

Most bars have a night or late-night mode that narrows the gap between quiet and loud passages, and a dialogue or voice setting that lifts speech. Used together after bedtime, they let you hear a film at a lower overall level, which is what the people upstairs and downstairs actually care about. Where the bar or its app supports it, we set these to switch on from a single button, and we label which button that is.

Stairwells and open doors carry sound up

A rowhouse stair is a tall open shaft. Sound from a TV in a parlor with its door open rises straight up it to the bedroom landings. Closing the parlor door, or putting the TV in the back parlor rather than beside the stair hall, does more for a sleeping child than any setting. We point out the path the sound will take in your house during the visit, so the household can decide where the TV lives with that in mind.

Summer windows and back gardens

Rowhouse blocks enclose a ring of back gardens, and in warm weather every rear window on the block opens onto the same shared space. A back-parlor TV with the windows up can be heard several gardens away, and bass carries further outdoors than people expect because nothing absorbs it. In summer the considerate setting is the same as the bedtime one: sub down, night mode on, and the volume set with the windows open rather than closed. If the household watches big games or films with the windows wide, closing the rear sashes for the loudest stretch does more than any setting.

Talk to the people on the other side

The single most useful thing in a shared rowhouse is a five-minute conversation. Tell the household downstairs or next door that a system is going in, and ask them to let you know if they hear it. With the owner's permission, we're glad to stand in the apartment below while the sub plays, which settles more arguments in advance than any measurement.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Slope Parlors and Shared Floors

How our installers approach a Park Slope parlor in general — a description of method, not a story about one visit

We walk the room before unpacking anything. The first pass is for things that can't be replaced: the mirror, the mantel, the cornice, the doors, the floor. Each one gets a mental exclusion zone, and the ladder and drop cloths are placed to respect them. Only then do we look at the wall where the TV is meant to go.

The second pass is for what's inside the wall. Pocket doors are opened and closed and their run measured. The chimney breast is tapped to find the flue. On a plaster partition, a magnet often finds the lath nails better than a scanner finds the studs. Every fixing point then gets a pilot hole, and a point that drills soft or hollow when its neighbours drilled firm is treated as a warning, not an anomaly.

The last pass is downward. Before the sub is placed we ask who lives under the room and how they use it, look at the floor for a firm spot near a bearing wall, and listen from the stair with the door open. In a Slope rowhouse the most important part of a soundbar job is often the part nobody sees.

FAQ

Park Slope Soundbar Installation FAQ

How many holes does a soundbar bracket actually need in an original plaster wall?
+

Usually two to four for the bracket itself. Most soundbar brackets are a single flat plate or a pair of small clips, each fixed with one or two screws, so the footprint is small compared with a TV mount. A plywood backer, used when the studs fall in the wrong place, adds its own fixings but spreads the load across more of the wall. Cable adds nothing if it runs in a surface channel, or one small entry and exit if it goes inside a hollow wall. Before drilling we mark every hole in pencil and show you, so nothing goes into the plaster that you haven't seen first.

The house next door is being gut-renovated — should we wait before anything is fixed to the shared wall?
+

If their work touches the party wall, it is worth waiting. Demolition, new joist pockets and chases cut into the brick from their side can shake loose fresh anchors or crack plaster on yours. Ask the neighbours or their contractor when the heavy work on that wall finishes. In the meantime, a console setup or a bracket that hangs the bar from the TV mount gives you sound now and isn't affected, and we can move the bar onto the wall once the work next door is done.

Do we need to buy an isolation pad or riser for the subwoofer before you come?
+

No, but it often helps. We place the sub and listen before recommending anything, and in a whole house with a solid floor it may not need one. Where the room sits over a tenant, a neighbour or a sleeping child, a dense isolation pad or platform under the sub is the most effective small purchase you can make. We don't sell them; we'll tell you the size that suits your sub and where it should stand, and a thick rug is a partial substitute in the meantime.

We're staging the house for sale — can the TV and soundbar look finished for open houses without drilling the parlor?
+

Yes. For a staged room we usually set the TV on a low console or a slim floor stand and put the bar on the console or on a bracket attached to the TV, with every cable hidden behind the furniture. Nothing touches the walls, so the parlor photographs as the buyers will inherit it, and the whole setup leaves with your furniture. It is priced as a console placement, $79, plus any streaming or remote setup you want for showings.

Are you insured if something goes wrong near an original feature?
+

Yes. AESS is licensed and insured, and we issue a certificate of insurance free to any co-op, condo or managing agent that asks. More useful than insurance, though, is not needing it: before drilling near a mantel, mirror, cornice or marble surround we point out the risk on the wall, explain what we'll do to protect it, and wait for your yes. If we judge the risk too high, we'll say so and suggest another position. The service agreement sets out the full terms.
