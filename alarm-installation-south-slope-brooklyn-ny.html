The Neighborhood

Why South Slope Properties Need Alarm Installation

South Slope has no official border, and the sources we read do not agree on one. Wikipedia treats it as the southern of Park Slope's three sections, after North Slope and Center Slope, inside a neighborhood bounded by Fourth Avenue, Prospect Park West, Flatbush Avenue and the Prospect Expressway. A neighborhood-history site that cites New York Times real estate listings gives 9th Street to the north, the Prospect Expressway to the south, Fourth Avenue to the west and Prospect Park West and Eighth Avenue to the east. The Department of City Planning's 2005 rezoning used yet another box under the name South Park Slope: 15th Street to 24th Street, Fourth Avenue to Prospect Park West, with Green-Wood Cemetery along the edge. A 2008 Brownstoner thread captured the argument in one line from a commenter who drew South Slope as 9th Street to 16th Street. For this page we work with the blocks from 10th Street to 19th Street between Fourth Avenue and Prospect Park West, which every one of those definitions includes in part, and we leave the blocks of the 20s to our Greenwood Heights page.

The building stock changes every few hundred feet. Brownstoner's 2006 House of the Day on a four-story frame two-family on 11th Street drew a commenter who pointed out that the blocks from 11th to about 15th Street below Fifth Avenue hold many more wood-frame houses than Park Slope proper. Its 2015 Building of the Day on 199 17th Street, between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, describes a simplified Italianate brick house of two stories and a basement on a 25-foot lot, standing by 1880, with an extension that makes it 53 feet deep, and says that by 1888 the block had filled in mostly with masonry row houses and a few remaining frame houses. Along Fifth and Seventh Avenues the buildings are the familiar three- and four-story walk-ups with a store at street level. On Fourth Avenue, the City Planning summary describes the edge of the 2005 rezoning as large lots with one-story warehouse and commercial buildings, rezoned to allow taller apartment buildings with affordable-housing incentives; many of those lots have since become newer apartment buildings.

Then there are the records. We read the NYPD's public complaint files and pulled every burglary whose published coordinates fall inside our 10th-to-19th-Street window. Between January 2021 and December 2025 there were 168. Fifty-seven were in apartment houses and thirteen in one- or two-family houses. Thirty-seven were in restaurants, bars and a fast-food counter, twelve in commercial buildings, nine at construction sites, seven in groceries and bodegas, and the rest in salons, small stores, parked trucks and a scattering of other premises. Only five of the 168 were recorded as attempts; every other one was logged as completed. In the same window, the city's 311 file holds 213 complaints about alarms making noise between January 2021 and late October 2026, and they arrive at very different hours from the burglaries.

That mismatch is the reason this page exists. An alarm in South Slope has two jobs that pull against each other. It has to report the real thing fast, because the records say break-ins here are usually finished, not abandoned halfway. And it must stay quiet when nothing is wrong, because a block of attached houses and walk-ups hears every siren, and the city's own records show neighbors calling about alarms most often in the middle of a weekday morning, the hours when apartment burglaries in our window were rarest. Every recommendation below is built to satisfy both records at once.

South Slope facts that shape an alarm design

  • Two precincts in one neighborhood. The NYPD complaint records in our window carry two precinct numbers. Burglaries north of roughly 15th Street are coded to the 78th Precinct, whose station house Wikipedia places at 65 Sixth Avenue, and those south of it to the 72nd, which Hoodline places at 830 Fourth Avenue. Over 2021 to 2025, 93 were coded 78th and 75 were coded 72nd.
  • Two community boards as well. A 2015 Baruch College neighborhood backgrounder describes South Park Slope, between 15th and 24th Streets, as part of District 7 with Windsor Terrace, Green-Wood Cemetery and Sunset Park; the blocks north of 15th Street sit with the rest of Park Slope.
  • Frame houses below Fifth Avenue. Brownstoner's commenters placed many wood-frame houses on the blocks from 11th to about 15th Street west of Fifth Avenue, and its 2006 listing described one as an owner's triplex over a garden-level floor-through.
  • A rezoning that changed Fourth Avenue. The City Planning Commission approved the South Park Slope rezoning in 2005, protecting the low-rise midblocks and allowing larger apartment buildings on Fourth Avenue, according to the agency's summary.
  • A clock factory that became apartments. The Brooklyn Public Library's history of the Ansonia Clock Company says its Brooklyn plant filled the block from Seventh Avenue and 12th Street to Eighth Avenue and 13th Street, was said to be the largest clock factory in the world, and has been turned into a 71-unit co-op.
  • A park gateway at 15th Street. Bartel-Pritchard Square, where Prospect Park West, Prospect Park Southwest and 15th Street meet, was named in 1923 for two local soldiers killed in the First World War and sits beside the 15th Street–Prospect Park station on the F and G lines.

The First Record

What Five Years of NYPD Burglary Records Say About South Slope

The NYPD publishes every complaint it records, with a premises type, a start time, a precinct and approximate coordinates, on the city's open data portal. On October 22, 2026, we queried two of those files, the historic file and the current year-to-date file, for every complaint coded as burglary whose coordinates fall inside a four-sided outline we drew around the blocks from 10th Street to 19th Street between Fourth Avenue and Prospect Park West, or Eighth Avenue south of 15th Street. The outline is ours, the coordinates in the file are deliberately approximate, and a complaint is a report, not a conviction, so treat the counts as a picture of patterns rather than a precise census. With those limits, here is what the file shows.

Who was burglarized

Between 2021 and 2025 the window holds 168 burglary complaints. By the NYPD's own premises codes, 57 were in apartment houses, 13 in houses, 31 in restaurants or diners, 5 in bars, 1 at a fast-food counter, 12 in commercial buildings, 9 at construction sites, 7 in groceries or bodegas, 5 in beauty or nail salons, 5 in small merchants, 3 in chain stores and 8 were burglaries of trucks parked on the street. The remaining handful were a bank, a check-cashing business, a dry cleaner, a shelter, a school, a supermarket, two private garages, two stores the file leaves unclassified, a storage room and one with no premises code at all.

Read the other way, homes and food-and-drink businesses account for 107 of the 168, roughly two in three. That split matches the street pattern anyone who walks here knows: side streets lined with walk-ups and row houses, and avenues lined with places that serve food and drink late. It also means an alarm company that only thinks about houses misses the businesses whose break-ins fill a fifth of the file, and one that only thinks about stores misses the apartments that fill a third of it.

When the burglaries began

Each complaint carries a "from" time, which is the earliest moment the burglary could have started. For a store that closed at 1 a.m. and was found broken into at 7, the from time is usually the closing time or an estimate made from video; for an apartment whose tenant was at work all day, it may simply be the time the tenant left. We grouped those start times into four blocks of six hours. The pattern differs sharply by premises.

For the 57 apartment burglaries, 22 began between midnight and 6 a.m., 22 between noon and 6 p.m., 11 between 6 p.m. and midnight, and only 2 between 6 a.m. and noon. For the 13 houses the numbers are small, but they lean toward the afternoon and evening: 1, 2, 6 and 4 across the same four blocks. For restaurants, bars and the fast-food counter together, 24 of 37 began between midnight and 6 a.m., and 9 more between 6 p.m. and midnight. Groceries and bodegas were 6 of 7 after midnight. Construction sites were the exception, with 7 of 9 starting between noon and 6 p.m., which fits sites that are open during the day and full of tools by afternoon.

Two cautions before anyone builds a schedule on this. First, small numbers swing easily; thirteen house burglaries over five years is not enough to predict next Tuesday. Second, a from time of noon for an apartment does not mean the burglar arrived at noon, only that the window opened then. Even so, the near absence of apartment burglaries starting between 6 a.m. and noon, 2 of 57, is strong enough to be worth designing around, and so is the dominance of after-midnight starts for anything that sells food or drink.

Completed, not attempted

The file marks each complaint as attempted or completed. Of the 168, 163 are marked completed and 5 attempted. Three of the five attempts were at commercial premises "in front of" the building rather than inside, which is how a pried gate or a smashed glass door that held would usually be recorded. Among the 74 complaints the NYPD classified as residential burglaries, 71 are recorded as having happened inside.

For alarm design, that ratio is the most important number on this page. A burglary that is almost always completed is a burglary in which nothing interrupted the person once they were through the door or window. An alarm cannot make a lock stronger, but it can make the first opening noisy and reported. That is why our South Slope designs start at the perimeter: the apartment's entry door and every window reachable from a fire escape, rear yard, extension roof or shop awning, and in a business the back door, the cellar door and the glass. Interior motion detectors are the second layer, not the first.

The line at 15th Street

Every complaint carries the number of the precinct that took it. In our window, both the 78th and the 72nd appear, and the split follows the street grid: complaints north of roughly 15th Street carry the 78th Precinct's number, and complaints south of it the 72nd's. Over five years the 78th's side recorded 93 burglaries and the 72nd's side 75. That matters less for how an alarm is built than for how its monitoring account is written, because the central station dispatches by address, and an address entered with the wrong cross street can send a call to the wrong place. We enter every South Slope account with the exact house number, the two cross streets and the floor, and we ask owners to look up their own precinct with the NYPD's online precinct finder rather than relying on a neighborhood name.

2026 so far

The current year-to-date file, whose latest burglary in our window was dated June 25, 2026, when we read it, adds 15 more: 4 in restaurants or diners, 1 in a bar, 4 in apartment houses, 2 in commercial buildings, and one each in a grocery, a drug store, a daycare and an outdoor mailbox. The same mix, at roughly the same pace, as the five years before it.

Year by year, and month by month

The yearly totals fell from 57 in 2021 to 21 in 2025, with apartment burglaries dropping the most, from 22 to 4. Restaurant and bar burglaries did not fall in a straight line: 14, 7, 2, 9 and 5. By month, December stands out with 25 of the 168, more than any other month, and November and February are the quietest at 9 each. By the weekday of the start date, Tuesday leads with 36 and Sunday is lowest with 17. None of this changes how an alarm is built, but it does suggest when an owner who has been meaning to install one should stop waiting: before the holidays rather than after.

The Second Record

What the 311 Alarm-Noise Complaints Say About the Same Blocks

The city's 311 file records every service request, and one of its categories is a noise complaint about an alarm, which the Department of Environmental Protection handles under the Noise Code. We ran the same outline against that file. From January 1, 2021 through October 21, 2026, it holds 213 alarm-noise complaints inside our window: 29 in 2021, 47 in 2022, 29 in 2023, 24 in 2024, 31 in 2025 and 53 in 2026 through late October. The file does not say whether the alarm was a building alarm or a car alarm parked at the curb, so some of these are not about anyone's house at all. Even so, the timing tells a clear story.

The hours neighbors call

Grouped by the hour the complaint was opened, the busiest single hour was 9 p.m., with 21 complaints, but the busiest stretch of the day was the morning: 46 complaints were opened between 9 a.m. and noon, and 63 between 7 a.m. and noon. Overnight, from midnight to 6 a.m., there were only 19. Weekday complaints far outnumber weekend ones, 171 to 42, with Tuesday the busiest day at 44 and Sunday the quietest at 18.

Put this next to the burglary file and the mismatch is hard to miss. The hours when neighbors most often reached for the phone about a ringing alarm, mid-morning on a weekday, are the hours when the burglary file shows almost no apartment break-ins beginning. Our field experience offers the obvious explanation, though the data cannot prove it: those are the hours right after a household leaves for work and school, when a system armed in a hurry trips on a door that did not latch, a cat, a radiator or a window left open an inch, and rings to an empty apartment while the neighbors listen through the wall.

The same pattern holds year after year. In the grid below, each row is one year and each column one hour of the day; the morning cluster appears in every year from 2021 to 2026, and 2026's total is already the highest of the six. By month, the 2021 to 2025 complaints are heaviest in January and February, in the middle of the heating season, and lightest in May and October.

What happened after the call

The 311 file also records how each complaint was closed. For 80 of the 213, the closing note says the Department of Environmental Protection did not observe a violation at the time of inspection and could not issue a summons. For 111, the department researched the complaint and closed it; 13 were closed as duplicates of a complaint already open, and the rest carry other notes. In plain terms, many alarms had stopped by the time anyone arrived, which is what New York City's Administrative Code § 24-238 intends: it requires building burglar alarms to shut off their audible signal automatically after fifteen minutes. None of that helps the neighbor who listened to the first fifteen.

So the second job of a South Slope alarm is not to appear in this file. That is a design task, not a matter of luck, and it shapes everything from which door is the entry door to how long the siren runs and who gets the first text. The section after the architecture cards below walks through the ten minutes after a household leaves, which is where most of the avoidable noise starts.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for South Slope's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up most of what we survey between 10th and 19th Streets. They differ in where the reachable openings are, how sound travels between neighbors, and what can be fixed to the walls.

Wood-frame row houses

Concentrated, by Brownstoner commenters' account, on the blocks from 11th to about 15th Street below Fifth Avenue. Often two families, an owner over a garden-floor rental, with thin party walls that carry a siren next door.

Brick Italianate rows

Two or three stories over a basement, like the 1880-era house at 199 17th Street, frequently with a long rear extension whose flat roof sits under the upstairs back windows.

Walk-ups over avenue stores

Three or four floors over a shop, bar or restaurant on Fifth or Seventh Avenue, with a street door shared by every tenant and a fire escape on the back or front.

Newer buildings on Fourth Avenue

Apartment buildings on lots the 2005 rezoning opened to larger construction: concrete or masonry floors, a lobby with a package area, and ground-floor commercial space.

Converted industrial buildings

The former Ansonia clock factory between Seventh and Eighth Avenues at 12th and 13th Streets, now a 71-unit co-op, is the largest example: big window openings, thick walls and a board that approves alterations.

Small storefront and mixed buildings

One- and two-story commercial buildings on the avenues, some with an apartment above and a cellar reached from a sidewalk hatch or a back stair.

Frame houses: wireless first, siren placement second

A wood-frame house is easy to fish wire through in places and impossible in others, depending on what a century of renovations left inside the walls. We survey with a wireless design as the default and run wire only where it is clearly simpler, typically from the panel to a keypad on the same wall. The more important decision is where the siren goes. Frame party walls carry sound well, so an interior siren mounted on the shared wall will be nearly as loud in the neighbor's bedroom as in yours. We mount it on an interior partition, near the top of the stairs, and set its run time short enough that a false trip ends quickly; the monitoring station, not the siren, is what summons help.

Brick rows with a deep rear extension

The 53-foot depth Brownstoner measured at 199 17th Street is typical of the rows here: a narrow front that hides a long house. The back of such a house is where the reachable openings are, because the extension's roof sits a few feet under the second-floor rear windows and the yard is usually fenced on three sides. We put contacts on those windows as well as the ground-floor ones, add a motion detector in the extension itself, and set the back door to report immediately rather than giving it the entry countdown the front door gets, since nobody in the household comes home through the yard at night.

Walk-up apartments over avenue stores

In a walk-up the alarm belongs to the tenant or the owner of one unit, and it stops at the apartment door. The building's street door, hallway and roof door belong to the landlord. For an apartment alarm we cover the entry door, every window on a fire escape, any window that faces a rear yard or the roof of the store below, and one motion detector placed to see anyone crossing from those windows toward the bedrooms. Nothing is fixed in the common hall. Adhesive-mounted sensors come off when you move, and the panel and siren go inside the apartment, away from the wall you share with the next unit.

Newer Fourth Avenue buildings

The newer buildings bring the opposite problems: concrete floors and walls that can weaken wireless signals, a lobby and package area run by management, and smart locks or video intercoms installed by the developer. We test signal strength from each sensor location to the panel before mounting anything, place the panel centrally rather than by the door when a concrete wall sits between the bedrooms and the entrance, and add a repeater if the test calls for one. The apartment alarm does not connect to the building's intercom or fire alarm system, and we say so in writing for the managing agent.

Converted industrial buildings and co-ops

Former factories like the Ansonia building have window openings far wider than a row house's, often with original or replacement steel or aluminum frames. Large windows usually mean glass-break detectors rather than a contact on every sash, plus contacts on the sashes that actually open. A co-op board will typically want to see what is being fixed to its walls and windows before work starts; we provide a device list and mounting description with the quote.

Small commercial and mixed buildings

One- and two-story buildings on the avenues often have a cellar reached from a sidewalk hatch or a rear stair and a roof that is easy to reach from the building next door. Those are the openings we cover first, along with the back door and the glass, because they are the ones nobody sees from the street after closing.

The Morning Gap

The Ten Minutes After You Leave: Designing Out the Alarms Your Neighbors Report

If the 311 file is any guide, the most common problem with an alarm in South Slope is not that it misses a burglar but that it rings at 10 a.m. in an apartment nobody is in. The burglary file gives no reason to switch the alarm off in the morning; it shows apartments broken into at almost every other hour, and a home left unarmed in the morning is still unarmed at noon. The answer is a departure routine and a set of settings that make a false trip after leaving unlikely and, if it still happens, short and quickly explained. This is our working method, built from the service calls we answer, not a rule set by anyone else.

Why the hour after leaving produces false alarms

Most false trips after departure come from a handful of causes. A door is pulled shut but does not latch, and the wind or the building's air pressure eases it open a few minutes later. A window is left open an inch for the cat or the radiator, and the curtain moves in front of a motion detector. A pet allowed the run of the apartment is heavier than the detector was set for. The heat comes on after everyone is gone, and warm air rises past a detector aimed at a radiator. Someone comes back for a forgotten phone, opens the door, and leaves again without entering a code. Each one is a design problem with a design answer.

The settings we use to prevent them

First, the panel will not arm with a door or window reported open, and it tells you which one by name, so the curtain-and-window trip is caught at the keypad rather than an hour later. Second, the exit delay is set to what your household needs to get from keypad to landing, usually well under a minute in an apartment, and the entry door is the only door that gets a countdown. Third, motion detectors are pet-tolerant where there are pets, aimed along a hallway rather than at a window or radiator, and tested walking the path the cat takes. Fourth, the system sends a push alert the moment it arms and the moment any sensor trips, so you know before your neighbors do.

A siren time chosen for a shared wall

The fifteen-minute limit in Administrative Code § 24-238 is a ceiling, not a recommendation. In a walk-up or a frame row house, a siren that runs for fifteen minutes is fifteen minutes of noise in three or four other homes. With monitoring, the central station is already calling you and, if needed, dispatching; the siren's job is to tell an intruder they have been heard and to startle them out, which happens in the first minutes. We usually set residential sirens here to run for a few minutes and then stop, with the panel staying in alarm and reporting until someone disarms it. Owners can choose a longer time, and we explain the trade.

When it rings anyway

Give the first alert to the person nearest the block on a weekday, not automatically to the account holder. If you work in Manhattan and your partner works from a café on Seventh Avenue, the partner is the first call. Teach everyone with a code how to silence and re-arm from the app, and how to see in the event history which sensor started it. Then tell the neighbor on the other side of the wall what happened. A one-line text after a false alarm does more for the block than any siren setting.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in South Slope

The businesses in the burglary file are concentrated where you would expect: the restaurant and bar blocks of Fifth Avenue, the cafés and shops of Seventh Avenue, the corner stores and delis on both, and the larger commercial lots along Fourth Avenue. Their break-ins happen at the other end of the clock from the apartment break-ins, and almost all of them were completed.

Fifth Avenue

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in South Slope: Questions Answered

Reading the burglary record

Almost none of the apartment burglaries in the NYPD's records for these blocks began between 6 a.m. and noon. Can we leave the alarm off in the mornings?

We would not. Two of fifty-seven is a small number, but an alarm left off in the morning is usually still off at noon, and the afternoon is one of the two busiest windows for apartment burglaries in the same file. The morning number is better used another way: it tells you that a system ringing at 10 a.m. is more likely a departure mistake than a burglar, so the alert that reaches your phone should come with the name of the sensor and an easy way to check before anyone panics. Keep the system armed whenever the apartment is empty, and spend the effort on making the departure routine reliable.

Nearly every burglary in the records was logged as completed, not attempted. Does that mean the doors and windows matter more than motion detectors inside?

For a South Slope apartment or row house, yes. A completed burglary means nothing stopped the person once they were through the first opening. A contact on that opening reports the moment it moves, while the person is still on the fire escape or in the doorway; a motion detector only reports once they are inside and walking. We design from the outside in: every entry door, every window reachable from a fire escape, extension roof, yard or store awning, then one or two motion detectors placed along the path from those openings toward the rooms that matter. Glass-break detectors fill in where a window is too large or too old for a contact.

The records place blocks north of about 15th Street in the 78th Precinct and blocks south of it in the 72nd. Does the monitoring center need to know which one covers our address?

The monitoring center needs your exact address, and its dispatch system works out the rest. What goes wrong in practice is not the precinct but the address itself: a building entered as "Park Slope" with no cross streets, an apartment number missing, or a corner building whose entrance is on the avenue while its mailing address is on the side street. When we open an account we enter the house number, both cross streets, the floor, which door the police should use and whether the street door is locked. Look up your own precinct on the NYPD's precinct finder so you know whom you are talking to if you ever follow up a report.

Reading the noise record

Most 311 alarm complaints on our blocks came in between 9 a.m. and noon on weekdays. What should our routine be in the minutes after the last person leaves, so ours is not one of them?

Arm from the keypad by the door you actually use, after a walk past the windows. If the panel refuses to arm because something is open, it names the opening; close it rather than bypassing it. Pull the door until you hear the latch, then wait on the landing until the app shows "armed." Check the push alert on the way to the train. If someone comes back for something, they disarm with their own code and re-arm on the way out, even for thirty seconds. And the pets stay on the side of any door the motion detector does not watch, unless the detector has been tested with them.

When the city's inspectors arrived on many of those complaints, the alarm had already stopped. Should we set our siren to run for less than the city's fifteen-minute limit?

In a walk-up or an attached house, we think so. The siren does its real work in the first minute or two, telling an intruder they have been detected and alerting anyone home. After that, the monitoring station and your phone carry the alarm. A siren that runs for fifteen minutes adds little but noise for the people on the other side of the wall. We usually set a few minutes for apartments and row houses, and the panel stays in alarm and keeps reporting until someone disarms it, so a shorter siren does not mean a shorter alarm. Businesses on the avenues sometimes want longer, which the code allows up to fifteen minutes.

Apartments, houses and renovations

Our apartment is above a Fifth Avenue restaurant, and most restaurant burglaries in the records began after midnight. Can a break-in downstairs reach our floor, and what should our own alarm watch?

Usually the restaurant's burglar is after the restaurant's register and stock, not the apartments, and the restaurant's own alarm is its business. Your concern is the shared route. If the restaurant's rear door, roof hatch or cellar opens into a yard or roof that also reaches your fire escape or rear windows, those windows are now reachable at the hours the file shows burglars on the avenue. Put contacts on them, keep your apartment armed at night in a mode that watches the windows while you are home, and ask your landlord whether the restaurant's roof access is locked. The alarm stays inside your apartment; nothing is installed in the restaurant's space.

Our building on Fourth Avenue is one of the newer concrete ones. Will wireless sensors in the bedrooms reach a panel by the front door through concrete walls and floors?

Often, but we test rather than assume. Before mounting anything, the technician holds each sensor where it will go and reads the signal strength at the panel. Concrete and metal studs can cut it sharply, especially across a bathroom or a kitchen wall full of pipes. If a reading is weak, we move the panel to a more central spot, add a small repeater, or choose sensors on a frequency that carries better. The panel itself reports to the monitoring station over cellular, so we also check that signal at the panel's final position.

We are living on the top floor while the two floors below are gutted. Can the lower floors be armed every evening after the crew leaves without arming us in?

Yes, with a panel that supports areas. The lower floors become one area with contacts on the doors and windows that are finished and motion detectors on each floor, and your floor becomes another. When the crew leaves, the foreman or you arm the lower area with a code that works only during working weeks. Your own floor arms separately at night. When the stairs between are the crew's only route up, a contact on the door at the top of the stair, or a motion detector on the landing, tells you if anyone comes up after hours. Once the renovation ends, the lower area is re-planned for the finished rooms.

Businesses on the avenues

Grocery and bodega burglaries in the records almost all started between midnight and 6 a.m. Our deli closes at 1 and opens again at 6. Is it worth arming for five hours?

Those five hours are exactly the hours in the file, so they are the hours that matter. The difficulty is not the value of arming but the habit of it: whoever pulls the gate down at 1 a.m. is tired and wants to go home. Make arming one action, a fob or a code at the back door, and set the panel to text the owner if it has not been armed by 1:30. The morning opener disarms with a personal code, so you can see who opened and when. A glass-break detector behind the front window and a contact on the back or cellar door cover the openings the file's burglaries most often used.

"Most burglaries happen during the day, while people are at work."

It is a reasonable national rule of thumb, but in our window the NYPD file says something more specific. Apartment burglaries split between the small hours and the afternoon, with almost none starting in the morning, and businesses were hit overwhelmingly after midnight.

"Alarm sirens deter burglars, so louder and longer is better."

The siren's deterrent effect, if any, comes in the first moments. After that, in a row of attached houses or a walk-up, a long siren mostly reaches the neighbors, and the city's 311 file shows how often they notice.

"A camera is enough; you don't need an alarm in an apartment."

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start the Survey at the Fire Escape

This is how we run a South Slope survey; it describes our method, not the story of one job. The technician does not start at the front door. He asks to see the windows first, beginning with any that open onto a fire escape, a store's roof, a rear extension or a yard, because those are the openings a completed burglary most often starts from and the ones a tenant forgets. He opens each one, checks how it latches, and notes whether an air conditioner or a window guard sits in it.

Then he asks who leaves last in the morning, and asks that person to walk out the way they really do, keys in hand. He times it. That number, not a factory default, becomes the exit delay. He looks at what is near every place a motion detector could go: a radiator, a vent, a sunny floor, the cat's route to the window. In a concrete building he holds each sensor where it will be mounted and reads the signal at the panel before drilling anything.

Finally he asks which neighbor shares the wall nearest the panel, and the siren goes on the opposite side. In our experience those three questions, about the windows, the morning and the wall, decide whether a system ends up in the burglary file's quiet hours or in the 311 file's busy ones.

South Slope Alarm Installation FAQ

What does an alarm for a South Slope walk-up apartment over a storefront cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

Most walk-up apartments start from the basic wireless figure of $600 and typically land between $600 and $1,200, depending on how many fire-escape and rear windows are reachable and whether the building's concrete or depth calls for a repeater. Central-station monitoring is optional from $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and New York City sales tax is added on the written quote. If your apartment includes a garden floor with a yard door, expect the price to move toward the upper end of that range.

Can the time we have to get out after arming be set differently from the time we get to come back in?
+

Yes. The exit delay and the entry delay are separate settings on every panel we install. We time the exit delay with whoever leaves last in the morning, keys and bags in hand, because an exit delay that is too short is a common cause of alarms that ring right after departure. The entry delay is set for the door you use coming home, and other doors, such as a back door or a garden door, can be set to report at once with no countdown at all.

Can a 311 noise complaint about our alarm lead to a summons?
+

It can. The Department of Environmental Protection handles alarm-noise complaints under the city's Noise Code, and the closing notes in the 311 file for our South Slope blocks show inspectors who arrived, found no violation at the time and could not issue a summons, which implies one can be issued when an alarm is still sounding in violation. We are not lawyers and do not quote penalty amounts. The practical answer is to keep your siren short, well inside the fifteen-minute limit, and to prevent the false trips that start most complaints.

Can the alarm keep a record of every false alarm, so we can see whether the same sensor keeps causing them?
+

Yes. The panel's event history records each alarm with the time and the name of the sensor that started it, and the app shows that history for months. After two or three false alarms we read it with you: if the same window or the same motion detector appears each time, the fix is that device's position, its sensitivity or the door it watches, not the whole system. A pattern of trips at the same hour usually points to heat, sunlight or a household routine.

A neighbor's alarm has been ringing behind our building for over an hour. What can we do about it?
+

First check that it is not a real emergency, and call 911 if you see signs of a break-in or fire. Otherwise, a building alarm that keeps sounding can be reported to 311, which routes it to the Department of Environmental Protection under the Noise Code. If you know the neighbor, a phone call or a note is often faster. Many owners do not know their alarm rings while they are out; ours can send them a push alert the moment it starts, which is one of the settings we recommend to every South Slope customer.

Our row house is deep, with a long back extension. Will a siren at the front be heard in the back bedrooms?
+

Often not well, especially through two or three closed doors in a 50-foot house. We place the main siren centrally, usually near the top of the stairs on an interior wall, and add a small second sounder or a voice sounder at the back when the bedrooms are there. That way the household hears the alarm wherever they sleep without turning the main siren up so far that the neighbors on the other side of the party wall hear it as loudly as you do.

If someone pries a sensor off the door frame while the alarm is off, will we find out?
+

Yes, on the sensors we install. Most wireless contacts and motion detectors have a tamper switch that reports when the cover is opened or the device is pulled from its mounting, whether the system is armed or not. The app shows a tamper alert by sensor name, and with monitoring the central station sees it too. Panels also report a sensor that stops checking in, so one that is removed and carried away does not simply go silent.

Can the alarm phone us with a spoken message instead of just a push notification we might swipe away?
+

Yes. Depending on the platform, the app can send push notifications, text messages and emails, and the monitoring station calls the people on your call list by phone, in order, when an alarm comes in. Without monitoring, some platforms can still place an automated call. We set which events go where with you: a real alarm can ring your phone, while a low-battery notice or a door left open stays a quiet message.

Should the alarm be in Stay mode or Away mode while a babysitter is at home with the children in the evening?
+

Stay mode, or a night setting, so the doors and the reachable windows are armed while people can move freely inside. Give the babysitter a personal code that works only during the hours she is booked, show her how to disarm if she needs to step out with the children, and add her to the call list for those hours if the monitoring station allows it. The history will show when she armed and disarmed, which many parents find reassuring.

Where the South Slope Facts and Figures Come From

Every South Slope fact and number on this page traces to an item below, all read on October 22, 2026. Anything not listed is ours: prices come from our own Brooklyn list, and the way we survey, set departure routines and siren times, and hand over a system is our working method, not a rule set by anyone else. Sources we tried and could not use are named at the end; nothing on the page depends on them.

  • NYC Open Data, NYPD Complaint Data Historic (dataset qgea-i56i) — every complaint coded as burglary from January 2021 through December 2025 whose published coordinates fall inside our outline of 10th to 19th Street, Fourth Avenue to Prospect Park West/Eighth Avenue: counts by year, premises type, start time, attempted or completed, location of occurrence and precinct. The outline was drawn by us from 311 address coordinates; NYPD coordinates are approximate by design.
  • NYC Open Data, NYPD Complaint Data Current (Year To Date) (dataset 5uac-w243) — the same query for 2026; the latest burglary in our outline was dated June 25, 2026, when read.

Your Alarm Is Ringing and Nobody Is Home: Call the South Slope Line

If you believe someone is inside, call 911 or let the monitoring center do it, and do not go in alone. If the event history shows a single sensor tripping after you left, it is far more likely to be an unlatched door, a pet or a heat source than a person, and the fix is usually small. Silence it from the app, tell the neighbors, and call (347) 934-8335 with the panel's brand and the sensor's name. A technician will normally arrive with the right part.
