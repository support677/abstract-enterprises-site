The Neighborhood

Why Crown Heights Properties Need Intercom Installation

Crown Heights runs from Atlantic Avenue down to Empire Boulevard and from Washington Avenue east to Ralph Avenue, with Eastern Parkway, the boulevard Olmsted and Vaux laid out in the 1860s, running across the middle. That geography matters less to an intercom than what was built along it. North of the parkway, the Crown Heights North Historic District alone counts 1,019 contributing buildings from about 1853 to 1942, ranging from freestanding houses and row houses to flats and apartment buildings. Along the parkway itself and on streets like Crown Street, Carroll Street, President Street and Lefferts Avenue to the south, the stock turns to large prewar apartment buildings, many of them five and six stories with elevators and lobbies that dozens of households share. And on the side streets between them sit the walk-ups, typically twenty to forty apartments behind one street door. Each of those building types puts a different load on an entry system, and each has a different person deciding what gets installed.

The common thread is age and scale. A large share of Crown Heights housing is old, shared and rent-regulated: one public listing counts 1,179 buildings in the neighborhood with rent-stabilized apartments. In a building like that, the intercom is not one household's purchase. It is building equipment that the owner must keep working, that tenants depend on every day for deliveries, home health aides, children coming home from school and visitors who do not have keys, and that tenants complain about when it fails. A search excerpt from r/AskNYC describes a Brooklyn rent-stabilized building with 120 open HPD violations whose list of building-wide problems begins with a broken intercom and buzzer that had not worked since 2023. We do not present that thread as a Crown Heights statistic. It is a picture of what residents are left with when the panel in the lobby stops working and nobody takes ownership of fixing it.

Crown Heights tenants have also organized around the condition of their buildings more visibly than in many neighborhoods. The Crown Heights Tenant Union, founded in 2013, organizes building-wide tenant associations, and in recent years Brownstoner has covered tenants at 567 St. Johns Place fighting through housing court for repairs, a building where HPD records cited 446 violations and the city ended up funding work including new front steps, and tenants on Union Street suing over elevators that break and a wheelchair lift out of service for more than a year. None of those stories is mainly about the intercom. They do explain why an intercom project in a Crown Heights building often involves more than the owner and the installer. A tenant association may be asking for it, a court-appointed administrator may be paying for it, and rent-stabilized tenants will want to know whether it changes their rent.

Then there is the lobby. News 12 Brooklyn reported in August 2025 that a man with a child in a stroller got someone to open the door at 240 Crown Street, a 135-unit building, checked that the lobby was empty and took two packages from a pile on the floor. Residents told the station that packages are often left piled in the lobby, especially around the holidays. Patch reported in 2022 that tenants at 501 Lefferts Avenue, a 60-unit complex, said the locks between the front vestibule and the rest of the building were defective and that a delivery worker had been beaten and robbed in the vestibule. Our door buzzer and camera pages cover the door hardware and recording side of incidents like these. For an intercom, they point to a simpler lesson: in a large building, a resident upstairs needs to see exactly who is at the door, couriers need a way to deliver that does not leave boxes in an open lobby, and the inner door has to lock behind every release.

Seven Crown Heights facts that shape an intercom before a panel is chosen

  • Rent stabilization is the norm, not the exception. openigloo's public list shows 1,179 Crown Heights buildings with rent-stabilized apartments. In those buildings, how the owner pays for a new intercom can show up on tenants' leases through the state's Major Capital Improvement process.
  • The state has treated new intercoms as capital improvements. Landlord v. Tenant summarizes DHCR rulings that upheld MCI rent increases for an intercom and door release system and for a video intercom, and one ruling found that tenant consent was not required to convert a buzzer system to an intercom.
  • The 2019 rent law limits those increases. Under the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act, MCI collection is capped at 2 percent a year, increases are temporary for 30 years, and no MCI increase is granted where fewer than 35 percent of a building's apartments are rent-regulated.
  • Keyless entry carries legal duties. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use fobs, apps, cards or biometrics for entry, and limits what they collect, how long they keep it and what they may do with it.
  • The historic districts are large. The Crown Heights North district has 1,019 contributing buildings designated in three phases between 2007 and 2015, and the Park Place district was designated in 2012. Exterior panels on those houses need care with the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • New buildings are arriving fast. New York YIMBY counted six completed buildings adding 230 units in June 2026, including 76 rentals at 827 Sterling Place and 58 at 645 Park Place, and the Atlantic Avenue Mixed-Use Plan approved in May 2025 allows about 4,600 homes on a 21-block stretch in Crown Heights and Bedford-Stuyvesant.
  • Even small new buildings advertise video. CityRealty lists 681 Franklin Avenue, a four-story, eight-unit rental built in 2016, with a video intercom, a sign that renters expect to see who is at the door whatever the building's size.

Repair Calls in Crown Heights

Need Repair in Crown Heights? A Lobby Panel That Rings Some Lines, Handsets Nobody Stocks, or a System Left Behind by a Previous Owner

Repair calls from Crown Heights buildings tend to follow the shape of the buildings. In a large elevator building, the most common pattern is a panel that still calls some apartments and not others, usually along a line: every C apartment on the upper floors, or everything above the fourth floor. That points to the riser, the bundle of cable that climbs from the cellar to each floor, and to the junction boxes where it branches. A pair can break inside a wall, a splice can corrode in a damp cellar, or a renovation in one apartment can cut a line that also feeds the apartments above it. The fix starts in the cellar with a tone generator and a tester, working up floor by floor until the last apartment that answers and the first one that does not are found. In a walk-up, the same symptom more often comes from a single splice in the stair hall or a handset whose wiring was disturbed when a kitchen was redone.

The second pattern is the system that dies all at once. When every apartment goes silent together, the first suspects are the power supply and the amplifier in the cellar or lobby ceiling, and the circuit that feeds them. In older buildings we often find that supply on a shared circuit, behind a locked door only the super can open, or on a shelf where water from a pipe above has dripped on it for years. The third pattern is the inherited system: a newer panel, often networked, installed by a contractor or developer who is no longer around, with nobody in the building holding the administrator login. Those calls are about recovering control as much as fixing hardware, and we say so in the quote. Brick Underground's intercom guide quotes Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith saying, "It's all in the wire connections," and in our experience that holds for most Crown Heights repair visits: the in-wall cable is often fine, and the fault is at a connection, a power supply or a station.

A repair visit is also where we find out whether repair still makes sense. If the handsets upstairs are a model nobody makes anymore, the panel is cracked and a third of the riser tests bad, chasing one dead line may only delay a replacement by a season. If the riser tests clean and the fault is one burned supply or one broken splice, a repair is the right call and we say so. Service calls are billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and the technician explains what was found before recommending anything larger. For a building where every apartment needs to be checked, the visit has to be arranged so the technician can reach each affected apartment, because in a shared system one apartment's wiring can affect the next.

Prewar elevator buildings: one riser, a hundred households and a decision that takes a meeting

The big apartment houses along Eastern Parkway and the streets south of it are the buildings where an intercom matters most and where it is hardest to change. One lobby door serves every household. One riser, often a multi-pair cable in a chase beside the elevator or in a stack of closets, carries every apartment's line from the cellar to the top floor, branching at a junction box on each landing. Over ninety years that riser has usually been through a lot: original cable, a replacement section from the 1970s, a splice from the last time a pipe burst, and a run somebody added when an apartment was split. The panel in the lobby may be the third or fourth one the building has had, mounted over the holes of the ones before it. The first part of our survey in a building like this is mapping that history, because it decides whether the new system can use the old riser or needs a new one, and that one fact moves the price more than any brand choice.

The second part is people. A hundred-apartment building has residents who answer the door with a handset, residents who would rather answer on a phone, elderly residents who want a large button and a loud ring, and home aides and family members who come and go daily. A lobby panel for that building needs a directory that a visitor can search quickly, a way to reach the super or the management office, and a plan for couriers. The decision itself is usually made by an owner and a managing agent, and in many Crown Heights buildings tenants will want a say, especially if the owner intends to seek an MCI increase. We prepare a quote that can be read by all of them: what is being replaced, what is being kept, what each apartment gets, and what the building will own when we leave.

Elevator buildings also bring the most cable route choices. Where the old riser chase is open, a new multi-pair cable or a network cable can often be pulled through it with the old one as a pull line. Where the chase is packed or crushed, the route may move to the elevator shaft's side of a wall, to a stair, or in the worst case to a painted raceway in a public hallway. Each of those has a different cost and a different look, and we price the main options so the owner can choose.

Side-street walk-ups: test the old pairs, then decide

The walk-ups on Crown Heights' side streets are the bread and butter of intercom work here. They are typically brick, five or six stories, with twenty to forty apartments, a vestibule with mailboxes, a cellar with the intercom transformer screwed to a wall, and a riser that runs up the stair hall or behind it. Most still have a wired audio system with a handset by each apartment's door. Many of those handsets have stopped working one by one, and owners eventually face the question of whether to keep patching or replace. In a walk-up, the most useful thing we can do before quoting is test the existing pairs to every apartment. If most of them pass, a two-wire video system can usually run picture, voice and door release over the old cable, and the walls stay closed. If some floors fail, we look for the conduit or chase the old cable used, because pulling new cable through an existing path is far cheaper than making a new one.

Walk-ups are also where the vestibule matters most. A typical layout has an outer street door, a small vestibule with the panel and the mailboxes, and an inner door into the stair hall. Which of those doors the intercom releases, which one locks and which one the mail carrier and the couriers can reach are decisions the owner should make on purpose, and we draw the layout on the quote. The 501 Lefferts Avenue complaints Patch reported, about locks between the vestibule and the rest of the building, are a reminder that the inner door is the one that keeps strangers out of the halls. A new panel is wasted if that door does not latch.

Single-family row houses: one door, a parlor floor and a historic district

A limestone or brownstone row house that is still one household needs something much smaller: a slim panel at the main door, often the parlor entrance at the top of the stoop, and a station or two inside, with an app for family members who want to answer from anywhere. Many families also want the door under the stoop to ring, because it is the door deliveries actually use. In the historic districts, the panel's location is the main design decision. The LPC's guidance on security fixtures favors locations that keep equipment off or less visible on the primary facade where an alternative exists, which in practice often means a door reveal, a vestibule or the side of the stoop rather than the face of the stone. Cable reaches the panel from inside the house, through the door frame or a small, sealed opening in a mortar joint, never down the front of the building. Our door buzzer page for the neighborhood covers the Landmarks process in detail; the intercom question we add here is how many stations the family needs and on which floors, because a four-story house with the kitchen on the garden floor and bedrooms above can miss a single station entirely.

Houses split into flats: one stoop, several households and wiring added one tenant at a time

Brownstoner's history of the Park Place district notes that at least four of its thirteen row houses had been converted to multiple dwellings by the end of the 1950s, and the same thing happened across Crown Heights. A house split into three or four apartments often has a row of mismatched bell buttons beside the door, each added when a unit was rented, each with its own thin wire running to a different floor, some ringing apartments that no longer exist. For these houses we start by mapping what each button really does, then propose a single small panel with one button per current household, a station in each apartment and one release that works the main door. Where the owner lives in the house, we usually give the owner's apartment a station that can also answer the garden-floor or rear door. The intercom law's threshold for a required system is generally eight apartments, so most divided houses are below it, but the self-closing, self-locking door rules still apply, and tenants who depend on a buzzer will expect it to work.

Stores with apartments above: separate systems for separate tenants

Brownstoner's look at Franklin Avenue describes commercial-residential buildings from the late 1880s to the early 1930s, and the same mix lines Nostrand, Kingston and Utica Avenues. In these buildings the residential door sits beside a storefront, sometimes in a narrow recess, and the apartments' intercom should behave as though the store were another building. That means a power supply on a circuit the landlord controls rather than the store, a cable path that avoids the store's ceiling where another route exists, and a panel placed where gates, signs and stacked deliveries will not cover it. When a store changes hands every few years, an intercom that was tapped off the store's electrical or cabled through its drop ceiling tends to be cut during the renovation.

New mid-rises and condos: decide on paper, then build

New York YIMBY's June 2026 roundup named six newly completed Crown Heights buildings: The Eve at 827 Sterling Place with 76 rentals, HELP Park Place at 575 Park Place with 44 apartments, a 32-unit condo at 630 Grand Avenue, 58 rentals at 645 Park Place, also addressed as 711 Franklin Avenue, a ten-unit condo at 755 Washington Avenue and ten rentals at 627 Franklin Avenue. Buildings like these use networked or app-based entry systems almost by default, and they fail in their own ways: a head-end on a shelf beside the boiler, no battery backup, a login held by a contractor who has left, or a subscription nobody remembered to renew. The good work on a new building happens before the walls close, with a home run to every apartment and every controlled door, a ventilated closet for the equipment, and a written handover. Field Notes

Field Notes: Surveying a Crown Heights Apartment House From the Lobby Panel Down to the Riser

These are the habits our technicians follow in a large Crown Heights building. They describe practice, not the story of any one job.

Count the households, not the buttons. An old lobby panel in a building that has combined or split apartments rarely lists the right number. The technician checks the mailbox count and the apartment numbers on each landing against the panel, and asks the super, so the new directory matches the homes that exist now.

Open the junction box on each landing. The riser's branch points tell the history of the system: original cloth-covered pairs, later replacement sections, splices in tape or in connectors. A landing with modern connectors and a landing with century-old splices may sit one floor apart, and that difference shows where trouble will come from.

Test every pair before quoting the reuse. A riser that looks fine can still have pairs broken inside a wall. Each apartment's pair is tested for continuity and quality, and the quote says how many passed. If a board is deciding between reuse and replacement, that number is the evidence.

Find the supply and its breaker. The intercom power supply or transformer is traced to its circuit and photographed. A supply sharing a circuit with a hallway outlet or a cellar light, or sitting under a pipe, is noted, because those are common reasons a whole building goes silent.

Walk the courier's route. Where do deliveries end up today? The lobby floor, the vestibule, a mailroom, a package shelf? The answer decides whether the panel needs a delivery credential, whether an outer door should release separately and where a camera should look.

Ask who signs and who pays. An owner, a managing agent, a co-op or condo board, a court-appointed administrator or a developer may each have the authority, and in a rent-stabilized building the owner may intend to file for an MCI. The answer changes what paperwork the job needs.

Switch the power off and try the doors. With intercom and lock power off, each controlled door is opened by hand from inside and with a key from outside, and the closer is watched to make sure it latches without help. The same test is repeated at handover.

Rent-Stabilized Buildings

Who Pays and Who Decides: A New Intercom in a Rent-Stabilized Crown Heights Building

In most neighborhoods, an intercom quote goes to one person who says yes or no. In Crown Heights, with 1,179 buildings that have rent-stabilized apartments according to openigloo's list, the quote often has a second audience: tenants who want to know whether the new system will raise their rent. That question has a real answer in state law and DHCR decisions, and owners and tenants both do better when they know it before the work begins. Nothing here is legal advice. It is a guide to public sources so owners, managing agents, tenant associations and tenants know what to ask a lawyer or DHCR, and we note where a source leaves a gap.

A new intercom can be a Major Capital Improvement. Landlord v. Tenant summarizes a 2013 DHCR administrative review in which the agency upheld an MCI rent increase for "the installation of an intercom and door release system" at a building's main entrance. The tenant had argued that her buzzer had not worked for a long time and that she wanted it repaired rather than replaced; DHCR's inspection found the new intercom working. Another DHCR ruling, from 2005, upheld an MCI increase for a video intercom system even though tenants said the previous security system had never worked. Neither building was in Crown Heights, but the rules are statewide.

Tenant consent is not what decides it. The same 2013 summary says DHCR found that "tenant consent wasn't required for landlord to change the buzzer system to an intercom system." In other words, an owner can usually replace a buzzer with an intercom without a tenant vote. What tenants can do is ask questions, file comments and complaints through DHCR's process, and make sure the work described in an application matches the work actually done.

The 2019 rent law put limits on the increase. According to a summary by Borah, Goldstein, Altschuler, Nahins & Goidel, the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act, effective June 14, 2019, caps MCI collection at 2 percent a year, amortizes approved costs over 144 months in buildings with 35 or fewer units and 150 months in larger ones, makes MCI increases temporary for 30 years, and requires DHCR to inspect and audit a quarter of MCI applications. Fobs, Apps and Privacy

Fobs, Phone Apps and the Tenant Data Privacy Law: What a Crown Heights Owner Has to Set Up Along With the Hardware

Many intercom projects in Crown Heights now include keyless entry: a fob reader at the street door, an app that opens the lobby from a phone, a code for couriers, and sometimes a camera that recognizes faces. Those features are useful, and they also bring a set of legal duties that the hardware does not handle by itself. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems, which HPD describes as keyless systems such as key fobs, mobile apps, RFID cards or biometrics, to control entry to the building, its common areas or individual apartments.

What can be collected. HPD's summary says owners and the companies that run their systems may collect only a listed minimum: things like the user's name, apartment and the areas they can access, preferred contact method, lease dates, and the time and method of entry for security purposes. Biometric data is allowed only if the system actually uses it. That rules out a long sign-up form that asks for more than the system needs.

Consent and a written policy. Owners must get express consent, in writing or through the app, before collecting reference data, and must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy. When we set up a fob or app system, we build the account list from the owner's resident list, and we remind the owner that consent forms and the policy need to be in place before credentials are issued. The policy itself is the owner's document, not ours.

How long data may be kept. HPD says authentication data must be destroyed no later than 90 days after it is collected or generated, unless it is kept in anonymized form. That affects how an owner sets the system's log retention. Many networked panels keep entry logs much longer by default, so the setting has to be chosen on purpose.

What owners may not do. The law bars owners from selling, leasing or disclosing the data except where the law requires it or the tenant consents, from tracking users outside the building with location services, and from using the data for purposes beyond access and entrance monitoring, including harassment, eviction, or tracking tenants' relationships. It also says an owner may not require tenants to use a smart access system to enter their own apartments. HPD notes a private right of action for unlawful sale of data. For Crown Heights tenants who worry that an entry log could be used against them in a housing dispute, those limits are the relevant ones.

What we do on the technical side. On a fob or app system we keep the administrator account in the building's name, set log retention to the owner's written choice, turn off features the building is not using, give each resident only the doors they need, and leave a mechanical key path at every controlled door. Where a building wants face recognition, we point out that it brings biometric data into the picture and that the owner should take advice before turning it on. None of this replaces the owner's legal obligations, but a system set up carefully makes them much easier to meet.

Lobbies and Deliveries

One Door, Many Households: Directories, Call Routing and Deliveries in a Big Crown Heights Lobby

In a 135-unit building like 240 Crown Street, the lobby panel handles more traffic than most small-business phone lines. Visitors need to find one household among more than a hundred. Couriers arrive all day. Home aides, contractors and family members come and go. And as News 12's report on the stroller theft showed, residents sometimes open the door for a stranger out of courtesy. An intercom cannot stop a resident from holding the door, but it can make the right choice the easy one and keep deliveries from becoming a pile on the floor.

Finding the apartment. Large buildings usually work best with a panel that lets visitors either scroll names or type an apartment number, with the apartment numbers on the panel matching the mailboxes and the doors upstairs. Residents who prefer privacy can be listed by apartment only. The directory should be maintained by the managing agent from a computer or phone, so a move-in does not need a service call.

Seeing who is there. Video at the lobby panel is the single biggest change for residents of an older building. A resident who can see the person at the door and hear them clearly is less likely to release for someone who just says "delivery." The camera has to be at face height for a standing adult and for someone in a wheelchair or pushing a stroller, with light on the entrance after dark.

Routing the calls. Each apartment's call can go to a wall station, to residents' phones, or both, and apartments can mix: an older resident keeps a wall station that works without the internet while a neighbor answers on a phone. A button or code for the super or the management office saves residents from being called for building business.

Handling deliveries. The pile on the lobby floor that residents at 240 Crown Street described is the most common complaint we hear about large buildings. A panel can issue couriers a code or credential that opens only an outer vestibule or a locked package room, not the inner door to the halls, and it can log when that door was used. Where the building has no package room, a locked shelf or cabinet behind the inner door, combined with a delivery code for the vestibule, keeps boxes out of reach of anyone who wanders in. We plan this with the owner on the survey, because it depends on the lobby's layout and on what the building is willing to give up for a package space.

The inner door. Every one of those features depends on a door that closes and locks behind each release. The panel, the strike and the closer are designed together, and we test the door with the power on and off before we leave.

The Atlantic Avenue Rezoning

Thousands of New Apartments on Atlantic Avenue: Specifying a Crown Heights Building's Intercom While It Is Still on the Drawings

For decades the blocks along Atlantic Avenue at Crown Heights' northern edge were zoned for one- and two-story industrial and storage buildings, which did not allow new housing. 6sqft reported that the City Council approved the Atlantic Avenue Mixed-Use Plan on May 28, 2025, allowing about 4,600 new apartments, about 1,900 of them permanently affordable, on a 21-block stretch of Atlantic Avenue and nearby streets in Crown Heights and Bedford-Stuyvesant, along with ground-floor commercial space. Council Member Crystal Hudson led the effort, and Community Boards 3 and 8 approved it. The buildings that follow will choose their entry systems on paper, long before the first tenants arrive, and that is where an intercom can be done well for less effort than it will ever take again.

Required, not optional. ButterflyMX's summary of the law notes that New York's intercom requirement generally applies to Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968, and that a compliant system must let residents speak with visitors at the main entrance and release the door remotely. Almost every new building on the rezoned blocks will be in that group, so the question for a developer is what kind of system, not whether to have one.

Separate residential and commercial entries. The plan aims for active ground floors with commercial space, which means most of these buildings will have a store or other business at street level and apartments above. The residential lobby should have its own panel, power and network, with the commercial tenants given their own door stations, so a future tenant fit-out never disturbs the apartments' system. A condo like the ten-unit building at 755 Washington Avenue, whose retail space YIMBY reported had not yet been leased, is a good example of a building that should run a cable to the future store's door now, while the walls are open, even if nobody knows yet who will rent it.

Where the equipment lives. A networked intercom needs a locked, ventilated room or closet with its own circuit for the switch, power supply, battery backup and patch panel. Small buildings often have no telecom room on the drawings at all. Settling the location early costs a few square feet; finding one after the walls are finished costs much more.

Cable to every apartment and door. One home run per apartment station, plus runs to the lobby panel, every controlled door, the package room, any rear or garage door and the future commercial doors, pulled while the framing is open and labeled at both ends. A spare run on each floor costs little now and makes later changes cheap.

Internet and outages. MetroCom's installer guide points out that app-based systems need reliable building broadband. New buildings should give the intercom its own internet connection or at least its own protected network, plus battery backup for the panel and the lock, and a fail-secure lock that stays locked when power drops but always lets people out by hand.

Affordable and market-rate units together. With about 1,900 permanently affordable apartments expected, many buildings will mix lottery and market-rate units. The entry system should treat every apartment the same: the same panel listing, the same station or app, and the same access to the package room.

Who owns it at the end. The administrator account should be in the building's name, not the installer's or the developer's, and a written record of every login, cable and device should be handed over when the building is sold, refinanced or turned over to a condo board.

Compliance notes for Crown Heights shops, offices and mixed-use buildings

  • Free exit at every controlled door. Any door with an electric strike or lock opens from inside without a key, code or button.
  • Residential doors relock on their own. Where apartments share a building with a business, the residential door closes and locks itself after every release, and business hours never leave it propped.
  • Reachable controls. Under 1 RCNY 42-01, push buttons stay no more than six feet above the floor and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet; businesses open to the public also follow accessibility reach rules, which usually put buttons lower.
  • Separate power and cable for separate occupants. The apartments' intercom gets its own supply and route, independent of a store's tenancy and renovations.
  • Life-safety devices stay clear. Stations and cable never block an exit sign, a fire alarm device, a sprinkler head or a door's swing, and low-voltage cable stays out of power wiring paths.
  • Video and privacy. A commercial video door station that records visitors should be set to keep footage only as long as the business needs it, and in a building with fobs or an app, the residential side falls under the Tenant Data Privacy Law.
  • Landlord approval in leased space. A tenant business gets the landlord's written approval before any new opening in a facade or any mounting on the building's exterior, and in the historic districts the owner checks with the LPC first.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Crown Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Crown Heights owners, managing agents, boards, tenant associations and residents are most likely to face: large and rent-stabilized buildings, keyless entry and data, row houses and divided houses, new buildings and the avenues. Each is answered here once and is not repeated in the FAQ below.

Large and rent-stabilized buildings

A contractor told our managing agent that the riser in our 1920s building has to be replaced along with every handset. What test would show whether that is really true?

A pair-by-pair test from the cellar to every apartment. The technician identifies each apartment's pair at the riser's starting point in the cellar, then checks it for continuity, shorts and quality at the apartment end, floor by floor. The result is a simple count: how many apartments have a usable pair and how many do not. If most pass, a two-wire video system can usually reuse them, and only the failed lines need new cable. If many fail, or the riser has been spliced so often that it is unreliable, replacement is the honest answer. Brick Underground's intercom guide says the core wiring inside the walls is usually fine even in older systems, and in our experience that is often true, but only a test settles it for a particular building. We put the test results in writing so the agent and the owner can compare quotes on the same facts.

Our owner plans to file with DHCR for the new intercom. What documents should the installer hand over so the application is complete?

An itemized contract and final invoice that separate equipment and labor, the model numbers of the panel and the apartment stations, the number of apartments served, a short description of what was removed and what was installed, and dated photos of the finished lobby panel, a sample of apartment stations and the cellar equipment. Proof of payment comes from the owner's own records. The 2019 rent law requires DHCR to inspect and audit a quarter of MCI applications, so the installed system should match the paperwork exactly, down to every apartment having a working station. We provide our documents; the owner and the owner's counsel prepare and file the application, and only DHCR decides whether it is approved.

Our building is run by a court-appointed administrator while repairs are made. Who can approve and pay for a new intercom?

Usually the administrator. When a housing court appoints a 7A administrator, as Brownstoner reported happened at 567 St. Johns Place, that person collects the rents and uses them for repairs, so the administrator is the one who signs contracts and pays invoices for building work. The owner of record may have no say in day-to-day repairs during that period. In some buildings the repair plan has to fit a scope already reviewed by HPD or the court, so the intercom may need to be listed in that plan. When you contact us, tell us who the administrator is, and we will send the quote to that office and match its format to what the administrator needs for the court or HPD.

Our tenant association wants to give the landlord a written list of what a new intercom should include. What belongs on that list?

Keep it to what residents actually need, written in plain terms. A useful list includes: video at the lobby panel so residents can see visitors; a working station or phone option in every apartment, including a choice that works without home internet for residents who do not use smartphones; an inner door that closes and locks after every release; a way for couriers to deliver without leaving packages in the open lobby; a panel that a wheelchair user can reach; and a clear answer on who to call when a station fails. If the owner plans to use fobs or an app, add the Tenant Data Privacy Law's requirements: written consent, a privacy policy and a key option for residents who want one. A list like that gives the landlord something concrete to price, and it gives the association a way to check the finished work.

Fobs, apps and data

Our landlord's new entry app asks for permission to use our phones' location. Is the building allowed to collect that?

Not for tracking you outside the building. HPD's summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners may not use satellite or similar navigation to track users outside the buildings, and it limits the data owners may collect to a listed minimum tied to entry. Some apps ask for location for a narrow purpose, such as unlocking a door only when the phone is near it, and a tenant can usually decline the permission and still open the door another way. If the app insists, the tenant should ask the owner for the building's data retention and privacy policy, which the law requires owners to provide, and raise the question with HPD or a tenant lawyer if the answer is unsatisfying. When we set up an app system, we turn off features the building has not chosen to use.

Could our landlord use the door's entry log to claim someone is living in an apartment who is not on the lease?

The Tenant Data Privacy Law is written to stop that kind of use. HPD's summary says data from a smart access system may not be used for purposes beyond access and entrance monitoring, including harassment or eviction, or to track tenants' relationship status, and it may not be used to collect data on people who are not tenants. Authentication data must also be destroyed within 90 days unless it is kept in anonymized form. How a court would treat a particular log in a particular dispute is a legal question for a tenant lawyer, not for an installer. What we control is the setup: we set log retention to the owner's written choice and keep the system to the features the building has decided to use.

Row houses and divided houses

Our limestone house in the Crown Heights North district was split into three apartments decades ago, and there are four bell buttons beside the door, one of which rings nowhere. Can one small panel replace them?

Yes, and it usually looks better than what is there now. We first trace each button to find out which apartment it really reaches and which wire is dead or connected to a unit that no longer exists. The replacement is a single slim panel with one button per current household, a camera if you want video, and a station in each apartment, with one release that frees the main door. Because the house is in the historic district, the panel's location and finish matter: a door reveal, the side of the stoop or the vestibule is often a better spot than the face of the stone, and the old holes are patched. The owner should check with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before anything is mounted on the facade, and we supply the panel's dimensions, finish and mounting details for that.

Our four-story brownstone has the kitchen on the garden floor and the bedrooms on the top floor, and nobody upstairs hears the bell. How many stations does a house like ours need?

Usually one on each floor where people spend time, and at least two in a four-story house: one near the kitchen on the garden floor and one on the bedroom floor, with a third on the parlor floor if that is where the family sits in the evening. Each station rings at the same time, and any of them can answer and release the door. A phone app can cover the floors in between, but a wall station is more reliable when someone is asleep or the phone is in another room. If the house also has a door under the stoop that deliveries use, a second small station there can ring the same stations with a different tone, so the family knows which door is calling.

New buildings and the avenues

Our new rental building advertises a "virtual doorman." What is actually behind that, and what happens when a resident is not home to answer?

A virtual doorman is a service layered on top of the intercom: when a visitor or courier rings, a remote attendant can answer through the panel's camera and speaker, check who they are and, depending on the building's rules, let them into a package room or the lobby. It is not a person in the building, and it depends on the panel, the building's internet connection and a paid service contract. When a resident is not home, the attendant can usually take a delivery into a secure area but should not let a guest upstairs without the resident's say-so. Residents should ask management how the service decides who gets in, what is recorded and for how long, and who to call if the panel itself is down, because the attendant cannot help if the hardware has failed.

Our restaurant on Franklin Avenue shares a cellar with the apartments above, and the apartments' intercom transformer hangs in our basement. Can it be moved out of our space?

Usually, yes, and it is often a good idea for both sides. A residential intercom whose power supply sits in a commercial tenant's cellar is at the mercy of that tenant's hours, renovations and circuit breakers, and the restaurant gets service calls it did not ask for. The supply can typically be relocated to a part of the cellar the landlord controls, or to a closet on the residential side, on its own labeled circuit, with the cable rerouted to it. The landlord authorizes and pays for that work, since the intercom is building equipment. If the residents' riser has to pass through your space, we protect it and label it so your next kitchen renovation does not cut it.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Crown Heights

These are claims that commonly appear in quick answers and cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, checked against what the Crown Heights research shows.

"Replacing an intercom in an old building means rewiring every apartment"

Quick answers often assume that a new system in a prewar building needs new cable to every unit, and price it that way.

Brick Underground's intercom guide says the core wiring inside the walls is usually fine even in older systems, with problems concentrated at connections, stations, the lobby panel and the power supply. Two-wire video systems are designed to run over a single existing pair, which is exactly what most Crown Heights walk-ups and elevator buildings already have.

The right first step is testing the riser pair by pair. In some buildings it fails and has to go; in many it passes, and the walls stay closed.

"Tenants have to approve a new intercom"

Some answers suggest a landlord needs residents' agreement before changing the entry system.

Landlord v. Tenant's summary of a 2013 DHCR ruling says tenant consent was not required for a landlord to change a buzzer system to an intercom system. In a building with 1,179 neighbors on the rent-stabilized list, that is worth knowing on both sides.

Tenants still have a voice: through DHCR's process if an MCI is sought, through a tenant association, and through complaints if the new system does not work. But a vote is not what decides whether it goes in.

"A landlord can pass the whole cost of a new intercom on to tenants"

Generic answers sometimes describe capital improvements as a straightforward way for owners to recover costs from rent.

Since the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act took effect in June 2019, MCI collection is capped at 2 percent a year, approved costs are spread over 144 or 150 months, increases end after 30 years, and no MCI increase is granted where 35 percent or fewer of the apartments are rent-regulated. DHCR also audits a quarter of applications.

An owner can still seek an increase for a qualifying intercom, but within limits, and only with paperwork that matches the installed system.

"App-based intercoms just plug in"

Product pages make cloud intercoms sound like a panel and a download.

MetroCom's installer guide notes that app-based systems need reliable building broadband, and most need a protected network, a power supply with battery backup and a door strike wired correctly. In an older Crown Heights building, the internet often arrives at a single apartment rather than the lobby, so the building may need its own connection.

For many buildings, a hybrid works best: wall stations on the tested old riser for residents who want them, and app answering for those who prefer phones.

"Fobs and apps are a convenience feature with no rules"

Many summaries present keyless entry purely as an upgrade.

In New York City, the Tenant Data Privacy Law covers multiple dwellings that use fobs, apps, cards or biometrics for entry. Owners must get written or in-app consent, provide a privacy policy, collect only a limited set of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and may not sell it, track tenants outside the building or require smart access to tenants' own apartments.

The hardware is the easy part. The consent forms, policy and log settings have to be planned together with it.

"Replacing a big building's intercom takes weeks of disruption"

Cost guides often imply long projects with residents cut off for days.

Brick Underground's guide quotes installers estimating about one day for a five-apartment building and about two days for a 100-unit building, depending on staffing. Our own schedules depend on the riser, access to apartments and the door work, and we give a schedule on the quote rather than a promise in advance.

What matters most for residents is the cutover: keeping the old system working until the new panel and each apartment's station are ready, so nobody goes without a way to let visitors in.

"Every apartment building has to have an intercom"

Quick answers often state that New York requires intercoms in all apartment buildings.

ButterflyMX's summary of the law says the requirement generally applies to Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968. Many Crown Heights row houses split into two to six apartments are below that line, though self-closing, self-locking entrance door rules still apply, and older large buildings have their own conditions.

The age, apartment count and conversion history of the specific building decide which rules apply. An owner near the threshold should check the code text or ask an attorney.

FAQ

Crown Heights Intercom Installation FAQ

Can a Crown Heights intercom estimate be given from photos, or does someone have to come out?
+

Many estimates can be given the same day from photos of the street door, the panel, the vestibule and the cellar power supply, and requests get a booked callback within two hours. Larger buildings and any job that depends on reusing the old riser need a survey, because only a pair-by-pair test shows what the cable can carry.

Do you charge to review our photos and answer questions before the survey?
+

No. Reviewing photos and answering questions costs nothing. The written quote follows the survey, and if a riser, a frame or a wall turns out to be different from what the photos suggested, we stop and explain before going further.

Is a building south of Eastern Parkway billed differently from one north of it?
+

No. All of Crown Heights, from Atlantic Avenue to Empire Boulevard, is billed at our Brooklyn rate. The price differences between buildings come from the riser, the apartment count and the door work, not from which side of the parkway the building is on.

Does a new intercom qualify for an MCI increase if only a fifth of our apartments are still rent-stabilized?
+

According to Landlord v. Tenant's summary of the 2019 rent law, no MCI increase is granted in a building where 35 percent or fewer of the apartments are rent-regulated, so a building with a fifth of its units stabilized would generally fall outside the MCI process. The exact count and any exceptions are a question for the owner's counsel or DHCR. This is not legal advice.

If DHCR approves an MCI for our intercom, how long does that charge stay on a rent-stabilized lease?
+

Under the 2019 rent law, as summarized by Borah, Goldstein, Altschuler, Nahins and Goidel, MCI increases are temporary and end after 30 years, and collection is capped at 2 percent a year. Approved costs are spread over 144 months in buildings with 35 or fewer units and 150 months in larger ones. Tenants with questions about a specific order should ask DHCR or a tenant lawyer.

Can our landlord switch the old buzzer to an intercom without the tenants agreeing?
+

Generally yes. Landlord v. Tenant summarizes a 2013 DHCR ruling that tenant consent was not required for a landlord to change a buzzer system to an intercom system. Tenants can still raise concerns with the owner, through a tenant association, or through DHCR if the owner applies for an MCI increase.

How long may the building keep the records its fob or app system collects?
+

HPD's summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law says authentication data must be destroyed no later than 90 days after it is collected or generated, unless it is kept in anonymized form. When we set up a keyless system, the log retention is set to the owner's written choice so the building can stay within that rule.

Can the intercom also control the laundry room, the bike room or the elevator?
+

Many systems can add readers or relays for other doors, so a fob or code can open the laundry or bike room for residents only. Elevator control depends on the elevator company and its equipment, so it is coordinated with them. Each extra door is listed on the quote with its own hardware and cable.

Do the old system and the new one run side by side until every apartment is switched over?
+

Where the building allows it, yes. We plan the cutover so the old lobby panel and handsets keep working while the new cable, stations and panel go in, then switch apartments over in a set order. The schedule on the quote says when residents can expect their new station and what day the old panel comes down.

Is a station that a tenant pulled off the wall covered by the warranty?
+

No. The warranty covers AESS-supplied products in normal use and excludes physical damage, unplugging, power surges, customer wiring and changes to internet or phones. A station damaged by a tenant or a contractor is repaired or replaced as a billed service call.

Can the panel on our row house be finished to sit quietly against limestone or brownstone?
+

Usually. Several manufacturers make slim panels in bronze, black, stainless and other finishes, and a panel set in a door reveal or the side of the stoop is much less visible than one on the face of the stone. In the historic districts, the owner should check the location and finish with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before it is mounted.

Can you install a panel or apartment stations our building already bought?
+

Yes, as labor-only work, if the equipment suits the apartment count, the riser and the doors. We check model numbers before the visit and flag anything missing. Customer-supplied equipment is covered by its manufacturer's terms, not ours.
