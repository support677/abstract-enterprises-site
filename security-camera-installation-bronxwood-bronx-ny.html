The Neighborhood

Why Bronxwood Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Bronxwood is a neighborhood where cameras became a public question long before most homeowners thought about buying one. Wikipedia describes Allerton as having two subsections, Bronxwood and Laconia, and places Bronxwood in the southern part, including the NYCHA developments Pelham Parkway Houses and Boston Road Plaza. CityNeighborhoods.NYC draws a tighter box, with Pelham Parkway to the south, Bronx Park East to the west, Waring Avenue to the north and Williamsbridge Road to the east, and describes an internal grid anchored by Bronxwood Avenue, Paulding Avenue and Barnes Avenue. The same source says the name emerged in the early twentieth century as developers marketed new residential tracts east of Bronx Park, and that the years from the early 1900s to the 1930s turned farmland into a stable, leafy urban neighborhood. That history is visible in the houses themselves, and it shapes almost every camera job we expect here.

Start with the largest property in the neighborhood. NYCHA's Pelham Parkway Houses were completed on June 30, 1950: twenty-three six-story buildings with 1,263 apartments on 23.74 acres bordered by Pelham Parkway, Williamsbridge Road, and Wallace and Mace Avenues. In 2012, after the beating death of an elderly resident, Evelyn Shapiro, CBS New York and NYCHA's own press release reported that Councilman James Vacca had arranged four million dollars to install cameras and repair locks at the development, to curb what was described as an alarming increase in gang and drug-related crime. NYCHA said at the time that no security cameras existed at either of the developments covered by the funding, and the officers investigating the murder believed cameras would have helped them find the suspect. By 2014, the Bronx Times reported, some 2,531 residents of the Pelham Parkway Houses were about to see 375 security cameras installed, and the tenants council president said she hoped they would cover both the interiors and the exteriors of the buildings.

The part of that story that matters most to a private owner is what came next. The Bronx Times also covered a separate lighting project at the Houses, and the council member who funded it said better lighting was crucial to making sure the cameras could clearly record criminal activity. That is not a slogan; it is the most common reason a camera disappoints its owner. A camera on a porch or a driveway that faces a dark side path, a shadowed gate or a car parked beyond the reach of the streetlight will record a grey smear at the moment you need a face. When we plan cameras for a Bronxwood house, the first night-time question is not which camera to buy but what light falls on each target, and whether the camera's own light or a fixture on the house needs to fill the gap.

The layout of the Houses carries a second lesson. City Journal has described the buildings as set on a diagonal away from the street, so that only by wandering around the courtyard can a visitor find individual addresses, and the Manhattan Institute has written about broken front-door buzzers and emergency heating units parked outside. Buildings that turn away from the street are hard to watch from the street; the useful views are at the doors, the paths between buildings and the places people must pass. The same is true, at a much smaller scale, of a Bronxwood two-family with a side entrance down a narrow walk: the camera has to be where the person has to go, not where it is easiest to screw a bracket to the wall.

Recent news shows why owners and boards keep asking. News 12 Bronx reported a woman attacked inside an elevator just before 11:30 at night near Mace and Bronxwood avenues, and in May 2026 an eighteen-year-old beaten and robbed during a clothing sale inside a building near Waring and Bronxwood avenues. Norwood News reported a shooting in May 2026 at 2355 Williamsbridge Road, beside Pelham Parkway Houses buildings. In October 2026, News 12 obtained surveillance video showing a white Mercedes-Benz flying through the busy intersection at Allerton and Holland avenues, a crash that blew out the front windows of nearby businesses; NBC New York described video of the car appearing to be parked on Allerton Avenue before it accelerated westbound. Each of those stories depended, in part, on someone's camera recording a usable picture and someone knowing how to get it off the recorder.

Property crime is the steadier worry. One crime-data site lists vehicle theft as the most prevalent crime type in Allerton, with an index 33 percent above the national average. In April 2026, Norwood News reported a Bronx District Attorney case in which sixteen people were charged with 252 thefts of cars, tires, rims and catalytic converters, nineteen of them in the 49th Precinct, which covers some or all of Allerton, and prosecutors said many of the crimes were captured on surveillance video. For a homeowner with a car in a gated driveway on Paulding or Bronxwood Avenue, that is a specific brief: a camera that sees the gate, the car and the ground beside the wheels, at night, from a height nobody can reach.

Then there are the houses. CityNeighborhoods.NYC describes primarily low-rise brick homes, two-family houses and small apartment buildings, many dating from the early to mid-twentieth century, and Wikipedia notes tenements scattered across the area, mostly within five blocks of the White Plains Road el. Building records on StreetEasy for two-family houses on Bronxwood Avenue show construction years from 1910 to 1965, and Openigloo lists hundreds of pre-war buildings under the Bronxwood name on streets such as Hering, Paulding, Wallace, Cruger, Holland, Barnes and Esplanade. A typical listing on Homes.com describes a twelve-unit all-brick pre-war building close to the 2 train. In the small enclave called Bronxwood Park, Forgotten New York describes two-family houses long since covered in aluminum siding, and WFUV describes gated driveways and large porches. Brick, siding, porches, gates and basements: each one changes how a camera is mounted and how its cable gets home.

We did not retrieve HPD, 311 or Department of Buildings records for camera work at Bronxwood addresses, and we found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district covering these blocks. Patch and BKReader returned nothing; the Bronx Times, Norwood News and News 12 Bronx carried the local reporting above. Light, Height and Hand-Off: Building Bronxwood Camera Systems That Produce Usable Video

The Pelham Parkway Houses story and the 2026 theft case point the same way. Cameras help when three conditions are met: the scene is lit, the camera is close enough and high enough to see what matters without being reached, and the footage can be found and handed over quickly. Most home systems fail on one of the three, not because the camera is cheap but because nobody planned for it. Light comes before the lens

On many Bronxwood blocks the street side of a house is reasonably lit and the side and back are not. A driveway that runs between two houses, a gate set back from the sidewalk, or a backyard behind a garage can be completely dark at two in the morning. Infrared cameras will still produce a picture there, but it is black and white, it loses colour detail such as the shade of a jacket or a car, and it can flare badly if a wall or fence is close to the lens. Cameras with their own white light, or a separate motion light aimed at the target, keep colour and detail where it matters, and they also make it obvious to someone at the gate that the house is watched. We check every position after dark on the survey, or ask you for night-time photos, and we tell you honestly where a camera alone will not do the job.

Height is a trade-off, not a rule

People assume a camera should go as high as possible so nobody can knock it down. On a two-story Bronxwood house, a camera at the roofline sees a lot of ground but very little face: everyone becomes the top of a head and a pair of shoulders, and a person crouched beside a car can vanish behind the car's roof. A camera at seven or eight feet under a porch roof or soffit gets the angle right but is easier to reach. We usually split the job: one high camera for the overview of the driveway and the yard, and one closer, lower camera at the place people must pass, protected by the porch roof, a soffit or a vandal-resistant dome. The 2026 theft case, with its tires, rims and catalytic converters, is exactly the kind of crime the low view catches and the high view misses.

Hand-off: getting a clip to the people who need it

The Bronx District Attorney's Crime Strategies Bureau launched BronxCAMS in 2018, a voluntary registry of private cameras. The DA's office says it has no access to registered cameras or their footage; when footage might help a case, it contacts the camera owner to view it and request a copy. That arrangement only works if the owner can find the right clip quickly and export it in a form someone else can play. At handover we show you how to search your recorder by date, time and camera, how to export a clip to a USB stick or your phone, and how to write down the time offset if the recorder clock has drifted. We are happy to walk you through the BronxCAMS enrollment page if you decide to register, but whether you do is entirely your choice.

What a Bronxwood camera should not record

Good footage is footage that stays lawful. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera that intrudes on their property, so every view on a Bronxwood house is aimed at your own porch, drive and yard, with privacy masks over the windows and yards next door. Cameras that capture sound fall under the state's eavesdropping law, which requires the consent of at least one person in a conversation, so we leave audio recording switched off on outdoor cameras unless you ask for it and understand the limits. And inside a two- or three-family house, nothing we install looks into a tenant's apartment.

Brick fronts from 1910 to 1965

The StreetEasy records for two-family houses on Bronxwood Avenue span half a century of construction, and the brick changes with them. Older brick can be softer and the mortar more crumbly; mid-century brick is usually harder and more even. Either way, a camera bracket belongs in the face of the brick, not the joint, because the joint is the weakest part of the wall and a bracket pulled by wind or a passing hand will slowly work loose there. We drill with masonry bits at a slow speed, look at the dust to confirm we are in sound brick, and use sleeve anchors sized for the mount. Where cable passes through the wall we drill once, slope the hole slightly downward toward the outside so rain cannot run in, sleeve it and seal it. On most brick fronts we fit a weatherproof junction box behind the camera, which hides the connection and spreads the bracket's load over more brick.

Aluminum siding and porch beams

Siding is easy to damage and easy to leak through. A camera screwed directly through aluminum siding dents it, and a camera on vinyl can crack it in cold weather; water then follows the screws into whatever lies behind. On sided houses we use a mounting block made for siding, so the camera sits on a flat, sealed base, and we find the framing behind it so the screws hold in wood rather than in thin metal or plastic. On houses with deep porches, like the ones in Bronxwood Park, the porch ceiling and its beams are often the best place for the front camera: sheltered from rain and snow, at the right height for faces, and with a short, hidden cable path back into the house. We look at the porch's construction before we promise that, because some porch ceilings are boarded and some are open.

Stairs and vestibules in three-family houses and walk-ups

In a three-family house or a small walk-up, the stair and vestibule are common areas the owner controls, and the camera there earns its keep by seeing the front door and the mailboxes. It must be placed so that an opening apartment door never shows the inside of the apartment. We mount stair cameras high near the top of a flight, pointed down the run of the stair and across the vestibule, then test them with the picture live on a phone while each door on the stair is opened in turn. If any door shows its interior, we move the camera or mask that part of the frame. The recorder for a building like this belongs in a locked cellar room or the owner's own apartment, not in a common hallway closet.

Pre-war masonry, plaster corridors and the cellar

The pre-war buildings near the el and along streets such as Wallace, Cruger and Holland Avenue were built with masonry walls and plaster partitions, and their cellars are the natural starting point for a camera system. Running cable up through a five- or six-story pre-war building is slower than in new construction: there may be an old chase or dumbwaiter shaft we can use, or the cable may need a neat surface raceway in the stairwell. Where cable passes through a floor or a fire-rated wall, the opening is sealed with fire-stopping material. Lobby cameras in these buildings face the people coming in, away from the street door's glare, and the corridor cameras are checked for lighting, because a plaster hallway with one weak fixture is the classic place where a camera records nothing useful.

Storefront cameras that help after the event

A shop camera has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. The first is the shop's own business: who comes in, what happens at the register, who goes to the stockroom. The second is the record of what happens just outside the door, which, as the October 2026 crash at Allerton and Holland showed, can matter to a lot of people besides the owner. We handle them separately. A door camera mounted inside, facing in, catches faces as people enter. A register camera sees hands, cash and the counter top rather than the customer's face. A camera above the door outside, or inside the window angled out, covers the entrance and the edge of the sidewalk, set so it records your doorway and frontage rather than the apartments across the street. The recorder goes in a locked back room or cabinet, on battery backup, rather than under the counter where it is the first thing a thief grabs.

Shops under the el and buildings with apartments above

Many storefronts on White Plains Road and Allerton Avenue have apartments over them and a shared side door. A shop that leases its space controls its own interior, but the facade, the shared entrance and the hallway belong to the building owner, and the apartments above are off limits. Before we mount anything outside, a tenant business should ask the landlord and send us the answer in writing. Inside, we run cable above a drop ceiling where there is one, along a neat raceway where there is not, and save the loud drilling for the hour before opening or after closing. Under the el, we also look at how the trains' lights and the steel structure affect the view from the door, and we adjust the angle so the camera is not fighting a bright gap between the rails.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • No general camera mandate. Security guidance on NYC building rules states there is no general city law requiring cameras; the legal requirements fall on intercoms and lighting. A shop installs cameras because the owner wants them, so the owner sets the scope.
  • A licensed installer. NYC Business lists a New York State Department of State license for installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance.
  • Sound needs care. A camera that records audio is subject to New York's eavesdropping law, which requires one party's consent, so we leave audio off at counters and doors unless the owner understands and accepts that responsibility.
  • Fire-rated openings are sealed. Where camera cable passes through a fire-rated wall or floor in a commercial or mixed-use building, the opening is closed with rated fire-stopping material.
  • New circuits are an electrician's work. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable powered from the recorder or a PoE switch. If a shop needs a new outlet or circuit for equipment, a licensed electrician does it, and the quote says so.
  • Private spaces stay private. No cameras in restrooms, changing areas or anywhere people expect not to be watched, and we say so plainly if a requested view crosses that line.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Bronxwood: Questions Answered

Houses, porches and driveways

Our two-family in Bronxwood Park is covered in old aluminum siding. How do you mount a camera on it without denting or cracking it?

We never screw a camera straight through the siding. A mounting block made for siding goes on first, giving the camera a flat, sealed base, and its screws go through to the framing behind so the weight is held by wood rather than thin metal. We seal around the block and the cable entry so water cannot follow the fixings in. Where the house has a deep porch, as many Bronxwood Park houses do, we often put the front camera on the porch ceiling or a beam instead, which keeps it out of the weather and avoids the siding altogether.

Under the porch roof or above the door frame: where does the front camera on a porch house do its job best?

Usually under the porch roof, near the outer edge, looking back toward the door and the steps. From there it sees visitors' faces as they climb up, and the roof shelters it from rain and snow. A camera right above the door looks down on the tops of heads and often stares into the porch light. We place the porch light beside or behind the camera's line of sight, never in front of it, and check the picture with the light on at night before we fix anything permanently.

There is no outdoor outlet anywhere on our house. How do the cameras get their power?

Through the same cable that carries the picture. Most wired systems we install use power over Ethernet: each camera's single cable runs back to the recorder or a PoE switch in the basement, which sends power out along it. So no outlet is needed at the camera, and a battery backup beside the recorder keeps every camera running through a short power cut. Only a few devices, such as some floodlight cameras, need their own mains power; if one is on your plan, the quote will say so and name who provides the outlet.

Thieves around here go after tires, rims and catalytic converters. What camera angle shows someone crouched beside a car in our driveway?

A low, close one. A camera at the roofline sees the car's roof and misses the ground beside the wheels, which is where that kind of theft happens. We pair an overview camera for the length of the driveway with a second camera mounted lower, under a soffit or porch roof, looking along the side of where the car is parked, with light on that strip. The 2026 case reported by Norwood News, with 252 thefts of cars, tires, rims and catalytic converters, is the reason we treat this as a separate view rather than hoping the overview covers it.

We close our driveway gate at night. Should the camera go on the gate post or on the house?

Almost always on the house. A camera on a gate post is easy to reach, gets shaken every time the gate is slammed, and needs a cable run underground or along the fence. A camera on the house wall or porch, aimed at the gate from within a car's length or two, sees anyone at the gate clearly and stays out of reach. If the gate is far back from the house, we may add a light aimed at it rather than move the camera closer, so the picture keeps its colour and detail after dark.

Owners, tenants and NYCHA residents

A Parkside Houses tenant once got an eviction notice over cameras. What does that mean for a Pelham Parkway Houses resident thinking about one?

It means checking the rules before buying anything. A 2012 report described a tenant at Parkside Houses, also in Community Board 11, who received an eviction notice saying she had breached her lease by installing cameras. NYCHA controls its buildings, including the 375 cameras installed at Pelham Parkway Houses, and residents' options depend on NYCHA's current guidance and their lease. Ask the management office and the tenant association first, get the answer in writing, and we will only work within what it allows.

The tenant in our upstairs apartment wants to put their own doorbell camera on the shared front door. Should we allow it?

That is the owner's decision, and it is worth thinking through. A tenant's doorbell records everyone using the shared door, including you, and stores that footage on the tenant's account, outside your control. Many owners prefer to install one camera covering the entrance on their own recorder and give the tenant a view-only login for it, or to agree in writing where the tenant's device goes, what it covers and that it comes down when they move. Either way, settle it before anything is mounted.

Co-ops and apartment buildings

After the elevator attack reported near Mace and Bronxwood avenues, our board wants to know who uses the elevator. Is the lobby landing enough?

The lobby landing camera is the most valuable single view, because nearly everyone passes it, and it can face the elevator doors so people are seen as they step in and out. A camera at each floor's landing adds a record of where people got off, but it must be aimed along the corridor without looking into any apartment as a door opens. We walk each landing with the board's representative, check the lighting there, and recommend the smallest set of positions that answers the board's question.

Does New York City's new tenant data privacy law apply to our building's security cameras?

Not to ordinary camera feeds, according to the summaries we read. The Tenant Data Privacy Act applies to Class A multiple dwellings with smart access systems, the kind that use key fobs, apps or biometrics to open doors, and Thomson Reuters Practical Law and ButterflyMX both note it does not cover standard video or security camera feeds. If your building also has a smart entry system, ask your attorney or managing agent how the law applies to that system, because it may have its own data rules.

Streets and storefronts

Why does the camera behind our shop window show nothing but its own glare at night?

Because its infrared light is bouncing straight back off the glass. A camera with night vision sends out invisible light to see in the dark; behind a window, that light hits the glass and reflects into the lens, washing out the picture. The fixes are to press the camera hard against the glass with a shroud, switch off its infrared and rely on outside lighting, or move it outside above the door in a vandal-resistant housing. We test the picture at night before choosing.

When something happens in the intersection outside our Allerton Avenue shop, what can a storefront camera realistically add?

A clear, time-stamped record of the moments around your door and the stretch of sidewalk in front of it, which is often what investigators and insurers need from nearby businesses. A camera aimed at your frontage will not see the whole intersection in detail, and we would not aim it across the street at apartments anyway. What makes the footage useful is an accurate clock on the recorder, enough storage that the clip still exists days later, and an owner who can export it quickly.

Footage, security and sharing

Can you help us sign up for BronxCAMS, and would the DA then be able to watch our cameras?

We can walk you through the enrollment page on the Bronx District Attorney's site, but the decision and the registration are yours. The DA's office says plainly that it does not have access to registered cameras or footage; BronxCAMS only tells the office that a camera exists, so it can contact the owner and ask to view or copy footage when it might help a case. Nobody watches your feed. What we make sure of is that you can find and export a clip when that call comes.

Could someone break into our camera system over the internet?

Any internet-connected device can be attacked, which is why setup matters. On every system we install we change the default passwords, give the owner a strong main password kept in writing, turn off remote features you do not use, update the recorder's firmware, and set view-only users for anyone else rather than sharing the owner's login. We also avoid opening ports on your router where the manufacturer's secure app connection does the job. If a user leaves, the owner deletes that account in a minute.

Several houses on our block want to cover the street together. Can neighbors share camera views?

Yes, as long as every owner agrees and each camera stays on its owner's property. Each house keeps its own recorder and footage, and owners can give each other view-only access to the street-facing cameras through the app. We help the group decide which houses cover which stretches so the views overlap at the gaps, and we keep every camera aimed away from other people's windows and yards. Put the arrangement in writing, including what happens when a house is sold.

Battery cameras are easy to put up and have their place, especially for renters who cannot drill. But they record in short bursts, need recharging, and depend on a WiFi signal that often weakens through Bronxwood's brick walls and at the far end of a driveway.

Registration only tells the office that a camera exists. When footage might help, the office contacts the owner to view it or request a copy, and the owner decides.

Would a dummy camera on our porch put people off just as well as a real one?
+

Sometimes it deters a casual passer-by, but it records nothing, so after a car break-in or a stolen delivery you have no footage at all. Experienced thieves often recognise dummies by their missing cables or blinking lights. A real camera under the porch roof with light on the steps both deters and records, which is why we do not install decoys.

Where does the technician who comes to Bronxwood work from?
+

Our Bronx office at 460 East Fordham Road, a short drive across the Bronx River Parkway. Requests are booked within 2 hours, and we confirm the arrival window when we book. The same office handles any follow-up visit or footage help after the installation.

We bought a two-family that came with a recorder nobody knows the password for. Can it be brought back into use?
+

Often, yes. Many recorders can be reset to factory settings, which wipes the old accounts and footage and lets us set new passwords in your name. We first check the make, model and condition, test the old cameras and cable, and tell you whether it is worth keeping or better replaced. Either way, the previous owner's access ends.

Our house faces the PS 76 schoolyard. Does that change how the cameras are set up?
+

Yes. The cameras still cover your own porch, gate and frontage, but we add privacy masks over the schoolyard and the school building so the recorder never stores views of the children or staff. We test the masks with the picture live before we leave, and show you where they are set in case you ever need to check them.

Our building looks out on the trees along Pelham Parkway. Will summer leaves block the camera?
+

Leaves can block a long view across the parkway malls, and moving branches can trigger motion alerts all summer. We aim cameras at your own entrance and the near sidewalk, which trees rarely hide, and set motion zones to ignore the branches. If you want a wider view, we look at it both in leaf and bare before promising what it will show.

Our backyard sits below the street, like the slope on Wallace Avenue. Does that affect where the cameras go?
+

It does. A camera mounted on the back of the house looks down on a low yard, which is good for overview but can miss faces near the back fence. On sloping lots we often mount the yard camera lower, aim it along the slope rather than down it, and check that the drop does not bring the neighbor's windows into view.

Is there a quicker way to find one moment in a whole night of footage than scrolling through it?
+

Most recorders we install can search by motion in a chosen area, so you can draw a box around the driveway or the gate and jump straight to the moments when something moved there. Many can also filter for people or vehicles. We set this up at handover and practise it with you on your own phone.

Will the night lights on our cameras shine into the house next door?
+

Infrared night vision is invisible to the eye, though some cameras show a faint red glow at the lens. Cameras with white spotlights are visible, so we aim them at your own gate or path and set them to come on only when motion is detected there. On close-set houses we check after dark that no light lands in a neighbor's window.

Can we get a written quote from photos instead of having someone visit first?
+

For many houses, yes. Send daytime and night-time photos of each spot you want covered, a photo of the basement ceiling and the recorder location, and the street address. We can often quote from that, and confirm details on the install day. Larger buildings, co-ops and shops usually need a walk-through first.

If we move out of the two-family we rent, can our cameras come with us?
+

If you paid for the equipment and the owner agreed to the installation, the cameras and recorder are yours to take. We can remove them, patch and seal the mounting holes and wall openings, and reinstall the system at your new home. Agree with the owner in advance what stays, such as cable inside the walls, and what goes.

Our house is on a corner. Does that change the camera plan?
+

Usually it adds a view, because a corner house has two street faces and often a side yard or garage open to the second street. We cover both approaches to the doors and driveway and mask the cross-street apartments. Corner houses also get more headlights sweeping across the cameras at night, so we angle them to keep glare down.

Our semi-attached house shares a wall with the neighbor. Can a camera be mounted on that shared wall?
+

Not without the neighbor's agreement, because a shared wall is usually not yours alone. We prefer to mount on the parts of the house that are clearly your own, such as your half of the front, your porch or your rear wall. If the shared wall really is the only good spot, get the neighbor's agreement in writing before we drill.
