The Neighborhood

Why Clinton Hill Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Clinton Hill was built for people who expected to be left alone behind their own front doors, and the houses still work that way: tall stoops, deep areaways, private rear gardens and long brownstone fronts that face the street with very few windows at eye level. Clinton Avenue was laid out as a boulevard along the crest of the hill in 1832. By the 1870s and 1880s it had become what people called Brooklyn's Gold Coast. Charles Pratt, the oil executive, built his mansion at 232 Clinton Avenue in 1874 and gave houses nearby to three of his four sons as wedding presents, and in 1887 he founded Pratt Institute on family land a few blocks away. Around those mansions grew blocks of Italianate and Beaux-Arts brownstone and brick rowhouses on Washington, Waverly, Grand, Cambridge Place, St. James Place and the side streets, plus a scattering of older wood houses on the Wallabout side toward the Navy Yard. The National Register listing for the historic district counts 1,063 largely residential contributing buildings put up between the 1840s and 1930. That is the housing a camera plan here has to work with.

Those buildings are lovely to live in and awkward to watch over. A brownstone stoop lifts the parlor-floor door well above the sidewalk, while the garden-floor door hides under it, behind an iron gate in the areaway. A camera aimed for one rarely sees the other. Many houses are now split, with an owner on the upper floors and one or two rental apartments below, so the people coming and going through the same gate are not all one household. The rear gardens are long, walled and often back onto other gardens or a community garden, which means a deck door can be reached by someone who never set foot on your street. And the bigger buildings, the 1940s Clinton Hill Co-ops towers on Clinton Avenue, the converted mansions and the walk-ups along Washington and DeKalb avenues, have lobbies where parcels pile up and residents buzz in people they do not know.

The local reports point at exactly those weak spots. Brownstoner reported in June 2011 that a house on Waverly Avenue between Gates and Greene avenues was burgled through the owner's deck, which overlooks Hollenback Garden, and that days later a man was seen climbing the garden's chain-link fence and dropping into a neighboring yard. DNAinfo reported in 2014 that a man dressed as a FedEx deliveryman was let into a Grand Avenue building by a neighbor and took a sweater from an unlocked apartment; he was caught on camera at the entrance. The Brooklyn Eagle reported a lobby package theft near Fulton Street and Washington Avenue in which the thief bit a 49-year-old woman who tried to stop him and drove off in a gold BMW SUV. And the Brooklyn Paper reported that a Washington Avenue tenant's Cartier, Rolex and Bulgari watches were not missed until weeks after they were last seen, at which point police went to the building's cameras. Every one of those cases turns on a camera at a specific door, deck or lobby, and on whether the footage still existed when someone asked for it.

The other half of the job is the rulebook. Inside the Clinton Hill Historic District and the Wallabout Historic District, the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews cameras on the outside of buildings. Its permit guidebook lets staff approve cameras without a public hearing when they follow set rules: no more than two cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage on a small residential or commercial building, no more than three on a large residential, commercial or institutional building, fixings at mortar joints or plain masonry rather than carved stone or cast iron, concealed conduit with no more than about ten inches showing, and equipment kept small and finished to blend. A camera bolted to a brownstone lintel with a loop of black cable down the face is the kind of work that turns a simple staff permit into a problem. We plan the front first around those rules, so the permit is routine and the rest of the system can go in on schedule.

  • Most of the neighborhood is landmarked: the Clinton Hill Historic District was designated by LPC on November 10, 1981, and the Wallabout Historic District, about 55 buildings on Vanderbilt Avenue between Myrtle and Park avenues, on July 12, 2011.
  • Front walls have a camera count: two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on small buildings and three on large or institutional ones, under the LPC permit guidebook.
  • Old housing, mostly masonry: 1,063 contributing buildings from the 1840s to 1930 in the National Register district, with wood houses mixed in on the Wallabout blocks.
  • Thefts are noticed late: the Washington Avenue watch theft was discovered in February for watches last seen in December, per the Brooklyn Paper.
  • Lobbies are a target: parcels taken from a lobby near Fulton and Washington, and a fake courier buzzed into a Grand Avenue building, per the Brooklyn Eagle and DNAinfo.
  • One precinct: the 88th Precinct at 298 Classon Avenue covers Clinton Hill along with Fort Greene Park and Commodore Barry Park, and its Romanesque Revival station house there was itself landmarked by LPC in 2014.

What Clinton Hill owners often add next. Once the stoop, the areaway and the deck are covered, owners of split brownstones usually want a video intercom at the street door so the garden-floor tenant can see who is ringing, and co-op boards ask about fob readers on the lobby door. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a video intercom or a reader is likely, a spare cable to the vestibule, so plaster and moldings are only opened once.

Something Already Happened, or a Camera Went Black? Getting Help Fast in Clinton Hill

Urgent calls from Clinton Hill tend to come in one of three shapes. The first is an incident: parcels missing from the vestibule, a garden door with fresh pry marks, a bicycle gone from the areaway, or a register opened at a shop on Fulton Street. The second is a slow discovery, the kind the Washington Avenue watch theft shows, where something has been missing for an unknown number of weeks and the question is whether the recorder still holds that period. The third is a dead picture, a camera that went black after a power cut, a new modem from the internet provider, a storm or a contractor working on the facade, and nobody noticed until they needed it.

For any incident, leave the recorder running and do not unplug it. Write down which camera and roughly what time, then copy that window to a USB stick if you know how; if you do not, a photo of the screen with the time showing is better than nothing until we can export it properly. If anyone was hurt or threatened, call 911 first and tell the officers you have video. The 88th Precinct is at 298 Classon Avenue, and detectives will usually tell you what format they want. For a slow discovery, the first job is to stop the recorder from overwriting the oldest days while you work out what you need, and that is something we can walk you through on the phone. For a dead camera, note which one, check whether the recorder and any small switch beside it still show lights, and please do not reset the recorder to factory settings, which can wipe passwords, remote access and sometimes the recordings themselves.

Then call (347) 934-8335 or send a WhatsApp message to our WhatsApp line with a photo of the recorder's label and the camera that failed. Requests are booked within 2 hours. We do not promise installation the same day, and nobody honest can on a landmarked brownstone where a front camera may need a permit first, but we can usually tell you on that first call whether the footage you need is likely still on the drive, what it takes to get it off, and whether the dead camera is a cable, a power supply or the camera itself.

The Landmarks Front

Planning Clinton Hill Front Cameras Around the LPC Permit Guidebook, Before Anything Is Drilled

On most Clinton Hill blocks, the street side of a building is regulated and the back is not, so we design the front to the Landmarks staff-permit rules and let the rear and the inside carry most of the coverage. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook, in its chapter on health, safety and building equipment, says staff can issue permits for security cameras when the work meets a short list of conditions, and that work which does not may be sent to the Commissioners for a public hearing. For an owner, the difference is weeks of waiting and drawings, against a routine sign-off. The conditions are specific enough that we can check a proposed layout against them on paper before we quote.

Two cameras on a single rowhouse front

The count is the rule owners feel most. With two cameras for a rowhouse front of 25 feet or less, we do not spend one on a wide street view that shows passing traffic in fine detail and faces at no useful size. One camera watches the stoop from a position that sees a face at the parlor door and the top steps; the other watches the areaway gate and the garden-floor door below, which is where most daily traffic, deliveries and garbage runs actually happen. A video doorbell or an intercom with a camera at the parlor door changes the arithmetic, and it is a question to settle with the Landmarks application rather than after it. Anything that needs a wider view of the street, such as a car or a bicycle, is usually better served from inside, through a parlor window, where the facade count does not apply.

Large buildings, institutions and the third camera

Clinton Hill has more large buildings than most brownstone neighborhoods. The mansion row on Clinton Avenue includes the Pratt houses, now partly used by St. Joseph's, and other Gold Coast houses that have been divided into apartments. Pratt Institute's 25-acre campus, Emmanuel Baptist Church at Lafayette Avenue and St. James Place, the Church of St. Luke and St. Matthew on Clinton Avenue and St. Mary's Episcopal on Classon Avenue are all institutional buildings with wide frontages. For buildings like these, the three-per-25-feet allowance and the longer frontage give real room, but the guidebook's spacing and discretion rules still apply. On a long institutional front, we usually tie each camera to a door that is actually used, rather than spacing them evenly for the sake of it.

Inside the line, outside the line

The Clinton Hill Historic District does not cover every block between Flushing and Atlantic avenues, and the separate Wallabout district is small. Before we quote a front camera, we check the address against the LPC district map, and we tell you in writing whether the work needs a Landmarks permit. If it does, we prepare the layout and cut sheets for the application; if it does not, we still follow the same habits, because neat cable and discreet cameras are better work on any old house. Rear cameras and anything inside generally do not need Landmarks review, so on a district house the rear and interior work can be planned and scheduled while the front permit is in process.

Recording Long Enough

The Theft Nobody Noticed for Weeks: Sizing Clinton Hill Recording to How People Actually Live

A camera system is only as useful as the oldest day it still holds, and in Clinton Hill the gap between a theft and its discovery can be long. In the Washington Avenue case the Brooklyn Paper reported, the watches were last seen in December and found missing on February 6, and police then reviewed the building's cameras. Whether any of that footage still existed depended on one setting most owners never look at: how many days the recorder keeps before it starts writing over the oldest file. A lobby recorder holding ten days would have nothing from December. Owners who travel, who keep a second home, who rent the garden floor to tenants they rarely see, or who manage a building from somewhere else should plan for exactly that situation.

Every four-camera package on our published Brooklyn price list includes two weeks of recording. That is enough for most single households who check their phones when an alert comes in and notice a missing parcel the same evening. It is often not enough for a building lobby, a shared vestibule, a rental floor, or a house that stands empty for part of the year. For those, longer storage costs a known amount, and it is worth deciding before the system goes in, because the drive has to be sized and fitted in the recorder at the start.

Security Camera Installation for Clinton Hill's Architecture

Few Brooklyn neighborhoods pack so many building types into so few blocks. A single walk from Myrtle Avenue to Fulton Street can pass a wartime co-op tower, a Gilded Age mansion, an Italianate brownstone row, a wood house older than the Civil War, a walk-up over a store and a church. Each one changes where the recorder lives, how cable gets from it to the cameras and who has to say yes.

Brownstone and Brick Rowhouses

The bulk of the historic district: Italianate and Beaux-Arts rows on Washington, Waverly, Grand, St. James Place, Cambridge Place and DeKalb Avenue, with stoops, areaways, parlor floors and long rear gardens.

Gold Coast Mansions

The freestanding and semi-attached houses of the 200 to 400 blocks of Clinton Avenue, including Charles Pratt's 1874 house at 232 Clinton Avenue, many now divided or in institutional use.

Wood Houses

Wood-framed dwellings in the Wallabout district on Vanderbilt Avenue, and older survivors such as the Gothic Revival wooden house at 284 Clinton Avenue, dated to about 1854.

Clinton Hill Co-ops

The tall brick buildings Equitable Life put up for Navy Yard defense workers and service members, first occupied in June 1943, on Clinton Avenue and toward Greene Avenue, now owner-occupied co-ops.

Walk-Ups and Flats

Tenement and flat buildings along the avenues and on the Wallabout side, often with a store at street level, a narrow lobby and mailboxes in the vestibule.

Churches and Institutions

Emmanuel Baptist, St. Luke and St. Matthew, St. Mary's Episcopal, the Pratt and St. Joseph's buildings and community spaces, with wide fronts and several doors in use at different hours.

Brownstones: the cellar is the highway

On a Clinton Hill rowhouse, the open joists of the cellar ceiling are usually the easiest way to get from a recorder to both the front and the rear. From there, a short hole through plain masonry reaches the areaway camera under the stoop, and a run along the rear wall reaches the garden. The parlor-door camera is the hard one, because the stoop and its stone sit between the cellar and the parlor floor. We look for a closet or a pocket behind the vestibule wall that drops straight down, rather than surface channel on plaster. Where plaster must be opened, we say so on the quote with the number of openings and who patches them, because original plaster and moldings are part of what people pay for in these houses.

Mansions: distance, and the larger allowance

A Clinton Avenue mansion can be several times the depth and width of a rowhouse, with a main entrance, a side door and sometimes a rear service entrance. Distance is the first issue: very long runs from a single recorder spot are possible on network cable within limits, but on the biggest houses a small switch partway, on its own battery backup, keeps every camera reliable. The second issue is who owns which part. A house divided into condominium apartments has common entrances that belong to everyone and private terraces that belong to one owner, and the board decides on the common cameras. The third is the Landmarks count, which for a large building may allow a third camera per 25 feet of frontage.

Co-op towers: board first, then the riser

The Clinton Hill Co-ops were designed by Harrison, Fouilhoux and Abramovitz and built by Starrett Brothers and Eken for wartime housing, then became owner-occupied co-ops. A building like that has a board, a managing agent and a super, and camera work in the lobby, laundry room or entrances goes through all three. We prepare a short proposal that lists every camera, what it sees, where the recorder sits, who can view it and how long footage is kept, because boards approve faster when those questions are answered up front. A shareholder's own camera, such as one inside the apartment, is a different conversation, and the house rules decide what can be fixed to the hallway side of an apartment door.

Business Security Camera Installation in Clinton Hill

Clinton Hill's businesses sit on a few strips: Fulton Street along the southern edge, Myrtle Avenue along the north, and the shops and restaurants on DeKalb, Washington, Vanderbilt and Classon avenues in between. Two business improvement districts touch the neighborhood. The Myrtle Avenue Brooklyn Partnership describes its area as the Fort Greene, Clinton Hill and Wallabout community, and the Fulton Street district run by the FAB Alliance was expanded so that it joins the Brooklyn Cultural District to the commercial strips of Fort Greene and Clinton Hill. Many of the stores are small, owner-run and housed in the ground floor of older buildings, often inside or at the edge of the historic district.

Fulton Street

Restaurants, groceries, cafes and specialty shops on the southern edge, the strip where the Brooklyn Eagle in 2019 talked to owners about repeated burglaries and teenagers walking out with goods.

Myrtle Avenue

The northern strip served by the Myrtle Avenue Brooklyn Partnership, with storefronts below apartments and the Pratt campus close by.

DeKalb Avenue

Restaurants and shops in the blocks around the Pratt campus, busy with students, with outdoor seating and late closing hours.

Washington Avenue

The north-south spine past Underwood Park, with corner shops, cafes and the Clinton–Washington Avenues subway entrances at Fulton Street.

Vanderbilt and Classon Edges

Small businesses on the western and eastern boundaries, including the Wallabout blocks of Vanderbilt and the stretch of Classon near the 88th Precinct.

Fulton Street: the door, the counter and the stockroom

The Brooklyn Paper reported that three men robbed Fulton Exotic World, a Clinton Hill shop, at gunpoint around 8:20 at night on February 9, took $700 from the register and about $5,500 in products, and ran off down Fulton Street, and that police were counting on the store's camera footage. In 2019 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that the owner of Otway, a Fulton Street restaurant, said her place had been burglarized four times in three years. Those are the two ends of a store's risk: a fast robbery during open hours and a break-in after closing. A camera plan that serves both has a door camera mounted to catch faces as people enter, not the backs of heads as they leave; a counter camera behind and above the staff that shows each customer's face and hands; a register view; and, for after hours, a camera on the back door or the roll-down gate. The recorder goes in a locked spot away from the counter, because a recorder that can be pulled off a shelf by the register leaves nothing behind.

When a store does not want to hand video to police

The 2019 Brooklyn Eagle story quoted the Key Food manager on Fulton Street saying he did not want to report a group of teenagers or turn video over to police. That reluctance is common, and it is the store owner's decision in most cases. A system we install records to a recorder the business owns, and nothing leaves it unless someone with the owner's login exports it. We show managers how to export a clip in its original format, with its time stamp, so that if they do decide to share it, the copy is usable; and we keep the system's remote access limited to the people the owner names. A subpoena or a warrant is a different matter, and that is a question for the business's lawyer, not for us.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Clinton Hill

  • Landmarks: a storefront inside the Clinton Hill or Wallabout historic districts follows the same LPC camera rules as a house, with the larger allowance only where the building qualifies as large or institutional. Cast-iron storefront elements are off limits for fixings.

Field Notes: How We Walk a Split Clinton Hill Brownstone, From the Areaway Gate to the Back Fence

This is the walk-through we do on a brownstone or brick rowhouse here, written in the order we do it, so owners know what to expect before we arrive. It is a method, not a story about any one house.

On the sidewalk. We start across the street, not at the door, and look at the front the way the Landmarks staff will: how wide the frontage is, where the mortar joints and plain stone are, which surfaces are carved or cast iron, and where a camera can sit discreetly. We check the address against the LPC district map. We note where the street trees and the stoop itself block a view, and where the streetlights fall at night.

At the areaway gate. Most daily traffic in a split brownstone goes under the stoop: the garden-floor tenant, deliveries, garbage and recycling, meter readers. We stand where a person would ring the bell and decide where a small camera can see a face at the gate and the garden-floor door together. Often that is the underside of the stoop or the side of the areaway, which keeps it off the primary face of the house.

On the stoop. We look at the parlor door, the vestibule and whatever doorbell or intercom is there now. If the owner wants a video intercom later, we plan for it now, because it may count against the front camera allowance and because its cable shares the same route.

In the cellar. This is where most cable will run. We look at the joists, the old wiring, the gas and water lines to keep clear of, and any signs of water. We trace unknown cables before deciding to reuse or remove them. We look for a dry, safe route to the front and the rear walls.

Through the house. We find the recorder's home: a closet inside the owner's part of the house with power, ventilation and a network connection, out of sight from the street door. We ask who needs to see which cameras, and who should not.

In the garden. We stand on the deck and at the back fence and look back at the house. Back on the sidewalk. We tell you what we found: which cameras need a Landmarks permit, which can go in right away, how many days of recording fits how you live, and what the written quote will include.

Security Camera Installation in Clinton Hill: Questions Answered

Our building on Clinton Avenue was one of the Gold Coast houses and is now divided into apartments. Do we get the larger Landmarks camera allowance, or the rowhouse one?

The LPC permit guidebook sets two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage for small residential or commercial buildings and three per 25 feet for large residential, commercial and institutional buildings. A big Clinton Avenue house divided into several apartments may well count as large, but the guidebook does not give a unit count or size cutoff, so the safe course is to plan for the count you are sure of and ask Landmarks staff about the higher one in the application. Either way, the other rules still apply: cameras spaced out, kept off the primary facade where another spot works, fixed at mortar joints or plain masonry, and finished to blend. On a wide mansion front, we tie each camera to a door that is actually used, and we let side and rear cameras, which are not counted against the street frontage, do the rest.

A Washington Avenue tenant didn't notice watches missing for weeks, and police only then went to the building's cameras. What should an apartment building owner set up so that footage would still be there?

Three things. First, enough storage: our packages include two weeks, and a lobby or vestibule recorder in a building where losses are found late should keep at least two months, which is a $270 upgrade on a four-camera system, or four months for $450. Second, continuous recording on the entrance, lobby and stair cameras, because the person who walks out with a bag at noon on a Tuesday looks ordinary and may not trip a motion alert. Third, a health alert that tells the owner when a drive fails or a camera drops, so the archive is complete when someone asks. It also helps to tell tenants how long footage is kept, so they report a loss before that window closes.

In the Grand Avenue case, a neighbor buzzed in a man dressed as a FedEx courier. How do we make sure the camera identifies whoever gets let in, no matter who pressed the button?

The camera has to watch the moment after the door opens, not just the bell. We place one camera inside the vestibule or lobby, facing the street door at head height from a few feet back, so it records a full face as each person steps in; a camera outside at the bell often sees a cap brim and a parcel. If the building has a video intercom, its own camera covers the person ringing, and the lobby camera covers the person entering, which are not always the same. With continuous recording and the clock kept accurate, the building can match the time a resident buzzed someone in to a clear picture of that person, which is what DNAinfo reported police had in the Grand Avenue case.

Our deck looks over a community garden behind the house, like the Waverly Avenue deck in the 2011 break-in. Can a camera cover the deck without filming the garden and its visitors?

Yes. The camera is mounted on the rear wall, high enough to be out of reach, and aimed down at the deck, the rear door and the stair to your yard. Privacy masking blacks out everything past your fence line, including the community garden and any neighbor's windows, so those areas are never recorded at all. Brownstoner reported in 2011 that the Waverly Avenue burglar came in by the deck and that a man was later seen climbing Hollenback Garden's fence; a deck camera framed this way records anyone who reaches your deck, from whichever direction, without turning your house into a lookout over a shared garden. Masking also helps keep you clear of complaints under New York's backyard camera law.

We rent the garden floor of our brownstone to Pratt students and the tenants change most years. How do we handle camera access when they move in and out?

Give each tenant household its own login with view-only rights to the cameras they share, typically the areaway gate and the street door, and nothing inside the owner's part of the house. When a lease ends, the owner deletes that login from the recorder or the app in a couple of minutes, and the next tenant gets a new one. Never share the owner's password. We set this up at installation and leave written steps, and we put a note in the lease file of which areas are recorded, so a new tenant knows on day one. That is also the time to remind tenants how long footage is kept, so a missing parcel is reported in time.

The robbers at a Fulton Street shop went straight to the counter. If someone takes or smashes our recorder in a robbery, is the footage gone?

It can be, which is why the recorder should never sit at the counter. We put it in a locked cabinet or a back room, on battery backup, with the cables to it run out of reach. For shops that want a second copy, we can set the recorder to push short event clips to the owner's phone or to a cloud service, and if that service has a subscription, the quote names it and says so. The camera on the counter is the one most likely to be knocked off its mount, so it goes high and behind the staff, where it sees faces and hands but is hard to reach.

Our building's entrance faces the Clinton–Washington Avenues subway stairs on Fulton Street. Can our camera cover the station entrance too?

Your camera can record what is visible from your own property, including the public sidewalk in front of your door, but it should be aimed at your entrance, not at the station. News 12 reported that in May 2026 a man was robbed at gunpoint on the Clinton–Washington Avenues platform; that kind of incident falls to the transit system's own cameras and the police. A building camera aimed down the subway stairs would capture mostly strangers at a distance and little that helps your building. We frame the entrance camera on your door and the few feet of sidewalk a person crosses to reach it, which is where a face is large enough to identify.

A thief took parcels from a lobby near Fulton and Washington and drove off in an SUV. Should a lobby system try to catch the car as well?

Sometimes. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that the suspect in that case bit a resident who tried to stop him and fled in a gold BMW SUV. A lobby camera shows the person; it does not show the street. Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Owner, Tenant, Co-op Board, Landmarks or Neighbor: Who Has a Say Over a Clinton Hill Camera

A camera job in Clinton Hill can involve more approvals than the hardware suggests. We sort these out before we quote, because a camera that has to come down later costs more than one that was planned right.

The owner. On a house you own, you decide what goes on the inside and the rear. If you own one condominium unit in a divided mansion or a newer building, the common areas and the outside walls usually belong to the condominium, and the board decides on cameras there.

Tenants. Renters generally need the landlord's permission to put cameras in shared halls, lobbies or on the outside of the building. A camera inside your own apartment, pointed at your own space, is a different matter, though the lease may say what can be fixed to doors and walls. If you rent a floor of a brownstone and want a view of the shared gate, the simplest route is to ask the owner for a view-only login on a system the owner controls.

Co-op boards. In the Clinton Hill Co-ops and other co-op buildings, cameras in common areas go through the board and the managing agent. Boards ask who can view the footage, how long it is kept and who can export it, and we answer those in the proposal.

Landmarks. Inside the Clinton Hill and Wallabout historic districts, exterior cameras on the street side need an LPC permit; staff can issue it when the work follows the guidebook rules described above. We provide the layout and manufacturer cut sheets.

Neighbors. On attached rows with shared garden walls, a rear camera that looks into a neighbor's yard is the most common source of friction. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a resident sue over a camera placed in a way meant to harass, annoy or alarm them in their backyard. We frame rear cameras on your own property and mask out the rest, and we are glad to show a neighbor the masked view if that keeps the peace.

Audio and private spaces. New York's eavesdropping law requires the consent of at least one party to record a conversation, and a sign on the wall does not by itself make audio recording lawful. Our cameras are set with audio off by default. Cameras never go where people undress or use the bathroom.

Licenses. New York requires a state license for businesses that install security systems, under General Business Law section 69-m. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, with insurance in place. A wired camera system is low-voltage work; where a new outlet is needed for the recorder, the quote says so, and it is done by a licensed electrician.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Clinton Hill

Search summaries and directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr answer camera questions for the whole country at once. Here is where those general answers part ways with what a Clinton Hill job actually involves.

"You don't need a permit to install a home security camera."

For most of the country and much of New York City that is true, and no building permit is needed for a typical camera system. But it misses the permit that matters most here. Inside the Clinton Hill Historic District and the Wallabout Historic District, exterior work on the street side of a building falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and its permit guidebook has a specific section on security cameras.

The good news is that LPC staff can approve cameras without a public hearing when the work meets set criteria: two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on small buildings, three on large ones, fixed at mortar joints or plain masonry, small, finished to blend and with almost no visible conduit. That is a routine permit, not an ordeal, if the layout is drawn to the rules from the start.

Where people get into trouble is installing first and asking later, or hiring someone who does not know the rules exist. A bright white bullet camera drilled into a carved brownstone lintel with cable running down the face is exactly what the guidebook says staff cannot approve.

"Wireless cameras are easier and work just as well."

Easier to put up, yes. Working as well is another matter in a brownstone. Wi-Fi signals lose strength passing through brick, stone and old plaster, and the rear garden of a Clinton Hill rowhouse can be several masonry walls and a long yard away from the router at the front of the house.

Battery cameras also tend to record only when they sense motion and then upload clips to a cloud plan, which is the opposite of what the Washington Avenue case calls for: continuous footage, kept locally, for weeks.

A wired camera on its own cable does not drop off when the microwave runs or the neighbor's network gets busy, and it records every minute to a recorder in your house. On a landmarked house, wired installation also lets the cable go straight through the wall behind the camera, which keeps the front clean for the permit.

"Labor to install security cameras runs about $80 to $200 per camera."

Per-camera labor figures like this one, which appears in an NYC intercom vendor's camera guide, lump together a camera stuck on a vinyl-sided garage and a camera on a brownstone where the cable must cross a cellar, rise through a wall and come out through stone. Those are not the same job, and an average tells a Clinton Hill owner little.

"A video doorbell is enough security for a townhouse."

A doorbell watches one door. A Clinton Hill brownstone typically has at least three ways in: the parlor door at the top of the stoop, the garden-floor door under it behind the areaway gate, and the rear door or deck to the garden.

"Cloud storage is safer than a local recorder."

Cloud storage protects against one thing well: someone stealing or smashing the recorder. It brings its own limits: a monthly fee per camera or per plan, a retention period set by the provider, and uploads that depend on your internet connection staying up.

"Security cameras can record audio as long as you post a sign."

New York's eavesdropping law turns on consent, not signs. At least one party to a conversation must consent for it to be recorded, and posting a notice does not by itself make audio recording lawful, as recording-law references for New York point out.

In a co-op lobby, a shared brownstone vestibule or a restaurant, cameras that pick up conversations among people who have not consented create legal risk for the owner.

Carved lintels and cornices are among the features that make these houses worth protecting; a small camera under the stoop or on a plain return wall usually sees the door better anyway.

What makes a Fulton Street shop land at the low end or the high end of the $1,500 to $5,000-plus commercial range?
+

Mainly the number of cameras and the building. A small shop with a door, counter, register and back-door camera and an easy cellar route sits near the low end. A deep restaurant with a dining room, outdoor seating, kitchen door, stockroom and office, long runs above a finished ceiling, a locked equipment cabinet, longer storage and a Landmarks storefront application moves toward the high end. Each of those is a line on the written quote.

Can we start with four cameras now and add more to the same recorder later?
+

Yes, if the recorder is chosen with spare channels. We usually suggest a recorder with room for more cameras than the first phase, and we leave labeled spare cable to likely future spots, such as the vestibule for a video intercom or the rear for a second garden camera, while walls and cellar are open. Adding a camera later is then mostly a matter of mounting it and connecting it, and on a district house any new front camera still has to fit the Landmarks count.
