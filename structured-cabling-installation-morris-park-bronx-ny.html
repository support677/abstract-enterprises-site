The Neighborhood

Why Morris Park Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

In Morris Park the internet usually reaches the house. Getting it from the basement to the rest of the house is the hard part. ISPReports lists two fiber providers covering most of the neighborhood, Optimum at 92.3 percent of homes and Verizon at 84.7 percent. It also reports Bronx-wide advertised top speeds of 8,000 Mbps for Optimum and 2,300 Mbps for Verizon. Those are the speeds a household pays for. What it gets in the far bedroom depends on what lies between the provider's box and that room: a few floors of brick and plaster, a router sitting in a basement corner, and usually decades of telephone wire and coaxial cable that were run for other purposes. Structured cabling replaces that guesswork with a deliberate path: one place where the provider hands off, one closet or cabinet the property controls, and a tested cable to every room, apartment, desk or device that needs one.

The housing explains why the path is rarely simple. Wikipedia describes Morris Park's housing as mostly one- and two-family homes of various styles, with several apartment buildings. ReinventNY's profile breaks that down: semi-detached brick two-families, freestanding single-family homes on modest lots, and pre-war multifamily buildings from the 1920s to the 1940s. It notes rowhouse blocks around Haight Avenue and Colden Avenue with the brick-and-limestone fronts that speculative builders put up for Italian and German immigrant buyers, and larger walk-ups of roughly six to thirty units clustered near Williamsbridge Road and White Plains Road. A broker quoted by Homes.com divides the neighborhood in two: Indian Village to the north, with Tudors on larger lots, and a south side that is more crowded, with more two- and three-family homes. One listing on the same guide sums up the typical house: a brick, semi-detached two-family with a full basement and a stand-up attic. For a cabling crew, that basement and that attic are the two most useful spaces in the house, because they run the full length of the building at both ends of every vertical route.

The age of a house tells us a lot about what is inside its walls. Our own count of New York City's PLUTO building records for the ten census tracts HPD groups as Morris Park, an area that runs a little wider than the neighborhood most residents would draw, found 2,089 two- and three-family lots. Of those, 169 were built before 1920, 437 in the 1920s, 334 in the 1930s, 406 in the 1940s, 475 in the 1950s, 163 in the 1960s, 26 between 1970 and 1989, and 79 since 1990. The same records show about 1,237 one-family homes, 138 walk-up apartment buildings and 25 elevator buildings, most of the elevator buildings dating from the late 1940s to the mid-1960s. In other words, nearly every house in Morris Park was built long before anyone imagined a network, and most were built before cable television. Each was wired for telephones first, then had coax added room by room, and in many cases has since had a router plugged into whichever jack the last installer happened to use.

The neighborhood's history shows how those houses arrived. John Albert Morris opened the Morris Park Racecourse in 1889, and the Belmont Stakes ran there in the 1890s and early 1900s; sources disagree about exactly when racing ended, with dates ranging from 1904 to 1910. Wikipedia also records the Morris Park Aerodrome operating on the old track in 1908 and 1909. After a fire in 1910, the land was divided into lots. The Department of City Planning notes that home building picked up after a streetcar line opened along Morris Park Avenue that same year and continued after the Second World War. Forgotten NY adds that the streets around Seminole, Pawnee and Narragansett Avenues trace back to an 1860s development called Westchester Heights, that houses on many blocks were not built until the 1920s, and that the neighborhood was not really finished until the 1970s. That long, block-by-block buildout is why two houses on the same street can have completely different walls: one may have plaster over brick from the 1920s, while its neighbor has drywall from a 1950s rebuild.

Who pays for the work, and who lives with it, also shapes every job. Wikipedia reports that 65.8 percent of occupied homes in Morris Park are rented, in a neighborhood made up mostly of small houses. Many of those rentals are an apartment in a two-family where the owner lives downstairs, or a unit in a three-family the owner manages without a managing agent. That changes the drop plan. The owner's household and the tenant's household may use different providers. Each needs its own connection and its own private network. The basement is usually shared space with only one route up. And the tenant, who did not choose the contractor, has to be scheduled, told what to expect and respected. We write plans with that in mind: a clear line between the owner's equipment and the tenant's, separate cables to each apartment, and drilling in an occupied unit only when the owner has arranged access.

Morris Park also has a working side that most outsiders miss. A 2018 Department of City Planning presentation on the Bronx Metro-North plan noted that Morris Park had more healthcare jobs than the Bronx as a whole, and listed Jacobi, Bronx Psychiatric Center, Weiler, Montefiore and Calvary hospitals, Albert Einstein College of Medicine and the medical offices at Hutchinson Metro Center. Jacobi, at 1400 Pelham Parkway South, opened in 1955 and has 457 beds. Calvary Hospital at 1740 Eastchester Road is a hospice and palliative-care hospital founded in 1899. Hutchinson Metro Center's Tower Two on Waters Place is a 280,000-square-foot medical office building fully leased to Montefiore. Around those campuses are smaller practices and offices in converted houses and low professional buildings; PLUTO lists eighteen lots in the area as professional buildings. Meanwhile, ReinventNY calls Morris Park Avenue the neighborhood's commercial spine of bakeries, butchers and social clubs. Each of these places needs wired connections for registers, front-desk computers, imaging equipment, printers, phones and cameras, usually in buildings that were never designed to carry them.

There is also new construction on the way. In August 2024 the City Council approved the Bronx Metro-North rezoning around four planned commuter rail stations, which reporting at the time said could allow roughly 7,000 new apartments. Council changes lowered the density and height limits near the Morris Park station, and amNY reported that two areas in eastern Morris Park were dropped from the plan to keep their suburban character. Simone Development describes a 33-acre expansion of the 42-acre Hutchinson Metro Center campus next to the planned station, possibly including life science labs, medical offices and retail. The station's opening date keeps moving. The MTA told Community Board 11 that limited service might start by 2027 using temporary platforms, THE CITY reported in May 2026 that Amtrak had raised the possibility of early 2029, and NY1 reported that full service to Penn Station could take until 2030. Whatever the date, every new building in that area will need a telecom room, risers and cable to every unit and office, and they cost far less to plan before construction than to add afterward.

Where this page describes walls, basements and closets, it is drawing on the building ages and types in the public record and on what we generally find in buildings of those kinds, not on inspections of any particular house.

  • Old houses, mostly brick: of 2,089 two- and three-family lots in HPD's Morris Park tracts, about 1,821 were built before 1960, by our reading of PLUTO, and listings describe brick semi-detached houses with full basements and stand-up attics.
  • Two households under one roof: 65.8 percent of occupied homes are rented, per Wikipedia, largely in owner-occupied two- and three-family houses, so separate networks per apartment are routine.

What Morris Park owners tend to add next. Once a house has a closet and tested cable to each floor, the next conversation is usually about the outside: a camera on the driveway or side door, a video doorbell that is wired instead of running on a battery, or a camera recorder that should sit in the closet instead of on a shelf. Those devices use the same pathways, so we ask about them at the walk-through and leave room in the drop count, even if the cameras are a year away.

Two Providers at the Curb

Optimum or Verizon, or Both: Where a Morris Park Network Should Start

In many Bronx neighborhoods, the provider decides the building's wiring because there is only one choice. Morris Park is different. With fiber from Optimum covering 92.3 percent of homes and Verizon covering 84.7 percent, according to ISPReports, a large share of households can choose between them and can switch later. That makes one decision more important than it first looks: where the house's own network begins. If it begins wherever the first provider's technician put the box, the second provider's technician will put a box somewhere else, and the house ends up with two half-networks and a router in the wrong place. If it begins at a closet or cabinet chosen for the house, both providers simply bring their lines to that point. Here are the arrangements we see, and how each one ages.

Choose the closet for the house, not for the installer

A provider's technician has one job: get service working to the account they were sent to. That usually means putting the box close to where the line enters, often on the front basement wall or in a ground-floor room beside the window where the line comes through. That spot is rarely the best place for a whole-house network. We pick the closet by looking at the whole building. Which spot in the basement has the shortest, cleanest routes up to each floor? Where does a stand-up attic give access to the top-floor rooms? Where can a lockable cabinet sit off the floor, away from the boiler, the water heater and any part of the basement that gets damp? Morris Park is low-lying and flat, and when a basement takes water in a hard rain, that matters. In an owner-occupied two-family, the closet also needs to be somewhere the owner can reach without going through the tenant's space. Once the closet location is settled, we run a single cable from each provider's handoff to the closet, and the provider's box can stay wherever its technician needs it.

Leave room for the provider you do not have yet

Nearly every Morris Park house we would wire today has one provider. Many could have the other within a few days. A plan that assumes the house will never switch leaves the next provider's installer to improvise. We leave a labeled spare cable or a pull string from the most likely second entry point to the closet, space and a power outlet in the cabinet for a second provider box, and a router that can take a second internet line if the owner wants one later. None of that costs much during the original job. It means the house can change providers or add one, and the only work is the provider connecting at a labeled point, not a new hole through the brick.

Separate apartments, separate providers, one shared basement

The most common Morris Park layout we plan for is an owner-occupied two-family, owner on one floor and tenant on the other, sometimes with a finished basement in use as well. Each household usually has its own account, and sometimes its own provider. The cabling has to respect that. Each apartment gets its own cable from the basement, ending in its own small panel or jack field inside the apartment, so each household's router lives in its own home and neither can see the other's devices. In the basement, the two sets of cable are labeled by apartment and kept apart, and any shared equipment, such as a camera recorder or a house-wide switch for the outside cameras, sits in a cabinet only the owner can open. If a third household lives in the building, as in the three-families Homes.com describes on the south side, we repeat the pattern for each one and pay particular attention to how cable reaches the top floor without going through anyone else's apartment.

What fast plans ask of the cable

ISPReports lists Bronx-wide advertised top speeds of 8,000 Mbps from Optimum and 2,300 Mbps from Verizon. Few households buy the top tier, but the trend in provider plans only runs one way. The cable in a wall stays there for decades. Cat6A is rated to carry 10-gigabit Ethernet over a full-length run, while Cat6 handles it only over shorter distances. In a Morris Park house, where most runs from the basement to the farthest room are short, either can be a sensible choice, and the drop plan names the category for each run and explains why. What matters more is that the run is a real, continuous cable terminated properly at both ends and tested, not a jack screwed onto a piece of telephone wire from the 1950s.

Find both entry points before anything else

Walk the outside of the house once and the inside of the basement once, and photograph every place a cable comes through the wall. In Morris Park, that might mean a gray box beside the front steps, a black line coming down the side of the house from a pole, a thin fiber drop clipped along the rear wall, or a bundle of old coax entering through a basement window frame. Each of those is a clue to which providers have served the house and where they ended up. If the current provider's box is inside, photograph it with its lights visible. Those few pictures tell us where the network has to start and whether a second entry point already exists.

Write the plan by room, not by total

For a house, walk each room and note what plugs in now and what you would wire if you could: the TV and streaming box, a home office desk, a gaming console, a printer, a doorbell or a camera. For an office, list each workstation, the front desk, any imaging or lab equipment, phones and printers, by room. Write it down by location, not as a total. Price follows the route, and a jack in the front bedroom on the top floor of a semi-detached house costs more to reach than one in the room directly above the basement closet.

For practices and offices, name who supports the computers

Most small practices and offices around the hospital blocks rely on an outside IT company, a software vendor, or a family member who knows computers. Tell us who that is. We run and certify the cable, build the closet and connect the provider handoff; the person who manages the computers often has views on switches, firewalls and where the phones connect. Bringing them in early avoids the familiar problem of a closet built one way and configured another.

For conversions and new buildings, send drawings first

If you are turning a one-family into a legal two-family, adding a floor or building new on a lot in the rezoned blocks, we would much rather read the plans before the framers start. Drawings show where walls and stairs will be, where an electrical room can hold a telecom cabinet, and which floors and walls carry a fire rating. From those we can mark sleeves, chases and the closet location and send them back to the architect and contractor while the changes are still lines on paper.

1920s brick-and-limestone rows: work from the middle of the house

In the attached and semi-detached brick houses of the 1920s, the outside walls and the party walls are masonry, often with plaster laid directly on the brick or on furring strips close to it. Fishing a cable down an outside wall in a house like that is rarely possible without opening it. The interior partitions are a different story. The walls between rooms, the wall beside the stair and the backs of closets are usually wood studs with plaster on lath, and many line up from floor to floor. So we work from the middle of the house outward. Cable rises from the basement through an interior wall or a stacked closet, reaches the attic if there is one, and drops from above into the top-floor rooms. Where a jack has to be on a masonry wall, we bring the cable across the floor or ceiling to a box on the nearest interior wall or into a low-profile surface raceway painted to match, and we show the owner the options before drilling. Limestone and brick on the front of the house stay untouched; any cable from outside comes in through the basement.

Postwar brick two-families: the basement and the attic do the work

The houses Homes.com's listing describes, brick and semi-detached with a full basement and a stand-up attic, are about the most cooperative older houses in the Bronx to cable. The basement ceiling lets us run horizontally under the entire first floor. The stand-up attic does the same above the top floor. Between them, a single vertical route, usually an interior wall, a closet stack or a chase beside the plumbing, connects the two, and from those three spaces we can reach nearly every room. The extra complication is the second household. We plan one route for the owner's floor and a separate one for the tenant's, each ending in its own apartment, and we agree with both households before drilling through a floor between them. Where the attic belongs to the top-floor apartment, we arrange access with the tenant and finish attic work in one visit.

Colonials and Tudors: longer runs and a yard to cross

The single-family homes on the larger lots, which Homes.com describes as colonial-inspired homes and semi-detached Tudors, usually have more rooms per floor and a room or two farther from the basement closet than any two-family does. Runs are longer, and the wall framing varies more, because many of these houses have been extended or remodeled. Tudors often have steep roofs with knee walls on the top floor, irregular ceilings and decorative plaster or wood trim in the main rooms. Walk-ups near Williamsbridge Road and White Plains Road: one riser, many households

In a 1930s walk-up of six to thirty units, every provider that has served the building has had to find a path from the basement to each apartment, and usually each one made its own. The vertical route is the key decision. The elevator buildings PLUTO lists in and around Morris Park, mostly from the late 1940s to the mid-1960s, are taller and have longer horizontal runs on each floor. The front room becomes a reception desk with several devices. Rules and limits for business cabling in Morris Park

  • The low-voltage permit exemption. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, provides that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A Bronx hiring guide describes it as effective December 21, 2025, and says it applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Another source we read says low-voltage work still needs DOB permits, so on commercial jobs we confirm the filing requirement for that specific job before starting.
  • Cameras and alarms carry their own license. The same guide says that for cameras, intrusion detection and multi-door access, the installer's Article 6-D security license, or a master electrician's registered waiver, should be confirmed with the New York Department of State.
  • Fire alarm is a separate trade. Fire alarm wiring is outside the data exemption and requires its own licensing and filings. We keep it out of data jobs.
  • Power belongs to the electrician. Where a cabinet, recorder or access point needs a new outlet or circuit, a licensed electrician does that work, and our quote says so.
  • Cable jackets must match the space. Plenum-rated cable in air-handling ceilings, at least riser-rated cable between floors, and outdoor-rated cable wherever a run leaves the building.
  • Seal every opening. Every hole through a rated wall or floor is sealed with a listed firestop product, left visible and photographed for the owner's records.
  • Get approval in writing. In a leased store or suite, the lease controls alterations. We ask for the landlord's written approval, and any insurance certificate they require, before the visit is booked.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Trace Both Fiber Lines to the Wall Before Choosing a Closet

We do not tell stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not about one address. It is the routine a technician follows in the basement of a brick two-family, which is where most Morris Park jobs begin.

We start outside. We note every line that reaches the house, from the street side, from a pole at the rear, along the side wall, and we photograph each one where it enters. Then we go downstairs with the owner and find each of those lines on the inside. One might end at a provider box with its lights on. Another might end at an old splitter feeding coax to rooms nobody uses anymore. A third might be an abandoned telephone block with wire running off in four directions. We put a tone on anything we cannot identify and follow it. Next we look up at the basement ceiling to see which way the joists run, where the plumbing stacks come through and where an interior wall above lines up with open joist space below, because that wall is usually the route up. We check for damp: water stains, a sump, or anything stored on pallets. We ask which parts of the basement belong to whom, whether a tenant uses it, whether there is a finished room, and who holds the keys. Only after every incoming line has been accounted for, and the owner has agreed on where the closet should go, do we start measuring routes. That order matters, because a closet chosen before the lines are traced usually ends up in the wrong place.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Morris Park: Questions Answered

Houses with a choice of fiber provider

We can get fiber from either Optimum or Verizon. How do we wire the house so switching providers later never means new cable inside?

Separate the provider's equipment from the house's network, and keep the house's network in one place. In practice that means a closet or cabinet, usually in the basement, where every room's cable ends on a labeled panel, and one tested cable from wherever the current provider's box sits to that closet. The provider's equipment hands service to the router in the closet, and the router feeds the rooms. When you switch, the new provider's technician brings the line to their box, connects it to the closet with that one cable, and nothing in the walls changes. Since ISPReports puts Optimum fiber at 92.3 percent and Verizon fiber at 84.7 percent of Morris Park homes, it is worth leaving a labeled spare cable or pull string from the second provider's most likely entry point to the closet as well. It costs little during the original job and saves a new hole later.

Can we keep both Optimum and Verizon connected, so one takes over if the other goes down?

Yes, and in a house with a home office or a practice that cannot be offline, it is a reasonable setup. Both providers bring their lines into the same closet, each is connected to a router that accepts two internet connections, and the router switches to the backup line when the main one fails. The cabling part is simple: one tested cable from each provider's box to the closet, and enough power and space in the cabinet for both boxes and the router, ideally with a battery backup. The ongoing cost is a second monthly bill, which is a choice for the household rather than something the cable requires. If you do not want to pay for two providers now, we can wire the closet for it and leave the second line ready to connect.

We are dropping our landline when the fiber goes in, and our older alarm panel reports over that phone line. What wiring has to change?

Check this before the landline is cancelled. Many older alarm panels use the telephone line to send signals to the monitoring station, and when the line disappears the panel can no longer report, even though the keypad still looks normal. The fix depends on the panel: some can take a cellular or internet communicator, and some are old enough that replacing the panel makes more sense. From the cabling side, the alarm panel needs either a network cable from the closet or good cellular reception where it is mounted, and the old phone wire to the panel should be identified and labeled rather than left as a mystery. Alarm work falls under its own license, which we hold separately from the data work. Ask your monitoring company what your panel needs, and tell us at the walk-through so the closet plan includes it.

Can the old phone wire inside our walls be used to pull the new network cable, so the plaster stays closed?

Sometimes, and when it works it is one of the neatest ways to wire an older Morris Park house. If the old telephone wire runs freely through the wall and is not stapled to the studs along the way, we can attach the new cable to it at one end and draw it through as the old wire comes out. Whether that works depends on how the wire was installed. Wire stapled to the framing, run through tight holes or buried in plaster will snap or jam, and pulling harder only risks cracking the plaster around the old box. We test each run before relying on it, and where it fails we fall back on fishing a new route from the basement or the attic. The old wire itself is not reused for data; it serves only as a pull line. Every new run is certified the same way.

Buying, converting and planning around construction

Before we close on a Morris Park house, what can we look at to tell whether its network wiring is worth keeping?

A few things can be checked in a normal showing or inspection without opening anything. Look at the faceplates: real network jacks have eight-pin ports and usually say the category somewhere, while old phone jacks are narrower and coax outlets have a screw connector. In the basement, see whether the wires from those jacks come back to one place, such as a panel, a cabinet or a labeled bundle, or end at scattered splitters and junction blocks. A single clean termination point is a good sign. Look for any labels or a printed map. Ask the sellers which provider they use and where its box is. What you cannot tell from looking is whether the cable actually performs. That takes a tester. If the house has real network wiring, a short visit after closing to test and label each run tells you exactly what you have, and if it fails, the drop plan starts from that information.

We are converting our one-family into a legal two-family. At what stage should the second apartment's cabling be planned?

At the drawing stage, before the framing goes up. A conversion usually adds walls, sometimes moves the stair, and creates a separation between the two apartments that must keep its fire rating. All of that affects where cable can travel and where each apartment's network can start. If the cabling is on the drawings, we can mark a route for each apartment from the basement, a sleeve wherever cable crosses the new rated separation, and a spot in each apartment for its own small panel, and the framers leave those spaces open. If it is added after the walls are closed, every one of those routes has to be fished or opened, and penetrations through the new separation have to be cut and sealed afterward. Send us the architect's set as early as you have it, even if it is preliminary.

MTA construction is running along Sackett Avenue into January 2027. Should we hold off on cabling until it is finished?

In most cases, no. The MTA's Penn Station Access project page lists construction along Sackett Avenue from August 2026 to January 2027 and bridge work on Eastchester Road in September and October 2026, but cabling happens inside your house or building, so outdoor construction rarely affects it. What construction can affect is access: parking, street closures and the occasional utility interruption. Tell us about any closure on your block when you book, and we will plan the day around it. If a provider needs to bring a new line to your house from the street, its schedule may be affected by work in the area, and that is between you and the provider. The inside wiring can be done and tested now, ready for whenever the provider connects.

Almost everything about the connection count. Our dental practice is adding another treatment room. What cabling does a new operatory need?

Plan it around the chair. A typical treatment room needs a drop at the chairside computer or monitor, one for any intraoral camera or imaging sensor station that connects over the network, one near the doorway for a wall-mounted screen or a scheduling device, and often a spare. Where the chair sits away from a wall, the cable has to reach it through the floor or ceiling, so we confirm the chair layout with the equipment vendor before choosing the route. The equipment vendor and your IT support will tell you which devices connect to the network and which use their own cables; we run and certify the network drops, label them by room and port, and bring them back to the panel where the rest of the practice connects. If the room is being built new, the drops go in before the walls close.

The Bronx Times reported in 2019 that the DA's program, run with the borough's business improvement districts, had nearly 1,000 cameras enrolled. We do not know its current terms, so ask the Morris Park BID what enrollment involves today. From the cabling side, a recorder that may be asked for footage should be in a locked cabinet, not on a shelf behind the counter. It should connect to each camera by its own PoE cable rather than over Wi-Fi, sit on battery backup so a power dip does not stop recording, and have its own network segment, apart from the register and guest Wi-Fi. If remote access is involved, it should come through the shop's router with secure logins in the owner's name. Those are good practice whether or not the store joins.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Morris Park

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask results for this neighborhood from our build environment, so we are not quoting them. Below are claims that come up repeatedly in quick online answers about home and small-business cabling, checked against the public record for Morris Park and our own practice. Many of those answers draw on national cost guides from marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Those guides give a rough sense of scale, but none of them has walked through a Morris Park two-family.

"If fiber is available at your address, the whole house gets fiber speed."

Fiber availability describes the street and the line to your house, not the rooms inside it. ISPReports says two providers offer fiber to most Morris Park homes, but the provider's line ends at a box, usually in the basement or a front room.

From there, the signal reaches the rest of the house over whatever is in place: Wi-Fi from a router fighting through brick and plaster, or old telephone and coax wiring never meant for data.

A tested cable from a closet to each room is what carries that speed to where it gets used. Without it, a multi-gig plan often ends up as an expensive connection to the basement.

"A mesh Wi-Fi kit is all a brick house needs."

Mesh kits help, and in a small apartment they can be enough. In a Morris Park house with brick walls, plaster on lath and two or three floors, each wireless hop between mesh units costs speed, and the units still need power and good placement.

Mesh works far better when each access point has its own cable back to the closet, so the units talk over a wire instead of through the walls. That is a cabling job.

Some things should never be on Wi-Fi at all: a home office desk, a gaming console, a camera recorder, a practice's front-desk computer. Each gets its own cable.

"Your internet provider will wire your rooms for you."

A provider's installation brings service to the account and normally ends at its box or router. Some technicians will run a single extra line as an add-on, but designing a network for a whole house, or keeping two apartments separate, is not their job.

In a two-family, the provider serves one household at a time. A tenant's provider may drill its own path through the owner's floor because no route to the upstairs apartment exists.

The owner who builds that route once, with a labeled cable to each apartment, decides how every future provider connects.

"Morris Park is all houses, so cabling here just means home offices."

Houses dominate, but they are not the whole picture. Our reading of PLUTO for HPD's Morris Park tracts shows about 138 walk-ups and 25 elevator buildings, and City Planning's 2018 presentation lists five hospitals, a medical school and the Hutchinson Metro Center medical offices in and around the neighborhood.

Morris Park Avenue has about 152 commercial and mixed-use properties in its BID district, and Williamsbridge Road and Eastchester Road have practices in converted houses and low office buildings.

Each of those needs a different approach: risers for apartment buildings, segmented networks for practices, locked cabinets for shops. A home-office answer covers only part of the neighborhood.

"Data wiring needs no permit now, so anyone can do it."

Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, removes the electrical permit for wiring below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. That changes the paperwork, not who may do the work.

The Bronx hiring guide we read says the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person, and another source says DOB permits still apply to some low-voltage work. That disagreement is a good reason to confirm the requirements for a commercial job before starting. Fire alarm wiring is a separate trade with its own licensing.

The practical test for an owner: ask who is doing the work, under what license, and whether any of it touches fire alarm or life-safety systems. Our low-voltage work is done under NYS license #12000287431.

"A national per-drop average tells you what your job will cost."

National averages blend open-ceiling offices with century-old houses. One NYC cost guide we read says a pre-war building with long, difficult runs can exceed the top of its range. Most Morris Park two-families are exactly that kind of building, even if the runs are short.

The same five drops might take an hour in a 1990s house with hollow walls and most of a day in a 1920s brick row where every outside wall is masonry and the attic is reachable only through a tenant's apartment.

That is why we price from a walk-through and a written drop plan. A calculator gives you a guess. A plan gives you a number you can hold us to.

"Wait for the new station; buildings near it will come pre-wired anyway."

New buildings in the rezoned blocks will have telecom rooms and cable, but what they get depends on what the drawings specify, and the opening date keeps moving. Reports range from limited service by 2027 to early 2029 to full service in 2030.

How to read a Morris Park drop plan

Picture an owner of a semi-detached brick two-family who lives on the first floor and rents the second. The plan would list a lockable cabinet in the basement with a small panel and battery backup; one cable from the current provider's box to the cabinet; an optional spare cable from the second provider's likely entry point; a route up an interior wall to the owner's apartment, with drops to the living room, a home office and a bedroom; a separate route to the tenant's apartment ending in its own small panel; a drop to the attic for a future access point; PoE cable to a driveway camera position; tracing and labeling of the old phone and coax wiring; optional removal of dead cable; and certification with a handover file. FAQ

Morris Park Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

If we cable two of our buildings on the same block in one project, does that bring the price down?
+

It can reduce some costs, but we do not promise a percentage. Doing both in one project means one round of planning, one material order and a crew that stays nearby instead of mobilizing twice, and those savings show up in the drop plan. Each building is still priced by its own walls, routes and drop count, so a hard building does not become cheap just because it is next door to an easy one. Ask for both on one quote and the plan will show each building on its own lines.

How do you put a price on a Morris Park cabling job before any wall is opened?
+

From the walk-through. We trace where each provider line enters, look at the basement ceiling and the attic, check which interior walls line up from floor to floor, and note what each wall is made of. Each drop then gets a route and a method, such as a fished wall, a stacked closet, a raceway or the attic, and a price based on that method. If something hidden changes the route once work starts, we stop and agree on the change in writing before going ahead.

Our semi-detached house shares a wall with the house next door. How do you make sure a drill never comes through on the neighbor's side?
+

We avoid drilling into the shared wall altogether. Routes in a semi-detached house run through interior partitions, closets, the basement and the attic, all of which belong only to your house. Where a jack has to sit on the shared wall, we use a shallow surface box or raceway instead of boring into the masonry. We identify the shared wall on the walk-through and mark it on the drop plan so every crew member knows where it is.
