Why NYC Properties Need Luminys Camera Installation

Most New York City camera systems were not designed. They accumulated. A lobby camera went in after a break-in, two more after a package theft, a recorder in the basement was replaced when the old one died, and somewhere along the way a super added a camera on the back door with whatever cable was in the van. For a decade one of the most common names on those recorders, especially in small apartment buildings and storefronts, was Dahua. That history is the reason Luminys matters here. According to the distributor Selectum, Luminys Systems was established following the sale and rebranding of Dahua USA's North American business. The Register reported in April 2024 that Dahua, which is on the United States entity list, sold its US subsidiary to Taiwan's Central Motion Picture Corporation for a reported fifteen million dollars, and that the emails sent to dealers were signed by Luminys System Corp. and Foxlink, a larger Taiwanese manufacturer with ties to former Foxconn president Terry Gou. For a New York owner with a Dahua recorder in the basement, that sale created a new question: what happens to my system, and what do I replace it with?

The second reason is compliance. ADI Global Distribution, one of the largest security distributors in the country, describes Luminys as offering NDAA-compliant cameras, software and kits built for integrators. NDAA compliance matters to buyers who deal with federal money, federal contracts or institutions that have adopted the same standard, and it matters increasingly to boards and managing agents who simply do not want to explain a brand's ownership at a shareholder meeting. The road has not been entirely smooth. SecurityInfoWatch reported that the FCC questioned Luminys' equipment approvals in 2025, then cleared them, withdrawing its earlier action and terminating the proceeding in an order released January 20. We mention that history because buyers ask about it, and because an honest installer should tell you where a brand came from before asking you to put it on your building.

The third reason is the buildings themselves. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council counts roughly 3.7 million housing units in the city and says more than 80 percent of them are in buildings over fifty years old, with about two million units built before 1947. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 housing supply report puts the share of units in pre-1947 buildings at 54 percent. Those are the 4- to 6-story walk-up tenements, the 8- to 12-story elevator buildings of the 1910s and 1920s and the big doorman buildings of the 1920s and 1930s, many with masonry walls that one renovation source puts at 12 to 18 inches thick. Walls like that stop WiFi and slow down every cable pull. That is why most New York camera work is a wiring job first: drops pulled up the interior riser, rated penetrations fire-stopped, and, in elevator buildings, the cab camera wired through the elevator's traveling cable. The camera brand sits on top of all of that, and Luminys' own technical wiki makes the same point from the other direction when it says that for many businesses the biggest obstacle to upgrading an analog system is not the cameras, it is the wire.

The fourth reason is what keeps happening in lobbies. CBS New York reported a package thief in an Astoria lobby who disabled the door lock with a screwdriver and took deliveries worth more than four hundred dollars, and three suspects who cut open packages inside a Park Slope lobby. Security.org's package theft survey found apartment residents more than three times as likely as other households to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent. A property manager quoted by Brick Underground pointed out how face coverings make people on camera hard to identify. Those reports point at the same practical need: a camera placed where faces actually appear, recording at enough resolution to be useful, and footage the building can find and export the morning after. That is a design question more than a brand question, and it is where most of our time on a Luminys job goes.

Finally, there is a timing reason this week. PR Newswire announced that Luminys is exhibiting at ISC East 2026, held November 4 and 5 at the Javits Center on the West Side of Manhattan. ISC East is a security industry trade show, and many of the people who manage cameras for New York buildings and businesses walk its floor. If you saw Luminys equipment there and want to know how it would fit your building, the rest of this page is written for you.

We want to be plain about what our research could not find. We searched for Reddit threads, Yelp reviews and neighborhood news about Luminys installations in New York City and found none; the only user discussions of Luminys we found were on the IPCamTalk forum, which is national. We did not query HPD violation data, 311 complaints or Department of Buildings permits for camera work. Where this page describes how buildings are wired, it draws on the building stock in the public record and on what we find in buildings of that age, not on a survey of New York camera systems.

Start with the recorder, not the cameras

In most small New York buildings the recorder is the part that fails first, because it lives in a basement closet or under a super's desk with poor ventilation, it runs a hard drive around the clock, and it rides through every power dip. The June 2025 heat wave showed how common those dips are: NY1 reported 10,019 Con Edison customers without power on the evening of June 25, 6,767 of them in Brooklyn and 3,016 in Queens, and noted that one customer can be an entire building. A recorder that has been through several summers like that may still show live video while quietly failing to save it. So the first thing we do on a Dahua-to-Luminys job is look at the recorder: its model, its drive health, how many days it actually holds, and whether it is recording at all. If it has failed or is close to it, replacing it with a Luminys NVR and keeping the cameras that test well is often the most sensible first step.

Then test every camera you want to keep

Keeping a camera is only worth it if the picture is still good. Old cameras in New York take a beating from salt air near the water, soot on busy avenues, heat in summer stairwells and water in places the building envelope leaks. We look at each one in daylight and after dark, check whether its infrared still lights the scene, look for fogging inside the dome and condensation behind the lens, and confirm it connects to the new recorder. Some will pass and stay. Some will connect but look poor at night, and those are the ones a client usually decides to replace with a Luminys camera once they see the two pictures side by side on the same screen.

Then decide about the wire

The wire is where New York jobs are won or lost. A run of decent network cable already pulled up a riser and through a lobby ceiling is valuable, because pulling a new one through prewar masonry can take longer than mounting every camera in the building. We trace each run with a tone generator, identify both ends, test it, and label it. Runs that pass stay in service. Runs that fail, or that turn out to be old coaxial cable from an analog system, go on a separate list with the options for each. Luminys' own wiki acknowledges that for many businesses the wire is the biggest obstacle to upgrading an analog system, and we would rather spend an hour finding that out than promise a clean swap that turns into an open ceiling.

What we will not do in a migration

We will not throw away a working recorder before the new one is recording and the building has its footage. We will not mix firmware between brands. We will not leave default passwords on the old cameras because they were "already set up that way". And we will not tell you a camera is compatible because a forum post said so; we will show you it working on your recorder, or show you that it does not.

Why the borough changes the price, not just the drive

A Manhattan lobby job and a Staten Island driveway job can use exactly the same Luminys camera and recorder. What changes is the time it takes to get the cable from one to the other. In Manhattan that time goes into service elevator bookings, building hours, protective coverings in finished corridors, and parking, which in much of the borough means a garage. On Staten Island it goes into longer exterior runs, trenching or aerial runs to a garage, and mounting heights on wood-frame houses. Brooklyn and the Bronx sit in between and are our base rate. That is why our equipment prices stay the same from borough to borough while the labor portion of each quote follows the borough, and why we publish each borough's list separately rather than one number for "New York."

Portfolios with buildings in more than one borough

For a managing agent with a walk-up in the Bronx, a mixed-use building in Queens and a brownstone conversion in Brooklyn, the useful thing about a single brand is not the camera. It is having one viewing app, one set of user accounts and one way of exporting footage when a tenant reports a stolen package. Luminys' FAQ for LumiCenter, its management software, says it carries no annual maintenance fees and supports native camera profiles, generic manufacturer profiles and ONVIF. In practice that means a portfolio can be brought under one screen over time, starting with the building whose recorder is failing first, without replacing every working camera in the others on the same day. Building Specialists

Luminys Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

New York packs nearly every building type in the country into five boroughs, and each one changes three things about a Luminys job: where the cable can travel, what the camera is fixed to, and who has to agree before anyone drills. These are the types that make up most of the city's stock and how we handle each.

Prewar Walk-Up Tenements

Four to six stories, built before 1929 under the older multiple dwelling laws. Thick masonry, a narrow stair, often a cellar with the electrical service and the old recorder. Cable rides the stair hall, an old dumbwaiter shaft or a riser.

Prewar Elevator Buildings

Eight to twelve stories from the 1910s and 1920s. Larger lobbies, a basement or mezzanine equipment room, and an elevator cab whose camera has to be wired through the traveling cable rather than WiFi.

Doorman and Co-op Buildings

The big buildings of the 1920s and 1930s, many now co-ops. Alteration rules, a board or managing agent, a certificate of insurance and set working hours. The hallway belongs to the corporation, not the shareholder.

Brownstones and Row Houses

Masonry fronts, often in a historic district, with stoops, areaways and rear yards. Cameras usually go off the street facade, with brick and brownstone drilled carefully and cable concealed inside.

One- and Two-Family Houses

The dominant type on Staten Island and in large parts of Queens and the outer Bronx. Wood frame or brick, driveways, detached garages and yards, and the longest exterior runs in the city.

Large Complexes and New Towers

Planned complexes like Parkchester and Co-op City, public housing developments and new glass towers. Concrete construction, existing camera systems to integrate with, and building engineers who set the rules.

Walk-ups: the riser is the job

In a five-story walk-up, a camera on each landing, one on the roof door, one in the rear yard and two in the lobby can easily mean ten runs to a recorder in the cellar. The question is where they travel. Many of these buildings have no vertical chase except the stair itself, so the cleanest route is often a painted raceway tucked into the stair molding, or an old dumbwaiter or chimney chase if the building has one that is no longer in use and safe to share. Where a run passes through a floor or a rated wall, the penetration is fire-stopped afterward, because an open hole between floors in a walk-up is exactly how smoke reaches the stairs. We bring a core drill and masonry bits on every walk-up job, and we place anchors in the face of the brick, not in the mortar joints, wherever a camera's mounting pattern allows.

Elevator buildings: the cab and the equipment room

An elevator cab camera is one of the most requested and most misunderstood pieces of a New York building system. The cab moves, so the camera's connection rides the elevator's traveling cable, the bundle that hangs from the cab to the hoistway wall. That work is coordinated with the building's elevator maintenance contractor, who controls access to the hoistway and the car top. We do not run a WiFi camera in a steel cab and hope; the cab is close to a Faraday cage. In the basement or mezzanine, prewar elevator buildings usually have a room where a rack can live with ventilation and a battery backup, which is the right home for a Luminys NVR.

Co-ops: the board owns the hallway

Connextivity's guide to New York apartment camera rules makes a point boards understand well: in a co-op, the hallway is common area controlled by the corporation, so an individual shareholder cannot install hardware there on their own. Lobby, hall, elevator and mailroom cameras in a co-op are a board project. The Cooperator has described how alterations begin with a request to the board and an alteration agreement, how boards regulate whether contractors may cut through walls, and how they ask for proof of insurance. Many doorman buildings also restrict contractor hours to weekdays and require the service elevator to be booked ahead. We work inside those rules and send the paperwork before the visit, not on the day.

Brownstones: off the front where you can

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook has a section on installing security cameras. It says staff can approve cameras that meet set criteria, that cameras belong off the primary street facade unless there is no other option, and that dome cameras should be finished to match the building. The Commission's 2018 rules wrote that staff practice down for equipment on facades and areaways, from intercoms to security cameras. For a brownstone in a historic district, that usually means a camera under the stoop, at the areaway gate, in a cornice return or at the rear, with cable running inside the house rather than across the brownstone. We take the photos and measurements an application needs and wait for the permit before mounting anything on a landmark facade.

Houses and large complexes

Houses on Staten Island and in eastern Queens are the most straightforward wiring in the city, with attics and basements to hide cable, but they bring the longest outdoor runs: a detached garage, a driveway gate, a back fence along an alley. Those runs need outdoor-rated cable, protection where they cross the yard, and surge protection where they enter the house. Large complexes and towers are the opposite. The wiring is in conduit and telecom rooms already, the building often has an existing camera system and a security office, and our job is to fit Luminys equipment into that infrastructure under the building engineer's rules.

Pair cameras with the front door. Most New York apartment buildings that add Luminys cameras to the lobby end up asking about the entry next. If the building uses fobs, cards, an app or biometrics to get in, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law governs that entry data, including destroying or anonymizing authentication data within 90 days. One intercom vendor says the law does not reach ordinary camera feeds that are not used for entry; we flag the question for the owner's counsel whenever a camera is meant to work alongside a smart entry system.

The Downtown Jamaica Partnership district around Jamaica Avenue, with retail, offices and transit traffic. Rear entrances and alleys matter as much as the storefront.

Pitkin Avenue, Brooklyn and Forest Avenue, Staten Island

Two more SBS-listed districts: Pitkin's long retail strip in Brownsville and Forest Avenue's mix of stores and services on the North Shore.

What a storefront camera has to do

A store camera has three jobs that a lobby camera does not. It has to show the face of the person at the counter clearly enough to identify them, which means a camera close to the register at head height, not a wide lens in the corner of the ceiling. It has to show the door, ideally both from inside, catching faces as people enter, and from outside, catching the sidewalk. And it has to hold up after closing, when the roll-down gate is down and the only light is the street. For most small retailers we plan the system camera by camera with the owner, write down which question each camera answers, and only then choose Luminys models and lens widths to suit.

Restaurants, warehouses and garages

Restaurants want the register and the back door, the cellar delivery hatch on the sidewalk, and the kitchen entrance, with cameras that survive grease and steam. Warehouses and garages want loading docks and gates, often with plate readers, and long cable runs through conduit where vehicles move. Luminys' physical installation guide for its license-plate cameras is specific: the angle between the lens and the edge of the farthest lane cannot exceed 30 degrees. In a narrow New York garage ramp, that rule usually decides where the camera goes before anything else does.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • State licensing. New York requires a Department of State security and fire alarm installer license to install CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft, movement, sound or fire. Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum notes that employees of a licensed company need Department of State ID cards, and that master electricians register rather than sit the full licensing requirements. Our license is NYS #12000287431.
  • Audio is a separate decision. Many cameras can record sound. New York's eavesdropping rules under Penal Law section 250.00 are one-party consent, and a store camera recording conversations it is not a party to raises questions a video-only camera does not. We leave audio off unless the owner asks for it and understands that.
  • Private spaces stay private. Penal Law section 250.45 makes it a crime to install a camera where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent. Restrooms and changing areas never get cameras.
  • Police sharing is voluntary. Any business can register camera locations with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org, according to 311. amNY reported in May 2024 that the Connect New York pilot, using Fusus technology developed by Axon, lets businesses choose how and when their footage is accessible to their precinct.
  • Storefront grilles. A 2009 city law required security grilles on business and mercantile buildings to be at least 70 percent see-through by July 1, 2026. NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the Council unanimously voted to repeal that deadline, so a compliant grille is required when one is replaced for other reasons. Either way, a see-through gate changes what an inside camera can see at night.
  • Help for small retailers may be coming. The Council's June 2026 budget announcement described Int. 553-A, which would have Small Business Services run a program helping small retailers buy and install security systems, and Int. 623-2026 proposed a camera and plexiglass pilot in high retail-theft ZIP codes; the latter was laid over in committee in February 2026. Neither is a program you can apply to today, as far as our sources show.
  • Cannabis dispensaries. The state counted 623 licensed dispensaries statewide in March 2026, the 600th on the Upper West Side. Their camera rules under 9 NYCRR section 125.3, including footage retention of 60 days, are stricter than any ordinary store's, and we design to that regulation when the client holds a license.
Field Notes

Field Notes: The Basement Closet Before a Dahua Recorder Comes Out

We do not publish stories about a particular client's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow the first time they open the door of a New York camera closet: a cellar room in a walk-up or the back of a super's office, an old recorder on a shelf, a monitor showing a grid of tiles, and a bundle of cables that nobody has touched since the last installer left.

Before anything is unplugged, we photograph the front and back of the recorder, every cable where it lands, and the label with the model and serial. We check the recorder's clock, because footage stamped with the wrong time is a problem in any police report. We look at how many days the drive actually holds, by scrolling back through playback rather than trusting a setting. If there is any footage the owner might need, we export it first. Then the tone generator comes out, and each run is traced, tested and labeled at both ends. Only when every cable has a name and a test result do we talk about which Dahua cameras stay on the new Luminys NVR and which runs need replacing. It takes longer than walking in with a new recorder in a box. It is also the reason the building still has its footage the day after we leave.

Moving off an old Dahua system

Our building's Dahua cameras still look fine. If you install a Luminys recorder, will they keep working on it?

In most cases the reports are encouraging, but we confirm it on your equipment rather than promise it. A St. Louis installer writes that most existing Dahua cameras are fully compatible with Luminys NVRs, Esentia's migration guide addresses the same question, and an IPCamTalk user reported a Luminys NVR running Dahua cameras without trouble. Cameras vary by model and firmware, though, and some older ones will connect but lose features. So on the day of the swap, each camera you want to keep is connected to the new recorder while the old one is still in place, and we check live view, recording and playback for each before the old recorder is retired. Any camera that does not behave goes on a replacement list with the reason written next to it.

The recorder is only a couple of years old. Can we keep it and just add new Luminys cameras to it?

That direction is the one forum users report trouble with. On IPCamTalk, one user described Luminys cameras as not backwards compatible with Dahua NVRs, and another, who had the reverse working, said Luminys cameras would not run on a Dahua NVR. SimplySecured's replacement guide takes up the same question. Dahua firmware also cannot be loaded onto Luminys products, so there is no workaround by updating one to match the other. If new cameras are going to be Luminys, we plan on the recorder being Luminys too. If the existing recorder is healthy and you only need one or two more cameras now, the honest alternative is to add cameras that the existing recorder supports and plan the Luminys move for when the recorder is due.

Is Luminys just Dahua with a different logo on the box?

The business came directly from Dahua's US operation: Selectum describes Luminys as established following the sale and rebranding of Dahua USA's North American business, and The Register reported that the buyer was Taiwan's Central Motion Picture Corporation, with dealer emails signed by Luminys System Corp. and Foxlink. On the hardware, opinions differ. One IPCamTalk poster thought the quality matched Dahua's at a higher price, even at wholesale; Esentia's migration guide addresses the rebranding question for its own customers. What is clearly different is the positioning: ADI describes Luminys cameras, software and kits as NDAA-compliant, and the software and cloud services carry Luminys names. For a New York building the practical point is that Luminys is a supported path off Dahua equipment, not a relabeled version of the old system's firmware.

Licensing, tenants and boards

Does the installer of a video intercom need a New York State license, or only camera installers?

Both, according to the Department of State's position as Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum describes it: a video intercom counts as a small CCTV system, so the security installer license applies. The license itself requires 81 hours of training, a background check and an application, per a licensing course provider, and the City's business portal lists it among the licenses needed to install or maintain security systems. That matters in New York lobbies, where a Luminys camera and a video intercom often share the same cable path and the same ceiling. Ask any installer for their license number; ours is NYS #12000287431.

We own a rental building. Can we put Luminys cameras in the hallways and lobby, and do tenants have to be told first?

Cameras in common areas such as lobbies, halls, elevators and mailrooms are common in New York rentals, and RecordingLaw is among the sources that address the question. The privacy limit is Penal Law section 250.45, which bars cameras where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy. On notice, the sources conflict: one law firm writes that New York does not require written notice before installing shared-area cameras, while another site says landlords must inform residents. For rent-stabilized buildings, Seneca Security notes that courts and DHCR take seriously anything that could look like harassment of a stabilized tenant, and advises documenting the security reason in writing and notifying tenants in advance. We follow that advice on every rental job and suggest owners ask their attorney about notice; this is not legal advice.

Our co-op wants an outdoor camera that covers the courtyard. Could it be seen as monitoring particular residents?

A Justia Ask a Lawyer question from April 2026 asked almost exactly this about a New York co-op. The attorney's answer was that video surveillance is legal as long as the camera is not aimed to look into an apartment when its door is open. We apply that in placement: courtyard and entrance cameras are aimed at shared paths, gates and doors, and we check each camera's view on the recorder for windows and doorways it should not see. Where a window cannot be avoided, many Luminys cameras support privacy masking, which blacks out part of the image. The board, not one shareholder, decides on common-area cameras in a co-op.

Can a Luminys camera go on the front of our brownstone in a historic district?

Possibly, but usually not where people first want it. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says staff can approve cameras that meet its criteria, that cameras should go off the primary facade unless there is no other option, and that dome cameras should be finished to match the building. In practice we look first at the areaway, under the stoop, the side or rear, and inside the vestibule, and we paint or select a housing color to match the masonry. If the facade is the only workable spot, we prepare the photos and details for the application and wait for the permit before mounting. With 1,460 individual landmarks plus historic districts across the city, we check every address before we quote a front camera.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Luminys Camera Installation in NYC

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about Luminys and about installing cameras in New York, set against the published sources and our field practice. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that feed many of those answers; Angi even publishes a New York average and typical range. They are useful as a rough sanity check, but none of them has seen your riser.

"Luminys is a Chinese brand, the same as Dahua."

This one mixes up where the company came from with who owns it. Luminys grew out of Dahua's US business, and that link is real and documented.

But The Register reported in April 2024 that Dahua sold its US subsidiary to Taiwan's Central Motion Picture Corporation, with dealer emails signed by Luminys System Corp. and Foxlink, a Taiwanese manufacturer. ADI describes Luminys' cameras, software and kits as NDAA-compliant, and SecurityInfoWatch reported that after questioning its equipment approvals in 2025, the FCC withdrew that action and closed the proceeding.

For a New York board, the accurate summary is short: Luminys came from Dahua's American business, is now under Taiwanese ownership, and sells itself on NDAA compliance. We give clients those sources rather than a slogan.

"Your Dahua cameras will work on Luminys, and Luminys cameras will work on your Dahua recorder."

Quick answers tend to say compatibility runs both ways. The user reports we found do not support that. Forum users describe Luminys cameras failing on Dahua NVRs, while Dahua cameras on a Luminys NVR are reported to work in many cases.

Firmware does not bridge the gap either: Dahua firmware cannot be loaded onto Luminys products, according to an IPCamTalk poster.

So the safe plan for a New York building is a Luminys recorder first, with existing Dahua cameras tested one by one on it, and new Luminys cameras added as old ones fail. We show each result on screen before the old recorder goes.

"Wireless cameras make installation simple in any apartment."

WiFi cameras are easy on a wood-frame house. In much of New York they are not. Prewar buildings can have masonry walls 12 to 18 inches thick, and a steel elevator cab blocks radio almost completely.

Remote viewing has its own limits. The City Council's data team reported that 24.5 percent of households had no broadband subscription in 2021 and 12.8 percent relied on a cellular plan alone, and some home internet services make direct connections to a recorder impossible.

That is why our Luminys systems are wired to a recorder in the building wherever possible, with LumiCloud or the app used for viewing. The cable is the work; the convenience comes after.

"Any electrician can install your cameras."

Some sites say NYC requires licensed electricians for wired camera installs. The Department of State's framework is different: installing CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement requires a state security installer license, with master electricians registering rather than sitting the requirements.

The City's 2024 electrical code, Local Law 128, describes low-voltage work under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data as generally not needing an electrical permit when done by a licensed electrician or qualified person. New power circuits are still an electrician's job.

So the answer is not "any electrician" and not "anyone with a ladder". It is a licensed security installer for the camera system and a licensed electrician for any new circuit, and we tell you which is which on the quote.

"New York City limits how many cameras a building can have."

We found this claim repeated online, including a figure of two cameras per 25 feet of frontage, but no source that ties it to a citywide rule. Our research could not verify it.

What does exist is the Landmarks Preservation Commission's criteria for cameras on designated buildings and in historic districts, which favor cameras off the primary facade and domes finished to match. That may be where the idea comes from.

On an ordinary building outside a historic district, the camera count is set by what needs covering and what the owner, board or landlord approves. On a landmark, Landmarks sets the terms, and we follow them.

"Cloud cameras mean you never lose footage."

Cloud storage is a good second copy. It is not a guarantee. If the building's internet goes down, a camera that only records to the cloud records nothing to the cloud until it comes back.

Luminys addresses part of this: a distributor describes LumiCloud cameras carrying a built-in 256GB SD card that buffers up to 27 days at 720p. And Luminys says that if a LumiCloud license expires, you keep full local access to cameras and NVRs.

For apartment buildings and stores in New York, where Con Edison outages and internet outages both happen, we still put the primary copy on a recorder in the building, on a battery backup, and use cloud as the copy kept off site.

"Professional camera installation costs about the same everywhere in the city."

National guides give one range for New York, and AI answers repeat it. Even commercial installers publish wide per-camera ranges, because they know the building drives the number.

Within the city, a Manhattan doorman building with service elevator bookings and garage parking takes more labor time than a Bronx walk-up, and a Staten Island house with a long run to the garage differs from both. Our labor follows each borough's rate while equipment is priced the same citywide.

That is why we publish a separate Luminys price page for every borough and a citywide page with a calculator, and why we never quote a number that is not on those lists.

Our Installation Process

Every Luminys job in New York follows the same eight steps, whether it is four cameras on a Staten Island house or forty across a Bronx elevator building. The order matters most in old buildings, where skipping the survey is the usual cause of a cable plan that changes halfway through the job.

1. Send the building and the current system

Photos of the recorder label, a few cameras, the lobby or storefront, and the building type. We tell you whether it is a new Luminys system or a move off Dahua equipment, and we book a visit within two hours of your request.

2. Clear the building's rules first

Board or managing agent approval, the alteration agreement, landlord and tenant-notice questions, Landmarks if the facade is involved, and the certificate of insurance the building asks for, before a drilling date is set.

3. Survey and test the old wiring

Every existing run traced with a tone generator, tested and labeled, and old footage exported if there is anything the owner may need, before any equipment is removed.

4. Design the camera plan and recorder

Each camera's position and view agreed, the days of footage you want to keep set, the recorder sized to match, and the Dahua cameras that will stay tested for compatibility.

5. Pull, protect and label the cable

Runs through risers, ceilings, conduit or core-drilled walls, penetrations fire-stopped, and both ends labeled to a written port map.

6. Mount the cameras and set up the NVR

Cameras fixed to masonry, ceilings or parapet mounts, connected to the PoE switch and the Luminys recorder, with resolution, schedules and retention set.

7. Connect remote viewing and accounts

LumiCloud or the mobile app for remote viewing, a separate login for each person, and every default or shared password removed.

8. Test, export and hand over

Every camera checked by day and after dark, playback proven for the agreed number of days, a test clip exported, and the port map and account list handed over.
