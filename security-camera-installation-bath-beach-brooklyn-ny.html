The Neighborhood

Why Bath Beach Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Bath Beach is a neighborhood of semi-attached houses and small apartment buildings laid out on long, numbered Bay streets, with a busy shopping street under the elevated D train at one end and Gravesend Bay and the Belt Parkway at the other, and that layout decides where a camera does useful work. Wikipedia describes it as primarily a working-class community of semi-attached houses and small apartment houses, set in Community District 11, with a 2020 population of 33,070. Its edges depend on whom you ask. Wikipedia separates it from Bensonhurst along 86th Street, puts Dyker Beach Park and its golf course across 14th Avenue to the northwest, and puts Gravesend across Stillwell Avenue to the southeast; another neighborhood guide stretches it to Bay Parkway and Avenue P on the east. Inside those lines, four long avenues run parallel to the water, Cropsey Avenue, Bath Avenue, Benson Avenue and 86th Street, crossed by the Bay streets from Bay 7th to Bay 54th. The 62nd Precinct, at 1925 Bath Avenue, covers it along with Bensonhurst and Mapleton.

The history explains the mix of buildings a camera installer meets on a single walk. Bath Beach began as part of the Dutch town of New Utrecht, settled in 1652. The Brooklyn Eagle records that in the mid-19th century freed slaves were given parcels of land here and created one of the first African American settlements, and that the area grew once a steam railroad linked it to Brooklyn in the early 1860s. Wealthy families built villas and yacht clubs along the shore; the Ulmer Park amusement grounds opened in 1893 and lasted about six years. When the elevated line over 86th Street opened in 1916, families poured in from Manhattan's Lower East Side, and the beach itself disappeared under the Belt Parkway around 1940. Since the 1990s, Wikipedia notes, many Asian, Eastern European, Central American and Middle Eastern immigrants have settled here. On the ground that reads as a 1910 frame Victorian on Bay 23rd Street, a row of brick two-family houses from around 1930, a 1915 walk-up on Cropsey Avenue, a 1951 garden-apartment complex, 17-story co-op towers from the late 1950s and 1960s by the parkway, and glass condo towers finished in 2023 and 2024.

The houses still dominate. City Department of Finance sales records show two-family houses as the most common type sold in Bath Beach in both 2022 and 2024, ahead of three-family and one-family houses, which matches what owners describe: an owner on one floor and a tenant on another, sharing a front door, a side path, a cellar and a yard. That shared ground is exactly where camera questions start, because each household wants to see who came to its door, and nobody wants a camera that records the other family's comings and goings for no reason. Apartment buildings form the second layer. NYBits profiles 20 co-op buildings in the neighborhood, from post-war walk-ups like 106 Bay 14th Street to mid-rise elevator buildings like the Royale at 30 Bay 29th Street. Waterview Towers at 1935 Shore Parkway runs to 325 units across two 17-story buildings and already has night-time security and a video intercom; Contello Towers at 2740 Cropsey Avenue has 321 units over 17 floors. The newest layer is the glass towers: 2230 Cropsey Avenue, 31 floors and 248 units with a full-time doorman, and Bayview Courtyard at 2286 Cropsey Avenue, 24 stories and 151 residences.

The local reports show what cameras are being asked to record. On the greenway, the absence of cameras became the story: police said a canvass had found no video, a license plate reader logged roughly 1,700 cars passing during the period around the attack, and detectives turned to drivers' dash cameras in the hope one of them caught something. Elected officials, including Councilmember Susan Zhuang in a letter to the mayor and the police commissioner, asked for cameras, light towers and more patrols along the path. In the house blocks, the Brooklyn Paper reported a home near 20th Avenue entered while the residents were on vacation with no signs of forced entry and $2,000 in cash and jewelry taken, four people climbing into a second-story window near Bay 23rd Street and 86th Street in January 2024, and, weeks later on 23rd Avenue near Cropsey Avenue, a suspect seen on a live camera forcing his way in and later arrested. News 12 reported five men forcing their way into a home near Bay 37th Street and 86th Street in February 2026, taking $10,000 from a tenant's bedroom, and returning two days later. Businesses appear too: the Brooklyn District Attorney's office listed a Bath Avenue grocery among a burglar's targets in 2022, the Brooklyn Paper reported $50,000 of tools taken near Shore Parkway and Eugene R. Romano Lane in December 2023, and News 12 counted Bath Beach among the neighborhoods hit in a 2026 pattern of 19 commercial burglaries that began with an object thrown through a window. We found no source that ranks Bath Beach blocks by crime, and we do not offer one.

  • Video asked for by the hour: after the greenway attack the NYPD asked anyone with recordings of the surrounding area between 7 and 9 p.m. to call Crime Stoppers, which only helps if owners can find that window quickly.

The Waterfront Gap

When Nobody Had Video: What the Greenway Case Teaches Bath Beach Owners About Cameras

We write about this carefully. A young woman from this neighborhood died, her family is grieving, and the investigation is open, with police offering a $10,000 reward for information. Nothing on this page is meant to second-guess the people who live along the water or to suggest a camera on a house could have prevented what happened. But the public reporting made one point that matters to every owner here: when detectives went looking for video around Bay 17th Street and Shore Parkway, they did not find any. Police said there were no cameras in the area. A license plate reader logged about 1,700 cars, and detectives hoped drivers with dash cameras might have caught something. Elected officials have since asked the city for cameras and light towers along the path. Until the city acts, the cameras that face the waterfront edge of Bath Beach are mostly the private ones on buildings along Shore Parkway, Cropsey Avenue and the lower Bay streets, and many of those were installed to watch a door, not a dark street.

Two things separate a camera that helps in a case like this from one that does not. The first is whether it sees anything at night. A cheap camera mounted high under a soffit and aimed down at a front gate will record the gate well and the street as a black band, because its infrared light carries only so far and the streetlight is somewhere else. A camera chosen and aimed for the dark, with a sensor made for low light, a lens set to the distance that matters, and a fast enough shutter that a moving person does not blur, will often show a figure, its clothing and the direction it went, even where neighbors say there are no lights. The second is whether the owner can find the right minutes. The NYPD's request after the greenway attack asked for recordings between 7 and 9 p.m. An owner with a recorder whose clock is wrong, whose cameras are named Camera 1 to Camera 8, and whose drive keeps only a few days, can lose that window before anyone knocks on the door.

What a building facing the water can reasonably cover

A camera on your building belongs on your building, aimed at your own entrance, your own frontage and the sidewalk in front of it. New York law does not stop a camera from recording what is visible from a public place, so a frontage view that includes the sidewalk and the near edge of the street is ordinary. What we do not do is aim a home camera across a parkway to watch a public path for hours at a time as though it were a city camera; that is the city's job, and it is what officials are now asking the city to do. In practice, a building on Shore Parkway or the lower end of a Bay street often gets one camera that looks along its own frontage toward the corner, framed so a person walking past at night appears large enough to describe, and set to record continuously rather than only on motion, because motion settings tuned to ignore passing headlights also tend to ignore a person on foot at a distance.

Light is part of the camera plan

Neighbors described the waterfront area as very dark, and darkness is the most common reason a night clip is useless. Infrared built into a camera lights the scene in front of it, not the street a hundred feet away. Where an owner wants the frontage watched at night, the plan often includes a light on the building itself: a fixture over the entrance or the side path that stays on from dusk, or one that brightens when someone approaches. We note it on the quote when a camera will only be as good as the light it gets, and a licensed electrician installs any new fixture or outlet. Light also changes behavior; a lit doorway is a less comfortable place to wait than a dark one.

Getting a clip to detectives without losing it

When police ask a block for video, the owner's job is simple: keep the recorder running, find the time window, copy it, and call the number police gave. We set every Bath Beach recorder so that is possible without us. Cameras carry street-and-direction names, the recorder clock is synced, the drive holds enough days for the street-facing views, and before we leave the owner exports a short test clip while we watch. If an owner is unsure, we will talk them through the export by phone. We do not view, post or share an owner's footage, and we suggest owners leave decisions about publishing a clip to the detectives working the case, especially while an investigation is open.

Two Households, One Lot

Front Steps, Shared Driveway, Back Yard: Planning Cameras for a Bath Beach Semi-Attached Two-Family

The typical Bath Beach house is attached to its neighbor on one side and open on the other, with a short front yard or a few steps up to the door, a side path or driveway, sometimes shared, and a fenced yard at the back. It usually holds two households, and often three. A camera plan for that kind of house has to do two things at once: show anyone who approaches either household's door, and keep each household's views to what that household needs. It also has to respect the neighbor on the attached side, whose front steps may sit two feet from yours, and the neighbors behind, whose yards meet yours at a fence.

The local reports suggest where to start. The Bay 37th Street case involved a tenant's bedroom; the Bay 23rd Street case began at a second-story window; the 23rd Avenue case ended through a front window; the 20th Avenue case left no sign of forced entry at all. Taken together they say a house plan should cover every way into the lot, front, side and back, rather than relying on a doorbell at the front door. They also say that indoor views, used carefully and with the household's consent, can matter on a house that will sit empty for a stretch.

The front: steps, door and curb from one corner

On a semi-attached house the front door often sits at the top of a short stoop or a few steps, with the downstairs unit's entrance beside it or around the side. A doorbell camera at the main door gives a good face at close range. A second camera mounted at the open corner of the house, around eight to ten feet up, looking back across the front steps and out to the curb, does two jobs: it catches a face as someone turns to leave with a box, and it covers the car at the curb. We frame that view to stop at your own frontage and the sidewalk, so the attached neighbor's door is at the edge of the picture rather than the middle, and we mask anything that would look into their windows.

The side: a path or a driveway two houses share

The open side of the house is usually the only route from the street to the back, which makes it the most valuable camera position on the lot. A camera at the front corner looking down the path, or at the rear corner looking back toward the street, records anyone heading for the yard coming toward the lens. Where the driveway is shared with the house next door, the camera still goes on your own wall, aimed along the strip, and we talk with the owner about telling the neighbor before it goes up. A camera that watches a shared driveway is ordinary; one that also looks into the neighbor's side windows is not, and those areas are masked in the camera settings.

The back: door, yard and the line of fences

Bath Beach yards back onto other yards, usually separated by low fences, with garages or sheds along the rear. A rear camera mounted on the back wall, looking down the yard toward the back door and the gate rather than straight down from the gutter, records faces on the way in and out. It stops at your fence. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records their backyard where they expect privacy, and in a row of yards that is a real consideration, not a technicality. Where the second floor has a rear window over a low roof or an extension, a camera on the back wall can be angled up enough to include it without taking in the neighbors' windows.

Inside: only when the household wants it

The 20th Avenue case, with no forced entry and the family away, is the kind of event an outside camera cannot explain alone. Some owners choose an indoor camera that watches the entry hall from the inside, set to record only while the household is away, so it never records daily life. It is entirely optional, it covers only the household that asks for it, and in a two-family the other household's space is never in view.

Semi-attached brick houses: the cellar route and the open side

Most Bath Beach houses give us one easy route: the cellar. Cable for the front corner, side path and back door runs along the joists and comes up through a small opening at each camera, so nothing crosses the face of the brick. Mounts go into mortar joints, which are easy to patch if a camera ever moves, and every outside opening is drilled sloping slightly down to the outside, with a drip loop in the cable and an exterior sealant around a grommet. The shared wall is left alone; cameras on a semi-attached house belong on the walls that belong to that house. The recorder usually goes in a closet on a living floor or a dry, locked corner of the cellar well above the floor, on a battery unit, with the internet router beside it when that is practical.

Frame Victorians: wood, porches and deep eaves

The handful of Victorians left in Bath Beach, such as the house a Bklyner report described at 122 Bay 26th Street between Benson and Bath Avenues, are wood frame with porches and eaves. Cameras mount through a block into the framing behind the siding, so they do not loosen or crack old boards. A deep porch changes the front plan: a camera under the porch ceiling near the door sees faces well but loses the street, and a camera at the outer porch post sees the steps and the walk but has to be placed so the posts and railings do not slice the picture. Cable travels through the attic or the cellar and, where the walls allow it, inside them; where they do not, it runs in a small paintable cover along the trim.

Walk-ups and garden apartments: vestibules, stairs and courtyards

The four-story walk-ups on Cropsey and Bath avenues have a street door, a vestibule, a single stair and a cellar, with mailboxes in the vestibule or the ground-floor hall. A camera inside the vestibule facing the street door records faces coming in; a second at the foot of the stair records who goes up; a cellar camera covers the boiler room door and storage. In the garden-apartment complexes, like the three-story, 84-unit buildings at 1758 Cropsey Avenue, the work spreads across several entrances around open ground, and the question is how each building's cameras reach the recorder. Where buildings share a cellar, cable stays inside; where they do not, it crosses in conduit or by a short wireless link. Owner, Tenant, Board or Next-Door Neighbor: The Rules Behind a Bath Beach Camera Job

Bath Beach has no historic district that we found, so the rules that shape camera work here are mostly about who owns which part of a property, who may record where, and who may do the installing. Here is how each plays out on the houses, walk-ups, towers and shops in the neighborhood.

Who may install. Co-ops and condos. City property. Cameras go on your building, your porch or your fence, never on a lamppost, street tree, sign pole or the parkway fence.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, block or siding is sloped outward and sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in an apartment building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

Does New York City require a Bath Beach homeowner to register a home security camera?
+

No. City Rule Lookup notes that New York City has no local ordinance requiring residential security cameras to be registered. What does apply is the law on where a camera may look, including Civil Rights Law section 52-a on recording a neighbor's backyard, and the rules of your building if you rent or live in a co-op or condo. Some owners choose to tell their local precinct or neighbors that cameras face the street, which can help if police are canvassing for video, but that is voluntary.

When police ask the public for video from a set window, such as 7 to 9 p.m., how do we find and save it before the recorder writes over it?
+

Leave the recorder running, open playback, choose the camera that faces the street or path, and go to the date and the time window police gave. Copy that window, plus a little before and after, to a USB stick or your phone, and do not trim or edit it. Write down which camera it came from and call the number police published, which after the greenway attack was the NYPD Crime Stoppers hotline. If you are unsure how to export, call us and we will talk you through it by phone; the sooner the better, because drives overwrite older footage on a schedule.

Can you quote a Bath Beach camera job from photos, or does a technician have to visit first?
+

Many house jobs can be quoted from good photos. Send the front steps and curb, the side path or driveway, the back door and yard, the cellar ceiling and the spot where the internet line comes in, plus a night photo of the front if you can. For walk-ups, garden complexes, towers, shops and yards we usually walk the property, because cable routes, firestopping and board or landlord rules decide the labor. Estimates are free either way and requests are booked within 2 hours.

The only internet line in our two-family runs into the upstairs tenant's apartment. Can the camera system use it?
+

It can record without internet at all, because the recorder saves everything locally; internet is only needed for phone viewing and alerts. If the owner's recorder sits on the tenant's network, the tenant controls the router and could unplug it, and both parties share a connection, so it works only with a clear written agreement. The cleaner options are a separate internet line for the owner, or a recorder that records locally and is viewed on the owner's own connection elsewhere in the house. We talk through which suits the house before we quote.

Will any camera work on our semi-attached house go through the wall we share with the house next door?
+

No. The shared wall belongs to both houses, so we leave it alone. Cameras go on the open side, the front and the rear walls that belong to your house, and cable travels along your own cellar ceiling and up through openings in your own walls. If the only sensible camera spot would need anything fixed to the shared wall or to the neighbor's side, we find another position or talk with you about asking the neighbor first.

Do you supply the cameras and recorder, or do we have to buy them ourselves?
+

Either works. Most Bath Beach owners have us supply the equipment, and we name every camera, recorder and drive on the quote, from brands including Lorex, Hikvision, Dahua and Uniview. Yes. When we book your visit we tell you who is coming and when. If anyone arrives claiming to be from us and you are unsure, call (347) 934-8335 before opening the door.

Can the recorder keep footage from our street-facing camera longer than footage from the indoor ones?
+

Usually, yes. Most recorders let us set recording quality, schedules and storage per camera, so a frontage camera that faces the street can record continuously at full quality while an indoor entry camera records only when the household is away. That leaves more of the drive for the views most likely to be asked for after an incident on the block. We size the drive so street-facing cameras keep two weeks or more, and the quote shows the expected number of days for each camera.

Can you take down the old cameras and brackets a previous owner left on the front of our Bath Beach house?
+

Yes. Removal is a line on the quote. We take down dead cameras, brackets and loose cable, trace what goes where in the cellar first so nothing live is cut, and fill the old holes in mortar joints or siding with a matching exterior filler or sealant so water does not get in. Where an old mounting position is still the right spot, we may reuse it to avoid drilling new holes, and the quote says which positions are kept.
