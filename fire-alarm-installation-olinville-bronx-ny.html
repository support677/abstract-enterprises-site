The Neighborhood

Why Olinville's Towers, Co-ops and Store Blocks Need a Fire Alarm Built Around Their Elevators

Olinville is easy to miss on a map. Bronx Community Board 12 describes it as a place "today considered part of the Williamsbridge neighborhood," sharing the 10467 ZIP code and sitting west of White Plains Road between Allerton Avenue and Gun Hill Road, with the 2 and 5 trains overhead. CityNeighborhoods.nyc draws the edges as East Gun Hill Road on the north, Bronx Park East on the west and Arnow Avenue on the south, and tells the origin story: the Olin family subdivided farmland here, and speculative builders laid out Olinville Avenue in the 1890s and sold the area as "the village by the park." The land joined New York City in 1895, when this part of the old Town of Westchester was annexed.

The village never stayed a village. Two large federal public housing campuses went up at the start of the 1950s. NYCHA's development data book lists Gun Hill Houses, completed November 30, 1950, as six 14-story buildings with 732 apartments on the blocks framed by Holland Avenue, White Plains Road, Gun Hill Road and Magenta Street. Parkside Houses, completed June 12, 1951, has 14 buildings of 6, 7, 14 and 15 stories and 877 apartments between Adee Avenue, White Plains Road, Arnow Avenue and Bronx Park East. Ten years later came 3000 Bronx Park East, a 20-story co-op with 297 apartments at the corner of Adee Avenue, built in 1961 according to CityRealty. Around them sit mid-century and prewar apartment houses, two- and three-story brick houses, and the storefronts that line White Plains Road under the elevated line.

That mix is why this page is about elevators more than anything else. A building with an elevator and a building fire alarm usually has smoke detection in the elevator lobbies and machine rooms that sends the cars to a safe floor, and the taller the building, the more depends on that working. In Olinville, almost all of that equipment is tied to buildings that were designed before today's codes, then rewired, re-equipped and modernized one project at a time. The 20 tower buildings on the two public housing campuses alone hold more than 1,600 apartments, and NYCHA counts 1,629 residents at Parkside and 1,373 at Gun Hill. Every time an elevator, a boiler room or a ground-floor space changes, the fire alarm has to follow.

The fire record shows the range of buildings involved. On March 6, 2019, ABC7 reported that 19 people were hurt, two critically, in a fast-moving fire in an apartment building on Holland Avenue, where many residents jumped from second- and third-story windows. On May 14, 2023, Norwood News reported a third-floor fire in a multiple dwelling at 3003 Olinville Avenue, just north of Parkside, put under control by 60 firefighters with no injuries. And on January 16, 2025, a fire on the seventh floor of 669 Arnow Avenue, a Parkside building between Olinville and Barker Avenues, brought 12 units and 60 fire and EMS personnel; it was under control by 8:59 p.m., and the FDNY's advice afterward was blunt: "During a fire, always close the door behind you!" None of those reports describe the buildings' fire alarm systems, and we draw no conclusions about them. What they show is that Olinville's fires happen in walk-ups, mid-rises and towers alike, and each type is handled differently under the code.

The city has also decided that Olinville should not grow much taller. The Department of City Planning's Olinville Rezoning, adopted by the City Council on December 8, 2005 after Community Board 12 voted 29 to 0 for it, covered 36 blocks and noted that "few buildings are higher than 70 feet" outside the towers. It mapped low-density districts on the blocks of detached houses, including a new R5A district limited to one- and two-family homes no taller than 35 feet, and kept higher districts along corridors such as Bronx Boulevard, Olinville Avenue and Barker Avenue. In practice, the tall buildings Olinville has are the ones it will keep, so most fire alarm work here is retrofit, replacement and repair in existing towers rather than brand-new construction. This page covers commercial and code-required buildings only; the smoke alarms inside one- and two-family houses are not part of this service.

Tell us the address and cross street, where the panel is, the exact words on its display, and one more thing that matters here more than in most neighborhoods: whether the elevators are behaving normally. A tower whose cars have been recalled to the lobby and won't return to service, or whose panel has lost its link to the central station while residents sleep on the fourteenth floor, is handled before a quiet store with a dusty detector.

Before the technician arrives, photograph the display, call your central station so they know what they're seeing, and press reset once at most. Don't pull batteries, don't cover a detector, and don't let anyone tell the elevator company to bypass recall so the cars can run. When a required fire alarm in New York City is out of service, the FDNY must be notified and a fire watch kept until protection is restored.

Building Specialists

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Olinville's Buildings, From 1950 Towers to a 1961 Co-op

What is a commercial fire alarm system? It is a building-wide system, built to NFPA 72 and the NYC codes, that detects fire or smoke in the places the code names, reports to a central station so the fire department is called, and operates the building's other life-safety equipment, from elevators to fans and doors. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? Initiating devices such as smoke detectors, heat detectors, sprinkler flow switches and pull stations report to a control panel; the panel decides what the signal means and runs the sequence the engineer wrote. In Olinville, five kinds of buildings carry nearly all of that work, and each is built differently.

Fourteen- and fifteen-story public housing towers

The towers at Gun Hill Houses and the tall buildings at Parkside are brick over a concrete frame, with long risers, a lobby at the bottom and an elevator machine room at the top. A 14-story building is well past the 75-foot line the city uses to define a high-rise, so its system is designed to high-rise rules. The cable routes are the shafts and the stair halls, and new devices on block or concrete walls usually go in surface raceway. NYCHA's own fact sheet on its fire alarm replacement program lists what such a project installs: a new control panel, communicators, strobes, magnetic door holders, manual pull stations, smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors, with construction typically running 24 to 30 months after planning, design and procurement.

Six- and seven-story buildings on the same campus

Parkside mixes its tall buildings with 6- and 7-story ones across the same courtyards. Those shorter buildings can fall under different rules than their neighbors: they may stay under the high-rise line, have fewer elevators and use smaller panels. On a mixed campus, the most common mistake is assuming one building's design fits the one next door. Each building's system starts from its own height, its own floor plan and what its ground floor holds.

Twenty-story co-op towers

3000 Bronx Park East is a postwar elevator building with a board, a managing agent and shareholders who own the apartments where work would happen. Its fire alarm decisions are board decisions, its budget is the building's reserve, and its schedule has to fit around elevator outages and residents who can't take stairs. The same is true of other mid-century co-ops along the park.

Prewar and mid-century apartment houses

Olinville Avenue, Holland Avenue and Wallace Avenue carry four- to seven-story apartment buildings, some with elevators and some without. Whether one of these needs a building fire alarm, and how much of one, depends on its number of apartments, its height above the lowest exit and the code it was built or altered under. Many have a small panel in the cellar watching the boiler room and a few common areas, and many have been altered so often that the drawings no longer match the walls.

Ground floors that aren't apartments

NYCHA's address lists for the two campuses read like a small town's main street: a day care center at 3440 White Plains Road, community centers at 745 Magenta Street and 2972 Bronx Park East, a senior center at 644 Adee Avenue, management offices, a laundry room, boiler rooms and trade shops for plasterers, painters, carpenters and the elevator crew. Three spaces at Gun Hill are listed as vacant community operations space. Each of those rooms is a different occupancy under apartments, and each one that reopens or changes hands is a moment to check what the fire alarm has to do there.

Compliance on every corridor

Fire alarm code compliance in New York City rests on three layers: the NYC Building Code decides when a system is required and what it has to include; NFPA 72, as adopted by the city, decides how it is built, tested and maintained; and the NYC Fire Code governs inspection, testing, records and what happens when a system is out of service. Fire alarm code compliance upgrades usually start from an FDNY violation, an insurer's report or a renovation that changes the occupancy. Fire alarm system design requirements are set by the design professional, not the installer, and fire alarm system inspection requirements continue for the life of the building. Accessibility rules add their own layer: fire alarm ADA strobe installation puts visible notification where the design requires it, so people who can't hear the horn still get the warning.

Field Notes

Field Notes: In a Mid-Century Tower, Walk the Elevator Machine Room Before Anything Else

On a survey of a 1950s or 1960s elevator building, the first room we ask to see is not the panel room. It is the elevator machine room on the roof. That room tells us more about the fire alarm than the panel does: whether there is a smoke detector up there at all, whether it is wired back to the fire alarm or to some older relay, whether the elevator controller has been replaced since the building opened, and whether anyone has written down which floor the cars are supposed to go to when the lobby detector trips. In towers that have been modernized one car at a time, it is common to find two controllers from two decades sharing one room, with the fire alarm wired differently to each.

The second stop is the lobby. We stand where firefighters will stand, look for the panel or annunciator, and check whether the floor numbers on the display match the floor numbers on the stair doors. Then we go to the cellar and the boiler room, because that is where old detectors, sprinkler valves and the oldest wiring tend to live. Only after all three do we open the panel and read its history.

The lesson we bring back to boards and managers is simple: in an elevator building, the fire alarm and the elevators are one system as far as a firefighter is concerned. Schedule elevator work and fire alarm work together, put the elevator company's contact on the fire alarm records, and never let a recall circuit be left bypassed after a modernization because the test date slipped.

Your Questions Answered

Olinville Fire Alarm Questions From Boards, Managers, Program Directors and Store Owners

Towers and elevators

Parkside Houses puts 6- and 7-story buildings beside 14- and 15-story ones across the same courtyards. Why can two buildings that share a campus need such different fire alarms?

Because the code looks at each building, not the campus. A building with an occupied floor more than 75 feet above the lowest level of fire department vehicle access is a high-rise, and high-rise buildings carry requirements a 6-story building does not, from how the elevators serve firefighters to how the fire department communicates inside. A 14-story tower clears that line easily; a 6-story building on the next path usually does not. The engineer designs each building from its own height, floor plan and ground-floor use, even when one management office runs them all.

New York City set January 1, 2020 for elevator door-lock monitoring and January 1, 2027 for replacing single-plunger brakes. While the elevator crew is in an Olinville machine room for that work, what should the fire alarm side check?

Those deadlines, from the Department of Buildings' list of retroactive elevator requirements, are elevator work, not fire alarm work, and the elevator contractor files them. But they put a mechanic in the machine room with the controller open, which is the cheapest moment to confirm the fire alarm side: that the machine room detector is present and reporting to the fire alarm panel, that the recall wiring lands where the drawings say, and that the controller still responds the way the approved sequence describes. If the elevator work changes the controller, the recall interface is retested with the fire alarm company present before the car returns to service.

What does the small fire hat symbol that lights up inside an elevator car mean, and what does the building fire alarm have to do with it?

The lit or flashing firefighter's hat tells passengers and firefighters that the car is under fire service control. The fire alarm usually causes it: a detector in an elevator lobby, the machine room or the hoistway sends the cars to the designated or alternate floor and turns on the indicator. In current practice, the hat flashes when the alarm came from the machine room or hoistway, warning firefighters that the elevator's own equipment may be affected. Which detectors do what is written into the sequence of operations and proved at the FDNY test.

3000 Bronx Park East went up in 1961 as a 20-story co-op. Does a building that predates the 1968 Building Code have to bring its fire alarm up to today's rules?

Not automatically. Existing buildings generally keep operating under the code they were built or last altered under, except where a specific law or rule reaches back and applies to existing buildings. What changes the picture is work: replacing a panel, altering a floor, changing a ground-floor use or modernizing elevators can bring the affected part of the system under current requirements, and the design professional decides how far that reaches. Inspection, testing and maintenance under the Fire Code apply to every existing system regardless of its age.

Ground floors under apartments

Gun Hill Houses lists three ground-floor community operations spaces as vacant. If one reopens as a youth or tenant program room, what happens to the fire alarm?

The reopening is treated as a use of the space, and the design professional checks it against the building's Certificate of Occupancy and the existing fire alarm design. A room that becomes a gathering space for groups may need manual stations, notification the occupants can hear and see, and possibly door release, and any change to what the approved system does is filed with the FDNY and acceptance-tested. A space that has sat empty also needs its existing devices tested before anyone relies on them, because detectors in closed rooms are easy to forget during annual inspections.

The PSS Parkside Center serves adults 60 and over on the ground floor of a public housing building on Adee Avenue. What does a fire alarm designer ask about a senior center that an apartment floor never raises?

Who is in the room, how fast they can leave and how they will know to go. A senior center has daytime groups, some members who use walkers or wheelchairs, some who don't hear well, and a staff who will lead everyone out. That shapes the number and placement of visible notification appliances, whether doors held open for wheelchairs should close on alarm, where pull stations sit so staff reach them first, and how the center's alarm relates to the building above. Presbyterian Senior Services says the Parkside Center is open weekdays from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., which also tells the designer when testing has to be scheduled.

Most stair and corridor doors in 1950s buildings were never meant to be held open. When do magnetic door holders tied to the fire alarm make sense, and when don't they?

They make sense where a door has a real reason to stand open in daily use, such as a corridor door in a day care or senior center, a laundry room entry or a lobby vestibule, and where the code allows it to be held open as long as it closes automatically on alarm. The holder releases when the fire alarm operates, and the door's own closer pulls it shut. They don't make sense on stair doors in apartment hallways that are supposed to stay closed all the time; there, the right fix for a door people wedge open is a working closer and building management, not an electromagnet.

During the 2023 gas outage at 735 Magenta Street, tenants cooked on hot plates for weeks. Does a long outage like that change anything the building's fire alarm company should do?

It can. News 12 reported that residents cooked on hot plates and that a home health aide considered the situation a fire hazard. A building fire alarm in a tower like that does not detect inside apartments, but cooking smoke drifts into hallways when doors open, and hallway or elevator lobby detectors near apartment doors are where nuisance alarms show up. During a long outage, a manager can ask the fire alarm company to watch the panel history for repeated activations, check that no detector has been covered or removed, and confirm that elevator recall resets properly after each event.

Schools, small buildings and new buildings

P.S. 41 on Olinville Avenue serves about 600 children in grades K to 5. Who is responsible for the fire alarm in a city public school building, and can a neighborhood contractor work on it?

Public school buildings are city property, and their capital work, including fire alarm replacement, is generally planned and procured through the city's school construction and facilities agencies under public bidding rules rather than hired by a principal or a parent association. A neighborhood company does not walk in and work on a public school's panel. Contractors reach that work through public procurement, with the same FDNY design, filing and acceptance requirements as any other building.

If an owner on Bronx Boulevard's R6 blocks replaces an old two-story house with a six-story apartment building, at what size does the new building need a fire alarm?

For a Group R-2 apartment building, Section 907.2.9 of the NYC Building Code calls for a fire alarm system when any apartment is three or more stories above the lowest level of exit discharge or more than one story below the highest one, or when the building has more than 16 apartments. A six-story apartment building passes the first test on its own. When does a building need a fire alarm system in general? When its occupancy, height, number of apartments or number of occupants crosses the thresholds in Section 907.2, and a building being designed from scratch is checked against all of them at once. That system is mostly detection in the spaces the code names, such as the boiler, electrical and elevator machine rooms and elevator lobbies, reporting to a central station, plus elevator recall if there is an elevator.

Residents of a Holland Avenue apartment building jumped from second- and third-story windows during a 2019 fire. How can the owner of a small walk-up find out whether the building needs a building fire alarm at all?

Start with three documents: the Certificate of Occupancy, any FDNY letters or violations on file, and any old fire alarm drawings or Letter of Approval. Count the apartments and the stories above the street exit. Then have a design professional compare that against the code the building falls under and any alterations since. Some small walk-ups need no building fire alarm and rely on apartment smoke alarms; others need one because of their size, a store below or past alterations. The 2019 report doesn't say what system that building had, and the answer for any building comes from its own records.

Ask a chatbot about fire alarms for a Bronx apartment tower and you get a summary drawn from national cost guides like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Here are seven claims those summaries repeat, and what they miss in Olinville.

"The fire alarm and the elevators are separate systems"

Bronx Community Board 12 treats Olinville as part of Williamsbridge, sharing the 10467 ZIP code. For a fire alarm it makes no difference: the FDNY files and inspects by building address, and the same TM-1 plan review, Project Authorization and acceptance test apply on every block.

Does a sprinkler waterflow signal on an upper floor of an Olinville tower call the elevators down?
+

It can. Some designs have waterflow start elevator recall, as a 2007 New York Real Estate Journal guide described for NYC residential buildings, while others leave recall to the lobby, machine room and shaft detectors. The approved sequence of operations for your building says which, and the FDNY acceptance test proves it.

What does the FDNY charge to review fire alarm plans for an Olinville building?
+

The NYC Business listing for the fire alarm plan application gives the fee as $420, or $525 where a complex technical analysis is needed, with no fee to resubmit within six months of a letter of deficiency. Design, installation and testing are separate and quoted per building.

What can a co-op board get done while its fire alarm plans wait for FDNY approval?
+

Line up the elevator contractor's dates, collect shareholder access for any in-apartment work, confirm the central station contract, settle the phasing plan floor by floor and order long-lead equipment once the scope is final. Installation itself waits for the Project Authorization.

What should a White Plains Road store owner do when a device the landlord owns inside the store shows trouble?
+

Call the landlord or managing agent and the building's fire alarm service company, give them the device location and what the panel or annunciator says, and don't remove or cover the device. A store tenant's own electrician should not touch the building system.

Does a new panel in an old Olinville tower mean rewiring every floor?
+

Not always. If the existing devices and wiring test sound and are compatible with the new panel, much of the field wiring can stay; if the building is moving from zones to addressable devices, or the cable is damaged or not rated for the job, floors get new cable. The survey and the engineer's design decide.

Can an elevator company bypass fire alarm recall to keep a car in service during repairs?
+

Recall circuits should never be left bypassed. During authorized elevator or fire alarm work the trades coordinate any temporary disablement, notify the central station and, where a required system is impaired, follow the FDNY's out-of-service rules. When the work ends, recall is restored and tested before the car carries passengers.

How is a fire watch handled in a 14-story building while the fire alarm is out of service?
+

The FDNY must be notified when a required system is out of service, and the owner provides a fire watch: trained people patrolling the affected floors to look for fire and call it in until the system is restored. In a tall building that means enough people to cover every affected floor, and a log kept until the system is back.

Should the staff of a ground-floor day care in a tower take part in the building's fire alarm tests?
+

Yes, in the sense of being told. Testing should be scheduled with the program's director so the children are not frightened or moved without reason, and staff should see the devices in their rooms work and learn which panel serves them.

Will a fire alarm upgrade in an Olinville tower change what residents see in their hallways?
+

Usually only a little. Most of the work happens in lobbies, elevator lobbies, machine rooms, cellars and shafts. Residents may see new detectors near the elevators, surface raceway on block walls and, where the design adds them, door holders or notification devices.

Does an Olinville tower's fire alarm need to cover the roof bulkhead and elevator penthouse?
+

Usually the elevator machine room in the penthouse is covered, because its detector helps control recall, and other rooms in the bulkhead are covered as the design requires. The engineer lists every room, and the survey confirms the devices are really there.
