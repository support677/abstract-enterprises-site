The Neighborhood

Why Highland Park Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Highland Park is a hill neighborhood. The houses sit on the slope of the terminal moraine that rises from Jamaica Avenue to Highland Boulevard and the 141-acre park on the Queens line, and Brownstoner's architecture writer describes the north-side houses on Sunnyside Avenue as standing above the street on steep stairs and graded lawns. Most of the housing went up between about 1900 and 1920. On Sunnyside Avenue between Barbey and Hendrix Streets, Brownstoner counts roughly ninety porch houses by the developer Frank Richards, in red and buff brick with red tile roofs. On Highland Boulevard, once the area's wealthiest street, there are Arts and Crafts houses, a limestone mansion and Stick style Victorians. On Arlington Avenue there are brick singles with Dutch gambrel roofs from 1908 to 1912. None of this is landmarked. What these houses share, for a fire alarm installer, is that they are tall, old and built around a stair.

That shape decides how a fire behaves and how a warning has to travel. Smoke rises up an open stair the way it rises up a chimney. A cellar with the boiler, the laundry and decades of storage sits at the bottom. Bedrooms are on the second and third floors, and in many houses a room under the roof has become a bedroom too. When the only alarms are a battery unit in the first-floor hall and another in the upstairs hall, a fire that starts at the bottom of the house can fill the stair before the people at the top hear anything, and a fire that starts at the top, in a room nobody is in, can grow while the family two floors down hears nothing at all. Linking alarms so that one sounding sets off every other one in the house is the single change that matters most here, and it is the part of the job we plan first.

The second thing that shapes the work is who lives in the house. Over a century, many of Highland Park's big single-family houses were divided. Some are now legal two-family houses, some are three-family houses that count as multiple dwellings under the city's housing rules, and some are still one family across three floors. The rules change with each. HPD's detector page says owners of multiple dwellings must put an approved smoke detector in each apartment and a carbon monoxide detector in each apartment where fuel is burned, within fifteen feet of every room used for sleeping. For a one- or two-family house the owner does not live in, HPD lists a carbon monoxide duty but no smoke detector duty. From January 1, 2027, every one of these homes with gas piping also needs a natural gas alarm. The New York City Department of Buildings, in an October 13, 2026 release, says that includes one- and two-family houses.

The fires nearby are recent and close. On November 25, 2025, News 12 Brooklyn reported a fire that tore through the top floor of a home on Highland Place between Etna Street and Ridgewood Avenue, at about 9:45 in the morning, with 60 fire and EMS personnel on the scene. On May 13, 2026, a fire began on the first floor of a three-story building at 503 Ridgewood Avenue at about 1:20 a.m.; hazmat crews were called because lithium-ion batteries were found, a full vacate order went up, and the Red Cross helped two households. A neighbor, Jose Mena, told News 12 that one of the residents "was trying to wake everybody up," and added: "This could've been worse — a lot worse. People were sleeping." Neither report says anything about smoke alarms, and we make no claim about what either building had. But the two fires describe the two cases this page is built around: a fire high in a house during the day, and a fire low in a house at night.

Richards porch houses: three floors over a cellar, and a stair that carries smoke to the top

The porch houses on Sunnyside Avenue are the most recognizable housing in the neighborhood, and the most typical of the fire alarm problem here. Brownstoner credits Frank Richards' Real Homes company with about ninety of them, designed by the architect William C. Winters and built between 1900 and 1920, with deep porches, Craftsman or Colonial columns, deep eaves and brackets. Inside, most follow a similar plan: a front hall with the stair, parlor and dining rooms on the first floor, bedrooms on the second, more bedrooms or a finished space on the third, and a full cellar with the boiler and laundry below. On the north side of the street, where the houses sit above the sidewalk on graded lawns, the cellar is often partly above ground at the back.

The stair is what we plan around. In a house like this, smoke from a fire in the cellar or on the first floor will climb the stair and collect at the top before it spreads across the bedroom floors, which is why an alarm at the top of each flight is the first device on our list, and a linked alarm inside each bedroom the second. Then the cellar: a smoke alarm at the bottom of the cellar stair, CO near the boiler and water heater, a natural gas alarm in the same room as any gas appliance, and, where the cellar is dusty or damp enough to make a smoke alarm unreliable, a heat detector that will still report a fire. All of it is linked so that the third floor hears the cellar and the kitchen hears the third floor.

These houses rarely make cable easy. The walls are plaster on lath, the floors are often finished hardwood the owner wants left alone, and the stair is open. For linked hardwired alarms we look first for the paths an old house already has: closets stacked above one another, a chase beside the chimney, the space behind a stair stringer, a baseboard that can come off and go back. Where no path exists without opening a finished ceiling, wireless interconnected alarms are often the better choice, tested in place through the masonry and plaster before we rely on them.

Big houses on Highland Boulevard: long halls, high ceilings and rooms nobody uses every day

The houses on Highland Boulevard were built for wealth. Brownstoner describes 278 Highland Boulevard, at Miller Avenue and Barbey Street, as a pre-1915 Arts and Crafts house with river rock, shingles and a steep Mediterranean tiled roof, and writes that the boulevard held mansions alongside smaller one-family houses, including a limestone mansion and Stick style Victorians. Houses like that have more rooms than most families use daily: a sewing room, a library, a third-floor guest room, a maid's room, a finished space under the roof that holds boxes.

Two choices make the biggest difference in a house like this. First, coverage for the rooms nobody is in, not only for the bedrooms: a smoke alarm in each room that holds anything that could burn, linked to the rest, so a fire in an empty library at three in the afternoon is announced in the kitchen. Second, testing on site from the far ends of the house. A linked alarm that sounds clearly in a compact house may be faint in a long upstairs hall behind heavy doors, and the only way to know is to sound one at one end and stand at the other with the doors shut.

Gambrel and re-sided singles: the outside tells you little about the inside

Brownstoner's look at the gambrel singles on Arlington Avenue makes a point that applies across Highland Park: few houses on that block keep their original brick, and many have been re-sided or otherwise altered. A house that looks plain from the sidewalk may have a finished attic, a rear addition, a second kitchen or a cellar apartment that the original plan never had. Gambrel roofs also mean the top-floor bedroom has two ceiling slopes, steep at the sides and shallow above, and the alarm has to go where smoke will actually reach it, not in the dead air at the peak or low on a steep slope.

Houses split into apartments: a building with more than one set of rules

When a Highland Park house holds more than one household, the detection plan has to answer two questions at once: what each household needs to be safe, and what the law requires of the owner. A three-family house is a multiple dwelling under the housing rules, and HPD expects the owner to put a smoke detector and, where fuel is burned, a CO detector in each apartment within fifteen feet of each sleeping room, give an adult in each apartment a notice about testing and replacement, post notices in the entrance area and keep records of install dates and expiration dates. A two-family house the owner does not live in carries HPD's CO duty but, by HPD's own description, no smoke detector duty, and a gas alarm duty wherever gas piping is present. A house where the owner lives on one floor and rents the other is the case owners ask about most, and we go through it in the questions below.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Sound It at the Bottom, Listen at the Top, Then Reverse It

Start with a floor-by-floor list of who sleeps where. Before anything is priced, the technician walks the house with the owner and writes down every room where someone sleeps, including the ones that do not look like bedrooms: a den that becomes a guest room at holidays, a third-floor room a grown child moved back into, a finished cellar room.

Then list everything that burns fuel or holds a battery. Boiler, water heater, gas range, gas dryer, a fireplace or stove, and anything that charges overnight: e-bike and scooter batteries, power tool packs, a phone charger plugged in behind a bed. Each one goes on the sheet with its room, because each one decides whether that room gets CO, a gas alarm, heat or smoke detection, and how far from the source it should sit.

Sound an alarm in the cellar and listen at the top of the house. With every bedroom door shut, one person presses the test button on the cellar alarm while another stands in the farthest top-floor bedroom. If the sound in that room is faint, or the top-floor alarm did not join in, the plan changes: a link that did not work, a device in the bedroom itself, or a second device on a long hall.

Then reverse it. The second test is the one most people skip. Sound the top-floor alarm and listen in the kitchen, with the range hood running and the radio on, the way the house sounds on an ordinary morning. A fire that starts in an empty room under the roof is the case the Highland Place fire brings to mind, and the people downstairs need to hear it as clearly as the people upstairs need to hear the cellar. When both tests pass, we write down the result, room by room, and leave a copy with the owner.

Natural Gas Alarms

Seven Weeks to January 1, 2027: Natural Gas Alarms in a Highland Park House

Most Highland Park houses have gas piping, and most of them have at least two gas appliances: a range in the kitchen and a boiler or water heater in the cellar, sometimes a gas dryer as well. That puts nearly every house in the neighborhood inside the new requirement. The Department of Buildings' October 13, 2026 release says owners of apartment buildings and private dwellings with gas piping, including one- and two-family homes, must have code-compliant natural gas alarms in every dwelling unit by January 1, 2027, under Local Law 102 of 2025, and that HPD code enforcement staff will begin inspecting for them next year. Here is how the pieces fit together in a house on the hill.

Which rooms get one. The Department of Buildings' FAQ on natural gas detection says that where a fuel-gas appliance is inside a dwelling, the alarm goes in the same room as the appliance, at least three feet and no more than ten feet away horizontally. For a typical Highland Park house, that usually means one in the kitchen and one in the cellar room with the boiler and water heater, and another if the gas dryer is in a separate laundry room. The same FAQ says that where gas piping or an appliance is directly below a dwelling unit, an alarm goes on or within twelve inches of the ceiling of the room above, which matters in a house divided into apartments with a boiler under the first-floor tenant's bedroom.

What kind. The FAQ says the alarms must be listed and labeled to UL 1484, and the October release adds UL 2075 as an accepted listing. Owners can choose hardwired, plug-in or battery models. The FAQ says hardwired alarms must be installed by a New York City licensed electrical contractor who obtains the required permits, while battery or plug-in alarms may be installed by others, including owners and occupants. In most Highland Park houses we install sealed battery or plug-in models listed to UL 1484, because they can go exactly where the rule says without opening plaster or adding an outlet. A carbon monoxide alarm does not count: CO alarms detect the product of burning, not the gas itself.

What owners of rented floors owe the tenant. HPD's page says that when gas alarms are required, the owner gives an adult in each apartment a notice about them, as with smoke and CO detectors, and keeps a record of install dates and the manufacturer's suggested end of life. In a one- or two-family house, HPD allows the owner to ask the tenant to reimburse $25 per CO or gas detector, with a year to pay. In a Class A multiple dwelling the figures are $25 per single-type detector, $50 for a two-type combination and $75 for a three-type combination.

What happens when one sounds. Fire Commissioner Lillian Bonsignore's advice in the Department of Buildings release is short: "if you smell gas or an alarm activates, leave the area immediately and call 911 from a safe location."

What Nearby Fires Show

Highland Place, Ridgewood Avenue and Miller Avenue: What Recent Fires Around Highland Park Teach a House Owner

The fires below were reported in and around Highland Park by News 12 Brooklyn and ABC7. We describe them only as those outlets did. We do not know what alarms any of these buildings had, whether any alarm worked, or what started any fire, and none of the reports say. We include them because each one points to a decision in a fire alarm plan for a house like yours.

Highland Place, November 25, 2025: a top floor in the middle of the morning. News 12 Brooklyn reported that fire tore through the top floor of a home on Highland Place between Etna Street and Ridgewood Avenue. The FDNY was called at about 9:45 a.m., 12 units and 60 fire and EMS personnel responded, and the fire was under control by 11:20 a.m. No injuries were reported. The decision it points to: a fire does not wait for night, and in a three-story house the top floor is often empty during the day. An alarm in the top-floor rooms that sounds only up there is an alarm nobody hears. Linking that floor to the floors where people spend their days is the fix.

503 Ridgewood Avenue, May 13, 2026: a first floor in the middle of the night. News 12 reported that the fire began on the first floor of a three-story building near Lincoln and Autumn Avenues just after 1:20 a.m., that 21 units and 79 fire and EMS personnel responded, that hazmat crews were called because lithium-ion batteries were found, and that a full vacate order was posted. The Red Cross helped two households, four adults and three children. The cause was under investigation. A neighbor said a resident was trying to wake everybody up. The decision it points to: in a building of more than one household, the warning should not depend on one awake person running through the halls. Alarms in the shared stair, linked where possible across the homes, are what reach the people who are asleep. And anywhere batteries are charged overnight, there should be a working smoke alarm in that room and a clear way past it.

Miller Avenue, January 2025: three attached addresses. News 12 reported a three-alarm fire on Miller Avenue in East New York, on the same street that climbs north to Highland Boulevard, in the early hours of January 6. Neighbors said it started at 185 Miller Avenue, and the report names 181 and 187 as well. Nearly 140 firefighters and EMS personnel responded, a firefighter and a 24-year-old man were seriously hurt, and the Red Cross helped four families, 17 people. The decision it points to: attached houses share walls, cocklofts and sometimes roof space. Your alarms cannot see a fire next door until smoke reaches your side, so the alarms at the top of your house, in the rooms against the party wall and in any shared roof space are the ones that buy time.

Compliance notes for Highland Park houses, apartment buildings and businesses

  • Smoke and CO in multiple dwellings. HPD requires at least one approved smoke detector in each apartment of a multiple dwelling and a CO detector in each apartment where fuel is burned, within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room used for sleeping, with an end-of-life alarm and never painted over.
  • One- and two-family houses an owner does not occupy. HPD lists a CO detector within fifteen feet of sleeping room entrances and posted CO and gas notices, and lists no smoke detector duty for these houses. We still recommend smoke alarms on every level of these houses, and the FDNY's free smoke alarm program exists to put them in homes like these.
  • Natural gas alarms from January 1, 2027. Required in every dwelling unit with gas piping, including one- and two-family homes, in the same room as the appliance, three to ten feet away, listed to UL 1484 (UL 2075 is also accepted in the Department of Buildings' October 2026 release). Hardwired units need a licensed electrical contractor and permits.
  • Battery alarms must be sealed. The Department of Buildings' bulletin on Local Law 112 of 2013 says battery-powered smoke alarms installed after that law must meet UL 217 and have a non-removable battery lasting at least ten years with an end-of-life warning. New York State has required since April 1, 2019 that smoke alarms sold in the state have a sealed ten-year battery or be hardwired.
  • Records and notices. HPD expects owners to keep the date each notice was posted, the date each device was installed, and each device's end-of-life date, for smoke, CO and gas devices alike. Our device list is built to give you exactly that.
  • Filed systems start with a design professional. The FDNY's fire alarm guide says a New York State registered engineer or architect prepares the plans and filing documents, initial review can take up to eight weeks, and the whole process from submission to final inspection averages about twenty to twenty-four weeks. Central station monitoring has to be active before the inspection is scheduled.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Highland Park: Questions Answered

These are the situations Highland Park owners, landlords and residents bring to us. Each is answered in full here and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Tall houses and the rooms at the top

The November fire on Highland Place burned a top floor in the middle of the morning. In our house everyone is downstairs during the day and the third floor is empty. How would we even know a fire had started up there?

Only if an alarm on the third floor is linked to the alarms where you spend the day. A battery smoke alarm in a top-floor room will sound when smoke reaches it, but behind a closed door and two flights of stairs it may be too faint to hear in a kitchen with the range hood on, a television going or children playing. Smoke from a fire on the top floor has nowhere higher to go, so it spreads across the ceiling up there and fills the floor before it starts down the stair, which means the people below may not smell anything for a long time. Interconnection solves it: when the third-floor alarm senses smoke, every alarm in the house sounds at once, including the ones in the kitchen and the living room. That can be done with hardwired alarms on a shared interconnect wire or with wireless interconnected alarms that talk to one another by radio. In the November 25, 2025 Highland Place fire, News 12 reported the FDNY was called at about 9:45 a.m.; the report does not say how the fire was noticed, and we do not know what alarms the house had.

The finished third floor of our house has short knee walls with little doors into the storage space behind them, under the slope of the roof. Does that hidden space need anything?

It depends on what is in it and what runs through it. Knee-wall spaces in old houses are often where the house's history ends up: holiday decorations, boxes of papers, old clothes, sometimes a stretch of old wiring or the junction box for a ceiling fixture in the room below. A fire that starts in there, or reaches it from outside through the roof, can burn out of sight behind a closed little door while the alarm in the bedroom stays quiet until smoke finds its way out. If the space holds stored things or wiring, a detector inside it, linked to the rest of the house, gives early warning that the bedroom alarm cannot. Because these spaces get very hot under a roof in summer and cold in winter, a smoke alarm is often the wrong choice there; a heat detector, or an alarm rated for the temperature range, is usually better. If the space is empty, has no wiring and the door stays shut, the alarm in the bedroom itself and the one at the top of the stair are the priority.

Our bedrooms on the second floor have transoms over the doors that we keep open in summer. Do open transoms change where the alarms should go?

Yes, in two ways. An open transom lets hot smoke from the hall flow into the top of a bedroom even when the door is shut, which can make a fire in the hall reach a sleeping person faster than you would expect, and it lets sound from the hall alarm into the room. Closed, the transom turns the bedroom back into a sealed room, and the alarm in the hall may be too faint to wake someone asleep inside. Since a transom is open part of the year and shut the rest, we plan for the worse case: a smoke alarm inside each bedroom, linked to the hall alarm, so the room is protected with the transom open or closed. We also keep the bedroom alarm away from the transom itself, because the air moving through it in summer can carry smoke past the alarm or, from a hot hallway, cause nuisance trips.

When the whole family comes for the holidays, people sleep in the den, the library and the sewing room. Do rooms that are only slept in a few nights a year count as bedrooms for the alarms?

Treat them as bedrooms. HPD's rule for multiple dwellings speaks of rooms lawfully used for sleeping, and for a private house no rule draws a line by how many nights a year a room is used. The practical point is simpler: the nights a den becomes a bedroom are often the nights a house is fullest, the kitchen busiest, the fireplace lit and the guests least familiar with the way out. A guest asleep on a sofa bed in a first-floor library is also one of the people least likely to hear an alarm on the second floor. So in a big Highland Park house, we put a linked smoke alarm in or just outside every room that sometimes serves as a bedroom, not only the rooms with beds in them year round.

Rented floors and the January 1, 2027 date

A neighbor at the Ridgewood Avenue fire said one resident was running through the building trying to wake everybody up. In our three-family, can the alarms in the three apartments be linked so that doesn't fall to one person?

They can, and in a house where three households share one stair, it is often the most valuable change an owner can make. Linking alarms across apartments means a fire in the first-floor apartment sounds the alarms on the second and third floors at the same moment, instead of leaving the people upstairs to wake up to smoke in the stair. In the May 13, 2026 fire at 503 Ridgewood Avenue, News 12 reported the fire began on the first floor of a three-story building just after 1:20 a.m., and the neighbor's account describes exactly the situation linking is meant to replace. We do not know what alarms that building had. There are trade-offs, and we go through them with owners before deciding. A linked system means one household's burned toast can wake the household upstairs, so we pick photoelectric alarms with a hush feature for kitchens, keep alarms out of the cooking area, and add heat detection in a kitchen where smoke alarms would trip constantly. And the shared stair and hall should have alarms of their own, linked to all three apartments, since that is the one route out for everyone.

Our house is being renovated and the gas company has pulled the meter, but the gas pipes are still in the walls and the stove and boiler will be reconnected in the spring. Do we still need gas alarms by January 1?

Plan to have them in before the gas comes back on, whatever the calendar says. The Department of Buildings ties the requirement to buildings with gas piping, and the January 1, 2027 date applies to dwelling units in those buildings; a house whose meter has been pulled for a renovation still has its piping, and it will have gas appliances again. How an inspector would treat a house with no gas service on the day of an inspection is a question for HPD or the Department of Buildings, and we do not want to guess at it for you. What we can say is practical: the moment gas is restored to a house that has been opened up, with new or reconnected lines, a reinstalled range and a boiler being started for the first time in months, is one of the moments a gas alarm is most useful. If the kitchen and cellar are still being finished, battery alarms listed to UL 1484 can be installed at the end of the work, three to ten feet from each appliance in the same room, before anyone sleeps in the house.

Our house is legally a two-family, but our grown children live upstairs and nobody pays rent. Do the rules for rented two-family houses apply to us?

The HPD duties for one- and two-family houses are written for houses the owner does not occupy, and you occupy yours, so HPD's CO detector rule for non-owner-occupied private dwellings likely does not reach you the way it would reach a landlord. Whether a particular family arrangement counts as owner-occupied is a legal question, and if it matters for a sale, insurance or a dispute, an attorney should answer it. The gas alarm requirement is broader: the Department of Buildings says owners of private dwellings with gas piping, including one- and two-family homes, must have natural gas alarms in every dwelling unit by January 1, 2027. A two-family has two dwelling units, so both your floor and your children's floor need them where there are gas appliances. Beyond the rules, a family two-family is exactly the house where we would link the two floors: the people upstairs are your own children, and you want them to hear a fire in your kitchen at night, and you to hear one in theirs.

The park, the woods and the power

Our house faces the woods of Highland Park. If a brush fire started over there at night, would anything in our house tell us?

Not reliably, and it is not what household alarms are for. Smoke alarms, heat detectors and CO alarms sense conditions inside the house. A fire in the woods across the street would only reach them if smoke came in through open windows or vents, and by then the people inside would likely smell it anyway. We are not aware of a reported fire in Highland Park itself or around the Ridgewood Reservoir, so we make no claim about the risk there. What we can tell you is what the FDNY has said about brush fires citywide: in an October 23, 2025 announcement, it described a historic number of brush fire incidents in 2024, a Brush Fire Task Force, and new smoke and flame detection cameras in Van Cortlandt Park, Edgecombe Park, Highbridge Park and the Marine Park Golf Course, with more placements under review. Highland Park was not on that list. For your house, the useful steps are ordinary ones: if you see smoke or flames in the park, call 911 rather than assuming someone else has, and keep windows on the park side closed when there is smoke outside.

Our house has an old laundry chute that drops from the second-floor hall straight into the cellar. Does that change anything for the alarms?

Yes, because a laundry chute is an open shaft between the cellar and the bedroom floor. A fire in the cellar, where the boiler, the dryer and stored things usually are, can push hot smoke up the chute and out of its door into the upstairs hall, sometimes before smoke has come up the cellar stair at all. And a fire that starts in a pile of laundry at the bottom of the chute is exactly the kind that smolders unseen. We plan for it in three ways. A smoke alarm in the upstairs hall, already part of any plan, is placed so that smoke coming out of the chute door reaches it quickly, but not right beside the door, where the draft could carry smoke past it. In the cellar, a detector near the bottom of the chute and the dryer, chosen for a dusty, linty space, which often means heat rather than smoke. And all of it linked, so the cellar device sounds upstairs. It is also worth making sure the chute door upstairs closes and latches, and that the bottom is not packed with laundry against a light fixture or the dryer vent. If the chute is no longer used, closing it off at the top is a job for a contractor, and one that removes a path for smoke into the bedroom floor.

The kitchen alarm goes off when we sear meat, and someone always pulls it down. Is it safe to just press the hush button instead?

Pressing the hush button is far safer than pulling the alarm down, and it is what the button is for. On most current smoke alarms, the hush or silence button quiets the alarm for several minutes while the kitchen clears, then the alarm returns to normal on its own. The danger with pulling it down is that it rarely goes back up the same day, and an alarm on the counter or in a drawer protects nobody. If an alarm trips every time you cook, though, the alarm is in the wrong place or is the wrong type. We usually move the kitchen smoke alarm farther from the stove, toward the door that leads to the rest of the house, choose a photoelectric model with a hush button, and, in a kitchen where even that is too sensitive, add a heat detector over the cooking area linked to the rest of the house.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Highland Park

AI answers and national guides from sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for a typical American house. These are the shortcuts that mislead Highland Park owners and tenants most often.

"One smoke alarm per floor covers a house"

A floor of a Highland Park house can hold four bedrooms off a long hall with heavy doors, and the third floor may be two flights above the people who need to hear it.

An alarm in the hall of each floor, unlinked, is the setup that lets a cellar fire grow while the top of the house sleeps, or a top-floor fire burn while the family downstairs hears nothing.

What protects a tall house is an alarm inside each bedroom and at the top of each flight, all linked, and a test from both ends of the house with every door closed.

"Every rental has the same smoke detector rules"

HPD's own page draws lines that national guides do not. Multiple dwellings need a smoke detector and, where fuel is burned, a CO detector in each apartment, within fifteen feet of each sleeping room.

For a one- or two-family house the owner does not live in, HPD lists a CO duty and posted notices but no smoke detector duty. In Highland Park, where houses range from one family to three, that difference is real.

The gas alarm rule cuts across all of them: from January 1, 2027, every dwelling unit with gas piping needs one, the Department of Buildings says, including one- and two-family homes.

"A carbon monoxide alarm also warns you about a gas leak"

It does not. A CO alarm senses carbon monoxide, which comes from burning fuel. Natural gas that leaks before it burns is a different substance, and a CO alarm will not report it.

That is why New York City now requires separate natural gas alarms listed to UL 1484, in the room with the gas appliance, three to ten feet from it, under the Department of Buildings' rule.

A house with a gas boiler and a gas range needs both kinds: CO near the bedrooms and the boiler, and gas alarms in the kitchen and the boiler room.

"Battery alarms are fine, just change the battery every year"

New York changed that. Since April 1, 2019, the state has required that smoke alarms sold here have a sealed ten-year battery or be hardwired, and the Department of Buildings' bulletin on Local Law 112 of 2013 says battery alarms installed after that law must have a non-removable ten-year battery and an end-of-life warning.

Alarms also wear out. HPD expects owners of rental housing to replace detectors when their useful life expires and to keep records of each device's end-of-life date.

In older Highland Park houses we often find removable-battery alarms of unknown age. The answer is replacement with dated, sealed or hardwired units, not a fresh nine-volt.

"Wireless linked alarms work in any house"

Wireless interconnected alarms are often the best answer in a finished old house, because they avoid opening plaster between floors. But early-1900s houses can have masonry walls, old plaster ceilings and a cellar enclosed by heavy foundation walls.

A link that works across an open floor plan can fail between a cellar and a third floor, and the failure is silent until the day one alarm sounds and the others do not.

We test every wireless link in place, from the farthest points of the house, before relying on it, and run cable where radio does not reach reliably.

"Installing smoke alarms is a quick DIY job"

Screwing a sealed battery alarm to a ceiling is simple, and the FDNY's free program will install smoke alarms for residents who request it. For one apartment, that may be all that is needed.

A whole Highland Park house is different: deciding which rooms need what, linking four levels, placing gas alarms by the Department of Buildings' distances, tracing an old hardwired chain, and leaving the dated records HPD expects from a landlord.

Hardwired work also has a legal side. The Department of Buildings says hardwired gas alarms need a licensed electrical contractor and permits, and the same goes for new 120-volt wiring for smoke alarms.

"The fire department will hear about it if the alarm goes off"

Household smoke and CO alarms make noise in the house. They do not call anyone. Unless a house has a monitored system, a fire in an empty house is reported only when a neighbor sees it or a passerby calls 911.

For a house that sits empty all day, or a store under the J that is empty all night, central station monitoring is what turns an alarm into a call, through our partners at $30 a month with a keypad or $45 with the app.

And for filed commercial systems, the FDNY's guide says the central station connection must be active before the final inspection can even be scheduled.

FAQ

Highland Park Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

We keep gasoline for the mower and cans of paint thinner in the cellar. Does that change the cellar detector?
+

It changes the storage more than the detector. Flammable liquids belong in small, closed, labeled containers, away from the boiler, the water heater and the dryer, ideally in a metal cabinet. For detection, a heat detector near that storage area, linked to the rest of the house, will report a fast-growing fire without tripping on fumes the way a smoke alarm might. The cellar smoke alarm at the bottom of the stair still stays.

We added a wood stove in the cellar. Which alarms belong near it?
+

A carbon monoxide alarm in the cellar, kept several feet from the stove, and CO alarms within fifteen feet of every bedroom upstairs, since CO from a stove or a blocked flue travels through the house. A heat detector near the stove, rather than a smoke alarm right above it, avoids false alarms when the fire is lit. Link them to the rest of the house so a problem in the cellar is heard on every floor.

We can't find any date on our old smoke alarms. How do we know how old they are?
+

Most alarms print a manufacture date on the back or side, which you see only after twisting the unit off its base. If there is no date at all, or the label has faded, treat the alarm as past its useful life and replace it. HPD expects landlords to keep each device's install date and end-of-life date on record, and a new alarm with a known date makes that simple.

One of us sleeps with a white noise machine and earplugs. Are there alarms that wake heavy sleepers?
+

Yes. Some alarms and accessories use a lower-pitched tone that many people wake to more easily, and others add a bed shaker or a strobe in the bedroom, triggered when the linked alarms sound. The first step is simpler: put a linked smoke alarm inside that bedroom, not only in the hall, and test it with the machine running and the door shut to hear what actually gets through.

We put up a real Christmas tree in the front parlor every December. Should anything change with the alarms that month?
+

Make sure the parlor and the hall outside it have working linked smoke alarms before the tree goes up, and test them that week. Keep the tree watered, away from radiators, the fireplace and space heaters, and turn the lights off at night and when you leave. A dry tree burns very fast, so the alarm that matters most is the one that reaches the bedrooms upstairs.

If the CO alarm goes off, will it shut down our boiler?
+

A household CO alarm does not. It sounds a warning and, if linked, sets off the other alarms in the house; it does not control the boiler. Burner shutdown devices are part of the heating system and are a question for your heating contractor. When a CO alarm sounds, get everyone out, call 911 from outside, and do not go back in until the FDNY or the utility says it is safe.

Should a tall house like ours have a family fire escape plan, and what should it include?
+

Yes. Walk every bedroom and agree on two ways out of each, a meeting place outside such as a particular tree or neighbor's gate, and who helps any child or older person on the top floor. Practice it once with the alarms sounding, at night if you can. In a three-story house, the people on the top floor have the longest way down, and the plan should start with them.

Our house is detached. Does it need fewer alarms than an attached house on a row?
+

Usually the same number inside, since alarms follow bedrooms, stairs and fuel-burning appliances, not the walls you share. What changes is the outside risk. An attached house can take fire or smoke from a neighbor through a shared wall or roof space, so we pay extra attention to the top floor and party walls. A detached house loses that risk but often has more rooms and more distance for the warning to cover.

Our hardwired alarms are an old brand, and the plugs on new alarms don't match. Do the wires have to be replaced?
+

Usually not. Most manufacturers sell adapters for common older connectors, and when none fits, the electrician fits a new connector to the existing wires at the ceiling box. What matters more is that every linked alarm in the chain is compatible with the others, so we replace the whole chain with one family of alarms rather than mixing brands that may not signal each other.

Should we keep a fire extinguisher along with the alarms, and where?
+

A small extinguisher in the kitchen, near the door rather than beside the stove, and another in the cellar near the stair is a sensible habit, as long as everyone in the house knows the alarm means get out first. An extinguisher is for a small fire you can fight with a clear exit behind you. Alarms are what tell you whether you have that chance.

Our 1910 house probably has lead paint under the newer coats. How do you drill without spreading dust?
+

We treat painted surfaces in a house that old as if they could contain lead. Holes are kept to the few a device or cable needs, drilled with a vacuum shroud at the bit, over a drop cloth, and the area is wiped and vacuumed before we move on. Sealed battery and wireless alarms need only small screw holes, which is one more reason they suit finished old houses.

If one of the new alarms stops working in its first year, who replaces it, and how fast?
+

For devices we supply, call us and we replace a defective unit under the product warranty described in the terms above. If you are a landlord, HPD's rule is that a detector that becomes inoperable within a year of installation because of a defect, not caused by the tenant, must be replaced by the owner within 30 days, so keep that clock in mind and call as soon as a tenant reports it.
