The Neighborhood

Why Riverdale Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Riverdale was built in layers, and each layer asks something different of a camera. The oldest layer is the estate suburb: the New York Preservation Archive Project describes a hundred-acre parcel bought in 1852 for suburban development by wealthy businessmen and split into seven estates near the railroad, the start of what it calls the earliest known railroad suburb of New York City. The Riverdale Historic District that protects part of that land was designated on October 16, 1990. Fieldston, laid out on the former Delafield estate with privately owned streets, followed in the twentieth century and became a historic district of its own in 2006. The Henry Hudson Parkway, completed in the 1930s, opened the area to middle-class buyers. Then came the apartment layers: Wikipedia records many six-story brick buildings in the 1950s and a run of larger brick towers of ten to twenty stories by the early to mid-1960s, many of them full-service doorman buildings, most of which went co-op by the 1980s, followed by a wave of new condominiums in Central Riverdale after 2005. Today the sections people name are Central Riverdale, Spuyten Duyvil with its cluster of high-rises, Hudson Hill, Fieldston, North Riverdale with its houses and small apartment buildings, and Villanova Heights, bounded roughly by Yonkers to the north, Van Cortlandt Park and Broadway to the east and the Hudson to the west.

One fact changes the whole conversation about cameras here. Wikipedia notes that, unlike most of the Bronx, Riverdale is mostly owner-occupied, and the NYU Furman Center puts the 2024 homeownership rate in Riverdale/Fieldston at 38.4 percent, above the citywide 32.7 percent. In a borough where most camera calls come from landlords, a Riverdale call usually comes from someone who owns what the camera will be mounted on: a homeowner, a co-op board, a condo association or a shareholder asking the board for permission. That person is paying for the footage and will live with the choices for years. It also means more people to agree. The Association of Riverdale Cooperatives and Condominiums counts more than a hundred member buildings, and in almost all of them an exterior camera, a hallway camera or a lobby change goes through the board. On the private-house side, a Riverdale Press op-ed this year observed that a Ring camera beside the front door has become as common here as a mailbox or a porch light, and warned that because these cameras often record sidewalks and neighboring property, people can be filmed without knowing it. Both points are true at once, and a good camera plan respects both.

The crime picture is calmer than most of the borough, and we would rather say so plainly. The Furman Center counts serious property crime in Riverdale/Fieldston at 7.6 per thousand residents in 2024, down from 10.6 the year before. What the numbers hide is the pattern the local reporting keeps returning to. News 12 reported a man on Arlington Avenue whose car, parked in his own driveway, was entered in February, March, May and September, the thief usually arriving between 4 and 6 a.m. in sweats, slippers and a baseball cap. CBS New York showed a thief on Fieldston Road trying a truck door that turned out to be unlocked, and a neighbor whose van was entered in her driveway behind a closed gate. In July 2026 the Riverdale Press reported residents calling for stronger police action after a series of car and catalytic converter thefts, after the 50th Precinct had offered free catalytic converter etching in May. In North Riverdale, a resident at West 260th Street and Tyndall Avenue watched his own Ring camera show someone taking his Amazon package on a Sunday night. Nextdoor posts describe a package delivered to a supposedly locked room in a building and gone when the resident got there. And on Riverdale Avenue, News 12 described two masked men breaking into Bokaguá Restaurant at about 4:35 a.m., one ripping the register from the bar while the other waited outside with a backpack.

Layered on top of all this is paperwork that most Bronx neighborhoods never see. Inside the Riverdale and Fieldston historic districts, the Landmarks Preservation Commission requires a permit before work begins, and its permit guidebook lists security cameras among the equipment it reviews. The whole Riverdale ridge, including Spuyten Duyvil and Fieldston, is mapped as a Special Natural Area District that protects steep slopes, rock outcrops and mature trees. Co-op proprietary leases and alteration agreements set their own rules, and the state's installer licensing law sets who may do the work for pay. No city law requires cameras in houses or apartment buildings, so every camera here is a choice. Our job is to make that choice pay off with footage someone can use, installed in a way the board, the Commission and the neighbors can accept.

Riverdale facts that decide where a camera goes

  • Owners, not just landlords: a 38.4 percent homeownership rate in Riverdale/Fieldston, per the Furman Center, means boards and homeowners make most camera decisions.
  • Towers with doormen: buildings such as the 13-story, 129-apartment co-op at 2400 Johnson Avenue (1969) and the 20-story, 339-unit tower at 2500 Johnson Avenue (1967) are typical of the stock, with lobby desks, garages and package rooms.
  • Two historic districts: the Riverdale Historic District holds 34 buildings on the old estates served by the Sycamore Avenue carriage alley; the Fieldston district covers about 250 houses on private streets.
  • A protected ridge: the Zoning Resolution describes the Riverdale Ridge as a place of steep slopes, rock outcrops and mature trees, all protected under the Special Natural Area District.
  • Thefts from driveways before dawn: the Arlington Avenue break-ins, the Fieldston Road case and the 2026 catalytic converter complaints all involve cars parked on private property.
  • New buildings still arriving: the Furman Center counts 2,087 apartments in buildings of four or more units built in Riverdale/Fieldston between 2010 and 2025, and construction of the 11-story River's Edge senior building began on the Hebrew Home campus in 2026.

What Riverdale owners tend to add next. Once the lobby or driveway cameras are working, boards often ask whether the street-door camera can show on the intercom handsets upstairs, and homeowners ask whether the gate can be opened from the same phone app that shows it. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an entry upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the door or gate, so the cellar ceiling or the soffit only has to be opened once.

Before Sunrise

The Hour Before Dawn: What a Riverdale Driveway Camera Has to Get Right

Look at when the Riverdale car cases happened and a pattern jumps out. The Arlington Avenue owner told News 12 the thief struck between 4 and 6 a.m. The Riverdale Avenue restaurant burglary was at about 4:35 a.m. These are the hours when a driveway is darkest, porch lights are off or dim, the street is empty and the household is asleep. They are also the hours most cameras handle worst. A camera that looks excellent at noon can, before dawn, drop into grainy black and white, blur anyone walking briskly, flare against a single porch bulb, or stare down from a high eave at the roof of a car and the top of a baseball cap. Choosing a better brand helps less than people expect. What decides whether a pre-dawn clip shows a face, a gait and a direction of travel is a handful of settings and placements that have to be chosen deliberately, house by house. Light the car, not the lens

Most Riverdale houses already have a driveway or garage light, and many have motion floodlights. A floodlight that suddenly blazes when someone steps into the drive often does more harm to the recording than good: the camera's exposure jumps, the first second or two goes white, and the person's face is lost in the change. A steady, modest light that stays on through the night, shaded so it falls on the car and the path rather than toward the camera, keeps most modern cameras in color mode all night. We look at what you have and usually re-aim or re-shade it rather than add a new fixture. Where a new outdoor light or outlet is needed, that is a licensed electrician's work, and the quote says so. Inside the historic districts, a new or changed exterior fixture can be a Landmarks matter of its own, so we raise that before anyone buys one.

Angle along the driveway, not down onto it

The temptation on a Tudor or Colonial Revival house is to tuck the camera high under a deep eave, where it is safe and hidden. That position is excellent for an overview of the drive and the street beyond, and poor for identification, because the camera looks steeply down. On most Riverdale driveways we pair two cameras: a high eave camera that shows the whole drive, the cars and the street entrance, and a lower camera on the corner of the house or the garage, looking along the drive at about head height, that records each person walking toward the cars. The low camera is the one that turns a theft report into a description. It is hard-cased, mounted on a solid surface, and cabled so the wire is not exposed where it could be reached and cut.

Alerts that know the hour

The Arlington Avenue owner's pattern, four visits in eight months, is the case for an alert rather than just a recording. We draw a detection zone around the cars themselves, set it to look for people rather than any motion, and switch it on only between, say, midnight and six. During those hours a person stepping into that zone sends a short clip to the phones you choose, and can, if you want, turn on an indoor light or chime. Daytime and evening, when family, guests and delivery drivers come and go, the zone stays quiet while recording continues. The aim is an alert you never learn to ignore.

Catalytic converters and the view under the car

Converter thieves work low and fast, often from the side of the car facing away from the house. A camera that sees only the side of the car nearest the door misses them entirely. Where the driveway layout allows, we place the low camera so it looks across the gap between the car and the property line, not just along the near side, and we make sure the light reaches that gap. Etching the converter, as the 50th Precinct offered in May, helps tie a recovered part to your car; a clear clip of the person helps tie the person to the theft. The two work together.

Where Boxes Disappear

Porch Steps, Lobby Desks and Locked Rooms: Following a Riverdale Package

A package in Riverdale passes through very different hands depending on where you live. At a North Riverdale house it sits on a porch step or behind a storm door until someone comes home, and the West 260th Street theft shows how quickly that window can be used. At a doorman tower it may be signed for at the desk, carried to a back room, or left by a courier in the vestibule when the doorman is busy. In a six-story building without staff, it may go into a room or a set of lockers that residents open with a key or a code, and the Nextdoor resident whose box vanished from a supposedly locked room shows that a lock alone does not settle much. Each stop needs a camera placed for that stop, because a camera at the front door cannot tell you which resident took which box from a shelf twenty feet away.

At the house: the step, not the street

A doorbell camera mounted beside the door often looks straight past the package, which is sitting on the step below it or on the porch floor to one side. On Riverdale houses with deep porches, stone steps or a front walk that climbs from the sidewalk, we add a small camera under the porch ceiling or on the house wall that looks down at the step where deliveries actually land, framed tightly so it records a face leaning over the box. It records continuously, and a person alert in that tight zone tells you when someone other than the expected driver bends down. Keeping the frame on the step also keeps the sidewalk and the house across the street out of the picture, which answers the privacy worry the Riverdale Press op-ed raised.

In a staffed lobby: the desk and the room behind it

In a doorman building the weak moments are the doorman's absences: walking a delivery up, helping a resident to a car, opening the garage. A camera on the vestibule from inside, recording a clear face at the inner door, and a second on any package shelf or back room, cover those minutes. The doorman's monitor shows both, and the recorder keeps a record of who signed for what, so a dispute weeks later can be settled from the footage rather than from memory.

In an unstaffed package room: the shelf and the door, from inside

Where residents let themselves in, the camera belongs inside the room, mounted high in a corner opposite the door, so it records each person's face as they enter and the shelves they reach into. A second view of the shelves themselves, at an angle that shows which section a box was taken from, settles most disputes. Lockers get a similar treatment: one camera that sees the face of the person standing at the locker bank. These cameras record continuously, and the board or the owner decides in writing who may review them. Because the room is a common area, in a co-op or condo the board approves it; in a rental the owner does.

Doorman towers: one security closet, many floors, many hands on the system

A Riverdale tower of the 1960s usually has its street entrance on the high side of the lot, a garage cut into the slope below, a service entrance somewhere along the back, and a cellar that ties them together. We look for a locked room near the lobby or in the cellar to hold the recorder, switch and battery unit, well away from the boiler and above any spot that has taken water. Cable for the lobby, vestibule and package room travels across the cellar ceiling and comes up through a small number of sealed openings; cable to upper-floor landings rides an existing riser, a pipe chase or a closet stack rather than the face of a hallway wall. Elevator cab cameras are a separate job: the signal has to travel down the hoistway in the elevator's own traveling cable or by a method the elevator contractor approves, and we coordinate with that contractor rather than improvise. Because doormen, porters, the super, the managing agent and board officers all touch the system, we set up separate accounts with different permissions and write down who holds each one.

Six-story brick buildings: no staff, so the cameras do the watching

The 1950s six-story buildings that line parts of Riverdale Avenue and the side streets rarely have a doorman. That makes the vestibule and package area the most important views in the building, followed by the cellar door, the rear yard and the elevator. The CBS report of a burglar who climbed a fire escape to a window with a broken lock in a building near Johnson Avenue and West 236th Street, a building that already had cameras front and back, is a reminder that rear and side approaches matter as much as the street door. Cable usually reaches the street door through the cellar ceiling and the landings through a stair or chase stack, and the recorder sits in a locked cellar room or the super's office. Alerts go to the super or the managing agent after hours.

Historic-district houses: stay off the stone, come in from the attic

The villas and estate houses around Independence Avenue, Sycamore Avenue and the Hudson Hill slopes are built of fieldstone, brick or stucco, often with deep wooden eaves and soffits. We mount cameras on the wood soffit or trim rather than drilling into stone faces, because a screw hole in a soffit board is small, hidden and easy to reverse, while a hole in historic stone is permanent. Cable comes down from the attic inside the soffit, or up from the cellar through an existing opening, so nothing shows on the facade. Cameras are chosen in a color that matches the trim. The Riverdale Historic District rules treat pre-1940 buildings as significant architecture and regulate walls, gates, gate posts, hedges, mature trees and excavation on slopes over 15 percent, so a gate camera or a cable run across a sloping lawn is planned with the Landmarks permit in mind from the first visit.

Fieldston and North Riverdale houses: garages, porches and private streets

Fieldston's houses, designed by many different architects in Colonial Revival, Tudor, Mediterranean and modernist styles, sit on streets owned by the Fieldston Property Owners Association, which runs its own security patrol. North Riverdale's private homes sit on ordinary city streets. In both, the camera work centers on the driveway and the garage, the front walk, the porch and the back door. Attached garages give an easy cable path through the garage ceiling; detached garages need a short buried or overhead run, planned around trees and, inside the Special Natural Area District, around protected slopes and roots. Inside the Fieldston historic district, exterior cameras go through Landmarks, and the district master plan encourages owners to work closely with City Planning and Landmarks staff.

A restaurant system built only around the register would have recorded half of that. The core views for a Riverdale Avenue restaurant are a camera just inside the front door at head height, recording one face per person entering; a camera over the bar and register from behind and slightly to the side; an overview of the dining room; and a camera on the back door and kitchen entrance from inside. Then add one camera behind the front glass looking out at the sidewalk and the curb, which records anyone waiting outside and the direction a group leaves in. Bottles and the register often go out in a bag, and a sidewalk view shows which way. The recorder goes in a locked office or closet out of reach from the bar, on battery backup, with the clock synced.

Banks, pharmacies and food shops on Johnson Avenue: busy counters, apartments above

Johnson Avenue's ground floors sit under apartment buildings, so the store's cameras share a facade, a cellar and sometimes a service door with the residential building. We keep the store's cameras on the store's doors, counters and stock rooms and away from the residential entrance next door, and we ask for the building owner's written agreement before mounting anything on the facade or in the shared cellar. Pharmacy counters need the register and the pickup area covered without recording prescription labels or screens, which we handle by aiming and by blacking out fixed areas of the picture in the camera. Kosher butchers and grocers often have walk-in coolers; a camera on the cooler door from outside the cold room avoids fogging and records who goes in.

Congregations, schools and campuses: alerts for the hours nobody is there

Riverdale has a large Jewish community and many synagogues and churches, along with independent schools and colleges spread across the ridge. Buildings like these are busy for a few hours and empty for many more. Their camera plans usually start at the entrances, the parking area and any side or rear door used for deliveries, then add the lobby or narthex and the office. The real design question is who receives alerts and when: for a congregation whose staff do not use phones on the Sabbath, alerts can route to a non-observant board member or a contracted guard during those hours. For schools, cameras stay out of classrooms, bathrooms and locker rooms, and the school's own policy sets who may view footage.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Riverdale: Questions Answered

Cars, driveways and gates

The Arlington Avenue thief came back to the same car four times, always between 4 and 6 a.m. If someone keeps returning, should the camera warn us while it is happening rather than just record it?

Yes, and a repeat visitor is exactly the case for it. Recording alone produces a collection of clips you watch the next morning. A live alert, limited to the small hours and to a tight zone around the cars, tells you within seconds that a person is standing next to your car at 4:40 a.m., when you can turn on a light, call 911 or both. We set person detection rather than general motion so cats, leaves and headlights do not trigger it, and we keep recording continuous so the approach and the departure are both on file. After the second visit, the clips from each night can be compared side by side for clothing, gait and direction, which is what turns a pattern into something the 50th Precinct can act on.

Our house sits far back from the road at the top of a long driveway. Can a camera near the street let us know a car is coming up before it reaches the house?

It can. A camera at or near the bottom of the drive, aimed along it, can send an alert when a vehicle crosses a line you draw just inside the entrance, and can trigger a chime in the house or a notification on your phones. Where the bottom of the drive is far from the house, the picture comes back over a buried cable, a cable run inside an existing conduit, or a point-to-point wireless link mounted on the house and a post, depending on distance, trees and slope. On the Riverdale ridge, any new trench or post footing has to respect the Special Natural Area District, so we look first for existing conduit or a route along the driveway edge before suggesting new digging.

The old phone line that ran our gate intercom down the drive is being retired. How does a new gate camera get its picture back to the house?

The old copper pair cannot carry a modern camera picture, so it needs a new path. If there is conduit from the gate to the house, we pull outdoor-rated network cable through it, which carries both picture and power. If there is no conduit, the options are a new buried run, which in a historic district or on a protected slope needs planning and possibly approvals, or a wireless bridge between the gate post and the house, which avoids digging but needs a clear line of sight through the trees. Many owners replace the gate intercom and add the camera at the same time, so one run or one link serves both.

Porches and package rooms

When the North Riverdale resident reported his stolen package, police said the report would close right away because the box was worth under a thousand dollars. If theft that small is not investigated, what is a porch clip actually good for?

More than it seems. A clear clip makes a carrier or retailer claim straightforward, because it shows the delivery and the theft with times. It lets neighbors on the block recognize the same person or vehicle when it comes back, and a run of clips showing the same person at several houses is the kind of pattern police do follow up. It also changes what you do next: once you know thefts happen on Sunday evenings, for example, you can redirect deliveries or ask for a signature. For any of that, the clip needs a face, not a hat, which means a camera framed on the step where the box sits.

Only residents can open our building's package room, and boxes still go missing. What should a camera in that room show so the board can tell who took what?

Two things: the face of each person entering, and the shelf they reach into. One camera high in the corner opposite the door records the face; a second, angled across the shelves, shows which section a box came from. With the clock synced and the clip time matched to the carrier's delivery record, the board can see which box arrived when and who handled it. The room should also be lit well enough that both cameras stay in color. In a co-op or condo, the board approves the cameras and adopts a short written rule on who may review the footage, so the record is used for disputes and not for curiosity.

Towers, lobbies and power

Our doorman regularly steps away to bring packages up or help residents with cars. What keeps watch over the lobby in those minutes?

A camera on the vestibule from inside, recording a clear face at the inner door, and a camera on the desk and the package shelf, cover the gap. If your building's intercom or access system can alert the doorman's handset or a phone when the street door opens, the doorman knows someone has come in while he is upstairs. When he returns, the desk monitor shows the last few minutes at a glance. Recording continues all the while, so anything that happened in his absence can be reviewed and saved.

How does a camera inside an elevator cab in a twenty-story tower send its picture down to the recorder in the cellar?

The cab moves, so the signal cannot travel on a normal cable fixed to a wall. It goes down the hoistway either in spare conductors of the elevator's traveling cable or over a wireless link designed for elevator shafts, and which one is used depends on the elevator and what its maintenance contractor approves. Work inside the hoistway is done with the elevator contractor present. We plan the cab camera with the managing agent and the elevator company, mount a hard-cased dome in a corner of the cab ceiling, and bring the picture to the recorder like any other camera.

During this July's heat wave Con Edison reduced voltage across parts of the northwest Bronx, including Riverdale, and our recorder kept restarting. Why does that happen, and what stops it?

News 12 reported that Con Edison cut voltage by 8 percent in parts of the northwest Bronx, including Riverdale, while about a thousand Riverdale customers lost power entirely. A recorder and a switch powering many cameras can draw more current as voltage sags, and some power supplies drop out and restart. A battery backup unit of the right type, one that corrects low voltage rather than just switching to battery in a full outage, holds the recorder, switch and modem steady through a sag and carries them through a short outage. We size it to the actual load of your system and test it before we leave.

Historic districts and the ridge

Our front hedge is part of the historic landscape and blocks the camera's view of the gate. Can we just trim it back for the camera?

Not without checking first. The Landmarks rules for the Riverdale Historic District regulate any change that affects a hedge or a mature tree, along with walls, gates and gate posts, and work cannot begin until the Commission has issued a permit. Routine maintenance and a substantial cutback are different things, and the line between them belongs to Landmarks, not to us. Often the better answer is to move the camera: a position on the house or gate post that looks through the gap in the hedge, or a low camera inside the gate looking out, gets the view without touching the planting.

We want a camera on a short post near our gate. On a lot inside the Special Natural Area District, does digging a footing for the post need City Planning's approval?

It may. The Zoning Resolution defines a site alteration in the Special Natural Area District to include excavating and land contour work whether or not any other agency requires a permit, and some small works on qualifying lots are exempt. Whether a post footing on your lot is exempt depends on the lot's size and slope and on what lies around the post, such as a protected tree's roots or a rock outcrop. Before anyone digs, we look at the site and, where it is not clearly exempt, recommend checking with the Bronx office of City Planning. Mounting the camera on an existing gate post or the house often avoids the question entirely.

Shops and congregations

In the Riverdale Avenue restaurant break-in, one man pulled the register off the bar while a second waited outside with a backpack. How do our cameras record the person outside, not just the one inside?

By adding a view that looks out. A camera mounted inside the front window, aimed at the sidewalk and curb in front of the restaurant, records anyone loitering outside, any vehicle that pulls up and the direction people leave in. Placed inside the glass, it keeps recording even if the door is smashed. Combined with a head-height camera inside the door and one over the bar, it shows the whole sequence: who waited, who went in, what they took and which way they went. Exterior lighting under the awning, if the building owner agrees, keeps that outward view in color before dawn.

The storefront next to ours on Riverdale Avenue has been empty for months. Can our camera keep an eye on its doorway too?

Partly. Your camera can include the stretch of sidewalk in front of your own store and the edge of the next doorway where it falls naturally in that view, which is public space anyone can see. It should not be aimed into the empty store or mounted on its facade without the building owner's agreement. If the vacant doorway draws people sheltering or dumping, tell the landlord or managing agent, who may want a camera of their own; in many buildings the same owner controls both storefronts, and a shared plan makes sense.

Our synagogue's staff do not use phones from Friday evening to Saturday night. Who should get the camera alerts then?

Someone who can act on them. Recording continues on its own, with no one touching anything. For alerts, the recorder can send notifications to different people on a schedule: staff during the week, and over the Sabbath a non-observant board member, a contracted guard service or a neighbor who has agreed to watch. Alerts can be limited to the times the building should be empty, and to the doors that matter most. We set the schedule once, test it on a weekday, and write down who is on it so it can be updated when people change.

Boards, Landmarks and the Law

The Rules Behind a Riverdale Camera Job, Laid Out Plainly

Riverdale has more layers of permission than any other part of the Bronx, and most of them are easy to handle once you know which apply. They come from five places: the state's installer licensing law, the Landmarks Preservation Commission, the Special Natural Area District, the co-op or condo documents, and New York's privacy laws.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires a license for anyone in the business of installing or servicing security systems, and a camera system used to watch for intrusion or theft falls under that rule. Owners putting cameras on their own property are not in that business, so a homeowner who mounts a doorbell is not breaking any law. A contractor doing it for pay should have a license. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, and we send a copy to any board, managing agent or homeowner who asks. New line-voltage power, such as a new outdoor outlet or a circuit for a gate, is electrician's work, and the quote says so when a job needs it.

Landmarks. If a building is an individual landmark or sits in the Riverdale or Fieldston historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission requires a permit before any work begins. The Commission's permit guidebook includes a chapter on health and safety equipment that lists security cameras, lighting and intercom panels, and it explains that staff can approve cameras that meet the criteria in its rules, which in practice favors small, discreet cameras with concealed cable placed so they do not harm significant features. In the Riverdale Historic District, the rules also cover walls, gates, gate posts, hedges, mature trees and excavation on slopes over 15 percent, and they treat the style of lights and fixtures on pre-1940 buildings as significant. We prepare the photos and the mounting details your application needs and wait for the permit before installing anything visible.

The ridge. The Special Natural Area District covering Riverdale, Spuyten Duyvil and Fieldston protects steep slopes, rock outcrops, ponds and mature trees. The Riverdale Nature Preservancy notes that trees of six-inch caliper and larger are protected and that any modification of a rock outcrop needs City Planning Commission authorization. A camera on a house is not affected. A trench or post footing on a sloping lot can be, so we plan cable routes along existing paths and conduits wherever possible.

Co-ops and condos. Most co-op proprietary leases require board approval for exterior cameras and any change to a building's appearance, and hallways are common areas under the board's control, so a shareholder cannot add a camera there alone. A board-approved project usually runs through the managing agent and an alteration or contractor agreement that names insurance requirements. A condo board plays a similar role. We work inside those documents and supply what they ask for.

Where a camera may look. New York does not prohibit recording what can be seen from public, such as driveways, porches and sidewalks. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives residents a claim against a neighbor's camera aimed where they reasonably expect privacy, and Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places like bathrooms and bedrooms without consent. On Riverdale's hillsides, where a house above can look down into a neighbor's garden or windows, we frame every view on your own driveway, gate, step and curb, and black out anything else. We set cameras to record video, not sound.

Is anyone required to have cameras? No general city law requires cameras in houses or apartment buildings. Buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a working two-way intercom, which is a separate requirement many Riverdale towers already meet.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Riverdale

Quick answers from search tools, AI summaries and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to treat Riverdale either as a generic Bronx apartment district or as a quiet suburb where cameras are a formality. These are claims we see repeated about cameras here, written as summaries rather than quotations, and what the sources and the work show instead.

"Riverdale is the safest part of the Bronx, so cameras are optional."

There is truth in it. The 50th Precinct, which covers Riverdale, Kingsbridge and Marble Hill, reports some of the lowest major-crime counts in the borough, and the Furman Center shows serious property crime falling from 10.6 to 7.6 per thousand residents between 2023 and 2024.

But the thefts that do happen here are concentrated where cameras help most: cars in driveways, catalytic converters, packages on porches and in building rooms, and a restaurant register before dawn. The Arlington Avenue owner's car was entered four times in a year.

Low crime is a reason to keep a system modest and well aimed, not a reason to leave the driveway unrecorded.

"Everyone already has a Ring doorbell, and that covers it."

The Riverdale Press op-ed is right that doorbell cameras are everywhere, and a doorbell does a good job at a single front door.

It does not see the driveway, the gate at the bottom of a long walk, the garage, the back of the house, a building's package room or a tower's garage ramp. Many doorbells record short clips only when they sense motion, and in the hour before dawn that can mean a clip that starts after the car door is already open.

For most Riverdale houses the doorbell is the first camera, not the system.

"Published Bronx price ranges tell you what a Riverdale system costs."

Installer directories and cost sites publish wide ranges for Bronx residential and commercial systems, and they are not wrong as averages.

The cost of a Riverdale job is decided by cable paths and paperwork more than by camera count: a run through a tower riser, an attic path to a historic soffit, a buried run to a gate on a protected slope, a coordinated elevator cab camera, or a board's insurance requirements. Two systems with the same number of cameras can differ greatly in labor.

That is why we quote in writing, line by line, after seeing the property.

"NYPD cameras already watch the neighborhood."

The city does fund cameras: PIX11 reported in 2025 that the Borough President's office was budgeting money for NYPD surveillance cameras and illegal-dumping cameras across the Bronx.

An older Riverdale Press article quoted a 50th Precinct spokesperson saying there were no NYPD cameras in the district at that time; we have not confirmed the current count. Either way, police cameras watch public intersections, not your driveway, porch or package room.

The Bronx DA's voluntary camera registry, described by the Riverdale Press, lets investigators know a private camera exists so they can ask for footage; it does not give anyone a live view.

"You can't put cameras on a house in a historic district."

You can, with a permit. The Landmarks Preservation Commission requires a permit before work on a designated building or a building in a historic district.

Its permit guidebook lists security cameras among the equipment it reviews and says staff can approve them when they meet the criteria in the rules. Small, discreet cameras with concealed cable, mounted on wood soffits rather than stone, are the usual approach.

The mistake is installing first and applying later, which can mean taking cameras down.

"In a co-op you can mount what you like on your own door or terrace."

Inside your apartment, a shareholder generally has room to act, aimed at their own space.

Outside it, most co-op proprietary leases require board approval for any exterior camera or change to the building's appearance, and the hallway is a common area under the board's control. Some boards also restrict doorbell cameras on apartment doors.

Ask the managing agent before buying anything, and keep the board's written approval with the receipt.

"A recorder keeps weeks of footage automatically."

Some do. Many do not. The Riverdale Press warned years ago that some recorders loop over existing footage after as little as a day, and that is still true of small drives and badly set systems.

How long footage lasts depends on the drive size, the number of cameras, their resolution and whether they record continuously. Requests from neighbors or investigators often come days or weeks later.

We size storage for the days you need and show you how to lock a clip so it survives the next loop.

Riverdale Security Camera Installation FAQ

How is a Riverdale camera job priced?

Each job is priced on our Bronx rate and quoted in writing after you send photos or we walk the property, with every camera, mount, cable run, recorder, drive and setup task on its own line. A setup-only visit for equipment you already own is $225 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks.

Can you install cameras on a house in the Riverdale or Fieldston historic district?

Yes, with a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit in place first. We favor small, discreet cameras with concealed cable mounted on wood soffits rather than stone, and we prepare the photos and mounting details your application needs.

Does our co-op board have to approve cameras?

In most co-ops, yes. Exterior cameras and anything in hallways or other common areas go through the board, usually via the managing agent and an alteration agreement. We work inside those documents and supply the insurance proof and details they ask for.

How do you make a driveway camera useful before dawn?

We pair a high overview camera with a low camera at about head height, keep a steady shaded light on the car rather than the lens, set a fast enough shutter, record continuously and schedule person alerts for the small hours around the cars.

How fast can someone help if a camera is down or a clip needs saving?

Every request is booked within 2 hours. Leave the recorder running, note the camera name and exact time, and call (646) 490-0629. We talk you through what can be done by phone and send a technician when the fix needs hands on the equipment.
