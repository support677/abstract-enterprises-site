The Neighborhood

Why Greenwich Village Properties Need Soundbar Installation

Most soundbar guides assume a rectangular room with a hollow wall and a landlord who doesn't care. The Village rarely gives you any of the three. Village Preservation describes a neighbourhood whose streets "meet at irregular angles and follow unique paths," because it was already partly built when the 1811 Commissioners' Plan laid its grid over the rest of the island. Christopher Street began life as Skinner Road; Amos Street only became West 10th Street in 1857; Christopher Park itself is a leftover triangle between Christopher, Grove and West 4th Streets that the city turned into a park in 1837. Lots cut to fit those old roads produce buildings that are wedge-shaped, shallow, or built around a rear yard, and the rooms inside them inherit the geometry. A soundbar is a directional speaker. In a room with no two walls parallel, where it points and what it bounces off matter more than the brand on the front.

The walls are as varied as the floor plans. The oldest houses here are Federal-style rowhouses, which Village Preservation dates to roughly 1790 to 1830: two and a half storeys, a pitched roof with dormers, a front of Flemish-bond brick. Several of the best-known rows are described in the same source as frame structures with brick fronts. In plain terms, the brick you see from the sidewalk may be the only brick in the house, and the wall your TV goes on may be timber studs and lath. A few streets south, the South Village tells a different story: modest Federal and Greek Revival houses, then tenements, small factories and storefronts, built for a working-class and largely Italian American neighbourhood from the late nineteenth century on. Those tenement walls are brick through and through. Then come the twentieth-century elevator buildings, Westbeth's converted Bell Labs complex on the far west side, and NYU's concrete towers south of the square. Each wall family wants a different drill bit, a different anchor and a different route for the cable.

Third, permission. The Greenwich Village Historic District, designated in 1969, covered more than 2,000 buildings across 65 blocks, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission called it the largest district designated at the time. An extension and then the 2010 Extension II, followed by the South Village Historic District in 2013, pushed protection further south and west. Landmarking mostly governs what you can see from outside, which we explain below. The bigger question for an indoor soundbar is usually your lease or proprietary lease. NYU alone owns and manages about 2,100 apartments around Washington Square, and its Faculty Housing rules say residents need prior written consent to alter an apartment. Co-op boards, rental landlords, a Mitchell-Lama cooperative and the not-for-profit that runs Westbeth all write their own versions. We find out which applies before the visit, not at the door.

Fourth, sound travels in old buildings in ways people do not expect. A timber-framed house carries vibration through its floors and stair; a tenement passes bass through its party walls and old plaster ceilings; a concrete tower sends low notes straight down through the slab. Village households also tend to be close together. NYU's house rules ask faculty residents to keep television volume in mind and limit instrument practice to 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. The same rules cite the city's quiet hours as starting at 10 p.m. A bar set up for the building, not the showroom, is worth more here than an extra speaker.

Finally, the Village has always been a working, performing neighbourhood. The Village Vanguard has occupied its triangular basement at 178 Seventh Avenue South since 1935; Cafe Wha? has been on MacDougal Street since 1959 and the Comedy Cellar at 117 MacDougal since 1982; the Blue Note is on West 3rd Street. Between them sit restaurants, bars, galleries, music schools and university offices, many of them in landmarked buildings with apartments upstairs. When a business here wants the game or a film on a screen with proper sound, it faces the same questions as a household, plus the city's limits on music heard inside the homes above.

So this page is organised by building and by room shape. If you already know what you live in, jump to the architecture cards. If you are not sure what is under the paint, the wall table and the Questions section will get you most of the way, and a phone photo sent to (929) 560-0737 usually settles the rest.

Greenwich Village in figures, for anyone planning a TV wall

  • More than 2,000 buildings over 65 blocks in the Greenwich Village Historic District at its 1969 designation, the largest district designated up to then (NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission).
  • About 700 buildings added to landmark protection by the 2010 Greenwich Village Historic District Extension II, the 2013 South Village Historic District and the 2016 Sullivan–Thompson Historic District (Village Preservation).
  • About 2,100 apartments owned and managed by NYU around Washington Square, most of its faculty living in Silver Towers or Washington Square Village (NYU Faculty Housing).
  • 535 apartments in the three 30-storey concrete towers of University Village, built 1964–66 and designated a city landmark in 2008 (Wikipedia, citing LPC and contemporary reporting).
  • 383 live-work spaces for artists at Westbeth, opened in 1970 in the former Bell Laboratories buildings and designated a city landmark in 2011 (Westbeth).
  • 34.0% homeownership in the Greenwich Village/SoHo community district in 2024, against 32.7% citywide, with no NYCHA public housing and 571 Mitchell-Lama apartments in the district (NYU Furman Center).
Off the Grid, Indoors

What the Old Street Plan Does to a Living Room

Everywhere else in Manhattan the 1811 grid hands builders tidy rectangular lots, and the rooms come out as rectangles. The Village kept the lanes, farm lines and estate boundaries it had before the grid, and its buildings still follow them. Here is what that means once you are inside, with the TV in one hand and the bar in the other. The buildings named below describe the area; none is presented as a customer.

Rooms with no right angles

Where two old streets cross at a slant, the corner building fills a wedge. Christopher Park, the triangle where the Stonewall National Monument now stands, is the open-air version of the same geometry, and the Village Vanguard's basement is famously triangular. In a wedge-shaped flat the long wall is often the one that runs along the narrowing side, and the sofa ends up facing a corner rather than a flat wall. Soundbars are designed to fire straight ahead and to bounce their side channels off walls at predictable angles. Put one on a wall that meets its neighbour at sixty degrees and the virtual surround arrives from somewhere odd. Our default in these rooms is to centre the bar on the seat, not on the wall, and to switch off virtual surround modes that rely on reflections the room cannot give.

Small houses, divided up

Federal rowhouses were built small, and some have since been split into floor-throughs or smaller flats. That leaves living rooms with a window wall at one end, a chimney breast or stair on one side and a party wall on the other. The longest unbroken wall is frequently the party wall, which in an old frame house may be brick or may be studs with brick infill, depending on when and how the row went up. We check, because it changes both the fixing and how much of your film the house next door hears.

Rear buildings and courtyards

Some Village flats look onto a rear yard rather than the street, and a few blocks were rebuilt around a shared garden: the 22 houses of MacDougal–Sullivan Gardens were remodelled around 1920 with their backyards combined. A flat that looks onto a courtyard or a garden shared by several houses is quiet by day and very quiet at night, so any sound that leaves through an open window carries further than the same sound on the street side. When the TV wall faces the garden, we set the bar's night mode and keep the subwoofer modest.

Half-storeys under the roof

The dormers that Village Preservation highlights on Federal houses mean a top floor with sloping ceilings and only short stretches of full-height wall, often between two dormer windows. A TV and soundbar fit there, but the screen usually has to come down lower than in a standard room so the bar clears the slope, and up-firing height speakers make little sense under a ceiling that tilts toward you. A compact bar on the wall or the console is often the right size.

The tenement plan

In the South Village, tenements stand among the older houses on Sullivan, Thompson and MacDougal Streets. Their apartments tend to be narrow, the party walls are brick, and the front room overlooks a street that can stay busy late. Here the issues are a solid wall that will not take a hidden cable, several flights of stairs, and a choice between the street-side room and a quieter rear room. We price the walk-up line honestly and plan a painted channel rather than promising concealment.

Superblocks and towers

The one place in the Village where everything is rectangular is the one built after the street plan was cleared away. Washington Square Village's two buildings were completed in 1959 and 1960, and the three University Village towers went up between 1964 and 1966, poured in concrete on a three-day formwork cycle. Inside those buildings the challenge reverses: square rooms, solid concrete walls, the same layout repeated floor after floor, and a lease held with the university.

Six building patterns, six different jobs. The rest of this page is about telling them apart in the first five minutes, so the visit you book is the visit you get.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Greenwich Village's Architecture

Sort the Village's buildings into families and most of the decisions follow. Each card below gives the usual TV wall, the usual trap and the way we deal with it.

Federal Houses, 1790s–1830s

Two and a half storeys, dormers, Flemish-bond brick on the street front — and in many rows, a timber frame behind it. Interior walls are lath and plaster on studs, so the bracket goes into framing we locate first, never into plaster alone.

Greek Revival and Later Rows

The rowhouses that followed the Federal era, on many blocks north and west of the square. Plaster on lath partitions, brick party walls, tall parlour ceilings and narrow piers between windows. Original mouldings stay untouched; the screen goes at seated eye height.

South Village Tenements

Walk-ups of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries on Sullivan, Thompson and MacDougal. Brick party walls, plaster finish, small rooms over a lively street, stairs instead of an elevator. Masonry fixings, painted channels and the walk-up line in the price.

Prewar Elevator Buildings

Apartment houses with doormen and service entrances, often run by a co-op board. Partitions of plaster over block or tile, a managing agent who wants a certificate, and contractor hours to respect. Anchors chosen for hollow masonry rather than guessed.

NYU's Concrete Superblocks

Washington Square Village (completed 1959–60) and the three 30-storey University Village towers (1964–66). Poured concrete, repeating layouts, neighbours on all sides and a lease that needs written consent for alterations. Hammer-drill fixings, or no fixings at all.

Westbeth and Industrial Conversions

Former factories and the old Bell Labs complex, turned into live-work units and lofts. Large industrial buildings, mixed finishes, studio space beside living space, and a not-for-profit owner with its own rules. We read each wall rather than assume.

Mews and Carriage Houses

Two-storey former stables on Washington Mews and MacDougal Alley, many now homes or university offices. Small footprints, low upper rooms and private gated streets, so access and the right wall are planned ahead.

Village businesses mostly trade in small rooms under homes. That combination drives every commercial job here: the screen has to be heard by the people in the room and not by the people upstairs after ten. University offices, department lounges, seminar rooms and student spaces, as well as the converted buildings of Washington Mews, which NYU began leasing and turning into offices and faculty housing around 1950.

The Far West Village

Westbeth's studios, performance and rehearsal spaces, and arts organisations, alongside restaurants and offices in former industrial buildings near the river.

Rules that shape a Village business installation

  • Music heard upstairs. NYC311 explains that between 10 PM and 7 AM, music from bars and restaurants may not exceed 42 decibels measured inside a nearby residence. DEP's published guide to the Noise Code also limits what reaches the street and sets a separate limit for bass. A subwoofer on a timber floor in an old building reaches those numbers quickly, so we set and lock levels on site.
  • Landmarked fronts. The Landmarks Preservation Commission lists interior work that affects the exterior of a building among the jobs that need its permit. A speaker on the facade, a cable through a historic storefront or a screen fixed into a landmarked window surround is a conversation with LPC first; a bar on an inside wall is not.
  • Electrical work. City rules require a permit and a Licensed Master Electrician for changes to electrical systems. New outlets behind a bar-top TV are an electrician's job; we work from existing power.
  • Accessible routes. The 2010 ADA Standards cap how far a wall-hung object may stick out into a circulation path at 4 inches when its leading edge is between 27 and 80 inches from the floor. Over a tight passage in a small restaurant, the bar goes above 80 inches or somewhere off the path.
Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Greenwich Village: Questions Answered

These are questions the Village's rooms and residents raise that a generic soundbar guide never reaches. Answers describe how we work; where a lease, a board or a building decides, we say so.

Rooms the old street plan shaped

Angled corners, carved-up houses and walls of books change how a bar sounds as much as where it hangs.

Our corner flat is wedge-shaped because the building sits where two Village streets meet at a sharp angle — which wall gets the TV, and where should the soundbar point?

Choose the wall that lets the bar face the main seat squarely, even if it is not the longest wall. A soundbar projects its centre channel straight ahead and creates width by bouncing sound off the side walls. In a wedge, one side wall is far away and the other is close and angled, so the bounce arrives unevenly and dialogue drifts toward the near wall. The usual best position is on the wall opposite the widest part of the room, with the sofa set square to it.

If the only practical TV wall is the narrow end, angle the bar toward the seats rather than mounting it flush. An articulating TV arm with the bar attached does this neatly, because screen and bar turn together. Then switch off any virtual surround or "room fill" mode and use the plain stereo or centre-weighted setting, which depends far less on walls the room does not have.

Many bars include a room-calibration routine using a microphone in the bar or a phone. In an irregular room it is worth running, but run it with the sofa in its final place and the windows closed, then listen to a dialogue scene from each seat before accepting the result. We do that on site and change it if the automatic answer sounds worse.

Our apartment was carved out of an old house, and the partition we want for the TV runs right into the middle of an original window — can a bracket go that close to the frame?

Usually yes, but not into the last few inches next to the window. When a house is divided, a new partition is often built against an existing window or bay, and the framing at that junction is a mix of old window casing, new studs and filler. Fixings close to the casing can split old wood or crack the plaster return, and the window may need to open for the fire escape or for air.

We scan the partition to find the first solid stud clear of the window junction, keep the bracket and bar clear of the sash so the window still opens fully, and keep the TV's edge off the casing. If the partition is a later stud wall, it is often the easiest wall in the flat to fix to and to run a hidden cable in, with the owner's agreement.

Two cautions. If the window is a required second way out, nothing we install will block it. And if the window sits in a landmarked front, nothing we fix touches the window frame itself; the bracket stays on the partition.

Our Federal house is a timber frame behind its brick front — will a subwoofer make the old floorboards and the stair hum through the whole house?

It can, more than in a brick or concrete building. Timber floors on joists act a little like a drum: low bass drives the boards and joists, and anything loosely fixed to them — stair treads, a loose floorboard, a glass-fronted cabinet — buzzes along. In a house shared with tenants, the people upstairs or downstairs hear that as a hum rather than as music.

Three things help. Put the subwoofer on a dense isolation pad or platform rather than straight on the boards. Keep it away from the stair and from the party wall. Then set the crossover and level so the sub blends with the bar rather than leading. Many households find the bar's own bass is enough in a small Federal parlour, and leave the sub out altogether.

We test with a bass-heavy scene at your normal volume, walk the house while it plays, and adjust until the stair and the floors stop joining in.

Our living room is lined with packed bookshelves on three sides — does that help or hurt how the soundbar sounds?

Mostly it helps. Shelves full of books of different depths break up reflections and absorb some of the upper frequencies, so a book-lined room usually sounds calmer and clearer than a bare one. Dialogue stays focused and harsh echoes fade, which suits films and the radio alike.

The side effect is that virtual surround and height effects, which rely on sound bouncing off flat walls and ceilings, get weaker. If you bought a bar for its surround effect, rear speakers will do more in a book-lined room than any processing mode. If dialogue was your complaint, the room is already on your side.

Two practical points: keep the bar's front clear of shelf edges so it is not partly boxed in, and do not put the bar inside a shelf bay unless it is designed to sit in an enclosure. We check both before choosing the height.

Music, practice and nights in the Village

This is a neighbourhood of musicians, students and late evenings. The questions follow.

Can a digital piano or keyboard play through the soundbar, and do our building's practice-hours rules then apply to it?

Often it can, if the bar has an analog or optical input, or accepts a Bluetooth source, and the instrument has a line or headphone output. A short cable from the instrument's output to the bar's input, or a small audio interface, does the job. Some bars delay their audio slightly to keep it in step with the picture, so latency matters for playing; we test with you at the keyboard, and if the delay is noticeable we suggest headphones or the instrument's own speakers for practice.

On the rules: once the bar is playing an instrument, treat it as an instrument. NYU's Faculty Housing rules, for example, allow instrument practice for reasonable periods between 9 a.m. and 8 p.m. only, and ask residents to keep TV volume in mind. Other buildings have similar house rules. A bar playing a piano at full level through a timber floor at night will be heard exactly like a piano.

If you practise in the evening, headphones on the instrument and a quiet bar setting are the neighbourly answer; we can set a separate saved level for the instrument input on bars that allow it.

Between sets our club plays the game on the bar TV, but once the band starts it must go silent instantly — what is the simplest way to give the bartender that control?

A single labelled remote or a fixed mute button behind the bar. Most soundbars mute instantly from their own remote; if the TV and bar are linked by HDMI-CEC, the TV remote mutes both. We programme one remote for mute and the house level only, label it, and leave it in a fixed place behind the bar so whoever is working knows where it lives.

Where staff should not adjust volume at all, set a maximum level on the bar or the TV if the model supports it, and save the "game" level as the default. Then the only action needed at the start of a set is mute, and at the end, unmute; the level comes back where it was.

In a basement room with odd angles, place the bar so the bar-top seats hear it and the stage does not. We aim it along the bar rather than into the room, and keep the subwoofer off entirely so low end never competes with the band's bass.

“A soundbar mount is a $30–$100 add-on”

Ask a cost guide and you will hear that a soundbar bracket is a minor extra: Angi's TV cost article puts the add-on at about $30 to $100, while Thumbtack quotes roughly $121 to $234 nationally for having a TV wall-mounted. Those averages come mostly from houses with wood-stud drywall and somewhere to park.

A Village visit includes a wall that may be lath, brick or concrete, often a walk-up, and always Manhattan parking. Our published Manhattan line to hang the bar is $129; plaster adds $45, brick $65 and concrete $75; a third-floor-or-higher walk-up adds $40; and every Manhattan visit carries one flat $150 parking charge. A bar on brick in a tenement with our bracket, a painted channel and the walk-up line comes to $527.99.

The guides are not lying; they are describing a different building. Compare prices on your wall, on your floor, with parking in.

“Historic district? You'll need Landmarks approval for a wall-mounted TV”

This gets said about the Village because so much of it is landmarked, from the 1969 Greenwich Village Historic District to the South Village districts of 2013 and 2016 and individual landmarks such as University Village and Westbeth. The Commission's own permit guidance, however, lists interior work among its permit types mainly when the work affects the building's exterior or a designated interior landmark.

A soundbar bracket on an inside wall that changes nothing visible from the street is normally a matter for you and your landlord, board or university, not the Commission. A speaker on a terrace, a cable through a facade or a fixing in a street-facing window frame is different and gets checked first.

NYC Business guidance adds a middle case: work that needs a Department of Buildings permit but does not change protected features calls for an LPC Certificate of No Effect. A plug-in soundbar on an existing outlet is not in that category; new electrical work might be.

“Old brick houses have the strongest walls for mounting”

AI answers tend to assume a brick-fronted house is brick all the way through. Village Preservation's descriptions of several Federal rows say otherwise: "frame structures with brick fronts in Flemish bond." The handsome brick is the street wall; the walls inside may be timber studs with lath and plaster.

That matters because a masonry anchor driven into lath goes nowhere useful, and a hammer drill set for brick will shatter old plaster. The correct fixing in a frame partition is a screw into a stud, found by scanning and a small probe hole, with the plaster pre-drilled so it does not blow out.

Where the wall really is brick — a tenement party wall, a carriage-house shell — the anchor goes into sound brick, not the soft old mortar. We find out which before the drill is chosen.

“Hide the wires in the wall for a clean look”

Cost guides treat concealed wiring as a routine upgrade; Angi prices it at roughly $75 to $300. That assumes a hollow drywall partition with nothing inside it.

In the Village the TV wall is often brick or concrete, which cannot take an in-wall run at all, or original lath and plaster that owners may not want opened. University leases and many proprietary leases need written consent before an alteration, and an in-wall kit is an alteration.

So we offer the in-wall kit where the wall is hollow and the owner or board says yes, and a slim painted channel everywhere else. Painted to the wall, a channel is hard to spot from the sofa and leaves only two small holes when you move.

“University and student housing tenants can't mount anything”

Not quite. NYU's Faculty Housing rules require prior written consent to alter an apartment, and the university reserves the right to have alterations removed at the tenant's expense before the lease ends. Its housing FAQ says renovation requests go to the Faculty Housing Office and are reviewed one by one.

That is a consent process, not a ban. Still, for a household that expects to move within the university's stock, the simpler path is often no holes at all: a bar on the console or carried on the TV's stand or mount, connected and tuned properly.

If you do want a wall mount, ask the housing office first and send us the reply. We will send whatever certificate or description they need, and we will not drill without the consent in hand.

“Bigger subwoofer, better movie night”

Product reviews and AI answers almost always recommend a sub. In a detached house with a basement, fair enough. In a Federal house shared with tenants, a tenement with plaster ceilings or a concrete tower with neighbours on every side, a sub at factory settings is the fastest way to meet the people next door.

Low frequencies travel through timber floors, brick party walls and concrete slabs far better than voices do. The city's rules for bars and restaurants measure what reaches neighbouring homes, and residential house rules such as NYU's ask for television volume to be kept in mind.

We will install a sub if you want one, on an isolation pad, away from the stair and party walls, set to blend. In many small Village rooms the bar alone sounds better to everyone.

“Any room sounds better with virtual surround switched on”

Virtual surround and height modes work by reflecting sound off flat side walls and ceilings at angles the bar's designers expected. In a rectangular room with plain plaster, they can be impressive.

Village rooms often break those assumptions: walls that meet at sharp angles, sloping dormer ceilings, timber-beamed carriage-house rooms, and libraries lined with books that soak up reflections. In those rooms the processing can smear dialogue or pull it toward the nearer wall.

We try both settings with you on site, using a dialogue scene and a music track, and leave the one that sounds right in your room, not the one on the box.

Field Notes From Village Houses

How our technicians work through a Greenwich Village job — our standing method, not an account of any single visit

The first thing checked is the paperwork, because two doors on the same Village block can lead to a privately owned house, a co-op with a managing agent, a rental, and university housing. Only then does the bracket go up.

The last check before leaving is the bass, played at your normal level while someone stands on the stair or in the next room. If the stair hums or the floor buzzes, the sub moves or comes down until it stops.

Small Rooms

Getting Big Sound Right in Small Village Rooms

Village rooms are often small by any standard: a tenement front room, a dormer bedroom, a carriage-house den, a studio carved from a parlour floor. Small rooms are not a reason to skip a soundbar. They are a reason to choose and set one differently.

Match the bar to the wall, not to the shop

A bar roughly the width of the screen or a little narrower looks right and is usually enough. In a room where the seat is only a few feet from the TV, a compact bar with a good speech mode outperforms a flagship whose drivers need distance to blend. We measure from the seat to the wall before recommending anything.

Let the room help

Soft furnishings, rugs and full bookshelves calm a small room's echo. Bare plaster, a wood floor and two windows do the opposite. Before reaching for a bigger bar, try a rug in front of the TV and curtains at the window; the same bar often sounds noticeably better.

Keep the bass in proportion

In a small room a subwoofer is rarely needed and easily overpowering. Bars with built-in woofers, set with a modest bass level, usually fill a Village front room. Where a sub is used, it sits on a pad, low, and away from shared walls and stairs.

Plan for the next move

Many Village residents rent, and some move between apartments in the same building or within the university's stock. A setup that relies on a console or a TV-mounted bracket moves with you in an hour. A wall-mounted bar can follow too: removing an old bracket is $45 on the Manhattan menu, and the new installation is priced like any other, with one parking line per visit.

Think about the late-night listener

Most bars offer a night mode that compresses loud passages and a speech mode that lifts voices. Set both up on the day so they are one button away. In houses shared with tenants, in tenements and in university buildings with stated quiet hours, those two settings do more for good relations than any amount of insulation.

Landmarks and the Outside Wall

Where Village Landmark Rules Do and Don't Reach a Soundbar

Because so much of the Village sits inside historic districts, people often assume the Landmarks Preservation Commission must bless anything they put on a wall. The Commission's published permit types tell a narrower story, and it is worth knowing exactly where the line sits before you plan a TV room, a terrace screen or a shop display.

Inside walls, inside rooms

The Commission lists interior work among its permit types when that work affects the exterior of the building — its example is HVAC louvres and vents — or when it affects spaces designated as interior landmarks. A bracket on a parlour partition, a bar on a console, a painted channel down to an outlet: none of these changes what anyone sees from the street, so in an ordinary house or apartment they are a matter between you and whoever owns or manages the building.

When a building permit is involved

NYC Business guidance explains a middle category. When work needs a Department of Buildings permit but does not change a landmark's protected architectural features, the Commission issues a Certificate of No Effect. A soundbar plugged into an existing outlet is not that kind of work. New wiring that needs an electrical permit might be, which is one more reason we work from existing power and leave new outlets to a Licensed Master Electrician.

Terraces, gardens and facades

The picture changes once something reaches outside. An outdoor speaker on a rear terrace visible from a street, a cable drilled through a front wall, a bracket in a historic window surround, a satellite dish or antenna on a roof — each can affect the exterior that the district protects. We flag these before work rather than after, suggest an indoor alternative where one exists, and leave the permit question to you and, if needed, your architect.

Individual landmarks with residents

Some of the Village's individual landmarks are also homes: University Village's three towers, designated in 2008, and Westbeth, designated in 2011. Living in a landmark does not by itself bring the Commission into your living room. What governs a soundbar inside those buildings is the lease and the owner's own rules — NYU's Faculty Housing consent process in two of the towers, a co-op board at 505 LaGuardia Place, and Westbeth's management for its not-for-profit owner.

Shopfronts and signage screens

For businesses, the rule of thumb is similar. A screen and bar inside the shop, on an inside wall, is your landlord's question. A screen fixed into a historic storefront, sound aimed out through an open front, or anything attached outside, is the Commission's question as well as the noise rules', and it is worth asking before the order goes in.

None of this is legal advice, and districts and individual buildings can carry conditions of their own. It is the working map we use to decide, before a visit, whether a job is simply a soundbar or something that needs a conversation with the Commission first.

Live-Work Spaces

Soundbars in Studios, Live-Work Units and Practice Rooms

The Village has housed working artists for a long time, and Westbeth made it official: when it opened in 1970 in the former Bell Laboratories buildings, it created 383 live-work spaces for artists and their families, with Richard Meier as renovation architect. The building still houses studios, rehearsal and performance spaces alongside apartments. Similar arrangements turn up in lofts and converted industrial floors further west. A TV and soundbar in a space that is also a studio needs a little more thought than one in a living room.

Keep the work zone and the screen zone apart

If one end of the room is a studio, put the TV and bar at the other end, facing away from the work area, so playback does not spill into a recording or a rehearsal. A bar aimed down the length of a long room carries much further than one aimed across it.

Choose fixings that come out cleanly

Studio walls get repainted, rehung and rebuilt for projects. A TV-mounted bar or a console setup leaves those walls free; where a wall bracket makes sense, we log the fixing positions so they can be found and reused later.

Use the right inputs

Many artists want to play their own material through the system. Most bars take HDMI, optical and sometimes an analog or Bluetooth source; a small audio interface can bridge an instrument or a laptop. We test for delay before you rely on it, because a bar that holds back its audio to match the picture can be awkward for live playing.

Respect the building's other residents

Live-work buildings are still homes for the neighbours. The same night-mode and bass settings we use in apartments apply, and a capped maximum level on the bar can save an argument after a late session.

Glossary

A Short Glossary for Village TV Walls

  • Flemish bond. A brick pattern alternating long and short faces in each course, typical of Federal-era fronts in the Village. Handsome on the street side; tells you nothing about what the interior walls are made of.
  • Frame house with brick front. A timber-framed building whose street wall alone is brick. Interior walls are usually lath and plaster on studs, so brackets go into studs.
  • Lath and plaster. Thin wood strips nailed across studs, coated in plaster. Strong when fixed through to a stud, weak and crumbly when an anchor relies on the plaster alone.
  • Party wall. The wall shared with the next building. In rowhouses and tenements it is often brick, and it carries bass to the neighbours more readily than an interior partition.
  • Sleeve anchor. A masonry fixing that expands inside a drilled hole. Set in the face of a sound brick, not in old lime mortar.
  • Certificate of insurance. Proof that the contractor carries liability insurance, made out to the names a board, agent or housing office specifies. We provide it free.
  • Certificate of No Effect. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's approval for work that needs a building permit but does not change a landmark's protected features.
  • Mitchell-Lama. New York's middle-income housing programme. In the Village it includes the co-op at 505 LaGuardia Place; the Furman Center counts 571 Mitchell-Lama apartments in the district.
  • eARC and ARC. The HDMI connection that carries sound from the TV back to the bar. eARC handles full-resolution formats; ARC is the older version.
  • HDMI-CEC. Short for Consumer Electronics Control: the HDMI feature that lets the TV and bar power on, switch inputs and change volume from a single handset.
  • Night mode. A bar setting that narrows the gap between quiet speech and loud effects, so late viewing does not travel through floors and walls.
  • Isolation pad. A dense platform under a subwoofer that stops it driving a timber floor or a concrete slab directly.

Our street-door buzzer hasn't worked for months. How does the technician get in?
+

The technician calls or texts from the stoop when they arrive inside the window, and someone comes down to open the street door. If nobody can, leave a key with a neighbour or the super, or arrange a time to meet at the door. A broken buzzer is worth reporting to whoever manages the building.

The soundbar's voice assistant keeps waking up when people shout on the street outside. Can that be stopped?
+

Yes. Most bars let you switch the built-in microphone off with a button or in the app, which stops false wake-ups entirely. If you use voice control, lowering the wake-word sensitivity where offered, or moving voice duties to a separate smart speaker away from the street-side window, usually ends it.
