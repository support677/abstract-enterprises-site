The Neighborhood

Why Sheepshead Bay Properties Need Alarm Installation

Sheepshead Bay is a working harbor wrapped in houses. The bay itself runs along Emmons Avenue, with the city’s ten fishing and party-boat piers between Ocean Avenue and East 26th Street, and the streets behind it climb gently north past the Belt Parkway to Avenue X, Avenue U and the B and Q station on Sheepshead Bay Road. Most of what people ask us to protect here is residential and small: attached two- and three-story houses, semi-detached and detached two-families, the last of the bungalow courts near the water, six-story apartment buildings and co-ops, and a few towers such as Avalon Brooklyn Bay on Voorhies Avenue. The shops are concentrated on Sheepshead Bay Road, Emmons Avenue, Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue, almost all of them with apartments or offices upstairs.

What makes alarm work different here is the ground. The Department of City Planning’s 2017 Resilient Neighborhoods report on Sheepshead Bay counted more than 2,000 buildings and 6,500 homes in the 100-year floodplain, and found that nearly 95 percent of the buildings in its study area were built before the first federal flood maps. During Hurricane Sandy, water in the study area reached four to eight feet above the ground, and up to ten feet for the homes south of the Belt Parkway. Small ground-floor businesses on Emmons Avenue and Sheepshead Bay Road saw up to six feet. The same report notes that many homeowners keep boilers and other critical systems in cellars below the flood elevation, and that the bungalow courts sit up to five feet below the street. An alarm panel, its battery and its communicator are critical systems too, and in an old house they were almost always put in the cellar, next to the boiler and the electric meter.

So the first question on a Sheepshead Bay survey is not "where is the front door?" but "where did the water stop?" A burglar alarm that floods with the cellar goes silent at exactly the moment the house is most exposed: power is out, the family may have left, and doors and windows have been forced open by water or by the people pumping it out. After Sandy, the Brooklyn Recovery Fund estimated that nearly 40 percent of Sheepshead Bay businesses would not come back, City Limits reported, and in ZIP code 11235 alone 406 private homes took more than $30,000 in damage. Water does not have to come from the bay, either. At 2:20 one February morning in 2026, a water main broke on Bragg Street between Emmons Avenue and Shore Parkway and flooded the garages and basements of at least five houses and an apartment building. "I was sleeping and my grandma is in the basement," a neighbor, Amir Aslamov, told News 12, "and my mom woke me up and all of a sudden the whole basement was flooded."

The burglary picture, by comparison, is steady rather than alarming. The NYPD’s CompStat report for the 61st Precinct, which covers Sheepshead Bay along with Manhattan Beach, Gravesend and Homecrest, shows 134 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, against 130 at the same point in 2025, an increase of 3.1 percent, but burglary is down 92.6 percent over 33 years. Grand larceny, which counts thefts without a break-in, is up 7.3 percent, from 504 to 541. The break-ins that do happen follow a few recognizable patterns: houses entered during the day while everyone is at work, such as the Ocean Avenue home near Jerome Avenue that lost $12,000 in jewelry between 8:30 in the morning and 7:30 at night in a Brooklyn Paper report; homes entered in the small hours, like the East 9th Street house broken into around 2 a.m. in March 2026; and commercial buildings entered from above street level, like the jewelry store whose burglars, News 12 reported, came in through a third-floor window at 4:30 a.m.

Put together, that is the brief for an alarm here. It has to report reliably over cellular, from a panel mounted where water will not reach it, with a battery that outlasts a long outage. It has to cover the lowest level of the building for water as well as for people. And it has to cover the openings burglars in this precinct have actually used: front doors during working hours, upper-floor windows reached from roofs and fire escapes, and the doors of shops and restaurants that close late and open early. That is usually a smaller system than people expect, placed more carefully than they expect.

Need Repair in Sheepshead Bay?

A Panel That Rode Out the Flood, a Keypad That Has Shown "Trouble" for Years, or a Siren Nobody Hears Over Emmons Avenue: Alarm Repair in Sheepshead Bay

Many of the alarm calls we get from Sheepshead Bay are about systems that already exist. A family buys an attached house near Nostrand Avenue and finds a keypad in the hall that beeps once a minute and a gray metal box in the cellar with a rusted door. A restaurant owner on Emmons Avenue takes over a lease and inherits a panel with a monitoring sticker for a company that no longer answers. A bungalow owner reopens the house in spring and the system will not arm because three sensors read "low battery" and one reads nothing at all. Each of these is usually a single visit.

The common thread is water and time. A panel that sat in salt water in 2012 and was dried out and switched back on may still light up, but its circuit board, its transformer and its battery terminals can corrode for years afterward, and the first sign is often a zone that faults at random or a communicator that stops checking in. Older panels may also depend on a phone line that was cut long ago or on a 3G or CDMA cellular network that carriers have since switched off, in which case the keypad still beeps while the signal goes nowhere. If the wiring in the walls tests sound, the usual fix is the panel-replacement line on our price list, with a new panel and cellular communicator mounted somewhere dry, rather than a whole new system. Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the brand name on the keypad and what its screen says, and send a photo of the box in the cellar. If a siren will not stop, we talk you through silencing it first, because on an attached block it is keeping more than one family awake. Service visits outside warranty are $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585), on our equipment or anyone else’s.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Sheepshead Bay

Flood-aware house alarms

For the attached and detached houses between the bay and Avenue U: the panel, battery and communicator mounted on the first living floor or higher, wireless sensors on cellar and garage doors that can be replaced cheaply if water reaches them, and water sensors at the lowest points.

Two-family and three-family systems

One panel divided into areas for each household, a shared area for the cellar, the garage and the side door, and separate call lists, so the tenant upstairs is not woken by the owner’s mistakes and the owner hears about water in the cellar even if the tenant is away.

Bungalow and court homes

Small, wireless systems for the bungalows in the courts and lanes near the water: every door and reachable window, a motion detector, water and low-temperature sensors for houses that sit empty in winter, and a panel placed as high as the little house allows.

Shop and restaurant alarms

For Sheepshead Bay Road, Emmons Avenue, Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue: gate and door contacts, glass-break detection, the cellar hatch and stairs, the roof door, an office or cash zone, a hold-up button, and opening and closing reports to the owner.

Apartment alarms for renters and co-op owners

For the six-story buildings, co-ops and towers: wireless contacts on the apartment door, fire-escape and terrace openings, a motion detector, smoke and carbon monoxide sensors, and nothing fastened to the building’s common areas.

Water, freeze and power alerts

Water sensors under boilers and water heaters, at sump pits and cellar stairs, low-temperature sensors for winter vacancies, and a power-loss alert from the panel itself, so a family in Florida or upstate knows the moment Con Edison drops the block.

Life safety on the same panel

Monitored smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors, including the carbon monoxide coverage HPD requires in rented one- and two-family homes with gas appliances, and heat detectors for kitchens and converted ground floors where smoke detectors false-alarm.

Takeovers, resets and post-flood rebuilds

Adopting an alarm a seller or a previous tenant left behind, resetting lost master codes, replacing dead phone-line and old cellular communicators, and moving a corroded cellar panel upstairs while keeping whatever wiring still tests sound.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Sheepshead Bay’s Architecture

The city’s planners describe Sheepshead Bay’s housing as predominantly one- and two-family homes, and that matches the requests we get. What varies is how far each building sits below the water line, how many households share it, and how much of it stands empty for part of the year. These six types cover nearly every address in the neighborhood.

The postwar attached row

The two- and three-story attached houses of the 1950s and 1960s that line the blocks between Nostrand Avenue and Coyle Street, often with a garage or a low entrance at street level, living space above it, and party walls on both sides.

The detached and semi-detached two-family

Older frame and brick houses on the lettered avenues and numbered East streets, with a front porch or stoop, a driveway along one side, a cellar, a yard, and an owner and a tenant on different floors.

The bungalow court

Small single-family houses from the 1920s set in clusters near the water, built for summer, some winterized and lived in all year, some boarded, sitting up to five feet below the street with narrow shared walkways.

The six-story apartment building and co-op

Brick walk-ups and elevator buildings across the neighborhood, including the city’s Sheepshead Bay Houses and Nostrand Houses, where a resident’s alarm protects one apartment and nothing in the halls.

The waterfront tower and newer condo

Buildings such as Avalon Brooklyn Bay at 1501 Voorhies Avenue, thirty stories with rentals below and condominiums above and an attended lobby, and the newer mid-rise condos that replaced older buildings on and near Emmons Avenue.

The shop building with a cellar

The attached one- to three-story buildings from the 1920s and 1930s on Sheepshead Bay Road, and the one- to eight-story buildings along Emmons Avenue, with retail at street level, apartments or offices above, and cellars used for stock, equipment and food preparation.

The postwar attached row: the garage is the front door

On many of the attached blocks built after the war, the house is entered at two levels. There is a front door up a few steps, and there is a garage door or a low service door at the street, opening into a garage, a storage room or, quite often, a room that a previous owner turned into living space. The planners noted in 2017 that some Sheepshead Bay homeowners had converted garages and storage areas into living space and called it a life-safety concern, and from an alarm point of view it is also the opening people forget. A roll-up or swing garage door at the sidewalk is out of sight of the windows above it, faces the street, and often leads straight to the stairs.

We put a contact on that door built for its type, a heavy-duty magnetic contact with wider gap tolerance for an overhead door, or a tilt sensor where mounting a contact is awkward, and a motion detector covering the path from the garage level to the stairs. If the ground-floor room is a bedroom or den, it gets a smoke detector and, if there is a gas appliance nearby, a carbon monoxide detector on the panel. And because this is the level that floods, the panel itself never goes here. It goes on the first living floor, where the keypad is anyway, and the sensors at garage level are inexpensive wireless units we can replace in an hour if water ever reaches them.

The detached and semi-detached two-family: two households, one cellar

In the older houses on the numbered East streets, the usual arrangement is an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, a cellar shared for the boiler, the meters and storage, and a side door off the driveway that is the door everyone actually uses. The Brooklyn Paper’s 61st Precinct reports give a fair picture of what happens to houses like these: a burglar who "burst through the front door" of a house on East 17th Street between Avenues S and T at two in the afternoon, and two men who posed as Con Edison workers and broke a window lock at a house on East 24th Street between Avenues Y and Z at noon. Both happened during the working day, when the owner and the tenant were likely both out.

The alarm for a house like this has three areas. Each apartment is its own, with its own codes, its own app and its own history. The shared area covers the cellar door, the side door if it opens onto shared stairs, and the water sensors in the cellar, and it is armed by whoever leaves last. Water alerts are set up differently from burglary alerts: they go to the owner first, wherever the owner is, because it is the owner’s boiler and the owner’s insurance, and then to the tenant, who can open the cellar door for a plumber.

The bungalow court: small house, low ground, long winters empty

The bungalows that survive near the water are some of the most distinctive houses in Brooklyn, and they ask for a very particular alarm. Brownstoner described them in 2015 as built in the 1920s, "originally intended exclusively for warm-weather use," in clusters near the coast, and the city’s report found that the courts sit up to five feet below the street. Many are reached by a narrow walkway rather than a street, so a burglar working on a back window is out of sight of everything except the neighbors’ walls. Many are lived in from spring to fall and closed for the winter.

For a bungalow we keep things wireless and compact: a contact on every door, contacts on every window that opens, one motion detector that covers the main room, and a panel mounted as high on an interior wall as the house allows. The city puts the base flood elevation for many bungalows at six to eight feet above grade, so in a house that has not been raised the panel goes near the ceiling of the main room, with its battery and communicator inside the same housing. A water sensor goes on the floor at the lowest corner and a low-temperature sensor on an inside wall, so an empty house in January tells you when the heat fails or water comes in. Because these houses are often shut for months, we set the panel to send a check-in to your phone every day, so silence from it is itself a warning.

The six-story building, the co-op and the tower

In the apartment buildings, an alarm protects one home. For a tenant in a six-story building, that means a contact on the apartment door, a contact on any window that opens onto a fire escape, a motion detector in the entry hall, and smoke and carbon monoxide detectors that report to your phone, all wireless, mounted with small screws or adhesive and removable when you move. The building’s doors, halls and fire alarm belong to the landlord or the co-op, and we do not touch them. In a doorman tower like Avalon Brooklyn Bay, nobody is climbing to a window twenty floors up, so the system is mostly the door, a motion detector, life-safety sensors and a water sensor under the washing machine and the kitchen sink, because a leak in a tower reaches several apartments below before anyone notices.

The shop building with a cellar: stock below, families above

On Sheepshead Bay Road, most buildings are attached, one to three stories, put up in the 1920s and 1930s, with a store at the sidewalk and apartments or an office upstairs. The city’s report found that most of these businesses use their cellars for mechanical systems, inventory and food preparation, which is why Sandy hurt them so badly. For an alarm, the cellar is both the place where the value sits and the place the equipment must not go. We cover the cellar with a contact on the sidewalk hatch or the stair door and a motion detector at the bottom of the stairs, add water sensors near the walk-in, the compressor and the hot-water heater, and mount the panel upstairs in the store’s back office, above the counter height that water reached in 2012. The apartments above get their own area, so the store’s staff and the families upstairs never share a code.

Below the Flood Line

Start Where the Water Stopped: Planning a Sheepshead Bay Alarm From the Cellar Up

Most alarm advice is about doors and windows. In a neighborhood where 95 percent of buildings went up before flood maps, the more important decisions are about height. Every alarm has four parts that must keep working when everything else has failed: the control panel, its backup battery, the communicator that sends the signal out, and the siren. In an older Sheepshead Bay house, all four are usually in one gray box on the cellar wall, at the height of a man’s chest, which in 2012 was under water in many houses near the bay and south of the Belt Parkway. The first thing we do on a survey is find out where the water reached in that building, from the owner’s memory, a mark on the wall, or the flood elevation for the block, and then mount everything critical above it.

That usually means the first living floor: a hall closet, the wall beside the main keypad, or the inside of a kitchen pantry. A modern wireless panel is the keypad, the brain, the battery and the cellular communicator in one housing, so moving it upstairs costs nothing extra. On older hardwired systems whose wiring all runs to the cellar, we can either move the panel upstairs and extend the wires, or leave a small expander module downstairs that will be cheap to replace, with the panel itself upstairs. Either way, the part that decides whether you hear about a break-in during a storm is out of the water.

The sensors on the lowest level are a different calculation. A door contact on a cellar or garage door that sits under water for six hours will probably fail; the cheapest answer is to use inexpensive wireless contacts there, accept that they may need replacing after a serious flood, and make sure the rest of the system keeps working without them. Every one of our panels can bypass a failed zone and stay armed on all the others, so a dead cellar contact does not stop you from arming the house the night after a storm. We also put the siren upstairs, inside the living space, rather than in the cellar, so it is audible and survives.

Then come the water sensors themselves. In a Sheepshead Bay cellar we place them at the lowest corner of the floor, near the boiler and water heater, beside any floor drain or sump, and at the bottom of the cellar stairs, because that is where water from the street, a backed-up drain or a broken main shows up first. The Bragg Street break happened just after 2:20 in the morning; a water sensor on the basement floor would have sounded a chime upstairs and sent a phone alert in the first minutes, long before anyone had 20 inches to deal with. Water alerts on our panels can be set to wake the house with a distinct tone and a phone message without triggering the burglary siren or a police dispatch.

Last is power. After Sandy, City Limits reported, the outages that followed the storm hit residents of the neighborhood’s public housing especially hard, and long outages are a fact of life near the water. A standard alarm battery is sized to keep the panel and its communicator working through a normal outage, not through a week, so for houses and shops that want more, we can fit a larger battery where the panel supports it and set the panel to report the moment mains power fails and again when it returns. That report is often the first thing an owner who has evacuated, or who winters in another state, learns about a storm at the house.

The Waterfront After Dark

Late Closings, Crowded Piers and Empty Buildings: What an Emmons Avenue Alarm Has to Handle

Emmons Avenue has a rhythm unlike any other commercial street in the borough. Restaurants and bars along the bay stay open late, the party boats come and go from the piers, and on a summer weekend the sidewalks can be busier at midnight than at noon. Bklyner reported in 2017 that about twenty party vessels were using the piers, with more than 3,000 people on the docks, the streets and the parking areas between cruises, and noise past 3 a.m. "The area used to be a fishing port, it’s too small to handle this many people every weekend," Peter Katsichtis of Yiasou on Emmons Avenue told the site. A few hours later the same street is nearly empty, and the restaurants are dark.

That rhythm shapes a business alarm in two ways. First, a siren on Emmons Avenue at 1 a.m. on a Saturday is easily lost among music, traffic and crowds, so the reporting matters more than the noise: the panel sends its signal over cellular to the owner’s phone and, if the business has monitoring, to the central station, within seconds of the first sensor. Second, the time between the last employee leaving and the first one arriving can be very short in summer and very long in winter, when some places close for days at a time. Opening and closing reports, sent to the owner’s phone every day, make it obvious when a building that should be armed is not.

The waterfront also has empty buildings, and empty buildings attract a particular kind of trouble. News 12 reported in April 2026 that teenagers broke into the former TGI Fridays at 3181 Harkness Avenue, closed since 2024, three times, smashing windows each time, filming themselves inside and on the roof, and spraying fire extinguishers through the building. The owner repaired the windows, they were broken again, and a fence around the property did not stop them. An empty building cannot be watched by staff, and a fence slows nobody who has already decided to go in. What catches the second and third visit is an interior motion detector and glass-break detectors reporting to the owner’s phone, ideally with a camera view on the same app, so the first broken window brings a call rather than a discovery days later.

Upper floors are the other waterfront lesson. The jewelry store burglars News 12 described in January 2026 entered through a third-floor window at 4:30 in the morning. The report did not say how they reached it, and we will not guess, but it is a reminder that on mixed buildings the windows above the shop are part of the shop’s perimeter. On a commercial survey here, we ask who controls the upper floors, whether they are occupied, and whether their windows can be reached from a roof, a fire escape or the building next door. Where they can, they get contacts on a separate area that stays armed whenever the floor is empty.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in Sheepshead Bay

The business side of Sheepshead Bay is mostly small and owner-run: restaurants, bakeries, groceries, pharmacies, medical and dental offices, salons, phone stores, tax and insurance offices, and the marine businesses that grew up around the piers. Brooklyn Paper has described Sheepshead Bay Road as a strip of low-rise mom-and-pop shops under rent pressure, and the city’s planners counted that 26 percent of the more than 1,000 retail businesses in Southern Brooklyn’s floodplain are in Sheepshead Bay. These six settings cover most of the businesses we hear from.

Sheepshead Bay Road at the station

The attached 1920s and 1930s shop buildings around the B and Q station, with stores at the sidewalk, apartments or offices above and working cellars below. Early openers and late closers share the same blocks, and portions of the strip have stood vacant since Sandy.

Emmons Avenue along the bay

Restaurants, bars, catering and takeout facing the piers, in buildings from one to eight stories under the Special Sheepshead Bay District rules, busy late into the night in summer and quiet for long stretches in winter.

The piers and marine businesses

The fishing and party-boat operators, bait and tackle sellers and the small offices and storage that serve them along the ten city piers between Ocean Avenue and East 26th Street, where equipment and cash both sit close to the water.

Restaurants and cafés: early starts, late finishes and a cellar full of stock

News 12 reported in February 2025 that My House Burgers and Shake on Sheepshead Bay Road was broken into around 6 a.m. on January 22, and that about $10,000, a MacBook Pro and an iPhone 13 were taken. "It can happen to any store any business," the shift manager told the station. Six in the morning is a telling hour: in a restaurant it is often the gap between the last cleaner leaving and the first cook arriving, and in winter it is still dark. An alarm that is armed at closing and disarmed at opening covers that gap only if it is actually armed, which is why the closing report matters as much as any sensor. The owner gets a text when the last person arms the building, and another if nobody has armed it by a set hour.

Inside, the plan follows the money and the stock. The office or the cash drawer’s overnight location gets its own zone with a contact and a motion detector, a safe gets a shock sensor, and the counter gets a hold-up button that signals the monitoring station silently during open hours. The front gets a contact on the gate and the door and glass-break detection behind the windows, and the back door, the cellar stairs or hatch, and any roof door get contacts. In a restaurant, a monitored heat detector in the kitchen is far better than a smoke detector, which every grill will set off. AESS does not service kitchen hood fire-suppression systems, and the burglar alarm is never tied into one.

Shops with a cellar: protecting what is downstairs without putting the alarm there

Many shops on Sheepshead Bay Road and Avenue U keep their most valuable stock, and their compressors, freezers and hot-water heaters, in the cellar. The city found that those cellars flooded in 2012, and ground-floor businesses on the strip saw water up to six feet. A burglar alarm for a shop like this has a split personality. The cellar needs to be covered for people, with a contact on the stair door or the hatch and a motion detector facing the bottom of the stairs, and for water, with sensors near the floor drain, the walk-in and the boiler. But the panel, the battery and the siren need to be upstairs, mounted high in the office or the back of the store, where they keep working if the cellar fills.

We also set the cellar up so it can be armed on its own. A shop that keeps its stockroom downstairs can arm the cellar all day and disarm it only when someone goes down with a code, so a door that should stay shut tells the owner when it opens, even during business hours. That works especially well where the cellar has a sidewalk or yard entrance that staff rarely use.

Empty and seasonal buildings: the business that is closed for weeks

Some waterfront businesses close for weeks in winter, and some buildings stand empty between tenants for much longer. The repeated break-ins at the former TGI Fridays on Harkness Avenue show what happens to an empty building that is only fenced: windows were broken, repaired and broken again. For a closed business, the alarm’s job is to make the first entry the last. That means interior motion detection and glass-break detectors, not just door contacts, because people who break windows on empty buildings rarely use the door; a contact on any roof hatch or bulkhead, since the video from Harkness Avenue showed a teenager standing on the roof; and phone alerts to someone who will actually go and look. A camera on the same app is the most useful add-on here, because it lets the owner see in seconds whether the alert is a break-in, a bird or a burst pipe.

Upper floors over the shop

A Sheepshead Bay commercial building is often three things stacked: a store at street level, an office or storage floor in the middle, and an apartment at the top. The jewelry store burglary News 12 reported in January 2026, with entry through a third-floor window at 4:30 a.m., shows why the upper floors belong in the plan. When the owner of the store controls those floors, their windows go on the store’s alarm as a separate area. When someone else does, we suggest the store protects its own ceiling and stairwell door with motion detection and a contact, so that anyone who comes in from above still has to cross a sensor to reach the shop.

Compliance notes for Sheepshead Bay homes, stores and restaurants

  • The siren has to stop on its own. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes of going off. On attached blocks and in bungalow courts, where a siren reaches many households, we set the cutoff well inside that limit.
  • The installer has to be licensed. The New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers under Article 6-D of the General Business Law, with 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting. Our license number is #12000287431, and any installer’s license can be checked with the Department of State before you hire them.
  • Rented houses need the right detectors. HPD requires smoke detectors in each unit of a multiple dwelling, and carbon monoxide detectors in non-owner-occupied one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of each sleeping room’s entrance. Monitored detectors on an alarm panel can meet that requirement and also report to the owner.
  • The neighborhood’s one landmark has rules. The former Lundy’s building at 1901 Emmons Avenue is, as the Brooklyn Eagle put it, Sheepshead Bay’s only city-designated landmark, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission has turned down added fixtures on its facade. A tenant there should expect any outdoor siren, strobe or camera to need the Commission’s review, so we plan those devices indoors or out of sight first.
  • False fire alarms can bring summonses. A Brooklyn homeowner wrote on Brownstoner’s forum that repeated false alarms through a monitored system led to an FDNY summons. Heat detectors in kitchens, smoke detectors kept away from steam and cooking, and test mode during any repair work keep a monitored panel out of that trouble.
  • Exits stay exits, and fire alarms stay separate. Nothing we install locks or slows a required exit, a roof door or a fire-escape window; contacts only report. A burglar alarm can carry smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors for a home or a small store, but it does not replace a building fire alarm system that a larger restaurant, school or apartment building may need.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Sheepshead Bay: Questions Answered

These are the questions homeowners, bungalow owners, renters and business owners between the bay and Avenue U bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from. General questions about price, monitoring and coverage are in the FAQ further down.

Houses in the flood zone

Our panel is still on the cellar wall where it was in 2012, and it seems to work fine. Is there any reason to move it if nothing is wrong?

Yes, if it is below the height water could reach again. A panel that works today still goes silent the moment it is submerged, which is exactly when doors have been forced by water and the house may be empty. If yours was under water in 2012 and was dried and restarted, its board and battery terminals may also be corroding slowly. We test it, and if the wiring is sound we move the panel or replace it on the first living floor and keep the existing sensors, which is usually the panel-replacement line on our list rather than a new system.

If the panel moves upstairs, what happens to the old wired sensors whose cables all run down to the cellar?

They can usually stay. We either extend the cables up to the new panel location through a closet or a chase, or leave a small wired expander module in the cellar, mounted as high as possible, which collects the old wires and passes their signals to the panel upstairs. The expander is inexpensive and quick to replace if water ever reaches it, while the part that reports and keeps its battery is safe above. Any old sensor that no longer tests reliably is replaced with a wireless one.

We are buying a house below the Belt Parkway, and nobody can tell us whether water got inside in 2012. How do you choose a safe height for the panel?

From the published flood elevation for the lot, the house’s own floor heights and anything the building tells us, such as a stain line, newer drywall at the same height in every room, or a replaced boiler. The city’s report put Sandy’s water at four to eight feet above grade in the study area and up to ten feet south of the Belt Parkway, so for a house there we start from the assumption that the cellar and possibly the first floor took water, and we mount the panel on the highest practical floor near the main entrance.

An older relative sleeps on the lowest floor of our house. If water comes in at night, what should the alarm do for her first?

Wake her, and wake whoever sleeps upstairs. A water sensor at the lowest point of her floor and another at the bottom of the stairs can sound a loud, distinct tone on a keypad or sounder in her room and upstairs, and send phone alerts to the family, without calling the police. The neighbor on Bragg Street described waking to find his grandmother’s basement flooded; a sensor on that floor reports in the first minutes. We also add a smoke and carbon monoxide detector in her room on the same panel, because the lowest floor is where the boiler usually is.

We live up near Avenue U, well away from the bay. Is any of this water planning relevant to us?

Some of it. The bay is not the only source of water in a cellar. The February 2026 flooding on Bragg Street came from a broken water main, and boilers, water heaters, washing machines and backed-up drains flood cellars on every block. You may not need the panel moved for flood height, but one or two water sensors in the cellar and a power-loss alert from the panel are cheap insurance anywhere in the neighborhood.

Bungalows and courts

Our bungalow is small enough that one motion detector sees nearly the whole house. Do the windows still need their own sensors?

Usually, yes, for two reasons. A motion detector only works when the house is empty and the alarm is fully armed; window contacts let you arm the perimeter while you are home asleep, which a bungalow on a quiet walkway needs as much as any house. And contacts trip as the window opens, before anyone is inside, while motion detection trips only once someone is already in the room. On a very small house, a mix of contacts on the windows that can be reached from the walkway and one motion detector covering the rest is the usual answer.

Taller buildings now surround our bungalow court. Will a cellular alarm still find a signal down there?

Probably, but we measure rather than assume. Brownstoner noted in 2015 that new development around the bungalows casts shadows over them, and the same buildings can weaken cellular signal in a court that already sits below the street. Before mounting the panel we check signal strength at the planned spot. If it is weak, we move the panel to the strongest wall, choose a communicator on a different carrier, or add a second reporting path over the house’s internet.

Shops, restaurants and congregations

Our cooks start prep in the cellar around dawn, long before the front opens. How does the cellar stay protected without the prep crew setting it off?

By making the cellar and the front two separate areas of one panel. The prep crew disarms only the cellar and the back door with their own codes when they arrive, and the front of the restaurant, the office and the cash zone stay armed until the manager opens. The owner sees exactly when each area was disarmed and by whom. That matters here because the January 2025 break-in on Sheepshead Bay Road happened around 6 a.m., the hour when a kitchen may already be awake and the front still closed.

Our top floor is storage that nobody visits for weeks, and its back windows face a neighbor’s roof. Does a storage floor really need sensors?

If its windows can be reached from that roof, yes. The jewelry store burglars News 12 reported in 2026 came in through a third-floor window, and a floor nobody visits is one where an open window goes unnoticed for days. Contacts on those windows and one motion detector by the stairs, on an area of the panel that stays armed around the clock, cost little and report the moment someone comes in, long before they reach the store below.

Our place on Emmons Avenue is busy until the early hours all summer and closed several days a week in winter. Can the alarm’s schedule follow the seasons?

Yes. The panel can expect to be armed by a different hour on each day of the week, and the schedule can be changed from the app when the season turns. If the building has not been armed by the expected time, the owner gets a text; on days the restaurant is closed, any disarm at all can send an alert. Opening and closing reports go to the owner every day, so a winter week with no one inside is never a week without news.

Our congregation’s building is full during services, but the coat room and office are out of sight. What can an alarm do while the doors are open?

Protect the office, not the coat room. An office or collection room can be its own area, armed all the time except when someone with a code is inside, so a door opening during services sends a silent alert to the person responsible. A coat room that people use during services cannot be armed while it is in use; News 12 reported in 2025 that a man took a jacket and wallet from a Sheepshead Bay mosque during prayers, and a camera covering the door is the honest answer there, not a sensor.

Apartments

Half the outlets in our older apartment are unreliable. Does a wireless alarm need a special outlet, and what happens when one fails?

It needs one ordinary working outlet for the panel’s small plug-in transformer, ideally one that is not switched by a wall switch, and we test it before mounting. If that outlet fails, the panel runs on its backup battery and sends a power-loss alert to your phone, so you find out at once instead of when the battery dies. The sensors themselves run on their own batteries. If the wiring in your building is a known problem, report it to the landlord; an alarm should never be the reason to keep using a bad circuit.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Sheepshead Bay

Ask a chatbot or a search summary about a home or shop alarm here and the answer is usually written for an inland suburb, with prices averaged by national lead sites such as HomeAdvisor and Angi. These are seven claims we see repeated, in the form they usually take, and what we actually find on jobs between the bay and Avenue U.

“Mount the control panel in a closet or the basement, out of sight.”

Out of sight is good advice. The basement is not, in a neighborhood where the city counted more than 2,000 buildings in the floodplain and water reached four to eight feet above grade in 2012. A panel in the cellar is the first part of the alarm to go under, and the part whose loss silences everything else.

We put the panel, its battery and its communicator out of sight on a living floor: a hall closet, a pantry, the inside wall of a bedroom. Only the inexpensive sensors stay downstairs, and the panel can keep working without them.

“Water sensors are a nice extra.”

In a dry suburb they may be. In Sheepshead Bay, the cellar is where the boiler, the meters, the stock and sometimes a bedroom are, and water arrives from the bay in storms and from the street when a main breaks, as it did on Bragg Street at 2:20 one February morning in 2026.

Here we treat a water sensor at the lowest point of each building as part of a standard alarm, not an extra, and we set its alert to wake the house rather than call the police. Brick Underground reported that most insurers credit monitored low-temperature alarms as well as burglar alarms, which makes the same sensors worth having on paper too.

“Burglary is rising everywhere, so get the biggest system you can.”

The 61st Precinct’s numbers are steady, not soaring: 134 burglaries in 2026 through November 8 against 130 a year earlier, a rise of 3.1 percent, and burglary down 92.6 percent over 33 years. Grand larceny, theft without a break-in, is up 7.3 percent, and much of it is not something a house alarm can stop.

What sizes a Sheepshead Bay alarm is the building: how many ways in it has, who shares it, where the valuables sit, how low it stands and how long it stays empty. A tower apartment with one door needs very little; an attached house with a garage level, a cellar and a tenant needs more.

We would rather install a system that gets armed every night than a large one that is switched off after a month of nuisance alarms. If a request includes sensors a building does not need, the quote says so.

“Wi-Fi alarms are fine; everybody has internet.”

Many kits report over the household’s internet. In Sheepshead Bay the router and the cable modem are often in the cellar, where the line comes in, so they flood and lose power with it. In a shop the router sits in the same room a burglar is standing in, and in a two-family it may belong to the tenant.

The panels we install report over cellular first, with internet as an optional second path. Vincent Ingrisano of Alarms R Us told Brick Underground, "you don’t want your wifi to go down and your alarm not to work," and on this coast that is a storm-season problem as well as a burglary problem.

“A loud siren is what scares burglars and alerts neighbors.”

On a quiet inland street, maybe. On Emmons Avenue on a summer night, a siren inside a closed restaurant competes with crowds from the party boats, music and traffic, and in a bungalow court it mostly wakes the neighbors, who cannot do anything about it.

What brings help is the report: a cellular signal to the owner’s phone and, with monitoring, a central station, within seconds of the first sensor. We still install a siren, indoors, sized to the building, with a cutoff well inside the fifteen minutes city law allows.

The same seven steps apply to a tower apartment on Voorhies Avenue and a restaurant with a cellar on Sheepshead Bay Road. What is different in Sheepshead Bay is the order: we start at the lowest level of the building and work up, because the height of the water decides where the most important parts of the alarm can go.

You text photos of every door, the cellar or garage entrance and the windows someone could reach, with the cross street, and tell us how high water came inside in 2012 or since, if you know.

Field Notes: The First Thing We Look For Is a Line on the Wall

On a Sheepshead Bay survey, the technician’s first stop is not the front door but the cellar stairs, with a flashlight. In a house that took water in 2012, there is often still a faint line on the foundation wall, a band of newer drywall, a boiler that is obviously younger than the house, or an electric panel that has been raised. Any of those tells us more than a flood map about how high to mount the alarm. Then we look for the old alarm box, which in older houses is usually on that same wall, and open it: a rusted transformer, green battery terminals or a board with white residue means the panel goes upstairs no matter how well it seems to work. Only after that do we walk up through the garage level, the living floors and the roof access, and the front door, which most owners want to talk about first, is usually the last opening we look at, because it is the one everyone already locks.

What should we ask a central monitoring station before we sign up?
+

Ask whether the agreement is month to month or for a fixed term, what it costs to cancel, whether the station handles water and low-temperature alarms differently from break-ins, who it calls first and in what order, and whether it will issue a certificate your insurer accepts. Is a store-bought kit good enough for a house in the flood zone, or does it need a professional install?
+

A kit can work if its base station is placed above the flood height, it reports over cellular rather than only over Wi-Fi, and its water sensors are in the right spots. Most kits leave those decisions to the buyer, and many end up on a cellar shelf beside the modem. We install customer-owned kits for $200 to $400, or a professionally designed system starts at $600 on our Brooklyn list.

Can our alarm’s call list include a neighbor or a community patrol before the police are called?

With central station monitoring, you set the order: the station can call your household, then a trusted neighbor or a patrol number you provide, then dispatch, depending on the signal. A community patrol director told News 12 in 2026 that his volunteers reached a car alarm in seconds because they were already nearby, which is the case for keeping close contacts high on the list.

Not well. Each house should have its own panel, codes and app, because the households arm and disarm on their own schedules and each owner is responsible for their own house and insurance. What neighbors can share is information: each system can send alerts to a neighbor’s phone as well as the owner’s, so whoever is home in winter knows when a house next door reports a broken window or water on the floor.

How do we choose between the wireless range and complete home protection for our house?
+

The wireless range, $600 to $1,200, suits one household on one or two floors with a handful of doors and windows. How many water sensors does a typical Sheepshead Bay cellar end up with?
+

Usually two to four: one at the lowest corner of the floor, one beside the boiler and water heater, one near a floor drain or sump if there is one, and sometimes one at the foot of the cellar stairs. A shop cellar with a walk-in or a compressor may need one or two more. Each is listed on the quote, so you can drop any you think you do not need.

Our attached house shares a cellar wall with the neighbors, and water usually reaches their side first. Can our alarm warn us when theirs fills?
+

Only if they want it. A sensor in their cellar would be part of their property and their system. What we can do is put a water sensor right against the shared wall on your side, at its lowest point, so you are told as soon as water comes through, and if the neighbors install their own system, each house can add the other’s phones to its water alerts.

Our business keeps equipment in a small structure with no outlet of its own. Can it be alarmed at all?
+

Sometimes. Wireless sensors run on their own batteries and can reach a panel in a nearby building if the distance and walls allow, so a shed or storage box within range of your main premises can often be covered from the main panel. If the structure is too far away and has no power, a battery-only device will not give reliable round-the-clock reporting, and we will tell you that rather than sell you one.

We own a two-family here but live out of state, and the tenants are home most days. Who should the water alerts and the break-in alerts reach first?
+

Usually different people. Break-in alerts for the tenant’s floor go to the tenant first, because they are on site and the area is theirs. Water, power-loss and low-temperature alerts from the cellar go to you and to the tenant at the same time, with the tenant’s permission, because you pay for the boiler and the repairs but the tenant can open the cellar door for a plumber within minutes. A local relative or a property manager can be added as a third contact.
