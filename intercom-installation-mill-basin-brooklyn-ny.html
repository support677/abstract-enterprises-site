The Neighborhood

Why Mill Basin Properties Need Intercom Installation

The second thing that shapes an intercom here is water. Nearly every Mill Basin property sits close to the inlet or Jamaica Bay, and First Street's model puts 3,136 properties, 98.8 percent of the neighborhood, at some risk of flooding over the next three decades. Brooklyn Paper reported Mill Island residents describing basements flooded and bulkheads unearthed by Sandy. The old habit in American houses is to put the doorbell transformer, and later the intercom's power supply, next to the electrical panel in the basement. In Mill Basin that habit can mean the whole system goes dark the first time water comes in, and stays dark until someone replaces parts that were never meant to get wet. Salt air works on the outdoor hardware more slowly, finding any gap in a gasket, any unsealed screw hole and any cable entry left open. An intercom designed for a lobby in Midtown is not automatically right for a gate post a block from the water.

  • One- and two-family zoning. Wikipedia describes Mill Basin as zoned predominantly for one- or two-family homes, so most jobs are private houses that no intercom law covers, and the owner decides what the system has to do.
  • Four building eras on adjacent blocks. Houses from 1920 to 1945 north of Avenue U, postwar ranches and split-levels, 1960s Mill Island detached and semi-detached homes, and 1980s and newer brick and stucco houses each put the door, the garage and the living space in different places.
  • Distance from the sidewalk. On the larger lots, a visitor at the sidewalk gate can be a long way from the kitchen, the upstairs bedrooms or the dock, which is why cable length and the number of indoor stations drive the quote more than button count.

Repair Calls in Mill Basin

Need Repair in Mill Basin? A Gate Station Gone Quiet, a Release That Hums but Will Not Open, or a System That Never Came Back After Water

Repair calls from Mill Basin tend to follow the layout of the lot. The most common is a call station at a sidewalk or driveway gate that has gone silent. Gate stations sit at the end of the longest cable on the property, often buried under a walk or a lawn, and they take the worst of the weather: rain driven sideways off the water, salt carried in the air, and a gate that slams and shakes the post every time it closes. When one stops working, the fault can be the station itself, the buried cable, a splice in a box at the foot of the post, or the power supply back in the house. The second most common call is a release that buzzes but does not open the door or gate. That usually means the strike or gate lock has drifted out of line with the latch, the lock is not getting enough power at the end of a long run, or the gate has sagged on its hinges so the latch binds. The third kind is specific to houses that have taken water: a system that worked until a storm and never came back, because the power supply, the distribution module or the network switch sat on a basement shelf below the high-water mark.

Two checks are worth doing before you call. If the front door or gate is not latching, lock it with its key or deadbolt while the intercom is down, since an unlatched door matters more than a quiet bell. Then find the intercom's power supply. If it has an indicator light and the light is out, check the breaker that feeds it; if the breaker trips again, leave it off. If the power supply or anything near it was under water, do not switch it back on at all, and tell us when you call. Call (347) 934-8335 or send photos on WhatsApp. Many single faults, such as a corroded gate station, a strike that no longer meets its latch, a failed power supply or a broken splice, are found and fixed in one visit. Service calls and fault tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we estimate the tracing time before we start. When a repair would cost a large share of a new system, or water has damaged parts that are no longer made, you get both options in writing.

Every Mill Basin job starts with three questions: how many ways there are onto the property, which one visitors actually use, and where in the house or yard the people answering spend their time. The services below cover the combinations we see most often on these blocks.

Sidewalk and driveway gate stations

A weather-rated call station on a gate post or pier where the visitor first stops, with a release for the gate lock and the cable buried in conduit back to the house.

Front-door stations for set-back houses

For houses with no gate, a station at the front door, placed where a driver walking up the drive will see it, with indoor stations wherever the family actually is.

Indoor stations on every level

Two-family systems

The Old Mill Basin house: short walk, old wire

The houses north of Avenue U are the oldest residential stock in the neighborhood, built in the years Forgotten New York gives as 1920 to 1945. Their doors are generally closer to the sidewalk than the postwar houses to the south, and some sit on lots with a narrow side path rather than a full driveway. The visitor's walk is short, so distance is less of a problem than in the newer sections, but the wiring is older. Many of these houses have had a doorbell since they were built, with a transformer in the basement and thin bell wire running up to a chime in the hall. That wire may have been extended, spliced and painted over through several owners. Before we decide whether any of it can be reused, we test it for continuity and for leakage between conductors, look at the insulation wherever it is exposed, and compare its length and gauge with what the new system needs.

The common result in these houses is a new two-wire or networked cable from the door to the equipment location, routed through the basement ceiling and up an interior wall, with the old bell wire left in place but disconnected and labeled. Where the house holds two households, each gets its own button and indoor station, and the street door's strike is sized so a release from the upper floor opens it as reliably as one from the first. Some of these houses also sit close to the commercial strips on Flatbush Avenue and Avenue U, where a front door sees more foot traffic than a house on the water. In those cases we put the station where it is easy to reach but hard to damage, and use a fail-secure strike so a power cut leaves the door locked from outside while still opening freely from inside.

The postwar ranch and split-level: one door, several half-floors

The ranches and split-levels of the early 1950s were built for families who parked in the driveway and came in through a side or garage door as often as the front. That is the first thing a survey checks: which door do visitors actually use? On many of these houses a delivery driver goes to the front door, while friends and family come to the side door off the driveway. If both need to ring, the system needs two outdoor stations that the indoor stations can tell apart. If only the front door matters, the side door stays on its key lock and the station goes on the front alone.

Inside, a split-level puts the kitchen, the bedrooms and the family room on different half-floors joined by short flights of stairs. A single monitor in the front hall can be heard from one or two of them at most. We plan indoor stations for the levels where people actually spend time, usually the kitchen and the bedroom level, and sometimes a lower family room. In these houses the lower level is also the part most exposed to water, so we avoid putting the power supply or any other equipment there if a higher spot is available; a closet on the main level or a utility space on the upper level is a better home. Cable usually travels through the basement or crawl space and up interior walls, and the frame construction of most of these houses makes that straightforward. Where a lower level has been finished, we look for the paths the electrician used rather than opening new ones.

The Mill Island detached and semi-detached home: the water at the back door

Community Board 18 describes Mill Island, along Jamaica Bay, as developed in the 1960s with detached and semi-detached one- and two-family homes. Many back onto the water. For an intercom, the semi-detached houses raise one specific question: what belongs to whom. A semi-detached house shares a wall with its neighbor and sometimes a front walk or a stretch of driveway. Stations, cable and conduit stay on the owner's side of every shared surface, and we do not anchor anything into a party wall without the owner confirming what is on the other side. Where two households in one house share a front door, the buttons are labeled and the apartment doors keep their own locks, as in any two-family.

The second question is the yard behind the house. On the water, families spend summer evenings on the back terrace or at the dock, out of earshot of a station in the front hall. Some owners want a second station at a rear gate; others want the front call to reach their phones; others want an outdoor-rated indoor station on a covered porch. Each of those has a different cable and weather requirement, and we price them separately so the owner can choose. Brooklyn Paper's report of Mill Island basements flooding in Sandy is the third consideration: in these houses, the equipment location is chosen first, and the cable routes are planned back from it.

The 1980s brick colonial: formal front, everyday side

The brick colonials that cityneighborhoods.nyc dates to the 1980s tend to have a formal front entrance, often with a wide door or a pair of doors, an attached garage, and on the water a terrace and dock behind. The front entrance is usually where guests and drivers go, but family members come and go through the garage. That makes the front door the natural place for the main station, with indoor stations spread through the house and, if the owner wants, a small station at the door between the garage and the house so someone pulling in can call upstairs. The brick face takes a station well if it is anchored into solid brick rather than a mortar joint, mounted on a backing plate where the surface is uneven, and sealed where the cable enters. Wide and double front doors need strikes chosen for the door's actual construction, and sometimes the inactive leaf needs its bolts checked so a release really does let the active leaf swing.

Many colonials of that era came with a factory room-to-room intercom or a simple doorbell, and some owners have since added a battery video doorbell. Those pieces rarely combine well with a new system. We usually leave a working chime in place if the family likes it, and replace the rest, so there is one system that every indoor station and phone can answer. If a home-automation system already runs the house, we check which door stations it supports before choosing one, rather than adding a second app nobody uses.

The new stucco house on the water: long runs and many entrances

StreetEasy notes that older Mill Basin houses have been razed and large houses with private docks built in their place, and cityneighborhoods.nyc describes 21st-century stucco mansions with docks lined with boats. These are the houses where an intercom covers the most ground. A typical layout has a pedestrian gate at the sidewalk, a vehicle gate across the driveway, a garage for several cars, a front door set well back, a rear terrace, a pool area and a dock. The earlier report of houses with elevators and a six-car garage on National Drive, and the 14,000-square-foot listing with a pool, boat slips and a spa, give a sense of the scale. Each entrance needs a decision: does it get a station, does it get a release, and which indoor stations and phones should it ring?

When the house is being built or rebuilt, the intercom belongs on the drawings. The best moment to pull cable is right after the electrician's rough-in, when the framing is open and the trench to the gate has not been backfilled. We pull a cable from each outdoor station location to the equipment spot, one from the equipment spot to each indoor station, one to each strike or gate lock, and a spare to the gate and the dock where the builder allows, all labeled at both ends and capped. Outdoor runs go in conduit. Stucco facades need care at every penetration: the station's back box is set before the finish coat where possible, and any opening cut afterward is sealed so water cannot get behind the stucco. The equipment spot is chosen above the design flood level, not in the lowest level next to the mechanical room.

Condos, gated communities and small multi-families: where the rules begin

A small number of Mill Basin buildings are not private houses. Redfin lists Mill Harbor Condominium as a gated community, and a month of Redfin listings showed condos, a townhouse and 23 multi-family units for sale in the neighborhood. In a condo or gated community, the entrance system usually belongs to the association, and individual owners control only the stations inside their units. Changes to an exterior station or to the community's entrance go through the board. In a multi-family building of three or more apartments, the city's self-closing door rules apply, and larger buildings can fall under the intercom requirement described in the rules section below.

The practical work in these buildings is different from a house. There is a directory or a button for each unit, a power supply sized for the farthest station, door hardware that must self-close and self-lock after every release, and records that a board or managing agent can keep. Where a garage or utility room has block walls, cable reaches the entrance through a cored hole that is sleeved and sealed afterward. Where the building sits low, the equipment goes as high as the building allows, and we discuss battery backup for the entrance with the board.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks Between a Mill Basin Gate and the Water

Find the real front door. The survey starts on the sidewalk with one question: where would a stranger go? On a house with a gate, a driveway and a side path, that is not always the door the owner thinks of as the front. The technician walks the route a delivery driver would take and notes where that person naturally stops and looks for a button.

Count the ways onto the lot. Pedestrian gate, vehicle gate, side path, garage door, rear gate, dock gate. Each gets a line on the sketch, with a note on whether it should ring, release, both or neither. Most owners are surprised by the number.

Look for the water line. In the basement or lowest level, the technician looks for marks on the walls, replaced drywall, raised mechanical equipment and anything the owner says took water before. The intercom's equipment goes above that line, and the sketch says where.

Check the gate itself. A gate that sags on its hinges, drags on the ground or bounces when it closes will defeat any lock. The technician swings it, watches it latch, and notes whether hinge or closer work is needed before an electric lock goes on.

Feel for the wind. Which way does weather come at the gate post and the front door? Stations go on the sheltered face of a pier or post wherever the visitor can still reach them easily, and every screw hole and cable entry gets sealed.

Switch the power off and try the doors. With the intercom and lock power off, each door is opened by hand from inside and with a key from outside. Each gate is checked to confirm it still opens from inside the property. This test is repeated at handover.

Ask who answers, and from where. Who is home during the day, on which floor, whether anyone spends evenings outside, and whether a second household or a live-in relative needs their own station. Those answers decide how many indoor stations there are and where they hang.

Above the Water Line

Building a Mill Basin Intercom That Survives Salt Air and the Next Flood

Most intercom advice assumes the equipment lives somewhere dry. In Mill Basin that assumption needs checking on every job. First Street's flood data puts almost the entire neighborhood at some risk over the next 30 years, Brooklyn Paper reported Sandy flooding basements on Mill Island, and anyone who has lived near the inlet through a nor'easter knows how salt air finds its way into outdoor hardware. None of this means an intercom cannot last here. It means a few decisions have to be made deliberately instead of by habit.

The equipment location comes first. Every intercom has a heart: a power supply, often a distribution module or controller, and on networked systems a switch. In older houses these sat beside the electrical panel in the basement, because that was where power was. In Mill Basin we choose the equipment location before anything else, looking for a dry closet or utility space on the main floor or higher, ideally on an inside wall, with a nearby outlet or a circuit an electrician can add. Cable routes are then planned outward from that spot to the doors, the gates and the indoor stations. If the only practical place is low, we mount the equipment as high on the wall as possible and tell the owner plainly what the risk is.

What goes low, and what does not. Some parts of the system have to be near the ground. A gate station sits at the gate. A strike sits in the door frame. Buried cable runs under the yard. Those parts are chosen to tolerate their location: outdoor-rated stations with gaskets, strikes rated for exterior doors, and cable rated for direct burial or run inside conduit. What we keep out of low places is everything that does not need to be there, especially anything with a circuit board and a power connection.

Sealing the outdoor stations. A station's weather rating describes how it was tested, not how it was installed. Water and salt get in through screw holes drilled into a gate pier, through a cable entry left open behind the station, and through a back box that collects condensation. We set stations on a backing plate where the surface is uneven, seal around the cable entry, use stainless or coated fasteners, and leave a drip loop so water running down the cable falls away before it reaches the station. On a stucco or brick face, the hole behind the station is sealed so water cannot travel into the wall.

Conduit for anything outdoors or underground. Cable to a gate or a dock leaves the house, crosses a yard and often passes under a walk or a driveway. We run it inside conduit, with sweeps rather than sharp bends so it can be pulled out and replaced later, and we seal the conduit where it enters the house so the conduit itself does not become a channel for water. Where a trench is dug, the owner gets a sketch showing where it runs and how deep, because the next contractor to dig in the yard should know it is there.

After water. If a system has been under water, the parts that were submerged should be treated as suspect even if they seem to work. We test cable that sat in water before reusing it, replace electronics that were submerged, and check connections in every box along the run for corrosion. Owners who are raising mechanical equipment or rebuilding after a storm can move the intercom equipment at the same time, which is usually cheaper than doing it separately later.

Power outages. When power goes out, an intercom stops calling unless it has battery backup. The doors, however, should behave predictably. A fail-secure strike leaves the door locked from outside and openable from inside and with a key. A gate lock should do the same. For a Mill Basin house, we recommend that combination, so that losing power during a storm never leaves the gate or the door unlocked, and nobody is ever trapped inside. Battery backup for the intercom itself can be added where the owner wants calls to keep working through a short outage.

Answer Without Opening

Seeing and Talking to the Visitor First: What an Intercom Changes at a Mill Basin Door

A video intercom changes the order of things. The visitor presses a button. Someone inside sees and hears them from wherever the nearest indoor station is, or on a phone, and talks to them through a closed door or a locked gate. Only then does anyone decide whether to release the lock or walk out. For deliveries, it means the driver can be told where to leave a package without anyone opening the door. For a stranger, it means the conversation happens at the property line rather than on the doorstep.

The Rules by Building Class

Mostly Private Houses, a Few Covered Buildings: What New York Law Asks of Mill Basin Entrances

Mill Basin's zoning makes it unusual in this silo. In much of Brooklyn, the intercom question is what the law requires of an apartment building. Here, most properties are one- and two-family houses that no intercom statute covers, and only a handful of buildings fall under the multiple-dwelling rules. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. These are the public sources, so owners can ask their attorney, board or managing agent the right questions.

One- and two-family houses. The city's best-known intercom requirements are written for multiple dwellings, which start at three apartments. An owner of a one- or two-family house who installs an intercom does so by choice. Once a tenant in a two-family relies on a door system, though, keeping it working becomes part of maintaining the apartment.

Buildings of three to seven apartments. HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and unit entrance doors in every type of multiple dwelling must be self-closing, and that broken locks or intercoms should be repaired immediately. Parker Custom Security summarizes the intercom rule this way: buildings with fewer than eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement, but the self-closing and self-locking door requirement still applies. Some of the multi-family houses in Mill Basin listings fall into this group.

Buildings of eight or more apartments. Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Mill Basin: Questions Answered

These are the situations Mill Basin owners raise most: properties with more ways in than anyone planned for, cable that has to reach the water's edge, what an intercom can and cannot do after a frightening local incident, and whether a dead system is worth fixing before a sale. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Many ways in, one place to ring

Our lot has a sidewalk gate, a driveway, a side path to the garage and a gate at the dock. Which one should visitors be sent to?

Usually the one a stranger would choose without being told. On most Mill Basin lots that is the pedestrian gate at the sidewalk, or the front door if there is no gate, because that is where a driver parks and looks first. That entrance gets the main station, the clearest label and the best light. The others are handled according to who uses them. A side path that only family uses can stay on a key lock or a keypad. A driveway gate may need a station at car-window height if visitors drive in. A dock gate usually needs a station only if guests really do arrive by water. The goal is one obvious place to ring, so visitors are not left choosing between three doors, plus stations at the other entrances only where they solve a real problem. We sketch every entrance on the survey and price each extra station separately, so the owner can decide which ones are worth it.

The house is large and spread over several floors. How many indoor stations does it really need?

Enough that someone in each place the family actually spends time can answer without walking more than a few steps. In practice that usually means the kitchen, the main bedroom floor, a home office if someone works there, and a lower family room or gym if it is used daily. Rooms that are rarely occupied do not need one, and a phone app can cover the yard, the pool and the dock for family members who carry their phones. Guest suites and staff quarters are worth a station if someone lives there. We ask the household to walk us through an ordinary weekday and an ordinary summer evening, and place stations along those routes. Adding a station later is possible, but it is cheaper to pull the cable during the first visit, even if the station itself is added afterward.

Our gate piers already have lamps on top. Can the gate station run off the power that feeds them?

Usually not directly, and it is rarely the best idea. Pier lamps are on a line-voltage circuit, often switched or on a timer, and an intercom station runs on low voltage supplied from the system's power supply or over its network cable. Tapping the lamp circuit would mean adding a transformer inside the pier, exposed to the weather, and the station would lose power whenever the lights are switched off. What the existing lamp circuit can offer is a path: if there is already conduit from the house to the pier, a low-voltage cable can often be pulled through a separate conduit alongside it, or through the same trench if it is reopened, but never inside the same conduit as the power wiring. We check what is in the pier and the trench before proposing a route, and the electrical side stays with a licensed electrician.

At the water's edge

Our bulkhead was rebuilt after Sandy. Can intercom cable run along it to a station at the dock?

It can, but the route has to respect the bulkhead and the water. Fastening cable or conduit to the bulkhead itself can damage a structure that was expensive to rebuild, and anything mounted on its face is exposed to spray and, in a storm, to waves. The better route is usually underground across the yard, inside conduit, ending at a short post or a sheltered wall near the dock entrance rather than on the bulkhead. The station goes high enough to stay out of normal splash, faces away from the prevailing weather, and is sealed at every opening. If the dock has its own electrical service, we coordinate with the electrician rather than borrowing power from it. Before any digging, buried utilities and any irrigation lines are located, and the route is drawn on the sketch the owner keeps. If the bulkhead contractor's drawings show tie-backs or anchors under the yard, we stay clear of them.

Can the dock station be shut down for the winter when the boat is out of the water?

Yes, and on some systems that is worth doing. A dock station that nobody uses from November to April still sits in the wind and salt, and if it rings during a storm because water gets into the button, it can confuse the household. Most systems let the owner disable one outdoor station in the settings without touching the others, so it stops calling the house until it is turned back on. Some owners go further and fit a weatherproof cover over the station for the winter. What we do not recommend is disconnecting the cable at the house each fall, because repeated disconnecting and reconnecting wears the terminals and tends to get done wrong in the spring. At handover we show the owner where the setting is, and we write it on the instruction sheet left with the system.

Safety and selling

After the attempted break-in on Mayfair Drive South, neighbors are talking about intercoms. What can one actually do for a house, and what can't it?

It can let you see and talk to whoever is at your gate or door, from anywhere in the house or on your phone, without opening anything. That means a stranger is dealt with through a locked gate or a closed door, a delivery driver can be told where to leave a package, and nobody has to walk out to the property line to find out who is ringing. It cannot physically stop someone who is prepared to force a door, climb a fence or break a window, and an intercom company that suggests otherwise is overselling. The strength of the door, the frame and the lock, the lighting at the entrance, and whether the household keeps doors locked are what resist force. Owners who want a record of who came to the door add cameras, and owners who want to control who can let themselves in add access control. We plan the intercom so it fits with either if the owner wants them later.

The gate intercom on the house we are selling has been dead for years. Is it worth repairing before we list?

It depends on what is wrong and on what the buyer will see. A large house with a gate station that visibly does not work raises questions during showings and inspections, and buyers tend to assume the worst about anything electrical near the water. If the fault is a single part, such as a corroded station, a failed power supply or a strike out of line, the repair is usually modest and leaves a working system to show. If the system failed because its electronics sat in a flooded basement, or the brand's parts are no longer made, a full repair may not be worth it for a house about to change hands. In that case, removing the dead station neatly and capping the cable may present better than leaving it in place. We can diagnose the system, give you the repair cost and the removal cost in writing, and leave a short report you can share with buyers either way.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Mill Basin

"Brooklyn buildings are required to have an intercom"

General answers about intercoms in Brooklyn tend to start from apartment buildings, because that is where most of the borough lives. They then present the city's intercom rules as if they applied everywhere.

Mill Basin is zoned predominantly for one- and two-family houses. The intercom requirement in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies to multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments, and smaller multiple dwellings carry door rules rather than an intercom rule. Most Mill Basin owners have no legal obligation to install one at all.

That changes the conversation. The owner is not buying compliance; they are deciding how the household wants to answer the door. The design should follow the family's routine, not a statute that does not apply to the house.

"A Wi-Fi video doorbell is all a house needs"

For a house where the front door is a few feet from the router and only one household answers, a video doorbell can be a reasonable choice. AI summaries often stop there.

On a Mill Basin lot with a gate at the sidewalk, the visitor may stand well beyond reliable Wi-Fi, through a brick or stucco wall and across a yard. Most doorbells also cannot release a gate or a door unless a lock is wired in separately, and they give two households one button and one account.

The question to ask is where the visitor stands, who answers, and whether anyone needs to let them in. If the answers are "at the door, one household, no," a doorbell may be enough. If not, a wired system is usually the better fit.

"Put the intercom power supply next to the electrical panel"

Installation guides commonly suggest mounting the power supply near the main panel, since that is where power is easiest to reach. In most of the country that is fine advice.

In Mill Basin, the main panel has often been in the basement, and First Street puts nearly every property in the neighborhood at some flood risk. Brooklyn Paper reported Sandy flooding Mill Island basements. A power supply on a basement shelf is the first part of the system to fail when water comes in.

We choose the equipment location before anything else, above the lowest level wherever the house allows, and plan the cable routes from there. That one decision does more for the system's life here than any brand choice.

"Any outdoor-rated station will hold up"

Product listings and AI answers treat a weather rating as the end of the discussion. If the station is rated for outdoor use, the reasoning goes, it will last outdoors.

The rating describes how the unit performed in a test, assembled correctly. Near the inlet and the bay, failures usually come from the installation: an open cable entry, screw holes drilled into a pier without sealant, ordinary steel fasteners that rust, a station mounted on the windward face of a post.

A rated station still has to be mounted, sealed and fastened with salt air in mind. We check the station's exposure on the survey and choose the mounting face and hardware accordingly.

"Intercom cost depends on how many buttons you need"

Cost guides on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr organize intercom prices by system type and number of units, and AI answers repeat those ranges. They describe apartment buildings better than houses.

A Mill Basin house usually has one or two buttons. What moves the cost is the distance to the gate, whether a trench and conduit are needed, how many indoor stations the house needs, the condition of the gate and door, and where the equipment can safely go.

The fair comparison is line by line: stations, cable and conduit, door and gate hardware, power and labor. Our quotes are written that way, so they can sit beside anyone else's.

"A video intercom keeps intruders out"

An intercom lets you identify and talk to a visitor without opening the door, which is genuinely useful. It does not resist force. The door, frame, lock, gate and lighting do that, and cameras and access control serve different purposes again.

We describe the intercom for what it does and, when an owner is worried about forced entry, look at the door hardware at the same time rather than letting the intercom carry a promise it cannot keep.

"Running cable to a gate is just a shallow trench"

DIY guides often describe burying a cable to a gate as a weekend job: dig a shallow trench, lay the cable, fill it in. AI summaries tend to repeat that.

In Mill Basin, the yard between the house and the gate can hold irrigation lines, lighting circuits and other utilities, and the ground can be wet. Cable laid loose in a shallow trench is easily cut by the next landscaper and cannot be replaced without digging again.

We run gate cable in conduit, sealed where it enters the house, with buried utilities located first and the route drawn on a sketch the owner keeps. It costs a little more on the day and much less over the life of the system.

FAQ

Mill Basin Intercom Installation FAQ

Friends sometimes arrive by boat and tie up at our dock. How do they let the house know they are here?
+

The simplest answer is a second outdoor station at the dock entrance on the same system, labeled so the indoor stations show that the call is coming from the water side. It rings the same monitors and phones as the front gate, and if the dock has a gate with a lock, it can release that gate too. The station sits on a short post or sheltered wall near the dock rather than on the bulkhead, high enough to stay out of normal splash, and is sealed against salt air. Families who rarely have guests by water often skip the station and rely on a phone call instead.

Can the driveway gate station be mounted so a driver can reach it without getting out of the car?
+

Yes. A station for drivers is mounted on a post or pier at car-window height, set back from the gate far enough that a car can pull alongside it before reaching the gate's swing. Some owners add a second, standard-height station on the pedestrian gate for people on foot, both ringing the same indoor stations. We measure the driveway, check which side drivers pull up on, and make sure the post does not block the gate or the sidewalk before choosing the spot.

Our intercom rings by itself when nobody is at the gate. What causes that?
+

The usual causes are water inside the gate station's button, a damaged buried cable letting conductors touch when the ground is wet, a corroded splice in a box along the run, or a power supply that is failing. Phantom calls that happen after rain or in damp weather point strongly to moisture at the gate end. We test the station and the cable separately, find the fault, and either reseal and repair or replace the part, rather than swapping the whole system on a guess.

We rent out one floor of our two-family. If the tenant's indoor station breaks, whose repair is it?
+

In most rentals the owner is responsible for keeping building systems like the door intercom working, unless the lease says otherwise or the tenant caused the damage. Courts have treated a working intercom as part of a habitable apartment once a tenant relies on it. We cannot give legal advice, so check your lease and ask your attorney if there is a dispute, but in practice the quickest path is to have the station repaired and keep the dated invoice.

Does putting an electric strike in our front door frame make the door easier to force open?
+

It should not, if the strike is chosen and fitted properly. A good strike is rated for the door and anchored into solid framing, and the door's deadbolt stays exactly as strong as before because the strike releases only the latch. Problems come from cheap strikes in weak frames or from cutting away too much of the frame. We check the frame before choosing hardware, and if the frame itself is weak we say so, because no strike can make a soft frame strong.

Our house has a residential elevator. Can there be intercom stations at the elevator landings?
+

Yes. Indoor stations can be mounted beside the elevator door on each floor, which is often where family members pass most often in a multi-level house. We do not mount anything inside the elevator cab or tie into the elevator's own controls or emergency phone, which belong to the elevator company and its inspections. Stations at the landings are ordinary indoor stations on the same system, wired through the walls near the shaft rather than the shaft itself.

We own a townhouse in a gated community. Do we need the association's approval to put a station on the outside of our unit?
+

Usually, yes. In gated communities and condos like Mill Harbor, exterior walls, entrances and the look of the fronts are typically governed by the association's rules, and a station on the facade or a new hole through an outside wall generally needs written approval first. Inside your unit, you normally have more freedom. Send us the rules or the board's requirements, and we can give the board a drawing of the station, its location and how the hole will be sealed.

Our router is at the far end of the house from the gate. Will a Wi-Fi gate station stay connected?
+

Often it will not, at least not reliably. Signal has to pass through brick or stucco walls and then cross the yard, and a station that connects on a dry afternoon can drop out on a wet night or when the house network is busy. We prefer a cable in conduit from the house to the gate, which carries power and data together and does not depend on Wi-Fi at all. If cable truly cannot reach the gate, an outdoor access point or wireless bridge can help, but we test the signal at the gate first.

How do we keep a landscaper or pool contractor from cutting the buried cable to our gate later?
+

Run it in conduit rather than loose in the soil, bury it at a sensible depth, and record where it goes. We leave the owner a sketch showing the route from the house to the gate with measurements from fixed points like the foundation and the walk, and where practical we lay a marker tape above the conduit before backfilling. Show the sketch to any contractor before they dig. If the cable is cut anyway, conduit lets a new cable be pulled through without digging up the whole route.

Kids on our block press the gate button and run. Can anything stop that?
+

A video station helps most, because you can see who pressed it before you bother answering, and many systems let you check the picture from a phone without speaking. Some stations can show a short message or play a brief tone so the person pressing knows the call went through and is being seen. Quiet hours can stop the indoor stations from ringing overnight while calls still reach a phone. The station itself is built to take repeated pressing, so pranks are an annoyance rather than a repair problem.

Can a station be mounted on our stucco facade without cracking it or letting water in behind?
+

Yes, with care at the penetration. On a new house, the station's back box is best set before the finish coat. On a finished facade, we drill with the right bit at low speed, use anchors suited to the backing behind the stucco, mount the station on a gasket or backing plate, and seal the hole and the cable entry so water cannot travel behind the finish. We look at the wall's condition first, and if there are existing cracks near the planned spot, we suggest a different location.
