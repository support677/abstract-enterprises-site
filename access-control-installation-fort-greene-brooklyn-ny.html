The Neighborhood

Why Fort Greene Properties Need Access Control

Fort Greene is small on the map and very mixed on the ground. Wikipedia puts it at a little over half a square mile, bounded by Flushing Avenue and the Navy Yard to the north, the Flatbush Avenue Extension and Downtown Brooklyn to the west, Atlantic Avenue to the south and Vanderbilt Avenue and Clinton Hill to the east, inside Brooklyn Community District 2 and the 88th Precinct, whose station house is at 298 Classon Avenue. Its ZIP codes, 11201, 11205, 11217 and 11238, are shared with neighbors, which is one reason callers so often describe their address by the nearest avenue instead. For a door installer, the useful fact is that four very different kinds of front door sit within a ten-minute walk of each other.

The first, and the one people picture, is the rowhouse door. Most of the neighborhood’s brick and brownstone rows went up in the 1850s and 1860s, and building had largely ended by the 1870s. Portland, Oxford, Cumberland, Carlton and Adelphi Streets were named for London terraces. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fort Greene and BAM Historic Districts in 1978, and the area was added to the National Register in 1983. The front doors of these houses were built before anyone imagined an electric lock: often a pair of tall wooden leaves with glass panels at the top of the stoop, opening into a vestibule with a second, inner door. Fitting modern entry to that door is a carpentry and hardware problem first and an electronics problem second, and in the historic districts it is a Landmarks question as well.

Brownstone owners have been asking how to do it for a long time. In one Brownstoner forum thread from October 2007, an owner with a pair of glass-paneled front doors about three feet wide and ten feet tall wrote, “We were told that there is no system to accomodate a buzzer (electric door strike) for the type of doors we have.” Another owner who had done it during a renovation answered that it was possible, but warned, “You’ll need to open up the walls, probably quite a bit.” In a later thread a door rebuilder said that “about 50% of the double doors i do get an electric latch.” Both are right. A paired rowhouse door can take a reader and an electric release; the work is in deciding which leaf does the locking, how power reaches it, and how much of the house has to be opened to get cable there.

The second kind of door is the tower lobby, and it is the newest. Along Ashland Place, Flatbush Avenue Extension and the western end of DeKalb Avenue, Fort Greene meets Downtown Brooklyn in a row of high-rises. New York YIMBY describes Eighty Nine DeKalb, a 30-story tower with 324 apartments that began leasing in the fall of 2025, as offering “keyless entry with smart home app integration” and a “24/7 doorman-attended lobby.” CityRealty lists Forte, at 230 Ashland Place, as a 28-story condo of 108 apartments with a full-time doorman. Earlier still, the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at One Hanson Place, landmarked in 1977, was converted to condominiums between 2005 and 2007. Buildings like these do not lack security staff. What they need is a credential system that keeps up with hundreds of residents, controls the doors behind the lobby that the doorman cannot see, and does not leave the building stranded if the company behind its app changes course.

The third kind is the arts building. The Downtown Brooklyn Partnership’s list of the Brooklyn Cultural District places a dense run of institutions on Fort Greene’s western blocks: BAM’s Peter Jay Sharp Building at 30 Lafayette Avenue and its Fisher Building at 321 Ashland Place, the Harvey Theater at 651 Fulton Street, BRIC and UrbanGlass sharing 647 Fulton Street, the L10 Arts and Cultural Center at 10 Lafayette Avenue, the Mark Morris Dance Center at 3 Lafayette Avenue, Theatre for a New Audience at 262 Ashland Place, and rehearsal and studio spaces on South Oxford Street. We name them only to describe the neighborhood; none is a customer of ours. Around them are smaller galleries, studios, schools and nonprofits in converted buildings, many sharing one street door among several organizations, with public hours that change with each season’s program.

The fourth is the shop door. Fulton Street, DeKalb Avenue, Lafayette Avenue and Myrtle Avenue carry most of the neighborhood’s businesses; the city’s business improvement district directory counts 179 ground-floor retail businesses along the Myrtle Avenue district’s 40 block faces, and the Fulton Area Business Alliance covers 37 block faces of its own. Many of those shops sit under apartments and share a building entrance with them. Brooklyn Paper’s 88th Precinct reports show the kind of door trouble this produces: an armed robber who “followed the victim into the building” on Clinton Avenue near DeKalb Avenue, and a vestibule on Fulton Street where “someone managed to break open the latch on the door” and rode away on a resident’s $4,000 scooter. Neither report gives a year, both incidents were on the Clinton Hill side of the precinct, and we do not cite them as a crime trend. We cite them because they describe doors, and doors are what we fix.

There is also the city’s own evidence about what goes wrong with entrances. In a March 2025 report, the NYC Department of Investigation described in-person inspections at NYCHA senior buildings and found that 9 of 28 lobby doors, about 32 percent, were not secure; one of its recommendations was that inspectors confirm “that the self-closing hardware functions properly.” Those were public-housing buildings, not Fort Greene rowhouses, but the lesson carries: most failed entrances fail at the closer and the latch long before they fail at the reader. Every Fort Greene job we plan starts there.

Stoop Door Won’t Latch, Lobby Reader Offline or a Shop Door Stuck Open: Repair in Fort Greene

Not every call is a new system. If the inactive leaf of your stoop doors has started to drift so the lock no longer catches, a reader in a tower’s service corridor has stopped answering its controller, or a shop’s side door on DeKalb Avenue now unlocks and never relocks, ring (347) 934-8335 and describe what the door is doing, or send a short video of it opening and closing. A visit is booked within two hours of the call. On old paired doors the cause is very often mechanical: a top or bottom bolt that has worked loose, a hinge that has dropped, or a strike that the door has shrunk away from over a dry winter. On newer systems it is more often a power supply on a shared circuit, a backup battery at the end of its life, or a controller that lost its network after a router was replaced. We work on systems other companies installed as well as our own, and we tell you plainly when a part is no longer worth repairing.

Converted lofts, former commercial buildings and storefront rows on Lafayette Avenue, Fulton Street and Myrtle Avenue, shared by several organizations or businesses. Separate accounts per tenant, event schedules and a shared street door that knows who is open.

What the materials mean for the install

Brownstone is a sandstone, softer than brick and often patched with a cement-based coating over the years, so we do not drill the carved face of a door surround. Readers and stations go on plain stone or brick, fixed at mortar joints where there are any, and the fixings are kept small so that a later change leaves little to repair. Where the stoop doors sit in a deep reveal, the side of the reveal is often the cleanest place for a device: it is plain, it is close to the door, and it is less visible from the sidewalk than the facade itself.

The doors are the bigger question. A century and a half of locks has usually left the edge of the active leaf bored, filled and bored again, and the inactive leaf held by surface bolts that have loosened. Before any strike goes in, the leaf that will carry the lock needs solid wood where the hardware mounts, and the inactive leaf needs top and bottom bolts that hold it rigidly, because an electric lock on one leaf is only as strong as the bolts that keep the other leaf shut. Glass panels are removed or protected during cutting. In towers the issue is the opposite: aluminum storefront stiles are narrow and hollow, so the lock usually goes into the panic device or the frame header rather than the door edge.

Two Leaves, One Lock: Electrifying a Pair of Stoop Doors

A pair of doors has an active leaf, the one that opens every day, and an inactive leaf that stays shut until someone needs the full width for a sofa or a piano. On most Fort Greene stoops the inactive leaf is held by a bolt at the top and another at the bottom, and the active leaf closes against it, latching into a strike mounted on the edge of the inactive leaf. That arrangement is the reason some owners are told an electric lock “can’t be done”: there is no fixed frame on the latch side of the active leaf to hold a strike in the usual way. It can be done, but the strike, or the lock, has to live on a door.

There are three sound ways to do it. The first puts an electric strike into the edge of the inactive leaf, where the old strike plate was. Hardware makers sell strikes designed for exactly this case; Allegion’s knowledge base, for example, names the Von Duprin 6223 as “the most common closed back strike” for a pair of doors with a mortise lock, and the 6221 as the open-back alternative “on inactive leaf,” while noting that its 5100 series “can not be used on a pair of doors.” Power then has to reach a door that swings, which means a cable through the inactive leaf’s hinge side by an electric hinge or a power transfer, or, because that leaf is almost never opened, a protected loop. The second puts an electrified mortise lock into the active leaf, so the lock itself releases and the inactive leaf keeps an ordinary strike; this suits doors whose original lock is beyond saving. The third, for doors that cannot be cut at all, leaves the stoop pair on its mechanical lock and electrifies the inner vestibule door instead.

Which one we recommend depends on what we find. A Brownstoner commenter who rebuilds doors observed that the work is “a lot easier to do if the door is on the bench and the glass is out,” and that matches our experience: if your doors are coming off for restoration anyway, that is the cheapest moment to bore them. If they are staying on their hinges, we take the glass into account, protect it, and choose hardware that needs the least cutting. In every case the inactive leaf’s bolts are checked and replaced if they are worn, the active leaf is adjusted to close and latch on its own, and a door position contact is added so the system knows whether the pair is actually shut, not only whether the lock is set.

What stays the same

The key cylinder stays. Every pair we electrify keeps a working mechanical key on the outside, and the inside lever or knob always opens the active leaf by hand, with or without power. The full-width opening stays, too: the inactive leaf can still be unbolted and swung wide for a move, and putting it back simply means re-engaging the bolts until the contact shows it closed. And the look of the doors stays. Nothing is mounted on the face of the leaves; the reader or station goes beside the opening, and the cable runs inside the door edge, the hinge or the reveal.

What the Landmarks Rules Say About a Box Beside the Door

Much of residential Fort Greene lies inside the Fort Greene Historic District or the BAM Historic District, and work on the outside of a designated building needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s approval. The Commission’s rules, in section 2-20 of Title 63, set out the standards its staff use for what they call health, safety and utility equipment. An entry panel is not a keypad on a sales sheet to them; it is equipment on a historic facade, and the rules read like a mounting specification.

Four of those rules shape almost every Fort Greene job. Location: equipment is not to go “on or in front of a primary façade unless there is no feasible alternative,” and light fixtures and intercom panels “must be installed within the door opening or adjacent to the door opening,” with “a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel per door opening.” An intercom panel “may also be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway.” Attachment: equipment is to be installed “at mortar joints or through plain brick, stone or wood to the greatest extent possible.” Finish: it should “match the surrounding building material or will not call undue attention to its presence.” And conduit: “no visible conduit or, if this is not feasible, a maximum of approximately ten (10) inches,” never “placed on decorative surfaces.”

For an access system, the one-panel rule matters most. If the call button, camera and fob reader are separate boxes, a stoop door can easily exceed what the rules contemplate. A single station that combines all three in one housing, sized to the plain stone of the reveal or mounted on the iron fence at the areaway, fits the rule and usually looks better too. Cameras are treated with the same restraint: section 2-20 asks that they be “as small and as limited in number as feasible” and placed “to avoid a cluttered or haphazard appearance,” so a station’s built-in camera is generally a better answer than a separate camera over the door.

How we plan a historic district door

We photograph the facade, the door surround, the reveal and the fence, measure the plain surfaces, and mark where the device and any visible fixing or conduit would sit. The plan shows the device at actual size against the stone, names its finish, and states how much conduit, if any, would be visible. The owner, or the owner’s architect or expediter, files with the Commission; we supply the drawings, product sheets and photographs the application needs, and we do not start exterior work until the permit is in hand. Inside the house, where Landmarks rules do not reach, the controller, cable and power supply follow the most direct route the house allows.

App-Only Lobbies on the Downtown Edge

The towers that line Ashland Place and the Flatbush Avenue Extension were sold and leased on convenience: a phone that opens the lobby, a guest list kept in an app, a doorman to sign for parcels. Eighty Nine DeKalb’s leasing announcement lists “keyless entry with smart home app integration” and guest registration in its resident app alongside the 24-hour doorman. That is a good resident experience while every part of it keeps working. The questions boards and managers bring us are about what happens when part of it does not.

The first question is dependence. A lobby that opens only for a vendor’s app depends on that vendor’s servers, its app updates and its business. Latch, the New York company whose smart locks and resident platform went into city rental buildings such as the Hell’s Kitchen building described below, was delisted from Nasdaq in 2023 while it worked on restating its finances, and announced it would become Door.com; TechCrunch’s report said nothing about what that would mean for existing buildings. We draw no conclusion about any one company’s service. The general lesson is enough: when a building’s doors can be opened only through one provider’s cloud, the building has handed that provider its front door. We design so that the readers, the controllers and the credentials work locally, and the cloud is a convenience for managing them rather than the only path through the door.

The second question is choice. When a Hell’s Kitchen landlord replaced lobby keys with an app and cards in 2019, tenants sued; The Real Deal reported that they “just want their old metal keys back,” and quoted one of them asking, “What happens if your phone dies?” New York City has since written some of those concerns into law. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access must get consent, keep a written privacy and retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and may not require smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment. For a tower, that means every resident has a non-app way through the doors, every apartment door has a key, and the data the system keeps is small and short-lived by default.

The third question is what the doorman cannot see. A front desk watches one door. Behind it are the loading dock, the trash room, the bike room, the service corridor to the garage or the street, the mechanical rooms and the roof. Those are the doors propped for a delivery, used by vendors who come every week, and opened by staff who change. They are also the doors on which the building’s fire safety plan depends, so their locks must release correctly on alarm and in a power failure. In our experience these back-of-house doors are where tower access control earns its cost: each vendor gets a schedule, each staff member a named credential, and every door held open too long sends an alert to the desk.

Business Access Control in Fort Greene

Fort Greene’s businesses sit on a handful of streets, and each street has its own kind of door. Wikipedia names Fulton Street, Lafayette Avenue and DeKalb Avenue as the main arteries, and Myrtle Avenue runs along the northern side by the park and the Whitman and Ingersoll Houses.

Fulton Street

The Fulton Area Business Alliance’s corridor, with restaurants, cafés, salons and service shops in the ground floors of older buildings, many with apartments above. The usual job is separating the shop’s staff door and cellar from the residents’ street entrance.

DeKalb Avenue

Restaurants, bars and small shops on the stretch facing the park and running east, plus medical and professional offices near the hospital. Side and back doors that need staff schedules, delivery windows and alerts when they are opened after closing.

Myrtle Avenue

A business improvement district of 40 block faces and 179 ground-floor retail businesses by the city’s count. Narrow storefronts with roll-down gates and a door to the stock room or cellar that the whole staff once shared a key to.

Lafayette Avenue and the Cultural District

BAM, the Mark Morris Dance Center, the Center for Fiction, the L10 Arts and Cultural Center and smaller galleries and studios. Shared street doors, rehearsal and studio rooms, stage and loading doors, and hours that follow a program.

Ashland Place and Flatbush Avenue Extension

Ground-floor retail and amenity spaces in the towers, with their own street doors and service corridors shared with the residential side. Clear separation between the retail tenant’s system and the building’s.

Atlantic Avenue and Hanson Place

The southern edge toward Atlantic Terminal, with offices and shops in larger buildings and the converted bank tower at One Hanson Place. Suite doors on cards, lobby integration and records for each tenant kept apart.

Shared buildings with several organizations

The Cultural District list shows how common shared buildings are here: BRIC and UrbanGlass share 647 Fulton Street, and the L10 Arts and Cultural Center at 10 Lafayette Avenue houses 651 Arts, BAM’s KBH space and the MoCADA gallery. Smaller buildings on the side streets do the same on a smaller scale, with two or three nonprofits, a school and a studio sharing one entrance. The door problem in all of them is identical. Each organization has its own staff, volunteers, students and visitors, its own hours, and its own reasons for wanting a record of who came in, and none of them should be able to see or change another’s.

The answer is one building controller with separate partitions: the street door and shared corridors belong to the landlord or the building’s manager, each organization administers its own doors and its own people, and the street door opens for anyone with a valid credential from any of them, during the hours that organization sets. A directory panel at the street door lists each organization and calls its front desk or its on-duty phone, so a courier for the third floor never has to be buzzed in by the first.

Rules that apply to Fort Greene business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on an exit path has to release when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs release hardware built into the door that works with one hand. A shop, studio or office exit opens from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling must be self-closing and self-locking, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and remote release. A shop that shares a street door with apartments cannot leave it on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to the residential doors. A shop’s own staff door is not covered; a shared residential door is.
  • Landmarks. A storefront or entrance inside the Fort Greene or BAM Historic District falls under the Landmarks rules for exterior equipment, the same as a house.
  • Electrical licensing. The city’s electrical code defines low-voltage work as signaling, communication, alarm and data wiring under 50 volts, and its permit section “shall not apply to low voltage electrical work.” The 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician; AESS does the low-voltage side and works under New York State license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Fort Greene: Questions Answered

Houses in the Fort Greene and BAM Historic Districts

Our house has an iron fence and gate at the sidewalk. Could the call panel and reader go on the fence instead of on the brownstone beside the door?

Often, yes. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s rules say an intercom panel “may also be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway,” as an alternative to the door opening itself. For a house whose door surround is carved, or whose reveal is too shallow for a station, the fence post or the masonry pier beside the areaway gate can be the cleanest place. It keeps drilling off the brownstone altogether, and a visitor is met before they climb the stoop.

The catch is the cable. A panel on the fence needs a cable from the house, which usually means a short sleeve under the areaway paving or along the base of the fence into the cellar wall. We price that route before recommending the fence, and we show the Commission’s staff exactly where the device and its cable go. If the fence panel only calls the house and the stoop pair still needs to release, the lock on the door is wired separately inside.

Getting cable from our cellar to the stoop doors might mean running some of it outside. How much visible conduit do the Landmarks rules allow on a Fort Greene front?

Very little. Section 2-20 of the Commission’s rules asks for “no visible conduit or, if this is not feasible, a maximum of approximately ten (10) inches,” and says the conduit “will not be placed on decorative surfaces.” So a run up the face of the stoop or across the brownstone is out. Ten inches is roughly the distance from the top of a stoop wall to a station mounted in the reveal, which is why the short, final hop is sometimes visible and nothing else is.

In practice we almost always avoid exterior conduit entirely. Cable comes up from the cellar inside the wall beside the door, through the vestibule floor, or up inside the stoop where there is a void below the steps, and enters the reveal through a single small sealed hole behind the device. When there is genuinely no interior path, we say so on the drawing and measure the visible length before anything is filed.

Our house on Washington Park faces west across Fort Greene Park, and the low sun hits the doors late in the day. Will a door camera show faces, or only silhouettes?

It can show faces, if the camera is chosen for it. A visitor standing with bright sky and open park behind them is the classic backlit scene, and an ordinary camera exposes for the sky and turns the face into a shadow. Door stations with wide dynamic range handle this much better, and the angle matters as much as the sensor: a station mounted on the side of the reveal, looking slightly across the visitor rather than straight out toward the park, sees less sky.

We check the light on the site visit, at the time of day you mention, and test the preview on the spot. Heat matters too on a sun-facing door; we choose a station rated for outdoor temperatures and avoid dark housings that sit in direct afternoon sun for hours if a lighter finish is acceptable to Landmarks.

We are turning our three-apartment brownstone back into a single-family house. What happens to the door system the rental layout needed?

Most of it gets simpler, and some of it can be reused. A divided house usually has a station or buzzer panel with a button per apartment, handsets or screens on each floor, and sometimes separate locks on interior doors that used to be apartment entrances. As one household, you need one call button, one place to answer it, which is usually your phone plus one indoor screen, and personal codes or fobs for the family and anyone who helps you.

If the existing cable from the cellar to the stoop is sound, the new station can use it. The old apartment doors on the upper floors can lose their locks or keep them as room locks; if any of them was a fire-rated apartment entrance door, ask your architect before removing it, since the conversion itself has building code consequences. This is also a good moment to put the controller somewhere sensible in the cellar, since the renovation will open walls anyway.

Towers and condo buildings on the Downtown edge

The lobby of our tower has a doorman around the clock, but the loading door, trash room and service corridor behind it do not. How should those doors be handled?

Each one gets its own reader, its own list of people and its own schedule, and every one of them reports to the desk. The loading door opens for the porters and the building’s regular vendors during delivery hours, and for anyone else only when the desk releases it after seeing them on camera. The trash and recycling room opens for staff and, if residents use it, for residents’ fobs without opening anything beyond it. The service corridor to the street opens from inside freely, as an exit must, and from outside only for staff.

The most useful single addition is a door contact on each of these doors, with an alert when one is held open longer than a minute or two. Back-of-house doors are propped for a delivery more than they are forced, and a desk that knows a door is standing open can send a porter to close it before someone walks in.

Management would like residents to use the building app to open their own apartment doors as well. Can they make that the only way in?

Not under the city’s rules for rental and other multiple dwellings. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law says owners may not require smart access, which includes apps and fobs, for a tenant to enter their own dwelling unit. An app-connected lock can be offered as an option, and many residents like it, but a tenant who declines still needs a way into their apartment that does not depend on the app, which in practice means a working key.

In condominiums the unit belongs to the owner, and the board’s rules and the declaration govern apartment doors; we still recommend a mechanical key on every apartment lock. Where we install app-connected locks, we choose models that keep a key override and a keypad, so a dead phone or a lapsed subscription never leaves anyone in the corridor.

The company behind our building’s entry app has changed its name and its business since we moved in. What happens to our doors if a provider stops supporting them?

That depends on how the system was built. If the readers and locks talk to a controller in the building, which keeps working on its own, the doors keep opening for the credentials already enrolled even if the cloud goes away; what you lose is remote management until the controller is pointed at a new platform. If the readers are tied to one provider’s cloud and fobs, the building may have to replace them to change providers. We survey your readers, controllers and credentials, list which can be kept, and price a staged changeover.

For any building choosing a system now, we recommend readers that speak open standards, credentials the building can order from more than one source, a contract that gives the board or owner its own administrator account and its data on request, and a mechanical override at every controlled door. TechCrunch’s 2023 report on Latch’s rebrand to Door.com said nothing about existing buildings, which is exactly why those safeguards belong in the contract.

Our condo building has a lower-level entrance that opens toward the passage to the trains. Should that door follow different rules from the street door?

Usually yes. Wikipedia notes that the Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at One Hanson Place has a lower-level lobby connected to the Atlantic Terminal subway complex, and a few other buildings near the terminal have similar below-grade entrances. A door like that faces a far busier stream of strangers than a street door on a side block, and it is rarely watched by the desk. It should open only for residents’ credentials, never stand on an unlock schedule, and have its own camera and a held-open alert.

It also has to work as an exit if the building’s fire safety plan relies on it. That is a question for the building’s engineer and its fire safety documents before we choose the lock. We then make sure the door always opens from inside, releases on fire alarm where required, and does not become the easy way into the building when the street door is busy.

Couriers arrive at our tower’s service door all day. Can the person at the front desk let one in without leaving the lobby?

Yes, and that is one of the most useful things a system does in a staffed building. A video station at the service door calls the desk, the desk sees and speaks to the courier, and a release button or an on-screen tap opens that door for a few seconds, with the release logged against the desk’s login. If deliveries go to a package room, the courier can be given a code that opens only the service door and the package room for the hours deliveries are expected, and nothing beyond.

Each doorman should have a personal login rather than a shared desk account, so the record shows who released which door. A contact on the service door confirms that it closed again after the courier left.

Walk-ups and divided rowhouses

Someone broke the latch of a Fulton Street vestibule door and rode off with a resident’s scooter. Can a system tell a door that was forced from one that was simply left open?

Yes, if the door has both a lock the system controls and a contact that reports whether the door is shut. When the door opens without a valid credential, a request-to-exit signal or a release from inside, the system records a forced door and can send an alert at once. When it opens properly and then stays open past a set time, it records a door held open. Those are two different alerts, and they lead to two different responses: one is a call to the police or a check on camera, the other is a reminder to whoever propped it.

A forced-door alert on a vestibule door is most useful at night, when nobody expects it to open. Many owners have it sent to the super and to one or two residents who have agreed to receive it, while everyone else is spared ordinary held-open notices.

Arts, community and commercial buildings

Our performance space’s public hours change with every season’s program. Can door schedules follow an events calendar instead of fixed weekly times?

Yes. Most current access platforms let you add dated exceptions on top of a regular weekly schedule, and several can read events from a shared calendar, so a public door unlocks for the doors-open time of a performance and locks again after the audience has left, without anyone changing the weekly schedule. Staff, artist and crew credentials run on their own schedules, which can also be tied to a production’s dates, so a visiting company’s access starts at load-in and ends after strike.

Two cautions. A door that unlocks for the public must still lock if the event is cancelled, so someone needs the authority to cancel an exception, and the calendar link should be read-only so that a mistyped entry cannot open the building at 3 a.m. We set up both and train the people who run the calendar.

Our nonprofit rents one floor of a building that two other arts groups share. How does the shared street door know which organization each visitor is going to?

Through a directory, not a single bell. A station at the street door lists each organization, and each listing calls that organization’s front desk, its staff phones or whoever is on duty, so the visitor reaches the right people and the right people release the door. Each group manages its own entries in the directory and its own credentials for staff and members, and the landlord or building manager controls the street door’s overall hours.

Records are kept apart in the same way: each organization sees its own people’s entries and nobody else’s, and the building manager sees door events without names unless an incident requires otherwise. That keeps three organizations on one door without any of them holding the others’ data.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Fort Greene

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Fort Greene door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim to any one of those sources unless we quote it.

“An electric strike can’t go on double doors.”

Owners still hear this. The Brownstoner owner in 2007 was told exactly that about a pair of tall glass doors, and a later poster wrote, “We’ve been told it can’t be done, others say it can.”

It can be done. Hardware makers publish strikes for the inactive leaf of a pair, and electrified mortise locks release inside the active leaf instead. What makes it work is the inactive leaf’s bolts, which must hold that leaf rigid.

The real limits are the condition of the wood, the glass and the route for power, and we price those honestly after looking at the doors.

“A keyless app is the modern standard for every lobby.”

Many new buildings advertise app entry, and residents like it. But an app is one credential among several, not a system.

Tenants in a Hell’s Kitchen building sued in 2019 to get their metal keys back, and New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law now bars owners of multiple dwellings from requiring smart access to enter an apartment. Every resident needs a way in that does not depend on a phone.

We set up apps, fobs, codes and keys side by side, and the doors keep working on site if the cloud is down.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

That is usually the price of a lock off the shelf. Industry guides put the installed figure much higher: Swiftlane’s 2026 guide quotes “$1,500 to $5,000 per door installed” for standard doors and $5,000 to $6,000 or more for busy perimeter entrances.

On our Brooklyn list one controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the cable and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500, and a video entry panel with fob access replacing a buzzer is $1,500 to $3,000.

A pair of stoop doors can need new bolts and more careful cutting than a single door, which moves a job up within its range, not off the list.

“Any device can go on any building.”

Generic answers rarely mention landmark rules. Inside the Fort Greene and BAM Historic Districts, exterior equipment needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s approval.

The Commission’s rules allow one intercom panel per door opening, ask for no visible conduit or about ten inches at most, and want equipment fixed at mortar joints or plain surfaces in a finish that does not call attention to itself.

That favors one combined station over a bell, a camera and a reader, and it rewards planning the device before buying it. In our experience, buying a panel first and fitting it to the door afterwards is how a historic district front ends up with a second round of drilling.

“A doorman makes access control unnecessary.”

A doorman watches one door. Towers on Ashland Place and DeKalb Avenue have loading docks, trash rooms, service corridors, bike rooms and roof doors that the desk cannot see.

Those doors are propped for deliveries and used by changing vendors and staff, which makes them the likeliest way in for someone who should not be there.

Readers, contacts and held-open alerts on back-of-house doors turn the doorman into the person who is told, instead of the person who has to notice. Named credentials for porters and vendors also mean that when a weekly vendor changes companies, one list is edited instead of a lock being changed.

“Once the reader is installed, the door is secure.”

A reader only controls a door that closes and latches. The Department of Investigation found that 9 of 28 lobby doors it checked at NYCHA senior buildings were not secure, and recommended checking that self-closing hardware works.

On Fort Greene stoops the usual weak points are a sagging active leaf, a loose bolt on the inactive leaf and a closer that has given up. Old wood doors also swell in humid summers and shrink over dry winters, so a latch that caught in August can miss its strike by February.

We fix those first, and we add a door contact so the system knows whether the door is actually shut.

“You need a city permit for any door wiring.”

Some answers treat every wire as electrical work. The city’s electrical code defines low-voltage work as signaling, alarm, communication and data wiring under 50 volts, and its permit section does not apply to it.

That does not mean no rules apply. The 120-volt outlet for a power supply is licensed electrical work, exit doors must meet the Building Code, and Landmarks approval is a separate matter entirely.

We tell each owner which of those apply to their doors before the job is priced.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Tug the Second Leaf First

This describes how we approach a paired rowhouse door, not a particular customer. Before we look at readers, we close the active leaf, hold it, and pull hard on the inactive one. On old pairs it often moves: the top bolt no longer reaches its keeper, or the bottom bolt sits in a worn hole in the saddle. If it moves, the pair can be opened with a shoulder no matter how good the lock on the other leaf is, so the bolts come first.

Then we go down to the cellar, because it decides the price. We look for the line of the stoop wall, a path up to the vestibule, a dry wall for the controller and an outlet nearby. Only then do we stand on the sidewalk and look at the stone beside the door, to choose a device that fits it and the Landmarks rules. A beautiful station on a pair that does not hold protects nothing.

Is a large touchscreen panel too much for a one-family rowhouse?
+

Usually, yes. A touchscreen directory earns its size in a building with many apartments or tenants, where visitors need to search for a name. A one-family house needs one call button, a camera and a reader, which fit in a station a few inches wide that suits a narrow reveal and the one-panel limit in the Landmarks rules. A divided house with two to four households usually needs a small station with one button per household.

Our building is still under contract with its current entry platform. Can you take over before that contract ends?
+

We can plan before it ends. We survey the current readers, controllers and credentials, list what can stay, and price a changeover timed to the contract’s end date, so the building is not paying two providers at once. Read the contract for notice periods, who owns the hardware and how the building gets its user list and data back. If the hardware belongs to the provider, the plan includes replacing it on the day service stops.

Does a door with a reader need any signs on it?
+

Some do. Where an exit door is electrically locked and released by a sensor on the inside, the NYC Building Code also calls for a manual release button marked PUSH TO EXIT near the door, which unlocks it for at least 30 seconds. A house door whose inside lever always opens it mechanically does not need one. We mark which of your doors need signs or buttons on the plan.

Will a reader on a stoop pick up a fob through a thick winter glove or from inside a bag?
+

Through a glove, generally yes; through a bag, less reliably. Fob readers read at a short range, a couple of inches at most, so the fob has to be brought close to the reader face. Most residents keep it on the key ring or in a coat pocket and tap it, gloves on. Phone keys that use Bluetooth can work from a pocket at a short distance, which many people prefer when they arrive carrying shopping.
