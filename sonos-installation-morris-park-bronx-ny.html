The Neighborhood

Why Morris Park Properties Need Sonos Installation

Morris Park is a neighborhood of houses, and the city has written that into law. The Department of City Planning describes most of it as single- and two-family detached homes and semi-detached housing, with small and medium apartment buildings kept to Morris Park Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. Building began around the turn of the twentieth century, picked up after a streetcar line opened on Morris Park Avenue in 1910, and carried on well past the Second World War. In 2005 the City Council adopted a rezoning that moved 11 full blocks and parts of 19 more into an R4A district, where only detached one- and two-family houses are allowed, and put another block and parts of five into R4-1, which permits semi-detached pairs too. Homes.com sums up today's stock as colonials, Tudor Revivals and attached rowhouses, many of them described in listings as solid brick or semi-attached brick, with the classic Tudors on larger lots up in the Indian Village section.

For a wireless music system, that is a specific kind of building. Sonos publishes a table showing how construction materials affect its wireless signal: wood, glass and synthetic materials are a low obstacle, brick and marble are a medium one, plaster and concrete are high, and metal is very high. A Morris Park house with a brick shell, original plaster partitions inside and a concrete cellar puts at least one of those materials between the router and almost every room. The speaker in the front room by the router is fine. The one in the back bedroom, the finished cellar or the converted attic is the one that plays happily on a quiet afternoon and then drops out on a Sunday evening when the whole family is home and every phone is streaming.

The part of the job that sets Morris Park apart is the internet itself. BestNeighborhood.org's coverage figures show Optimum's fiber and cable service available to about 92 percent of the neighborhood and Verizon's fiber to about 85 percent, but it also shows T-Mobile Home Internet, a 5G fixed-wireless service, available to about 92 percent, with Verizon's own fixed-wireless service and Starry further behind. Plenty of households here have switched to one of those boxes because it was cheaper or simpler. Sonos's system requirements ask for a "high-speed wired internet connection such as cable, fiber, or DSL" and list "wireless internet connections such as satellite, mobile hotspots, or LTE routers" among the setups it does not support. A Sonos Community thread from October 2022 describes exactly what that can look like: four Sonos products that kept playing while the app repeatedly lost control of them on a T-Mobile gateway. Another member in the same thread reported no trouble at all running Sonos behind a separate mesh router on a 5G connection. The difference between those two households is usually the router that sits between the gateway and the speakers, and that is the first thing we check.

The second is who lives under the roof. Wikipedia's figures for the neighborhood tabulation area put the population at 25,077 in 2020, with 65.8 percent of homes renter-occupied, and Homes.com gives a similar two-thirds split. In a neighborhood of houses, most of those renters are not in large buildings; they are upstairs or downstairs in a two- or three-family house, and current listings describe separate-entrance lower-level apartments, full finished basements and walkout basements. Each of those households tends to have its own internet account and its own router. A Sonos installer who treats the house as one home ends up with speakers joined to the wrong network, an app that shows the other family's rooms, or a cable that runs through somebody else's living room without anyone asking.

Then there is the avenue. The Morris Park Avenue Business Improvement District covers 188 properties along about a mile of Morris Park Avenue from Unionport Road and Amethyst Street to Williamsbridge Road, according to CityLand's account of its 2017 approval, and the BID's Avenue NYC district study counted 235 storefronts on the avenue. It is a street of family restaurants, salons, bakeries and offices, many with apartments directly above. Patricia's of Morris Park, at 1082 Morris Park Avenue, lists private dining for 20 to 85 guests on OpenTable, plus a smaller room in its wine cellar. Music for rooms like those has to suit dinner service, a christening party and the family living upstairs, all at the same time.

So the brief for a Sonos installation here is plain. Find out which internet the household uses and put a router Sonos supports at the center of it. Decide which product gets a cable, using the walls the house actually has rather than the ones on the box. Give each household its own system, with routes everyone has agreed to. Then do the ordinary craft properly: soundbars level and connected to the right port, ceiling speakers cut in only after the joists and pipes are found, the Sub kept off a wall shared with next door, outdoor speakers aimed at your own yard, and sensible limits set before we leave.

Internet First

Fiber, Cable or a 5G Box: The Internet Decides the Morris Park Sonos Plan

Every Sonos product talks to the router, and to the app on your phone, over the home network. Sonos's system requirements spell out what that network has to be: a high-speed wired internet connection such as cable, fiber or DSL; a router offering 2.4 GHz, because every Sonos product supports that band; the app and every product on the same subnet; and personal security such as WPA2 rather than the enterprise kind offices use. The same page lists what Sonos does not support: satellite, mobile hotspots and LTE routers; guest networks and portal log-ins; wireless range extenders; routers that broadcast only 5 GHz; Ethernet over power; and VPNs that block local traffic. In most of the city the internet part of that list is a formality. In Morris Park it is a real decision, because so many households have more than one kind of service on offer.

When the internet arrives through a 5G gateway

A 5G home-internet box is a cellular modem and a Wi-Fi router in one case. It has to sit wherever it gets the best signal from the nearest tower, which in a brick house is often a front window or an upper floor rather than the middle of the home. That puts the router function in a poor place for the rest of the house, and Sonos has, in its own words, no support for "LTE routers." The October 2022 Sonos Community thread about T-Mobile Home Internet shows how it fails in practice: the speakers keep playing, but the app loses them every few minutes, so nobody can pause, change the track or turn the volume down. The same thread includes a member running Sonos without trouble on a 5G connection through a separate mesh router.

That is the arrangement we build. The gateway stays where its signal is strongest. A cable runs from it to a router or mesh system that Sonos supports, placed where the house actually needs it, and every Sonos product and every phone that controls Sonos joins that router's network and nothing else. Where the gateway lets its own Wi-Fi be turned off, we turn it off so nobody's phone wanders onto it. Where it does not, we give the two networks clearly different names and make sure the Sonos system and the family's phones all live on the second one. Sonos needs the phone and the speakers on the same subnet, so the rule in a 5G house is simple: one network for music, and everyone who wants to control the music on it.

We are careful not to promise more than that. Sonos does not list 5G gateways as supported, and a cellular connection can slow or drop in ways a fiber line will not. A separate router fixes the local problems, which are the ones behind most "app can't find my speakers" calls. It cannot make the cellular link itself behave like a wire, and if music streaming stutters at busy times of day, the answer may be a different internet plan rather than different speakers. We will tell you which one it looks like before you spend money on either.

When the internet is fiber or cable

Optimum and Verizon both bring their lines in at an outside wall, usually at the basement or ground floor of a Morris Park house, and the technician tends to set the router down right beside that entry point. That is the right place for the provider's terminal and often the wrong place for the router. A router on a basement shelf in a corner of the house has to push its signal up through a floor and across brick or plaster partitions to reach the rooms people actually use. Where the provider's equipment allows, we run a cable from the entry point to a more central spot and put the router there, or we leave the router where it is and give one Sonos product or one access point upstairs a wired connection back to it.

Fiber and cable also make the wired Sonos setup straightforward. Sonos's wired option needs one product connected to the router by Ethernet at all times, a router that supports Spanning Tree Protocol, and, in a mesh system, that wired product connected to the primary node. We confirm all three before we choose which product carries the cable, because a soundbar wired to a secondary mesh point looks connected and still behaves badly.

What the provider's box does not tell you

The box on the wall says nothing about how many products in your house are old enough to care. According to Sonos's list of supported Wi-Fi modes, products still on the older S1 app are limited to 2.4 GHz and cannot use WPA3, while newer products such as the Arc Ultra, Era 100, Era 300, Sub 4 and Move 2 can use 5 GHz up to Wi-Fi 6. A household that bought a speaker in 2016, a soundbar in 2020 and a portable last summer can work perfectly on one router and fall apart on the next. Before any install we list every product, check it against the router that will carry it, and write the result into the handover note, so the next time the provider sends new equipment you know what to ask for.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Morris Park's Architecture

City Planning's 2005 rezoning study and today's listings describe the same neighborhood: detached and semi-detached one- and two-family houses on the side streets, three- and four-family houses mixed in, Tudors on bigger lots to the north, and apartment buildings on the two main avenues. Each of these handles signal, cable and sound differently.

Semi-Detached Brick Pairs

Two homes sharing one masonry wall, often with a driveway down the open side. The shared wall blocks some of the neighbors' Wi-Fi and carries bass in both directions, so the Sub never goes against it, and the router stays toward the middle of the house rather than on the open side where signal leaks out.

Detached Brick One- and Two-Families

Brick houses with a basement, a front room facing the street and bedrooms upstairs. A wired soundbar, a basement Amp feeding a kitchen ceiling pair, and one access point near the stairs for the upper floor cover most of them.

Indian Village Tudors

The larger lots in the northern part of the neighborhood, with Tudor detailing, steep roofs and more rooms. Longer cable runs, a finished attic or two, and yards big enough for real outdoor speakers set these jobs apart.

Colonials

Center-hall and side-hall houses with a staircase in the middle. The stair landing is often the best spot for an access point, and the rooms either side of the hall make natural Sonos zones.

Three- and Four-Family Houses

The multi-unit houses that make up much of the current listings. Three or four routers stacked within a few feet of each other, three or four households, and usually one basement where every cable begins.

Houses with a Separate Lower Apartment

Listings describe separate-entrance lower-level apartments and walkout basements. The basement may belong to a tenant, so the owner's cable route upstairs is planned with that household before the visit.

Attached Rowhouses

Narrower homes in a continuous row, with neighbors on both sides. Both side walls are shared, so bass placement, router channels and rear-yard speaker aiming all matter twice as much.

Mid-Century Brick Houses

Houses built after the war, like the 1955 house a Homes.com listing describes on Bogart Avenue. Brick outside, but more drywall and fewer plaster partitions inside, which changes both the Wi-Fi and how cable can be fished.

Avenue Apartment Buildings

The small and medium buildings City Planning places on Morris Park Avenue and Williamsbridge Road. Renters get no-drill layouts; owners in co-ops work through the building's alteration rules before anything is cut.

Inside the walls: what we check before cutting or fixing

Ceilings. Sonos's in-ceiling manual asks the installer to carry out an obstruction survey with a stud finder before cutting, looking for joists, conduit, pipes, heating, ducts, pocket doors and air returns, and to drill a small pilot hole and probe with wire wherever there is doubt. The 6-inch speaker needs an opening 8.25 inches across and about 4.73 inches of depth; the 8-inch needs 10.125 inches and about 5.32 inches. Its clamps grip a ceiling up to 1-1/4 inches thick with the caps on, or 1-7/8 inches with them off. Older Morris Park kitchens that were updated by laying new board over the original plaster can come close to those limits, so we measure through the pilot hole rather than guess. The manual also tells installers to consult local building codes before running speaker wire through ceilings, which we do as a matter of routine.

Walls. Sonos describes the Era 300 wall mount as stud-mounting only and not for drywall, with about 2.5 inches of clearance and a joint that swivels 15 degrees. In a brick house the walls that have studs are not always where a speaker is wanted, and a brick wall needs the right anchors and a careful drill. We decide early whether an Era 300 goes on a framed wall, on a stand or on a shelf, and we never improvise a fixing the manufacturer does not describe.

Houses that were gutted and rebuilt. Plenty of Morris Park houses kept their brick shell and were redone inside in drywall during a renovation. That helps Wi-Fi, because drywall is a much smaller obstacle than plaster, but it also means the old phone lines and mystery jacks may have been cut, rerouted or abandoned behind the new walls. Before we rely on any existing cable, we trace it with a tone generator and test it, and before we cut new board we find out what the renovation left inside.

Shared Houses

Upstairs, Downstairs and the Side Entrance: Sonos in Morris Park's Multi-Household Homes

Sonos was designed around one household: one network, one account, one set of rooms that can be grouped together. A great many Morris Park houses hold two, three or four households, and current listings describe separate-entrance lower-level apartments as a selling point. The basement in those houses may be someone else's home, and the internet line for the whole building often comes in through it.

Start with who owns which network

Our default is a separate Sonos system for each apartment, on that apartment's own network, registered to that household's own Sonos account. A Sonos product sees only the other products and phones on its own network, so separate networks keep the downstairs music in the downstairs app. If a family wants one system across two floors, for instance parents on the ground floor and grown children upstairs, that can be done on one shared network, but it is a choice to make on purpose and write down. It should never happen by accident because one router reached both floors.

The lower apartment and its door

When the lower-level unit has its own entrance and its own tenant, the basement equipment area may sit inside their home or behind their door. If the owner upstairs wants a wired soundbar or an access point, the cable has to leave that space and climb past the tenant's ceiling. We plan the route with both households before the day of the visit: a common stair, a closet stack that does not open into the lower unit, a chase alongside an existing pipe, or the outside of the rear wall in an exterior-rated cover entering through a sealed hole. Nobody's apartment is drilled on the strength of the other household's permission, and the cable is labelled at the basement end with the name of the unit it serves.

Routers stacked in one brick box

In a three- or four-family house the routers can sit within a few feet of one another vertically. Brick floors and plaster ceilings separate them a little, but routers on the same channel still compete for the same air, and Sonos's interference guidance names other routers and access points among the devices that cause trouble. It also recommends keeping its products at least 18 inches from other electronics, and lists cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras and microwaves in use among the culprits. With each household's agreement we look at which channel each router is using and spread them apart, and we make sure no household is using an extender that reaches across the floors.

Owner, tenant and the equipment in between

Many owners live in one unit and rent the others. Equipment an owner installs in a tenant's apartment should be clearly the owner's, listed in the handover note, and joined to a network the owner controls only if the tenant agrees. More often we recommend the tenant's Sonos stays the tenant's: bought by them, on their internet, registered to their account. That keeps the music, the account and the responsibility in the same hands, and it keeps the owner out of the tenant's listening habits.

Bass and the floor you share

A Sub on a wood floor sends vibration into the joists another household lives above or below. In a multi-family house we keep the Sub off shared walls and out of corners, set it on a rug or isolation feet, adjust Sub level after Trueplay instead of leaving it at the factory default, and switch on Night Sound for late viewing. The family upstairs will not notice whether the soundbar is an Arc Ultra or a Ray. They will notice the bass.

Yards and Larger Lots

Yards, Driveways and Porches: Outdoor Sonos on Morris Park's Lots

Homes.com's overview of the neighborhood mentions small yards, driveways and garages, and its listings range from private driveways to shared ones and private garages. Up in Indian Village the lots are larger. That makes outdoor music one of the most common add-ons we are asked about, and one of the easiest to get wrong on a street of close-set houses.

Sonos's guide to its outdoor speakers is frank about installation: it says "most people prefer their surface-mount outdoor speakers to be professionally installed," notes that the included brackets "can be installed on wood, brick, or even stone," and rates the speakers IP66, which it calls highly weather-resistant. They are passive, so they need an Amp, and Sonos says one Amp "can reliably power up to three pairs" of them. The Amp itself is an indoor product, so the usual layout in a Morris Park house puts the Amp on the basement shelf or in a back room and runs speaker cable out through the rear wall to a pair mounted under an eave, a porch roof or the overhang of a rear deck.

Aim at your own yard

Sonos's guide recommends pointing outdoor speakers at the listening area and pivoting them downward when they are mounted high. On a Morris Park lot that advice does double duty. A pair mounted under the rear eave and angled down at the patio table sounds fuller at lower volume, and it throws far less sound over the fence than a pair aimed level across the yard. On a semi-detached house we keep the speakers on your half of the rear wall and turn them slightly inward, away from the shared line.

The driveway side and the garage

The driveway side of a semi-detached or detached house is often the only exterior wall with room for a speaker, and it is also the wall closest to the neighbor's windows. We look at it, but we rarely choose it for the main pair. A detached garage at the end of the driveway is a separate question: if it needs music or a network point, the cable has to cross open ground, and that is quoted after a walkthrough because the surface, the distance and any shared driveway all affect the work.

Portable or wired

For a household that sits outside a few evenings a month, a portable Move 2 or Roam 2 carried out from the kitchen may be the honest answer. For one that eats outside most summer nights, wired outdoor speakers on an Amp are the better buy, because they are always in place, always on the network and grouped with the kitchen at a tap. We will say which one your yard and your habits point to.

Commercial Sonos

Business Sonos Installation in Morris Park

When the City Planning Commission approved the Morris Park Avenue BID in 2017, CityLand reported that the district covered 188 properties along 1.06 miles of a low-density, pedestrian street whose commercial overlays allow restaurants, boutiques, specialized retail, health, legal and financial offices, and beauty, hair and nail salons. The BID's Avenue NYC study surveyed 125 merchants on the avenue in the winter of 2020 and found that 85 percent rented their space, and it described the businesses as family-owned and often multi-generational. Rules that apply to business music in Morris Park

  • Measured where it lands.   • No electrical permit for speaker cable. Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation in Morris Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come out of the houses, internet services and businesses described above. Each is answered in full here; shorter practical questions are in the FAQ further down the page.

Which internet you have

The 5G internet box has to sit in our front window to get a signal. Where does that leave the router and the wired Sonos product?

Leave the gateway in the window, where it reaches the tower, and stop asking it to be the router for the whole house. We run a network cable from one of the gateway's Ethernet ports to a router or mesh system Sonos supports, placed somewhere central, often the stair landing or a closet near the middle of the house, or in the basement with an access point upstairs. The wired Sonos product, usually the soundbar or an Amp, then connects by cable to that router. Every Sonos product and every phone that controls them join the new router's network. If the gateway's own Wi-Fi can be switched off, we switch it off; if it cannot, we name the two networks so nobody confuses them. Sonos lists LTE routers as unsupported, so the music system should never depend on the gateway's Wi-Fi directly.

A Fios technician is coming next week to install fiber. What should we ask for while they are here so the Sonos plan works afterward?

Three things. First, ask where the line will enter and where the router will sit, and push for the most central spot the technician will allow rather than the nearest corner of the basement. Second, ask that the router keep its 2.4 GHz band switched on and use personal WPA2 or WPA3 security, because every Sonos product needs 2.4 GHz and Sonos does not support enterprise security. Third, if you want the old network name and password kept, ask for that too; in our experience Sonos products usually rejoin on their own when the new router uses exactly the same name and password. Say no to any plug-in extender the technician offers for weak rooms, since Sonos does not support them. Afterward we can run the cable from the new router to the soundbar or an access point.

We kept the provider's router and plugged our own router into it. Now the Sonos app finds the speakers only some of the time. Is the second router the reason?

Very often, yes. Two routers in a row create two separate networks. Sonos needs the app and every Sonos product on the same subnet, so if your phone wanders onto the provider's Wi-Fi while the speakers sit on yours, the app cannot see them, and it looks like the speakers dropped out. The fix is to choose one network for Sonos and everyone who controls it. That usually means turning off the provider's Wi-Fi, or switching your own router into access-point mode so the house has one network, or at least renaming the networks so phones stop drifting. We check where every Sonos product actually connected and move any stragglers onto the right network.

Inside Morris Park houses

In a center-hall colonial, does the router or access point belong on the stair landing?

Often it does. The stair hall in a colonial is the one space that is open to every floor and sits between the front and back rooms, so a router or access point on the landing reaches the bedrooms above and the rooms either side of the hall below through fewer walls. The provider's line still comes in at the basement, so we run a network cable up from there, usually through a closet that stacks above another closet or along the side of the stair, and fix the access point high on the landing wall or on a shelf. The rooms on each side of the hall then make natural Sonos zones: living room on one side, dining room and kitchen on the other.

Our brick house was gutted and redone in drywall inside. Does it still behave like a brick house for Wi-Fi?

Partly. Sonos rates plaster as a high obstacle and wood and synthetic materials as low ones, and drywall on studs behaves much more like the low end, so a renovated interior usually lets the signal travel between rooms much more easily than the original plaster did. The brick shell still matters: it weakens the signal on the way to the yard, the garage and the detached house next door, and the basement walls still block the floor below. Renovations also add things that do get in the way, such as metal ductwork in new soffits and appliances grouped in a new kitchen. And the old cable inside the walls may have been cut, rerouted or abandoned. We test before trusting any existing jack, and we usually find that fewer products need a cable than in an unrenovated house.

The lower-level apartment in our house is empty between tenants. Is that the time to run cable through it?

It is often the best time you will get. With nobody living there, we can reach the basement ceiling, the closets and the utility area without disturbing anyone, and run the owner's cable up to the floors above along the route that makes the most sense. We label every cable at the basement end with the unit it serves, keep runs tidy along joists rather than across the middle of a ceiling, and write the route into the handover note so it can go into the next lease. If you expect the next tenant to want their own internet, we can leave a spare labelled cable to their living room at the same visit, so their provider has a clean place to start.

Businesses on the avenues

Will streaming music in our shop slow down the card reader on the same internet?

Rarely because of the internet plan itself. A single music stream uses a small share of a typical fiber or cable connection. Where card readers stall, the cause is more often a crowded Wi-Fi network in a small shop, with staff phones, a tablet, cameras and customers' devices all competing. We put the card reader on a cable or on its own network where the payment provider allows, keep Sonos on a separate network from customer Wi-Fi, and give the wired Sonos product its own cable so the speakers stop competing for the air. On a 5G gateway the connection itself is more variable, so we look at the plan as well before blaming anything else.

Our private party room shares a wall with the main dining room. How do we keep a party's music from spilling into dinner service?

Mostly through placement and limits. The party room's speakers go on the walls or ceiling furthest from the shared wall and are angled inward, toward the tables, never toward the partition. Several small speakers at a modest level fill the room better than one pair turned up, and they put less energy into the shared wall. Any Sub stays off that wall and off the floor's joists. Then the party room gets a saved volume limit that hosts can work within, separate from the dining room's. We test with music playing in both rooms at once and listen from the dining room side before setting the final number.

Each barber in our shop wants their own music at their chair. Can Sonos do that?

Not in a single open room. Sonos plays one stream per zone, and zones are rooms with their own speakers; chairs a few feet apart in the same space would simply hear each other's music. What works in a barbershop or salon is one shop zone with a playlist the team agrees on, controlled from a shop tablet so nobody's personal phone runs the system, and a second zone only if the shop has a genuinely separate space such as a back room or a front waiting area. Barbers who want their own music at the chair are better served by their own headphones between clients.

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Morris Park

The seven statements below turn up again and again in cost guides, product marketing, marketplace listings and templated installer pages, which is exactly the material answer engines summarize. Each one is checked against a named source and against the houses, internet services and storefronts of Morris Park.

Claim one: "Sonos works on any internet connection"

It is an easy assumption. Sonos products connect to Wi-Fi, a 5G home-internet gateway puts out Wi-Fi, and so the two should simply work together. Most product listings say nothing about where the internet comes from.

Sonos's own requirements say otherwise. They ask for a wired internet connection such as cable, fiber or DSL, and they name "wireless internet connections such as satellite, mobile hotspots, or LTE routers" as unsupported. In a neighborhood where BestNeighborhood.org shows T-Mobile Home Internet available to about 92 percent of addresses, that is not a footnote.

In practice many 5G households run Sonos well, and a Sonos Community member reported exactly that with a separate mesh router behind the gateway. The working version always has a router Sonos supports between the gateway and the speakers. The failing version usually has the speakers on the gateway's own Wi-Fi.

Claim two: "Sonos sets itself up, so an installer is never needed"

For a single speaker on a kitchen counter, this is close to true, and we tell customers so when they ask. The setup screens are good, and a Beam or an Era 100 next to the router usually needs nobody's help.

Sonos's own guides draw the line elsewhere. Its outdoor speaker guide says "most people prefer their surface-mount outdoor speakers to be professionally installed," and its ceiling speaker guide says architectural speakers often require a professional installer, because they involve an Amp, holes in walls or ceilings and cable runs.

There is also a case the marketing leaves out: products that set up perfectly and then drop out because of brick walls, a second router or a 5G gateway. Self-setup describes the first ten minutes, not the network the speakers have to live on for years afterward.

Claim three: "Add an extender, or the provider's extra Wi-Fi box, and the dead spot is gone"

It is the most common advice for a weak back bedroom, and internet providers often hand out add-on boxes for exactly that purpose. For a laptop, it may well help.

Sonos lists networks using wireless range extenders as unsupported, along with Ethernet over power adapters. In a brick house a speaker that ends up leaning on an extender is usually the first one to vanish from the app when the house gets busy.

The fixes Sonos supports are a cable from the router to one Sonos product, a cable to a proper access point on the same network, or a mesh system with the wired Sonos product connected to the primary node. Whether a particular provider box counts as an extender or a wired mesh point depends on how it is connected, which is why we check it rather than assume.

Claim four: "Professional installation runs $500 to $1,000 a room"

National cost guides publish ranges like that. Angi's article on whole-home stereo systems gives $500 to $1,000 per room for additional zones, $50 to $100 an hour for labor on two- to three-hour jobs, and $100 to $500 for wall or ceiling modifications, without naming Sonos at all.

National averages say nothing about a Morris Park house, and Angi's own Bronx page for home audio installation shows no prices until you enter a ZIP code. A Sonos job here is often a soundbar mount and a system setup, which on our Bronx list is $129 plus $125, or a cable run of up to 25 feet for $99.

Larger jobs, with ceiling pairs, Amps and outdoor speakers, do cost more, and we quote them after a walkthrough. The point is that the number comes from the house and a published list, not from a national per-room average.

Claim five: "Running speaker wire needs an electrician and a permit"

It sounds sensible, because speaker cable goes into walls and ceilings just as electrical cable does, and the two look similar to anyone who is not handling them.

New York City's Local Law 128 of 2024 treats them differently. It defines low-voltage work as wiring designed to run at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, and Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 says an electrical permit is not required for installing it when a licensed electrician or qualified person does the work. Wiring that ties into life safety systems or switches lighting and power is a different matter.

So speaker and network cable for Sonos is low-voltage work, which AESS does under NYS license #12000287431. A new outlet behind the TV or a new circuit for an equipment shelf is electrical work, and we say so in the quote before anything is booked.

Claim six: "It is a Bronx neighborhood, so it is all apartments and a soundbar on a stand covers everyone"

Generic Bronx advice tends to picture large apartment buildings, where renters cannot drill and a plug-in setup is the only sensible option.

Morris Park is mostly houses. Then we walk the rooms with a phone running a simple speed check and note the walls in between: brick, original plaster, renovated drywall, a duct run, a kitchen full of appliances. In a two- or three-family house we ask who lives on each floor, who has the basement, and whether anyone has ever shared Wi-Fi with another unit. Only then do we choose which product gets the cable, where the Sub goes and what the network note will say.

Morris Park Sonos Installation FAQ

How much of our internet plan does Sonos use while it plays?
+

Not much. A music stream to a Sonos speaker uses a small share of a typical fiber or cable connection, and grouped rooms playing the same music share one stream rather than each pulling their own. TV sound sent from the television to a Sonos soundbar does not use the internet at all once the TV has it. Where a household notices Sonos competing with other devices, the problem is almost always Wi-Fi congestion inside the house, which a cable to the busiest Sonos product usually solves. On a 5G home-internet plan the connection itself can vary through the day, so we look at that separately.

Can you give us a list of the Sonos serial numbers for our homeowner's insurance?
+

Yes. The handover note we leave already lists every Sonos product by room, and on request we add each product's model and serial number, read from the product label before it is mounted or from the Sonos app afterward. Keeping that list with your insurance papers makes a claim after a theft, a fire or a burst pipe far simpler, and it also helps if a product ever needs warranty service. There is no extra charge when we record it during the installation visit.

Our only ladder spot for the yard speaker is on the driveway we share with next door. Do we need the neighbor's OK?
+

We ask that you get it. A shared driveway usually means part of the ground, or a right to use it, belongs to the house next door, and a ladder and a technician standing there for an hour is something your neighbor should know about in advance. In practice most neighbors are happy once they know the day and the time. We keep the ladder clear of their cars, finish that part of the job first, and do not set up on their side of the line without their permission.

Our TV is already on the wall. Can the soundbar go under it without the TV coming down?
+

Usually, yes. We fix the soundbar bracket below the screen at the gap Sonos recommends, connect the bar to the TV's HDMI ARC or eARC port, and dress the cables with a cord cover or, on a framed wall, the in-wall kit. On our Bronx list that is $129 for the mounting, $84.99 if you need the Sanus bracket, and $125 for the system setup. The one exception is a mount that hides the TV's ports so completely that nobody can reach them; then the TV is tilted or lifted off briefly to connect the cable, and put back level.

We dropped cable TV and stream everything through the TV's apps. Does that change the soundbar setup?
+

It makes it simpler. With no cable box, the soundbar takes its sound straight from the TV through the HDMI ARC or eARC connection, and the TV's own remote can control the soundbar's volume once HDMI-CEC is switched on in the TV's settings. We check that the TV is set to send its sound to the external speaker rather than its own, test a few of your streaming apps, and set Night Sound and dialogue settings so late-night viewing stays polite to the rest of the house.

Which HDMI port on the TV should the soundbar use?
+

The one marked ARC or eARC. Most TVs have several HDMI inputs but only one that can send the TV's sound back out to a soundbar, and plugging the bar into any other port leaves it silent. We also switch on the TV's HDMI-CEC feature, which each brand names differently, so the TV and the soundbar turn on together and the TV remote controls the volume. If your games console or streaming stick was using the ARC port, we move it to another input first.

If an access point is needed, which kind do you put in, and does it have to match our router?
+

It has to join the same network, and the simplest way is usually a unit from the same family as your router or mesh system, connected back to it by cable rather than by Wi-Fi. What matters most for Sonos is that it runs as an access point on your existing network, not as a second router creating a separate one, and that a wired Sonos product connects to the main router or the primary mesh node. We confirm the model with you before buying and price it after the walkthrough.

Can the wired Sonos product plug into a network switch instead of the router itself?
+

Yes, as long as the switch connects back to the main router, so the Sonos product is still on the same network as everything else. That is often the tidier answer in a basement where the router has run out of ports. Sonos needs the network to support Spanning Tree Protocol for its wired setup, and some managed switches need settings adjusted before Sonos behaves, so we check the switch rather than assume. An inexpensive unmanaged switch is usually the least trouble.

If we change our Wi-Fi password later, will every speaker need to be set up again?
+

Not if it is done in the right order. Update the network details in the Sonos app while the old network is still running, then change the password on the router, and the speakers follow. Having one Sonos product wired to the router also helps, because it stays reachable even when the wireless details change. If the password is changed first, each wireless product may have to be reconnected one by one, which is a setup-only visit if you would rather we did it.

If we later move the 5G box to a different window for a better signal, does the Sonos setup have to change?
+

Not the Sonos part. Because the music system lives on its own router, not on the gateway's Wi-Fi, moving the gateway only means moving the cable between the gateway and that router. We leave the router in its central spot and, where the house allows, leave enough cable or a second route so the gateway can move between a front and a rear window without anyone drilling again. The speakers, the rooms and the settings stay exactly as they were.
