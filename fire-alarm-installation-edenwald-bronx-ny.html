Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Edenwald's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every fire alarm call we take in Edenwald. They were built in different decades, by different methods, for different numbers of families, and those differences decide how alarms are powered, where cable can go and what rules apply long before anyone talks about brands.

The prewar brick rowhouse

Attached brick houses on narrow lots, often from the 1920s and 1930s, with plaster walls, a full cellar and two floors of living space above.

The postwar two-family

The most common house in the neighborhood: an owner's apartment and a rental apartment, stacked, often with separate entrances and separate electric meters.

The three-family from the 1960s to 1980s

Three stacked apartments sharing a hall and stair, frequently with a garage or utility space at street level, and squarely inside the multiple dwelling category.

The early-1930s two-story walk-up

Four to six apartments over a cellar, clustered on East 241st Street and Cranford Avenue just below the Mount Vernon line.

The cottage and the renovated Cape Cod

Colonial revival cottages and Cape Cod–style houses, some newly renovated, with half-story bedrooms under the roof and new finishes over old framing.

The mid-size apartment building

Postwar buildings of six stories or so, such as the 72-apartment building city records list on Baychester Avenue from 1964, where a building system is expected.

The prewar brick rowhouse: plaster, a deep cellar and very little room to hide a wire

Attached brick houses from the 1920s and 1930s were built with solid party walls, plaster on wood lath or on masonry, and floors framed in wood joists that run from party wall to party wall. That framing is good news for an installer in one direction and bad in the other. Running cable between joists from the front of the house to the back on one floor is usually possible from the cellar, through the joist bays, but running it straight up from the cellar to a bedroom ceiling two floors above means finding a path through plaster that was never meant to be opened. The common answers are old ones: a closet stacked over a closet, the space beside a chimney chase, a pipe chase beside the bathroom stack. When none of those exists, we do not chase plaster to prove a point. A rowhouse with a single household can be protected with sealed ten-year battery alarms on a wireless link, which avoids holes altogether, or with hardwired alarms on the floors where wiring is easy and wireless-linked units on the floors where it is not, provided they come from the same manufacturer's family and are designed to work together. The cellar of a house like this usually holds the boiler, the water heater, the electric service and decades of stored belongings, so it gets heat detection where dust and moisture would trouble a smoke alarm, and a smoke alarm at the bottom of the cellar stair where smoke heading upstairs would pass. Carbon monoxide alarms go on the floors where people sleep, not only in the cellar, because the purpose is to wake the household.

The postwar two-family: two households, two meters, one fire

A two-family built in the 1950s usually has easier walls than a prewar rowhouse, with early drywall or plaster on gypsum lath and a little more room in the framing, but it has a harder social layout. Two households live one above the other, often with separate front and side entrances, separate electric meters and separate leases or none at all if family members share the house. A fire in either apartment threatens both, and the household most at risk is the one asleep in the apartment where the fire is not. That is why linking matters so much here. A set of alarms in the downstairs apartment that only sounds downstairs is not enough when the upstairs family's bedrooms are directly over a downstairs kitchen. Our plan links the alarms across both apartments, so that one sounding sets off the others, while keeping each apartment's alarms powered from that apartment's own circuit or battery, so that one household's electric bill or breaker never decides whether the other household is protected. Where running an interconnect wire between apartments would mean opening finished ceilings, a wireless link between the two sets does the same job. We also look at the side stair or rear entrance that the upstairs apartment uses, because that path to the street is the one the upstairs family will take in a fire, and an alarm placed where it covers that stair does more good than one placed by habit in the middle of a living room.

The three-family from the 1960s to 1980s: a shared stair that belongs to everyone

Three-family houses are where Edenwald's fire alarm work gets serious, and the reason is partly legal and partly physical. Under the Multiple Dwelling Law, three families living independently make the building a multiple dwelling, which puts the owner under a heavier set of obligations than a one- or two-family owner carries. Physically, many of the neighborhood's three-families were built between the 1960s and the 1980s with one entrance, one hall and one stair serving all three apartments, sometimes with a garage or boiler room at street level beside the entrance. That shared stair is the only way out for at least two of the three households, and a fire in it, or a fire whose smoke reaches it, is the most dangerous event the building can have. Our plan for a building like this has three layers. Each apartment gets its own smoke and CO alarms near its sleeping areas. The common hall and stair get detection at each landing and at the top, where smoke collects first. And the ground-level garage or boiler room gets heat detection and, where vehicles or fuel-burning equipment are present, carbon monoxide coverage for the apartment directly above. How those layers connect is a decision we make with the owner, because linking every apartment to the stair means a burnt dinner in one apartment can wake the whole house, while not linking them means a stair fire might only be heard in the hall. We usually recommend that the stair and the garage sound in every apartment, while each apartment's own kitchen-side alarm stays local to that apartment, and we put that choice in writing.

The early-1930s two-story walk-up: four to six apartments and an old cellar

The small apartment buildings near East 241st Street and Cranford Avenue are a different animal from the houses around them. City lot data shows nine two-story buildings with four to six apartments on East 241st Street and three on Cranford Avenue, all built between 1930 and 1934, close to the Mount Vernon line. Buildings like these were put up long before today's fire alarm requirements and have usually been altered several times since: new boilers, new kitchens, rewired apartments, perhaps an old bell or a small panel left behind by a previous owner. Our first visit to a building of this kind is a survey, not an installation. We find out what is in each apartment and in the halls, whether any building panel exists and what condition it is in, what the boiler room holds, and what paperwork the owner has, including the Certificate of Occupancy. From that we can tell the owner whether the building needs good apartment alarms and hall detection, whether an existing system can be repaired like-for-like, or whether any change the owner wants would move the job into plans filed with the FDNY. The low height of these buildings means voice announcement systems, which the code ties to buildings over 125 feet, never come into it, but the shared stairs and the cellar boiler room deserve the same care as in a three-family, multiplied by the number of households.

The cottage and the renovated Cape Cod: new finishes, old framing and bedrooms under the roof

Colonial revival cottages and Cape Cod–style houses bring two particular conditions. The first is the half-story bedroom: rooms on the top floor with ceilings that slope down toward knee walls, often with a short flat section at the peak. Smoke rises to the highest part of a room, so an alarm in a sloped bedroom belongs near the high point, within the distance the manufacturer allows from the peak, not low on the slope where hot air may form a pocket above it. The second is the renovation. Homes.com lists newly renovated Cape Cod–style houses in Edenwald, and a renovation is when alarm wiring is easiest to get right and most often gotten wrong. Open walls let a contractor run interconnect wiring to every bedroom for very little money, but we regularly see renovated houses where new alarms were mounted on old boxes, where a combination alarm went into a hallway too close to a bathroom door, or where the cellar was finished as a bedroom or den without any alarm at all. If you are renovating, the moment to call is before the drywall goes up, when we can mark every alarm location, the interconnect route and any carbon monoxide positions for your contractor or electrician. If the renovation is already finished, we test what is there, room by room, and tell you plainly what passes and what does not.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading an Edenwald House Before a Single Alarm Comes Down

Count the doors and the bells first. Standing on the sidewalk, the technician counts entrances, mailboxes and doorbell buttons. Three buttons and one door usually means a three-family with a shared stair. Two doors, one at the front and one up a side stair, usually means a two-family where the upstairs family has its own way out. That count tells us how many households we are protecting before anyone has said a word.

Turn every existing alarm over. Each alarm comes off its base long enough to read the date, the model and whether it is wired, battery-only or sealed. Most Edenwald houses we see have a mix of all three, bought at different times by different people, and the mix is usually why a linked set does not behave as one.

Press one test button and walk. With one alarm sounding, the technician walks every floor and the cellar, listening for which others join in, and asks someone to stand in the farthest bedroom with the door shut. If the upstairs bedroom cannot hear a cellar alarm, the house does not have the protection its owners think it has.

Stand in front of the boiler and the stove. We note what burns fuel, where its flue runs and how far it is from the bedrooms above, and we ask where phones, scooters and tools are charged, because charging spots go on the survey sheet now.

Ask who lives where, plainly. Who sleeps in the cellar, who is hard of hearing, which tenant works nights. The answers move alarms more than any rule does.

Compliance notes for Edenwald buildings and businesses

  • Alarms in almost every home. Built Engineers' summary of New York City requirements notes that smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are required in almost all residential occupancies, even where a central fire alarm system is not.
  • The FDNY approves fire alarm plans. The NYC Business portal says a Project Authorization is required after a fire alarm plan is approved, before construction begins and before an inspection can be scheduled.
  • Who has to be named on a filing. The same portal says a fire alarm plan filing requires a licensed Master Electrician and an FDNY S97 certificate holder.
  • Finished means finished. The system must be 100 percent complete and pre-tested before the FDNY inspection, and the as-built riser diagram needs a signed Functionality Statement.
  • The Buildings Department is no longer where the plans go. Metropolis reports that Local Law 195 of 2018 removed the requirement to file fire alarm design plans with the DOB, and that while certified installers and electricians may professionally certify some work, core building fire alarm systems must still be certified by the FDNY.
  • Low buildings are treated differently. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has its own direct exit to the street, a court or a yard. That describes many new two-family houses; it does not erase the need for smoke and CO alarms.
  • Panels are not simple swaps. Moving or replacing a control panel needs new FDNY plans, and under DOB Buildings Bulletin 2024-001 a full replacement in a building that would otherwise need an Auxiliary Radio Communication System still requires one.
  • No Landmarks layer here. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission district in Edenwald, so exterior devices on private buildings do not carry the LPC review that applies in historic districts. The National Register listing of the Edenwald Houses is federal, not city landmarking.
Alarms or a System

Where an Edenwald Building Crosses From Household Alarms to a Filed Fire Alarm System

The most useful thing we can tell an Edenwald owner on the first call is which side of a single line their building sits on. On one side are household smoke and carbon monoxide alarms: devices that sound inside the home, are chosen and placed for the people who live there, and can be installed without plans going to the FDNY. On the other side is a building fire alarm system: a control panel, detection and notification throughout the building, a design approved by a registered professional engineer or architect, a filing with the FDNY, a Project Authorization, a pre-test and an inspection. Most of the neighborhood sits firmly on the first side. A small part sits on the second. And a few buildings sit near the line in ways that are easy to misjudge.

Firmly on the household side. One-family houses, cottages and Cape Cod–style houses, and nearly all two-family houses, need good alarms rather than a panel. That does not mean anything goes. The alarms still have to be the right type in the right places, and in a two-family the most important work is linking the two apartments so a fire in one is heard in the other. The 2022 Building Code's treatment of low buildings, which does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer when each unit is separated by one-hour fire partitions and has its own direct exit, reflects the same idea: in a small building with separated homes and direct exits, the alarms inside each home do the work.

Near the line. Three-family houses are where owners most often guess wrong in both directions. Some assume that a third apartment automatically requires a full panel and horns in every room. Others assume that because the building is a house, it needs nothing more than a battery alarm per floor. The truth depends on the building: its height, how the apartments are separated, how they reach the street, what the Certificate of Occupancy says, and what alterations have been done over the years. What is certain is that a three-family is a multiple dwelling under state law, that every household needs working alarms, and that the shared hall and stair deserve detection of their own. We survey, tell the owner what we see, and when the honest answer is "this needs a design professional's opinion," we say so rather than guessing.

On the system side. Larger apartment buildings, buildings that already have a building fire alarm system, and many commercial and assembly spaces sit here. For them the questions are about repair versus redesign, about filings and authorizations, and about scheduling work so that the system is never out of service longer than the Fire Code allows. A change of use can also move a building across the line: a ground floor that becomes a store, a house that becomes a program space, or a basement that becomes a gathering room. When an owner is planning any change like that, we would rather hear about it before the lease is signed or the walls are opened, because the fire alarm question is cheapest to answer early.

What we do on each side. On the household side, we design, install, link, test and document the alarms ourselves. On the system side, we survey existing systems, repair them like-for-like, and install, label and pre-test new or altered systems to approved drawings alongside the design professional and the other parties the FDNY filing requires. We are clear about which role we are playing from the first conversation.

Winter Fires

Frozen Hydrants on East 227th Street: Why Warning Time Matters Most in a Bronx Winter

A fire in the middle of the house threatens two households at once. In a stacked house, a fire on the second floor sends smoke up toward the apartment above and threatens to cut off the stair the ground-floor household might also use. The people upstairs are in the most danger and usually have the least warning, because they are farthest from the smoke's source until it reaches them. That is the strongest argument we know for linking alarms across apartments. When the second-floor alarm sounds in the third-floor bedrooms too, the family upstairs gets the same head start as the family where the fire began.

Cold weather adds minutes nobody plans for. In winter, people in Edenwald houses keep windows shut, run heat longer and sometimes add space heaters to cold rooms. Outside, snow and ice slow everyone down, and the CBS report shows that even the fire department's water supply can become a problem. No alarm can thaw a hydrant. What an alarm can do is move the moment of discovery earlier, so that by the time those outside problems arise, everyone inside is already out. In practice, that means alarms in every place a fire could start while people sleep, linked so every household hears every alarm, and a household plan that everyone knows: two ways out of each apartment where the building allows, bedroom doors closed at night, a meeting spot across the street, and the 911 call made from outside, not from inside while gathering belongings.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Edenwald: Questions Answered

These are the situations Edenwald owners, tenants, landlords and business operators bring to us: two- and three-family houses with households that depend on each other, winter fires and their aftermath, small apartment buildings with paperwork nobody has seen in years, and storefronts being leased for the first time. Each is answered fully here and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Two- and three-family houses

In our two-family, each apartment has its own electric meter. If the alarms are linked, whose circuit powers them?

Each apartment's alarms should be powered from that apartment, and the link between the two apartments should be planned so it does not depend on either household's breaker. Hardwired smoke alarms run on house power with a battery backup, and most manufacturers want the units that are wired together on the same circuit, or spell out exactly how to handle more than one; the instructions for the model decide what is allowed. In a two-family with two meters, running one apartment's interconnect wire into the other apartment's circuit is where things go wrong: one family's tripped breaker or unpaid bill should never decide whether the other family's alarms work, and mixing circuits from two separate electric services is not something we do. The clean answer is usually two hardwired sets, one per apartment on that apartment's own circuit, linked to each other by the manufacturer's wireless interconnect, so a fire in either apartment sounds in both. Where an apartment has no circuit that can sensibly feed alarms, sealed ten-year battery alarms from the same wireless family do the job without any wiring. The power these alarms draw is tiny, so the electric bill is never the real issue. When a new circuit or box is needed, that part is done by a licensed electrician, and we coordinate the work so the alarms go up the same day.

Our three-family has a garage at street level under the first apartment. What does that garage change for the people living above it?

It changes what kind of detection goes in the garage and adds carbon monoxide coverage upstairs. A garage under living space brings two risks: fire from vehicles, stored fuel, tools and anything charging down there, and carbon monoxide from an engine left running or fuel-burning equipment. Ordinary smoke alarms do not belong in the garage itself, because exhaust, dust and wide temperature swings cause false alarms and shorten their life. A heat alarm suited to garages goes there instead, linked to the alarms in the apartments so a garage fire is heard where people are sleeping rather than only in an empty garage. Carbon monoxide alarms go in the apartment directly above the garage, near its sleeping areas, and on any level where the garage connects to the house through a door or a stair, because that is the path the gas would take. We also look at the door between the garage and the shared hall: it should close on its own and stay closed, and any gaps around pipes or cable passing from the garage into the building should be sealed with fire-rated material. If anyone charges e-bikes, scooters or tool batteries in the garage, we note where, and we place the heat alarm with that spot in mind.

In our three-family, the tenants share one hall and stair to the street. What detection belongs in that shared hall, and who looks after it?

The hall needs detection of its own, and the owner should treat it as the building's, not any one tenant's. A shared stair in a three-family is the escape route for most of the households, so smoke reaching it is the most dangerous thing that can happen in the building. We put smoke detection at the top of the stair, where smoke collects first, and at each landing, and we make sure the hall alarms sound in every apartment, so a fire in the stair is heard behind closed apartment doors. Inside the apartments, each household gets its own smoke and carbon monoxide alarms near its sleeping areas. The city's housing rules generally put installing alarms on the owner and day-to-day care, such as testing and not disconnecting them, on the people who live with them; HPD publishes guidance worth reading for your building. In practice, we give the owner a one-page list showing every device, where it is and who tests it: the owner tests the hall and stair, each household tests its own apartment. We also check that apartment doors onto the hall close properly, because a door left open in a fire turns one apartment's smoke into the whole building's problem.

We bought a newly renovated Cape Cod–style house, and the seller's contractor put in new alarms. How do we check the work was done properly?

Start with what you can check yourself in fifteen minutes, then decide whether you want a technician to confirm it. Take each alarm off its base and read the label: the date of manufacture, whether it detects smoke, carbon monoxide or both, and whether it is hardwired or battery-only. Look at where they are. There should be smoke alarms in or near every sleeping area, including the half-story bedrooms under the roof and any finished cellar room someone might sleep in, and carbon monoxide alarms on the levels where people sleep. In sloped bedrooms, the alarm should be near the high point of the ceiling, not partway down the slope. Then press one test button and walk the house: in a properly linked system, every alarm should sound. If only the one you pressed makes noise, they are not linked, or were linked to units that were later swapped. Ask the seller or their contractor for receipts and instructions, which tell you the model and whether the alarms were meant to interconnect. If you want a technician to check everything, the visit is free when it is part of a quote for new work; otherwise it is a service visit at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and you get a written list of what passes and what does not.

I rent the upstairs of a two-family, and the alarm in my hallway died weeks ago. My landlord keeps saying he will get to it. What can I do?

Ask in writing, keep a copy, and if nothing happens, report it to the city. A text or letter that says which alarm, where, when it stopped working and that you have asked before creates a record the landlord cannot later dispute. If the alarm is not repaired or replaced within a reasonable time, call 311 and report a missing or defective smoke or carbon monoxide alarm, which goes to HPD. In the meantime, you do not have to sleep unprotected: a battery-powered smoke alarm from any hardware store, mounted on the ceiling or high on the wall near your bedroom, is a sensible stopgap, and a plug-in or battery carbon monoxide alarm does the same for CO. Do not take down or disconnect the landlord's alarm, even if it is dead, because that can confuse whose device is whose. Keep the receipt for anything you buy. We work for whoever controls the building, so in a rental the landlord is the person who books and pays for our installation, but we are glad to give your landlord a quote if you pass our number along, and many owners are relieved to have one company take care of both apartments at once.

Winter, fire next door and the edges of the house

The East 227th Street fire started on the second floor of a three-story house, and firefighters fought frozen hydrants. What should a family in a similar house take from that?

Take from it that the minutes before the fire department arrives belong to the household, and that the household needs to hear the alarm early wherever the fire starts. We do not know when that fire happened or whether the alarms in the house worked, because the report we found does not say, and we will not guess. What is clear is the shape of the danger. A fire in the middle floor of a stacked house threatens the families above and below at the same moment, and in winter things outside the house can slow help down. For a family in a similar house, the practical steps are these. Make sure alarms on every floor are linked, so a second-floor fire sounds on the third floor and in the cellar too. Put smoke alarms inside bedrooms or right outside them, not only in living rooms. Sleep with bedroom doors closed, which slows smoke. Know two ways out of each apartment where the building allows, and make sure windows that serve as a second exit actually open in cold weather. Agree on a meeting spot across the street, and make the 911 call once you are outside. Alarms cannot fight a fire or thaw a hydrant; they can make sure everyone is already outside when those problems begin.

After a fire in the attached house next door, our ceilings and alarms were covered in soot. Do the alarms have to be replaced?

Yes, in almost every case. Smoke and soot that are heavy enough to darken ceilings can get into the sensing chamber of a smoke alarm and coat the sensor of a carbon monoxide alarm, and once that happens the device may become oversensitive, chirp constantly or, worse, respond more slowly to a real fire. An alarm that still makes noise when its test button is pressed has only proven that its horn and battery work, not that its sensor is clean. We replace alarms that have been through a fire or heavy smoke exposure, and we check the wiring and interconnect while we are there, because heat traveling through a shared party wall or cockloft can also damage cable you cannot see. Before anything comes down, photograph the soot on the ceilings and the alarms in place, and keep the old alarms until your insurance adjuster has seen them or told you they are not needed, since the damage to your house may be part of a claim. If your house was also affected by water used to fight the neighbor's fire, tell us, because wet cellar wiring and a soaked electric panel change the order of the work, and an electrician may need to clear the electrical side first.

Our enclosed front porch drops below freezing in winter. Can a smoke alarm go out there?

Usually not, and you do not need one there for the house to be protected. Every household smoke and carbon monoxide alarm has an operating temperature range printed on its label and in its manual, and for many household models the bottom of that range sits well above freezing. A smoke alarm mounted on an unheated porch through a January cold snap may chirp, false-alarm or simply fail when it is needed, and its battery drains faster in the cold. The better plan is to keep the porch outside the alarm layout and protect the house from just inside the front door: a smoke alarm in the hall or front room, close enough to the porch door that smoke from a fire on the porch would reach it quickly once it found its way inside. If the porch holds things that can burn or catch, such as stored furniture, a space heater, or batteries left charging, a heat alarm rated for unheated spaces is an option, linked to the alarms inside so it is heard where people are. And if anyone sleeps on that porch in summer, it is a bedroom for those months and needs to be treated as one, with an alarm that suits its conditions.

Small apartment buildings and FDNY paperwork

Our two-story 1930s building near East 241st Street has five apartments and a boiler in the cellar. What fire detection is typical for a building that size?

Every apartment needs its own smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, the halls and stairs need detection, the boiler room needs heat detection, and whether the building also needs a building fire alarm system depends on its history and what you plan to change. Buildings like yours were built before modern fire alarm rules and have usually been altered since, so the requirement is not obvious from the outside. The 2022 Building Code's treatment of low buildings applies to how buildings are built and altered under that code; for an existing building, what governs is the rules in force when it was built and altered, any work you do now, and the Certificate of Occupancy. On a survey, we document every apartment's alarms, look for any panel or old bells in the halls and cellar, check the boiler room, and ask for the building's paperwork. Then we tell you which of three paths you are on: good apartment and hall detection with no filing, like-for-like repair of an existing system, or a change that needs a design professional and an FDNY filing. If your building happens to sit north of the city line, it is in Mount Vernon, not the Bronx, and the rules and paperwork are Westchester's.

What is an FDNY Project Authorization, and can any work start before we have one?

It is the FDNY's go-ahead to build a fire alarm system whose plans it has approved, and the installation covered by the filing should not start until it is issued. The NYC Business portal describes the sequence: the fire alarm plan is filed and approved, a Project Authorization is issued after that approval, and only then does construction begin and an inspection get scheduled. For an owner, the practical effect is on the calendar. If a renovation schedule assumes the fire alarm contractor can start on day one of construction, but the plans are still under review, the fire alarm will hold up the whole job. Work that is not part of the filed system can still go ahead: a survey of the existing building, removal of nothing that protects anyone, ordering equipment once the approved design is fixed, and keeping existing alarms working while the paperwork moves. What we will not do is install devices that belong to the filed design before the authorization exists, because that work could have to be removed, and because the system must be installed exactly as approved. We help owners build that waiting time into the schedule from the start.

The FDNY sign-off list includes an as-built riser diagram with a signed Functionality Statement. What is that, and how does it get prepared?

The riser diagram is the one-page map of the system: the panel, each floor, and the devices and circuits on each floor, drawn the way they connect. "As-built" means it shows what was actually installed, not what was planned, so any change made during installation, such as a detector moved a few feet because a pipe was in the way, has to appear on it. The NYC Business portal lists the as-built riser diagram with a signed Functionality Statement as part of the sign-off package, which in practical terms ties a signature to the claim that the system, as drawn, works as intended. The statement is completed by the parties the FDNY application names, and we do not present ourselves as the signer. What we supply is what the statement rests on: a device-by-device record of every detector, pull station and notification device, its location, its label and the result of its test, plus the changes we made in the field, marked up for the design professional. Keeping that record accurately during installation, rather than reconstructing it at the end, is what keeps sign-off from becoming a scramble when the inspection date arrives.

An older architect told us fire alarm plans go to the Buildings Department. Is that still how it works?

Not for the fire alarm plans themselves. Metropolis reported that Local Law 195 of 2018 removed the requirement to file fire alarm design plans with the Department of Buildings, and fire alarm plans now go to the FDNY, which reviews them, issues the Project Authorization and inspects the finished system. That does not mean the Buildings Department disappears from your project. If the fire alarm work is part of a larger renovation, the construction work may still need DOB permits, and electrical work has its own requirements. The same reporting notes that certified fire alarm installers and electricians may professionally certify some fire alarm work, but that core building fire alarm systems must still be certified by the FDNY, so there is no shortcut for a building's main system. If your architect learned the process years ago, it is worth asking the design professional preparing your fire alarm plans which path they intend to use, because the answer shapes the schedule. We work to whichever approved path applies and keep the paperwork that path needs as the installation goes along.

Edenwald Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Should the carbon monoxide alarm go right next to our gas stove?
+

No. A gas range can release a brief puff of combustion gases when a burner lights, and a CO alarm mounted right beside it is more likely to sound for nothing and to be disconnected by an annoyed household. Carbon monoxide alarms belong on the levels where people sleep, near the bedrooms, and at the distance from cooking and fuel-burning appliances that the manufacturer's instructions set out. We place them where they will wake you for a real problem and stay quiet during dinner.

Why do smoke alarms always seem to start chirping in the middle of the night?
+

A weak battery often gives out at night because houses cool down when the heat is turned back, and a cold battery delivers less power, so the alarm reports a low battery in the small hours. An alarm that has reached the end of its life also chirps, regardless of the battery. If a chirp continues after a fresh battery, check the date on the back. If it is more than ten years old, the whole alarm needs replacing.

Our bedrooms have ceiling fans. How far should a smoke alarm be from them?
+

Far enough that the fan's airflow does not blow smoke away from the alarm or push dust into it. Manufacturers give a recommended distance from fans and air vents in their instructions, and we follow it for the model we install. In small Edenwald bedrooms where the fan is in the center of the ceiling, that often means mounting the alarm closer to the door, which also helps it catch smoke coming from the hall.

Our hall alarm goes off every time someone takes a hot shower. Why, and where should it go instead?
+

Steam rolling out of a bathroom looks like smoke to many smoke alarms, so an alarm placed just outside the bathroom door will keep sounding. The fix is to move it a short distance along the hall, away from the bathroom door but still close to the bedrooms, and sometimes to choose a model less prone to steam. Running the bathroom fan and keeping the door closed while showering helps too. We never fix this by removing the alarm.

We just painted our ceilings. Can the smoke alarms be painted to match?
+

No. Paint can block the openings that let smoke into the sensing chamber, and a painted alarm may stop working without any warning sign. When you paint, take the alarm off its base, or cover it while painting and remove the cover the same day. If an alarm has already been painted, it should be replaced rather than cleaned. Most alarms come in white or almond and blend in well with a freshly painted ceiling.
