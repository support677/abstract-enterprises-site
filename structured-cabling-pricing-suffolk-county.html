The Neighborhood

Why Suffolk County Properties Need Structured Cabling Priced Building by Building

Suffolk is ten towns spread over roughly 86 miles, from the Nassau line at Melville and Amityville out to Montauk, and its buildings were mostly put up for some other purpose than the one they serve now. The Census figures compiled by Stacker put the median Suffolk home at 1970, which makes the county's housing younger than most of upstate New York but old enough that almost nothing was built with network cable in mind. On the commercial side, the Hauppauge industrial area that CoStar describes as holding the largest share of available industrial space on Long Island is full of one-storey warehouses from the 1960s and 1970s that have since been cut into smaller bays, each with its own office corner and its own tangle of cable left by previous occupants.

That history is why we stopped quoting Suffolk cabling as “so many drops at so much each” and nothing else. The drop price is the honest floor. Above it sit the costs that come from the building: a lift to reach a 17-foot clear ceiling in a flex bay, a landlord who wants every cable on J-hooks and every penetration fire-stopped in a Route 110 office tower, a crawl through a low attic in a split-level, a fiber link and a trench to a barn on a North Fork vineyard, or a decision about how high to put the patch panel in a South Shore house that was raised after Sandy. None of those are exotic in Suffolk. They are the normal job, and a quote that leaves them out is not cheaper, only incomplete.

There is also distance. A Suffolk job is a longer day for any crew coming from western Long Island or the city, and on the East End the drive itself is part of the schedule — the East Hampton Star has written about the morning “trade parade” of contractors on Montauk Highway through Amagansett. Our area pricing handles that with a single, published factor of 1.20 on the Brooklyn base rates, so the distance is visible rather than hidden in padded labour hours.

Finally, the money around the job has its own local rules. Suffolk raised its share of the sales tax on March 1, 2025, taking the combined rate from 8⅝% to 8¾%. Whether that tax shows on your invoice depends on whether the work counts as a capital improvement under the state's rules. Public jobs for districts, villages and towns can fall under New York's prevailing wage law, which changes the labour cost entirely. A useful Suffolk cabling price has to say which of those apply before anyone signs.

Suffolk facts that change a cabling price

  • Median home built in 1970 — Stacker's ranking of New York counties by median year built, from Census 2023 five-year estimates, places Suffolk at #47 of 50. Wood-frame houses of that era hide cable well but rarely have an open path from basement to second floor.
  • A 1975 Hauppauge warehouse with 17-foot clear height — a current listing at 320 Oser Avenue shows 20,114 square feet with about 3,700 square feet of office. That ratio, a small office corner in a tall bay, is typical of the buildings our Suffolk flex quotes cover.
  • Suffolk led Long Island office leasing in the first half of 2026 — Cushman & Wakefield figures reported by CRE Daily give Suffolk 50.2% of leasing volume and an Island-wide vacancy of 11.8%, with the largest lease a 73,500-square-foot deal in Hauppauge. Tenants moving into second-generation space usually inherit someone else's cabling.
  • Combined sales tax of 8¾% since March 1, 2025 — set out in the state Tax Department's notice ST-25-1, which raised Suffolk's local rate from 4¼% to 4⅜%.
  • 578 farms and 79 wineries — the 2022 counts cited by the Shelter Island Reporter. Many are working properties with several buildings, and the cable between them is usually the expensive part.
  • Station Yards at Ronkonkoma — 53 acres beside the LIRR station planned for about 1,450 homes at completion, according to the Governor's office, alongside commercial space. New construction is the one setting where walls are open when the cable goes in.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling Pricing for Suffolk County's Architecture

These are the building types we price most often in Suffolk, and what each one does to the cost of a drop. The same drop count can produce very different totals depending on which of these you are in.

Re-divided industrial and flex bays

Hauppauge, Bohemia, Ronkonkoma, Holbrook, Farmingdale. Tall open-web steel or deck ceilings, an office corner with a drop ceiling, and cable left by earlier tenants. Lift days, J-hooks and clean-up are the cost drivers; the drops themselves are straightforward.

Office park suites

Melville, Huntington Station, Islandia, Hauppauge's office buildings. Suspended ceilings, landlord-run risers and tenant standards that set the cable grade, support spacing and labelling. Plenum cable is needed only where the ceiling is used as a return-air space, which varies by building.

Houses built around 1970

Ranches, split-levels and high ranches in Brookhaven, Islip, Smithtown and Huntington. Wood framing, a basement or crawl space, a low attic and finished rooms. Routes are found, not built, and fishing time is the variable.

Farms, wineries and estates

The North Fork from Riverhead to Orient, and working properties on the South Fork. Several buildings, long distances and outdoor runs. The cost sits between the buildings, not inside them.

Raised South Shore houses

Lindenhurst, Amityville, Babylon and other South Shore streets flooded in Sandy. The living space is now a storey higher and the ground-level enclosure is limited to parking, access and storage, so the cabling plan starts above the flood line.

New construction

Station Yards at Ronkonkoma, new retail and medical buildings in growing hamlets, custom homes on the East End. Open walls make drops faster, but the job is tied to the builder's schedule and often needs certification paperwork.

How each building type changes the method, and the price

Flex bays. We price the office corner and the warehouse floor separately. The office corner behaves like any small office: drops at $210 or from $300, a wall cabinet, a panel. The warehouse side is where Suffolk flex quotes grow, because anything above about 12 feet means a scissor or boom lift, and because an open deck has nothing to hang cable from. We list the J-hook or tray footage at $10 a foot and name the lift on its own line, so you can see exactly how much of the price is the building's height.

Office suites. The landlord's rules are read before the quote, not after. If the building requires Cat6A, or plenum cable throughout, or a particular brand of patch panel, the price reflects it. Where the suite is second-generation space we usually find cable from the previous tenant above the tiles. Reusing it can be cheaper, but only if it tests clean and ends somewhere useful; otherwise the honest choice is to remove what is abandoned and run new.

Houses. In a 1970s Suffolk house the cheapest route is usually through the basement and up interior walls on the first floor, then through the attic and down for the bedrooms. Split-levels add a half-storey with no direct path, and finished basements take away the easy route underneath. We price each drop the same and tell you up front which rooms will need a surface raceway instead of a hidden run.

Farm and multi-building sites. Copper data cable is limited to 100 metres of channel, and on a vineyard the distance between the office and the tasting room or barn is often more than that. The answer is usually fiber, sometimes a point-to-point wireless link. Fiber needs a trench or an aerial route, termination at both ends and testing; the termination and testing are on our price list, and the trench is quoted from the actual ground.

Raised houses. FEMA's guidance for elevated buildings limits the space underneath to parking, access and storage. We keep the panel, the switch and the carrier's equipment on the living floor and route cable down only where it must go, in a way that can be replaced if the lower level floods again.

Houses used as offices. Along village main streets and roads such as Route 25A, many accountants, lawyers, therapists and small medical practices work from converted houses. They combine the house problems — wood framing, an attic, a basement, plaster in the older ones — with business needs such as a closet for the equipment, a drop at every desk and a printer, and sometimes a landlord. We price them as houses for the route and as offices for what is at the end of each drop, and we look closely at the attic, because a converted attic office is often where the cable has the least room.

New construction. A drop in an open stud wall goes in faster than one fished into a finished room, but the list price does not change; what changes is the number of visits. A new-build quote shows rough-in and trim-out as separate trips, and certification is usually included because the builder's contract asks for it.

Commercial Structured Cabling

Business Structured Cabling Pricing in Suffolk County

Suffolk's commercial cabling work clusters along a handful of corridors, and each corridor has a pricing pattern of its own.

Long Island Innovation Park at Hauppauge

The former Hauppauge Industrial Park, renamed in 2019, with roughly 1,350 businesses on about 11 square miles in a 2017 count. Manufacturing, distribution and offices side by side; most of our flex-bay pricing comes from here.

Route 110: Melville, Farmingdale, Huntington Station

Office parks, R&D buildings and warehouses with direct access to the Long Island Expressway. Multi-tenant towers where landlord standards and after-hours work shape the quote.

Ronkonkoma, Bohemia and MacArthur Airport

Industrial and logistics buildings around Long Island MacArthur Airport, and the transit-oriented Station Yards development beside the LIRR. A mix of tall-bay retrofits and new construction.

Stony Brook and the Route 25A corridor

University-related research tenants, medical offices and professional suites in converted houses along 25A. Documentation and testing are often written into the contract.

Riverhead: Route 58 and Calverton

Big-box and strip retail along Route 58, the hospital campus, and the Calverton enterprise park. Retail drops for registers and cameras, and long runs in large single-storey footprints.

Westhampton and the Gabreski business district

The Hampton Business District on county land at Gabreski Airport, and seasonal businesses across the South Fork. Scheduling and access, not drop count, are the main variables.

Rules that show up on Suffolk commercial cabling quotes

  • Return-air ceilings. Cable in a ceiling used for environmental air must be plenum-rated. Whether a particular Suffolk suite's ceiling is a return-air space is a question for the building's mechanical layout, so we check it on the walkthrough and price the plenum upgrade, $48 a drop, only on the runs that need it.
  • Fire-stopping. Any cable that passes through a rated wall or floor has to be sealed with a listed fire-stop system. Office landlords in Melville and Hauppauge usually ask for photos of each sealed penetration at handover.
  • Accessibility. Wall jacks and devices in public areas are placed within reach ranges used for accessible design. In shops and medical suites this sometimes moves a jack the owner wanted lower or higher.
  • County and town licensing. Suffolk's electrical licensing article, Chapter 563 Article XI of the County Code, applies across the county except in towns and villages that run their own licensing law. We work under our New York State low-voltage license #12000287431 and tell you if your town's building department asks for anything further for your job.
  • Digging. Any trench for a fiber or conduit run between buildings needs a New York 811 notice at least 48 hours ahead. In the Central Pine Barrens, land clearing in the Core is restricted, which can rule out a straight-line trench across undeveloped ground.
  • Public work. Cabling for a school district, library, village or town can be public work under New York Labor Law Article 8, which brings prevailing wage rates set each July 1. That is a different labour basis from this price list, and we say so on the quote.
Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Pricing in Suffolk County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Suffolk owners and managers ask once they have a quote in hand, grouped by what they are really about. The general questions about drop prices, cable grades and minimums are answered on our main structured cabling pricing page; the ones here are the Suffolk ones.

Reading a Suffolk cabling quote

Why does our Suffolk quote show the same drop at one price but a higher total for the warehouse side?

Because the drop is the same and the route is not. Every Cat6 drop on a Suffolk quote is $210 and every Cat6A drop starts at $300, whether it ends at a desk in the office corner or at a camera on the warehouse wall. What changes on the warehouse side is everything the drop needs to get there: footage of J-hooks or tray at $10 a foot across an open deck, the lift needed to work at height, and sometimes a second small closet so the run stays within the 100-metre channel limit.

We keep those items on separate lines on purpose. If a quote rolls them into a higher per-drop figure, you cannot tell whether you are paying for cable or for the building. With them split out, you can see that a ten-drop office corner and a ten-drop warehouse floor in the same Hauppauge building really are different jobs, and you can decide, for example, to move a camera to a lower mounting point and drop the lift day.

What exactly is the 1.20 Suffolk factor paying for?

Our prices start from one base list, set for Brooklyn, and each area we serve has a published factor. Suffolk's is 1.20, so a $175 Brooklyn Cat6 drop is $210 here and a $500 Brooklyn minimum is $600. The factor reflects what is genuinely different about doing the work in Suffolk: longer travel for the crew, fewer drops finished per day because the crew starts later and leaves earlier, and buildings that tend to be spread out rather than stacked.

What the factor does not cover is anything specific to your building. A lift, a trench, removal of abandoned cable or a fiber link are listed separately, as they would be in Brooklyn. And the service rate for repairs, $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, is not multiplied at all; it is the same everywhere we work.

A contractor from Nassau quoted less. How do we compare the two quotes fairly?

Put them side by side line by line and check five things. First, is every drop terminated at both ends, labelled and tested, or just pulled? Second, is the cable grade the same, and is plenum cable included where the ceiling needs it? Third, does one quote include a rack or cabinet, panels and patch cords while the other assumes you already have them? Fourth, is height, pathway support and clean-up of old cable priced, or left as “to be determined”? Fifth, does the price include a test result for each drop you can keep?

Quotes that differ by a lot usually differ on one of those five, not on the per-drop number. We are happy to look at a competing quote with you and point out where it is not like for like, including where ours costs more for a reason you might decide you do not need.

Are ferry and barrier-island jobs priced the same as the mainland?

The per-drop prices are the same, but island work is quoted individually because the boat sets the working day. On Shelter Island, reached by ferry from both forks, and on the Fire Island communities served from Bay Shore, the crew's hours are limited by the crossings, materials have to travel as freight, and the National Park Service limits contractor vehicles on parts of Fire Island.

In practice we plan these jobs to finish in one trip, which means more preparation: pre-terminated patch cords, a full materials list checked twice, and a design agreed before anyone boards. The quote names the trips and the freight so they are not a surprise.

Building history and what it costs

Our Hauppauge bay was split off a bigger building. How is the old cable above the ceiling priced?

It is priced in one of three ways, and we tell you which before work starts. If the old cable tests clean, runs to a closet that is inside your space and goes somewhere you need, we re-terminate and certify it, and it costs less than a new drop. If it runs to a closet that now belongs to the bay next door, it is useless to you even if it is perfect, and we leave it or remove it. If it is abandoned, damaged or of unknown origin, removal is listed as its own line.

Re-divided buildings in the Innovation Park are where this matters most, because the closet and the carrier's entrance often ended up on the other side of the new demising wall. Tracing which cables are yours takes time, and that time is cheaper than discovering halfway through the job that half the ceiling is someone else's.

Our warehouse ceiling is over 17 feet. How does height change what we pay?

Height does not change the drop price, but it adds equipment and time. Above about 12 feet we work from a scissor lift or boom lift, and a lift has to be delivered, used and collected, so it is listed as its own line for the days it is on site. An open deck with no ceiling grid also needs a support system for the cable, which we price at $10 a foot for J-hooks or tray.

The way to keep the cost down is to plan every high-level task for the same lift days: access points, cameras, any fiber to a mezzanine. A Suffolk flex tenant who adds one access point a month after the main job pays for the lift twice.

The Melville landlord sent a cabling rulebook. Will following it cost us more?

Sometimes, and you should know by how much before you sign the lease work letter. Typical office-building rules call for Cat6A or plenum cable, support at fixed intervals, labels in a set format, fire-stopping with photos, removal of any cable you abandon, and work outside business hours in shared areas. Each of those has a line on our list or adds time, so the quote shows the difference between “to code” and “to this landlord's standard.”

In our experience the expensive surprise is not the rulebook itself but the removal clause at the end of the lease. If the landlord can require you to take out every cable you installed, budget for that now.

Our house was built around 1970, with a finished basement and a low attic. Which route costs less?

The drop price is the same either way; the route decides how many drops can be hidden and how long they take. In a ranch the attic usually reaches every room, and the cost is the crawl. In a high ranch or split-level, the lower level and the upper level often have no direct path between them, and a finished basement removes the easy route under the first floor.

We walk the house, look for the closets that stack, the plumbing chases and the return-air paths that cable must not use, and then tell you which rooms can be fed invisibly and which need a short surface raceway. Rooms in a 1970s Suffolk house that look hard are usually reachable; it is the one room over the garage that tends to need a creative answer.

Can one quote cover the office, tasting room and barn on a North Fork property?

Yes, and it should, because the cost is in the links between the buildings. Each building is priced like a small job of its own: drops, a small cabinet, a panel. The link between them is usually fiber, because copper data cable cannot exceed 100 metres of channel and outdoor runs need cable rated for burial or conduit. Fiber termination is $42 per connector and OTDR testing $48 per strand on our list; the trench or aerial route depends on the ground and is quoted after we have walked it.

Where trenching is impractical — across a vineyard in production, for example — a point-to-point wireless bridge between the buildings is often the cheaper and less disruptive choice. We price both and let the site decide.

Our South Shore house was raised after Sandy. Where can the panel and cable go?

On the living floor, not underneath. FEMA's technical guidance limits the enclosed space under an elevated building to parking, building access and storage, and in Lindenhurst, Amityville and Babylon the National Weather Service records repeated flooding south of Montauk Highway since Sandy. Equipment that sits on the lower level is equipment that will be replaced.

We put the panel, switch and the carrier's handoff in a closet on the raised floor and run cable down only to what genuinely belongs below, such as a camera at the garage door, in conduit that can be pulled out and replaced. The drop prices do not change; the layout does.

A building near the Ronkonkoma station still has open walls. What are we paying for at this stage?

You are paying the same per-drop price, spread across two visits instead of one. At rough-in we pull cable into open studs and boxes and leave it coiled; at trim-out, after drywall and paint, we terminate, label and test. Open walls make each drop faster, and that shows up as a shorter schedule rather than a separate discount on our list.

Two things are worth adding while the walls are open: spare drops to places you might need later, and empty conduit or pull strings to the ceiling. Both cost little now and a great deal once the walls close. If the builder's contract asks for certification, $24 a drop covers a printed test for each one.

Tax, public work and paperwork

Does Suffolk's 8¾% sales tax get added to a cabling invoice?

It depends on whether the work is a capital improvement, and that is a tax question about your job, not a rule we can apply blindly. New York's Tax Department says a capital improvement has to add to the value or life of the property, become part of it, and be intended as permanent. Where those tests are met and the customer gives the contractor a Form ST-124, no sales tax is charged on the job; the contractor pays tax on the materials instead.

Repairs and services that do not meet the test are taxable at Suffolk's combined rate, which has been 8¾% since March 1, 2025. We show tax as a separate line, never inside the drop price, and if you are unsure which treatment applies we suggest asking your accountant before the invoice is issued.

We are a school district or a village. Why is our cabling priced on a different basis?

Because public work in New York usually carries prevailing wage requirements. Under Labor Law Article 8, a contract with a public agency that employs laborers, workmen or mechanics on public work must pay the rates the Department of Labor sets each July 1 for that county and trade. The state's broadband guidance adds separate rules for projects funded through ConnectALL.

This page's prices assume private work. For a Suffolk district, library, fire district, village or town we request the project's wage schedule and build the price from it, so the number reflects the law rather than our standard list.

What do we receive at the end that the next contractor can use?

A port map showing every jack and the panel port it lands on, the labels on both ends that match it, a floor plan with the drop locations, and, if you paid for certification, a test result per drop. Those documents are part of the job price, not an extra.

Suffolk businesses change contractors, landlords and IT companies often, and cable is the one thing that stays. A map that the next person can read is worth more than any single drop on the invoice.

Budgeting and phasing

We expect to grow in two years. What should we pay for now so we do not pay twice?

Pay now for the things that are expensive to add later: spare drops at the walls you are likely to use, a larger panel than today's port count, and a rack or cabinet with room to spare. A 48-port panel costs $150 against $90 for a 24-port, and the second panel installed later costs a visit as well.

Do not pay now for active equipment you do not need yet. Switches and access points get cheaper and better; cable in a wall does not change. The cheapest growth plan in a Suffolk office is usually more cable now and less hardware.

Can we split the work — cable this year, cameras and Wi-Fi later?

Yes. The cable for future cameras and access points can be pulled and terminated now, tested and labelled, and left with a cover plate or a coiled service loop in the ceiling. When the devices are bought, the second visit is a mounting job rather than a cabling job. Each phase is priced from the same Suffolk list, and if the second phase drops below the $600 minimum on its own, the minimum applies to it.

The one thing we advise against splitting is anything that needs a lift or a trench. Those costs repeat in full each time.

Is it cheaper to repair our existing cable or replace it?

If most of the cable is sound and the problems are a few bad terminations or one damaged run, repair is cheaper: a service visit at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum usually finds and fixes several faults. If the cable is the wrong grade for what you plan to run, was installed without support, or nobody knows where half of it goes, replacement costs less over the life of the building.

The way to decide is to test before you choose. A certification test on a sample of drops tells you what you have, and that result is useful whichever way you go.

Distance, size and the end of the lease

Our Route 58 store is one long room. At what point does the length start to cost money?

At about 90 metres of permanent cable, which leaves room for patch cords inside the 100-metre channel that Ethernet allows. Below that, a longer run adds very little: the cable itself is cheap, and a long straight run through a reachable ceiling is quick to pull. Above it, the design has to change, and that is where the price moves.

In a big single-storey footprint like the retail buildings along Route 58 in Riverhead, the usual answer is a second small cabinet near the far end, fed from the main closet by fiber or by a short copper uplink, so that the drops on that side stay short. That adds a wall cabinet at $300, a panel and the link between the two, and it is cheaper than discovering after installation that the furthest registers and cameras will not hold a connection.

We measure the route, not the floor plan, because cable follows the ceiling grid and goes around obstacles. A store that is 250 feet long on paper can need a 330-foot run to its far corner once the route is walked.

Our office is near the water on one of the forks. Does salt air change the cable or the price?

Inside a conditioned building it changes nothing; standard cable and jacks are fine. It matters for anything outside or in unconditioned space — a camera on a dock building, a link to a boathouse, a jack in an open-sided barn. There we use outdoor-rated cable in conduit, sealed boxes and stainless or coated hardware, and the run is priced with those materials named.

The drop price stays at $210 or from $300; the outdoor materials and any conduit are listed beside it. The bigger saving is in design: keeping as much of the cable as possible inside heated space and running the shortest possible outdoor section.

The lease says we must remove our cabling when we leave. What will that cost?

Removal is priced from a walkthrough, because it depends on how the cable was installed and what the landlord wants left behind. Cable that was run neatly on J-hooks and labelled can be traced and pulled quickly; cable that was tied to pipes and other trades' supports, or that nobody can identify, takes far longer, and patching and tile replacement may be part of it.

Two things make the eventual removal cheaper, and both are decided at installation: keeping a port map that shows exactly which cables are yours, and agreeing with the landlord in writing which cable stays as part of the building. Many Suffolk landlords are happy to keep tested, labelled cable for the next tenant.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Pricing in Suffolk County

We could not open Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask or Answer the Public from the environment this page was built in, so we have not quoted them. What we can check is what the published cost guides and owner forums say, which is where AI summaries draw their numbers from. Here is where those claims hold up in Suffolk and where they do not.

“A Cat6 drop costs $125 to $250”

Fixr's cost guide, updated January 31, 2025, gives a contractor drop at $125 to $250 including materials and labour, and that range is quoted widely. Our Suffolk Cat6 drop is $210 and Cat6A starts at $300, so the guide is not far off for the drop itself.

The trouble is that a range like that describes the easy case: a drop in a reachable ceiling, terminated and done. It says nothing about a 17-foot flex bay, a split-level with no path between floors or a barn across a field, which in Suffolk are ordinary jobs rather than exceptions.

So use the national range to sanity-check the per-drop line on any quote, and then look for the lines that sit next to it. A Suffolk quote that matches the national range but has no lines for height, pathway or links between buildings has not finished pricing your building.

“Labour runs $45 to $75 an hour”

The same guide puts cabling labour at $45 to $75 an hour and estimates 40 to 48 hours for 2,000 feet of cable with its drops. Those are national averages and say nothing about what a licensed, insured contractor costs on Long Island.

We do not price installation by the hour at all; new work is priced per drop and per line so you know the total before we start. The only hourly rate we publish is for repairs and service calls on existing cable, $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and it is the same in every county.

If you compare our per-drop price with a national hourly figure, compare the whole job rather than the rate. A drop that takes an hour and a half including termination, testing and labelling at a cheap rate can cost as much as one at our list price, and comes without a test result.

“Low-voltage installers charge about $50 a drop”

This figure comes up in owner forums. In one r/homelab thread about a builder asking $600 per drop in an unfinished house, commenters suggested low-voltage installers often charge about $50 a drop, with other replies citing $75 to $150.

Those numbers describe cable pulled into open studs in a house under construction, often left unterminated for the owner to finish. That is a real price for a real service, but it is not the same product as a jack, a panel port, a label and a test result.

In Suffolk the open-wall case does exist, around Station Yards and in custom homes on the East End, and our open-wall drops go faster. We still terminate and test every one, because a cable that has never been tested is the one that fails after the drywall goes up.

“Open walls make cabling cheap, so new construction should cost far less”

Open walls do make each drop faster, and the forum thread above is right that pulling cable before drywall is easier. Where the claim goes wrong is in assuming the saving is all on the cable.

New construction brings its own costs: two visits instead of one, work tied to the builder's schedule, certification paperwork the general contractor asks for, and coordination with electricians and HVAC installers over shared space. On a Suffolk new-build those can take back much of what the open walls saved.

The real saving in new construction is in what you add while you can — spare drops, conduit to the ceiling and outdoor runs placed before landscaping — because adding them later is expensive.

“Cost depends mainly on cable length”

Cost guides list cable length, number of drops and complexity as the drivers, and summaries tend to repeat length first. But Fixr's own figures put Cat6 cable at about 15 to 25 cents a foot as material, which means even a long run adds little in cable.

In Suffolk the cost lives in the route, not the length. A 60-foot run across an open deck that needs J-hooks and a lift costs more than a 150-foot run through a tidy drop ceiling. Length matters chiefly when it passes 100 metres and the design needs a second closet or fiber.

When you read a quote, ask how the run gets from one end to the other. That answer explains the price better than any footage figure.

“Prices are the same across Long Island”

Some cabling pages, including older pages of ours, have said that Long Island work is priced the same as the city. We now publish area factors instead: Nassau at 1.15 and Suffolk at 1.20 on the Brooklyn base.

The reason is practical. A crew working in Riverhead or Southampton spends part of its day travelling, finishes fewer drops per day, and needs more planning for materials than a crew a few blocks from its base. Absorbing that silently would mean padding hours or cutting corners.

A published factor means a Suffolk customer can check the arithmetic: Brooklyn base times 1.20, rounded to the dollar where it does not come out even. If a quote's numbers do not follow from the list, ask why.

“Add your local sales tax to the total”

Calculators often finish by adding sales tax to everything. In New York, whether tax is due on an installation depends on whether it is a capital improvement under the Tax Department's three tests, and in Suffolk the combined rate changed to 8¾% on March 1, 2025.

A permanent cabling installation in a building can meet the capital improvement tests, in which case the customer completes Form ST-124 and is not charged sales tax on the job, while the contractor pays tax on materials. A repair visit is treated differently.

So a calculator that simply adds 8¾% may overstate one job and understate another. We show tax separately and let your accountant confirm the treatment.

A Suffolk Cabling Checklist That Shortens the Quote

The fastest Suffolk quotes come from owners who can answer a handful of questions about the building before we arrive. None of them need technical knowledge; they are about what you can see and what you have been told.

  • What was the space before you? Part of a larger warehouse, another company's office, a house, a barn, or new. This tells us where to look for old cable and closets.
  • What is overhead? A tile ceiling, an open steel deck, an attic, or finished plaster or drywall. A phone photo looking straight up in each main room answers most of it.
  • How high? A rough ceiling height in the tallest area. Anything above about 12 feet changes the equipment we bring.

With those answers and a drop count, the remote estimate is usually close enough to budget from, and the walkthrough becomes a check rather than a discovery. If you do not know some answers, that is fine; we find them on the walk.

The site lines that are not on the list, and why

Some costs depend so completely on the building that a published number would mislead you. We describe them here so you know what to expect on a Suffolk quote and why each one appears.

Lift days. Above roughly 12 feet we work from a scissor lift or a boom lift. The cost depends on the lift needed for the height and the floor, how many days it stays, and delivery to your address. On flex jobs we schedule all high work into the fewest lift days we can, because the lift is charged by the day whether it is used for one access point or twenty.

Trenching and conduit between buildings. The ground decides the price: lawn, gravel drive, asphalt, vineyard rows or a hedge line each take different equipment and restoration. Distance is less important than what has to be crossed. Every trench starts with an 811 notice, and in the Central Pine Barrens Core we look for a route that does not involve clearing.

Removal of abandoned cable. Priced by how much there is and how it was installed. A ceiling full of unidentified cable from three tenants ago takes longer to clear than a single bundle that was labelled. Where a landlord requires removal, it is a line on the quote, not a favour.

Ceiling tiles and patching. Older tiles in Suffolk offices and shops crack when lifted, and some are no longer made. If they look brittle on the walkthrough we list replacements; in houses, if a drop needs a small access hole in a finished wall, the quote says where and who patches it.

After-hours access and escorts. Some Route 110 and Hauppauge office buildings allow cabling in shared areas only outside business hours, or with the building's own engineer present. If the landlord charges for that, the charge appears as the landlord's fee, separate from our lines.

Island trips and freight. Shelter Island and Fire Island jobs list the crossings and any freight separately, because they depend on the boat schedule rather than the work.

Field Notes: Price the Building Before the Drops

When I walk a Suffolk building for a cabling price, I count drops last. First I ask what the building was before. A flex bay that used to be part of a bigger warehouse tells me to look for the old closet and the carrier's line on the wrong side of a new wall. An office suite that has had three tenants tells me to lift a tile and see how much is up there already. A house tells me to find the basement, the attic hatch and the closets that line up from floor to floor.

Then I look up. How high is the ceiling, is there a grid or an open deck, and is the space above it moving air? Those three answers decide most of what is on the quote besides the drops. On farm properties I walk the ground between the buildings, because that walk usually decides the largest single line.

What deposit books a Suffolk cabling crew?
+

Larger Suffolk cabling jobs are booked with a 50% deposit, which reserves the crew days and pays for cable and hardware ordered for your building; the balance is due on completion and handover. Can we get a budget figure from a floor plan and photos before anyone visits?
+

Yes. A floor plan with the drop locations marked, the town, and photos of the ceilings, the closet and any outbuildings let us build a budget range from the Suffolk list. The range says which lines still need a site check, usually height, the return-air question and inherited cable. The walkthrough then turns the range into a fixed price.

Is the Suffolk price list the same for homes as for businesses?
+

The list is the same line by line. A house simply uses different lines: drops, perhaps a small cabinet, rarely any support footage or certification. A business more often needs panels, a rack, plenum where the ceiling requires it and test results. Homeowners are not charged a commercial rate, and businesses are not given a residential shortcut.

Can you price cable for a camera system we already bought?
+

Yes. Each camera is a drop on the Suffolk list, priced the same as any other, with outdoor locations noted where the cable has to pass through an exterior wall. We cable to your recorder or switch, test each run and label it, and you or your installer mount the cameras, or we quote mounting separately.

What happens to the price if we add drops halfway through the job?
+

Added drops are priced at the same Suffolk rate as the original ones and written up as a change before we pull them, so the invoice still matches what you signed. If the additions push the job into a higher volume tier, the tier applies to the whole drop count. Adding drops while the crew and any lift are still on site is far cheaper than a return visit.

Is financing available for a larger Suffolk cabling project?
+

Customers can apply for financing through our partner Wisetack, using the prequalification link on this page. Approval and terms are set by the lender, not by us. If financing matters for your decision, apply before the walkthrough so the quote can be sized to what is available.

Can you price cabling for a building we have not leased or bought yet?
+

Yes, and it is a good time to do it. A walkthrough before you sign tells you how much usable cable is already there, where the carrier enters, whether the ceiling is a return-air space and what the landlord's rules will add. That figure belongs in the lease negotiation or the purchase budget, not in the first month's surprises.

Will you work out the drop locations with our IT company and phone vendor?
+

Yes. We prefer to agree the drop plan with whoever will install the switches, phones and access points, so ports, labels and patch cords match their equipment from day one. A short call or a marked-up floor plan from them is usually enough, and it avoids paying for drops in the wrong place.

Can you reuse the jacks and wall plates already in our walls?
+

Only when they match the new cable and are in good condition. A Cat5e jack on a new Cat6A run lowers the whole drop to the jack’s rating, and old plates are often cracked or painted over. Where the existing plate and box are sound we reuse the box and fit a new jack and plate as part of the drop price, so nothing extra appears on the quote for it.
