The Neighborhood

Why New Utrecht Properties Need Access Control

Most of Brooklyn’s neighborhoods were named by developers. New Utrecht was named by its founders. Wikipedia’s history of the town, drawing on the New Netherland Institute, dates it to 1652, when land on the bay opposite Staten Island passed to Cornelius van Werckhoven, a surveyor from Utrecht; after his death Jacques Cortelyou laid out twenty lots and named the settlement Nieuw Utrecht. It became a village in 1657, had its own charter from Peter Stuyvesant in 1661, and was one of the six original towns of Kings County. It kept its independence until the City of Brooklyn annexed it on July 1, 1894. Its center, the part people still call New Utrecht, sits inside what maps now label Bensonhurst, around 84th Street between 16th and 18th Avenues.

The old town left marks you can still walk past. The New Utrecht Reformed Church, a congregation chartered in 1677, stands on 18th Avenue between 83rd and 84th Streets in a building put up in 1828 from the stones of its first church of 1700. The burial ground was consecrated in 1654. Between 16th and 18th Avenues, 84th Street is roughly twice the width of its neighbors, because, as Forgotten New York’s walk of the avenue explains, it was once the roadbed of the Brooklyn, Bath and Coney Island Railroad and part of the old Kings Highway. That railroad’s route became New Utrecht Avenue, and in 1916, under the Dual Contracts, the BMT built the elevated West End line over it. Today the D runs on that steel down the length of the avenue, through the 62nd Street, 71st Street and 79th Street stations, before turning onto 86th Street.

Put those layers together and you get the doors this page is about. Along the avenue, attached two-story buildings hold shops on the ground floor and apartments above, and the apartments are reached through a narrow street door between two storefronts, a few feet from a steel column. Because the avenue runs on a diagonal across the street grid, it leaves wedge-shaped lots and the odd “bowtie” intersections that the city’s Department of Transportation is now redesigning. Off the avenue, the side streets hold early twentieth-century apartment houses and long rows of attached brick houses, some with original stoops and iron railings, built for families of modest means rather than for show.

The crowds come on a timetable. The 62nd Street/New Utrecht Avenue station, where the elevated D meets the open-cut N, counted 1,200,551 riders in 2025 according to the MTA figures Wikipedia reports. New Utrecht High School, on 16th Avenue at 80th Street, had 3,125 students in 2023–24, and the city’s June 2026 safety proposal for the avenue notes more than 3,000 students arriving and leaving every school day through the intersection by the 79th Street station. A few times a year the Friends of Historic New Utrecht fill the church grounds for events such as Liberty Weekend.

Three conditions are particular to the el. Noise first: a 2009 study in the American Journal of Public Health measured MTA subway platforms at a mean of 81.1 dBA, and a New York Daily News report from 2000 recorded peaks above 100 decibels around apartments beside elevated tracks. An intercom call made while a train passes is often lost at both ends. Light second: the structure shades the sidewalk, so a door station camera that looks fine on a side street can show a dark silhouette under the el at noon. Third, the buildings are attached and often share roof space; when a fire started in a restaurant on New Utrecht Avenue in May 2022, ABC7 reported that flames spread through the ceilings of two adjoining buildings. Where the door controller and power supply sit matters in a row like that.

Most owners here pair entry control with a New Utrecht intercom installation or security cameras in New Utrecht, and we plan them together when that saves a visit.

New Utrecht facts we checked before writing this page

  • Founded 1652, annexed 1894. One of the six original towns of Kings County; town records were kept in Dutch until 1763; annexed by the City of Brooklyn on July 1, 1894 and part of Greater New York from January 1, 1898 (Wikipedia, New Utrecht). Sources disagree on the founding year: the city’s Municipal Archives guide gives 1652 for the town and 1657 for village status.
  • Old town center. Wikipedia places the main thoroughfare of the old town at 84th Street between 16th and 18th Avenues; the street’s extra width there is the old railroad bed and Kings Highway (Forgotten New York, 2016).
  • Reformed Church. Present church built 1828, parish house 1892 (architect Lawrence Valk), parsonage 1906; designated a New York City landmark on March 15, 1966, with the parish house and grounds added on January 13, 1998; listed on the National Register in 1980, with a boundary increase in 2001.
  • Liberty Pole. The first went up in 1783 as the British left; the present pole, the sixth, was raised in 1946 and still carries the 1783 weathervane (Forgotten New York). The Brooklyn Reporter gives its height as 106 feet.
  • The el. West End platforms at 62nd Street opened June 24, 1916; the elevated line over New Utrecht Avenue, 86th Street and Stillwell Avenue was built under the 1913 Dual Contracts. The Sea Beach (N) station below opened June 22, 1915 and has been on the National Register since 2005. Four elevators opened at the complex on July 19, 2019.
  • Street work. The city has a roughly $24 million capital project to rebuild New Utrecht Avenue from 65th to 77th Streets, where Brooklyn Paper reported 69 crashes and 80 injuries over five years (February 2023); a separate June 2026 proposal to Community Board 11 covers New Utrecht and 17th Avenues from 78th to 81st Streets.
  • Police and board. The neighborhood falls under the NYPD’s 62nd Precinct at 1925 Bath Avenue and Brooklyn Community Board 11; north of about 60th Street the avenue enters Borough Park and the 66th Precinct.

Building Specialists

Access Control for New Utrecht’s Architecture

The six building types below cover nearly every door we are asked about between the 62nd Street station and the old town center. They were built at different times for different people, and each one asks for something different from a reader, a lock and the cable that joins them.

Two-story shop rows under the el

Shops at street level with one or two floors of apartments above, attached on both sides, reached through a narrow door between storefronts. The most common entrance on New Utrecht Avenue itself, and the one this page spends the most time on.

Three- and four-story walk-ups over stores

Slightly taller mixed-use buildings on the avenue corners and on 18th Avenue, with a vestibule big enough for an outer and an inner door and a stair that serves six to twelve apartments.

Wedge and corner buildings

Where the diagonal avenue crosses 16th, 17th and 18th Avenues and the numbered streets, lots end in sharp points. These buildings often have two street faces, short vestibules and entrances right at the corner where pedestrians gather.

Early twentieth-century apartment houses

Brick apartment buildings like those Forgotten New York pointed out at 17th Avenue and 78th Street, built for tenants of middle-class means, with a front door on the side street, a courtyard or areaway, and a cellar door for the super.

Attached brick houses near the old center

Long rows of attached brick houses with oriel windows, stoops and, on some, their original iron railings, on 17th Avenue between 81st and 84th Streets and nearby. Many are one- or two-family homes; some have a basement unit.

Former showrooms and halls

Large ground-floor rooms built for furniture showrooms and the catering halls that once gave a block near 76th Street the nickname “Wedding Row.” Several are now stores or offices with a front door on the avenue and a fire exit to a side street.

What the materials mean for the install

Narrow piers. Between two storefronts the masonry pier beside an apartment door can be little wider than the door frame itself. On many of these doors there is no room for a surface reader on the wall, so we look at three other places: a slim mullion reader on the frame, a reader set through the door’s glass lite or sidelight, or a reader inside a vestibule if the street door is left on its closer and latch. Each changes what the visitor sees and where the cable runs; the schematic below shows the usual arrangement.

Old frames. Apartment doors on the avenue have often been rehung more than once. Before a strike goes in we check that the frame is solid, the hinges are tight and the door pulls itself shut and latches without being pushed. If the door does not latch on its own, no lock will make it secure.

Shared roof spaces. In a two-story row the space under the roof may run over the neighbors. We do not put controllers, power supplies or cable in it, and we do not route through a neighboring building’s ceiling, even when it would be shorter.

Under the Steel

Entry Systems for Doors Under the D Train

A door under the el lives in a different environment from a door one block away. We plan for three things at every New Utrecht Avenue entrance before choosing a single part.

Noise: answer the door by sight

The AJPH study of New York transit noise put the mean on MTA subway platforms at 81.1 dBA, and its loudest single platform reading was 90.2 dBA. A street door sits below the platform and the track rather than on it, but anyone who has stood at a door on the avenue while a train passes knows the call is gone. One Reddit user who lived beside the D on New Utrecht Avenue described a kitchen window that looked straight out onto the platform. An old audio-only buzzer cannot work in that.

So we design the call to be answered by sight. A video door station shows the caller to the tenant on an indoor screen and in a phone app at the same moment. The station itself shows the visitor a light and a line of text when the call is ringing and when the door is open, so a delivery driver does not have to hear the click of the strike. Voice still works between trains, and good stations suppress steady noise, but nobody depends on it.

Light: the station has to see a face in the shade

The structure shades the sidewalk for most of the day, with bright bands where light comes through between the tracks. A camera door station facing that mix shows a dark figure against a bright street. We choose stations with wide dynamic range, angle them so the brightest patch of sidewalk is not directly behind the visitor, and where the owner agrees, add a small, shielded light over the door that switches on with the call. It helps the camera and it helps the visitor find the right button.

Signal: assume the wireless link will be poor

A lot of steel, a stair hall and two storefronts sit between an avenue door and the apartment router. Cellular and Wi-Fi door stations do work on some of these doors, but we test the signal at the door during the survey and plan a cable back to the building whenever the reading is marginal. A wired station also gets its power from the cable, which takes one more thing off the list of what can fail.

Repairs and upgrades to the call side of the system are handled by the same team as intercom installation in New Utrecht, so the door station, the indoor screens and the reader can be specified together.

Crowds on a Timetable

Stations, a Large High School and the Old Church Grounds

New Utrecht’s busiest doors are busy at predictable times. Planning around those times is cheaper than buying heavier hardware for the whole day.

The 62nd Street transfer

The 62nd Street/New Utrecht Avenue complex joins the elevated West End line to the Sea Beach line in its open cut below, where Bath Junction once connected the two railroads. Its main station house is at the southeast corner of 62nd Street and New Utrecht Avenue, and a second fare control at the north end serves 60th Street. With about 1.2 million riders a year, the doors around it see a steady stream at rush hours. For street doors here we size the closer and hinges for a heavy-use door and keep the reader out of the path of people walking to the stairs.

71st Street, 79th Street and 18th Avenue

Further south the D stops at 71st Street near Lt. Joseph Petrosino Park, a 0.87-acre park at New Utrecht Avenue and 70th Street, and at 79th Street, the stop listed for New Utrecht High School, before turning onto 86th Street for the 18th Avenue station. The 79th Street area is where the city’s June 2026 proposal describes a “bowtie” intersection with more than thirty possible vehicle movements, missing crosswalks and a slip lane from 17th Avenue, and where it found that young people made up 21 percent of injuries against a borough average of 9 percent.

New Utrecht High School

The school opened in 1915, moved to 80th Street and 16th Avenue in November 1924, and is now one of the city’s largest high schools. For a residential door nearby, the school day creates two short, intense periods: arrival and dismissal. A vestibule with an outer door that is open and an inner door that is locked becomes a waiting room at those times unless the inner door re-latches every time and someone notices when it does not. We set door-held alerts tighter during those bands and leave the rest of the day alone. The church grounds and the cemetery

The Friends of Historic New Utrecht hold events on the church grounds at 18th Avenue and 84th Street and in the old cemetery on 16th Avenue; in 2025 Liberty Weekend ran from 11 a.m. to 4 p.m. on Saturday, May 31, with a cemetery tour the next day.

The city’s curb work on the avenue

Two city projects touch the doors on New Utrecht Avenue. The first is the capital reconstruction of the stretch from 65th to 77th Streets, which Brooklyn Paper reported in February 2023 at about $24 million after the Department of Transportation decided milling and repaving were not enough; construction was then expected to start in 2026, with Council Member Justin Brannan pushing for sooner. The second is the June 2026 proposal to Community Board 11 for New Utrecht and 17th Avenues between 78th and 81st Streets: closing the slip lane, adding a pedestrian island at 79th Street, new signals at 78th and 81st Streets, new crosswalks, curb extensions and a wider sidewalk triangle.

The Department of Transportation describes full street reconstruction as replacing more than a foot below the surface and usually rebuilding curbs and sidewalks too. For a building owner that means three practical things. Do not set a gate post, bollard or reader pedestal in the sidewalk shortly before the city digs it up. Expect the sidewalk level at your door to be reset, and plan to have the door bottom and threshold checked afterward. And keep any conduit you need inside your own property line, because the sidewalk itself belongs to the city and work in it needs city approval.

Rules that apply to New Utrecht doors

These are the rules we most often have to account for on New Utrecht jobs. They are summaries for planning, not legal advice; the building’s architect, engineer or lawyer has the final word on how they apply to a specific door.

  • Free exit. The NYC Building Code’s sections on electrically locked egress doors (1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9) cover sensor-release and request-to-exit doors: a person inside must always be able to leave, release must happen on loss of power and on fire alarm where required, and the manual release must be within reach. On the narrow stair doors of the avenue we prefer a strike with mechanical exit, which leaves free exit to the door’s own hardware.
  • Multiple dwellings. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires a self-closing, self-locking entrance door and an intercom or buzzer in many older multiple dwellings, which describes most walk-ups over stores on the avenue. An access system has to keep those features working rather than replace them.
  • Tenant data. The Tenant Data Privacy Act (Local Law 63 of 2021) sets rules for owners of multiple dwellings who use electronic or smart access: what data may be collected, how long it is kept, and a written policy for tenants. HPD’s page on the law is the starting point.
  • The sidewalk. City rules require a Department of Transportation permit for sidewalk work over 25 square feet, and structures placed in or under the public sidewalk generally need a revocable consent. We keep posts and conduit on private property unless an approval is in hand.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in New Utrecht: Questions Answered

These questions come from the doors described on this page: apartment entrances between shops under the el, corner buildings on the diagonal avenue, buildings near the stations and the high school, and owners and shopkeepers along the avenue. Each answer starts with the short version.

Apartments over the avenue’s shops

Our apartments are reached through a narrow street door squeezed between two shops under the el. Where does a reader fit when there are only a few inches of wall?

Usually on the door frame, through the door’s glass, or just inside it, rather than on the wall. A pier a few inches wide will not take a standard reader without overhanging a shop’s glass or sign.

We look at three positions in order. A slim mullion reader fits on the latch side of the frame itself and needs only a small hole into the frame for its cable. If the door has a glass lite, some readers can read a fob through glass, so the reader sits inside, out of the weather and out of reach. If there is a vestibule, the street door can stay on its closer and latch while the reader and lock go on the inner door, where there is more wall.

Whichever we choose, the cable runs up the stair wall to a closet on the landing or the second floor, not sideways into either shop. The visitor still needs to see where to present a fob or press a call button, so we add a small label or a lit symbol if the reader is not obvious.

Trains pass overhead every few minutes. How does a caller at the street door talk to a tenant when the train drowns out both ends of the call?

They don’t have to talk. The call should be answered by sight. A video door station shows the caller to the tenant and shows the visitor, by a light and a line of text, that the call is ringing and that the door has opened.

In practice the station rings an indoor screen in the apartment and the tenant’s phone app at the same moment. The tenant sees who is there and taps to open. The visitor sees “door open” and hears nothing they need to hear. Voice still works between trains, and better stations filter steady noise, but the system no longer depends on it.

If the building keeps its old handsets for tenants who prefer them, we can often run both on the same door during a changeover. What we avoid is a new audio-only panel on the avenue; it repeats the problem that made the owner call.

An older tenant on our top floor can’t get down the stairs quickly. What is the easiest way for her to let a visitor in without a smartphone?

An indoor screen on the wall with one large open button. It rings like a doorbell, shows the caller and opens the street door with one press; no app, no password.

We mount it where she already spends her day, at a comfortable height and near a chair, rather than by the apartment door. Screens with a loud ring and a flashing light help when the television or the train is loud. If a family member manages her visitors, the same call can also ring that relative’s phone, and either of them can open the door.

Our roof space runs over the stores next door. Where should the controller and lock power supply sit so trouble in a neighboring store doesn’t take our doors down too?

Inside your own building, in a closet or on a landing wall, never in the shared roof space or a shop’s ceiling.

In an attached two-story row the space under the roof may connect over several buildings. A leak, a fire or a contractor working next door can reach equipment there without anyone in your building knowing. When a fire began in a restaurant on New Utrecht Avenue in May 2022, ABC7 reported that it spread through the ceilings of two adjoining buildings. We would rather run an extra twenty feet of cable inside your walls than leave a controller where you cannot see it.

The controller and its battery-backed power supply go in a locked metal enclosure on a wall you control, the cable stays inside your building, and the lock is set so that if the power fails the door still opens freely from inside. If the building has a fire alarm, we tie the release into it where the code requires.

Under the steel and on the corners

It stays dim under the el even at midday. Will a camera door station show a usable picture of whoever is at our door, and what light should we add?

Yes, if the station is chosen and aimed for the shade, and usually with a small light over the door.

The problem is contrast more than darkness. Under the structure a visitor stands in shade with bright strips of sky and street behind them, and an ordinary camera turns them into a silhouette. We choose stations with wide dynamic range, tilt them so the brightest patch of sidewalk is not in the middle of the picture, and test them at the survey at the times the door is used most.

A small, shielded light above the door, switched on by the call or by a motion sensor, makes the biggest difference. It is cheap, it helps the visitor find the right button, and it does not spill into the windows upstairs if it is aimed down.

Will a door station on a cellular or Wi-Fi link work right under the steel structure, or does it need a cable back to the building?

Sometimes it will, but we plan for a cable and use wireless only if the signal test passes.

A street door on the avenue has steel overhead, masonry piers on both sides and often two shops between it and the apartment router. During the survey we measure the cellular and Wi-Fi signal at the door itself. If it is strong and steady, a wireless station can save a cable run. If it is marginal, the station will drop calls at exactly the moments people complain about.

A cabled station also takes its power over the same cable, so there is no battery to change and one less thing to fail. In most walk-ups the run is short, up the stair wall to the second floor, and costs less than repeated service calls to a wireless unit that keeps losing its connection.

School, station and busy days

We are a short walk from New Utrecht High School and the 79th Street station. Students wait in our vestibule at dismissal. Can the inner door tighten up during those hours without slowing residents down?

Yes. The inner door stays locked all day; what changes during dismissal is how quickly the system notices a door held open.

Residents still enter with a fob or phone in a second, exactly as at any other hour. During the arrival and dismissal bands we shorten the door-held alert, so a door propped by a backpack sends a notice to the super within seconds rather than minutes, and a small sign in the vestibule asks people to wait outside. The rest of the day the alert goes back to its normal setting so nobody is pestered.

We do not lock the outer door against the street during school hours unless the building already does so; residents’ own children and visitors need to reach the call station. The times are set from the school calendar and a week of watching the door, not guessed.

Our building faces the 62nd Street station complex. Should our street-door hardware be heavier-duty than a typical walk-up’s?

For the closer, hinges and strike, yes. For the reader, not necessarily.

A street door beside a busy station is opened far more often than a typical walk-up door, and it is pushed and pulled by people in a hurry. That wears closers, loosens hinges and knocks strikes out of line. We use a heavy-duty closer with a back-check, ball-bearing hinges if the old ones are worn, and a strike rated for frequent use and fitted so the latch drops fully into it.

The reader itself does not wear with use the same way. What matters is where it sits: out of the path of people heading to the stairs, at a height that is hard to kick or hit with a bag, and in a housing that resists stickers and graffiti. We revisit the door after a few weeks of use, because a busy door shows its adjustments quickly.

Owners and businesses along the avenue

When a storefront tenant moves out, what should the landlord recover from the door system before the next tenant moves in?

The administrator login, every credential the old tenant issued and a clear line between the shop’s doors and the building’s.

Many shop tenants on the avenue install their own keypad or reader on the back door. When they leave, the landlord should get the installer’s name, the administrator account or a factory reset, and any codes or fobs that still open the door. If the reader is tied to the tenant’s phone account, it has to be removed from that account before anyone else can manage it.

Check, too, whether the shop’s cable passes through the building’s stair or cellar and whether any of the shop’s equipment opens a residential door. We often find a stock-room keypad wired to the same power supply as the apartment door, which means a shop tenant’s electrician can switch off the tenants’ door. We separate those when we take over the building.

We are buying a mixed-use building on New Utrecht Avenue. What should we ask the seller about the existing entry system before closing?

Who installed it, who holds the administrator account, what credentials exist and whether the hardware is still supported.

Ask for the installer’s name and invoice, the brand and model of the controller and readers, and written confirmation that the administrator account or cloud subscription will be transferred to you at closing. Ask for a list of every active credential, which tenant or shop holds it and what it opens. Ask whether any door is on a lock that has to release on fire alarm and when that was last tested.

If the seller cannot answer, plan on a takeover survey before or soon after closing. Either way, every credential should be reissued the week you take over.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in New Utrecht

From our build environment we could not open Google’s AI Overview results for New Utrecht searches, so nothing below quotes them. These are general claims that keep turning up in quick online answers, consumer guides and national cost pages such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, checked against what New Utrecht doors actually need.

“A video doorbell is all a small building needs.”

Quick online answers often treat a video doorbell as the modern replacement for a building intercom. For a single house it can be. For a walk-up over a New Utrecht Avenue shop it misses the point: several households share the street door, each needs its own call, and the door has to be unlocked electrically for a visitor.

A doorbell rings whoever installed the app. It does not open the street door unless someone has wired a strike to it, it has no list of who may enter, and it has no log of who did. Its microphone and speaker face the same train noise that defeated the old buzzer.

What a small building needs is a multi-tenant door station or a reader plus a call panel, with one credential list. That can be as small as one door and six names, and on our published Brooklyn pricing a single controlled door starts at $1,500.

“Wireless is easier than running cable.”

It is easier on the day of installation. Many consumer guides recommend wireless door stations and locks because they avoid fishing cable through old walls.

Under the el, wireless trades installation effort for reliability. Steel overhead, masonry piers and two shops between the door and the router make a weak link likely, and a door station that drops calls is worse than none. Battery-powered locks need changing on a schedule that a landlord with tenants upstairs rarely keeps.

In a walk-up the cable run is usually short: up the stair wall to a landing closet. We test the wireless signal at the door during the survey and use it when it passes. When it does not, the cable is the cheaper choice within a year.

“Magnetic locks are the simplest lock for any door.”

Maglocks are simple to mount and appear in many generic install guides. On a narrow apartment door that opens onto a stair they are rarely the best choice.

A magnetic lock releases whenever its power is cut and needs a sensor or button to let people out, so it brings its own code requirements for free exit and fire alarm release. On a door that a hundred people pass in rush hour, a sensor that misreads can leave the door unlocked or, worse, slow someone leaving.

An electric strike with the door’s own lever for exit keeps free exit mechanical, works on narrow frames and stays locked if the power fails, while the door still opens from inside. We use maglocks where they are the right tool, typically on glass doors with no room for a strike.

“Access control prices are about the same everywhere.”

National cost pages publish a single average per door, and those numbers are often repeated as if they apply in Brooklyn. They mix house locks, office doors and commercial buildings, and they rarely include the electric strike, the power supply or the cable.

In New Utrecht the price is driven by things a national average cannot see: whether the door is under the el and needs a video station, whether the cable can go up the stair or has to cross a shop, whether the frame needs repair first, and whether the job has to be done before the shop opens.

Our published Brooklyn figures, which New Utrecht pays without any surcharge, run from $1,500 to $2,500 for a single controlled door with reader, strike, wiring and ten fobs. The written price after the survey is the one that counts.

“Face recognition at the door is the modern standard.”

Some product marketing and AI summaries present face recognition as the obvious next step for entry. For residential buildings in New York City it brings legal and practical problems.

The Tenant Data Privacy Act limits what owners of multiple dwellings may do with biometric data collected by smart access systems and requires a written policy, consent and data limits. Under the el, the lighting that defeats ordinary cameras also defeats face matching, and a door that fails to recognize a tenant in the shade is a door that gets propped.

Fobs, cards and phone credentials are cheaper, work in any light and are easy to cancel. We install biometric readers only for commercial spaces where the owner has a clear reason and the paperwork in place.

“Install it and forget it.”

Many quick answers end at installation day. A New Utrecht street door is a moving, slammed, rained-on object, and the system around it holds a list of people that changes every time a tenant moves.

Closers drift, strikes go out of line and batteries age. Tenants move out and their fobs keep working unless someone cancels them. Shop tenants change and leave their codes behind.

We hand over a short checklist: test the door after a power cut, check that it latches on its own, and review the credential list when anyone moves. On the busiest doors we come back after a few weeks to adjust the closer and strike, because that is when the problems show.

“A neighborhood’s crime rate tells you what system to buy.”

Some automated advice starts from crime statistics and recommends heavier systems for higher numbers. Address-level crime scores for New Utrecht Avenue are published by data sites, but they say little about a particular door.

What decides the right system is the door itself: who shares it, how often it is used, what is beside it, and what goes wrong now. A door near the 62nd Street station needs heavy hardware because of use, not because of a statistic. A door near the high school needs a tighter held-door alert at two times of day.

We start every quote from the door and the people who use it, and we would rather sell a well-adjusted strike and closer than an expensive system on a door that does not latch.

Field Notes: Watch the Door Close Before You Price It

This describes how we survey busy street doors in general, not a particular customer. Before we measure anything at a door on a busy avenue, we stand across the sidewalk for a few minutes and watch it work. How many people go through? Does it close behind each of them, or does it stop an inch short and sit there? Does someone push it shut, or prop it with a foot while they talk? Does the latch click, or does the door rattle in the frame when a train goes over?

Five minutes of watching tells us more than the owner’s description, because owners stop seeing a door they pass every day. A door that does not finish its swing will defeat any reader, and a door that bangs open every time someone leaves will knock any strike out of line within a month. So the first line of most of our quotes on avenues like this is not a reader. It is a closer, a hinge or a strike adjustment.

FAQ

New Utrecht Access Control FAQ

Can the electrical equipment of the subway interfere with fob readers mounted near the tracks?
+

It is rare, and we test for it rather than assume. Fob and card readers work at 13.56 MHz or 125 kHz over a few inches, and a reader on a street door is far enough from the track power and signal equipment that interference almost never shows. What we do see under the el is noise on long, poorly shielded cables and power supplies sharing a circuit with a shop compressor. During commissioning we test read range with several fobs and phones while trains pass, and if it drops we fix the cable or the power rather than blame the trains.

Can you do the installation before the shops downstairs open, so we do not block their entrances?
+

Yes. On the avenue we often start early so drilling and cable work at the street door is finished before the stores beside it open, then do the inside work on the stair and landing during the day. Where the cable has to pass through a shop, we arrange that part with the shop tenant for a time they choose. The quote says plainly if a job needs two visits because of shop hours, and there is no surcharge for working around them.

The only cable route from our street door to the closet runs through the ceiling of the store on the ground floor. How do you handle that?
+

With the shop tenant’s written agreement and at a time they choose. We explain the route, the size of the holes and how long it will take, lift only the ceiling tiles we need, and leave a labeled pull string for future work. The cable is run in its own sleeve or conduit through the shop so it is clearly the building’s and not the shop’s. If the tenant will not agree, we look for another path up the stair, even if it is longer, rather than leave the building dependent on a store’s ceiling.

Our old buzzer wiring seems to run into the building next door. What should be done with it?
+

Attached rows on the avenue were often wired decades ago with whatever path was easiest, and we regularly find old buzzer or bell wires that pass through a party wall or a shared roof space. We trace the wire, confirm what it still powers, and cut and cap it at your side of the wall once nothing of yours depends on it. We do not enter or alter the neighbor’s building; if their side needs work, we tell you so you can tell them.

Do you work for the Reformed Church, the high school or the library nearby?
+

We mention them on this page only to help people find their way around the neighborhood. None of them is a client, and we do not claim any work on their property. Institutions like these have their own procurement rules and contractors. Our work in New Utrecht is for private owners: landlords, homeowners, shopkeepers and offices.

Our street door is steel with a glass panel and a mail slot. Can a reader go through the door rather than beside it?
+

Often, yes. Some readers can read a fob through glass, so the reader can sit inside the glass panel, out of the weather and out of reach, with its cable running down to the hinge side through a power transfer to the frame. The mail slot stays as it is. Whether it works depends on the type of glass and any wire mesh in it, so we test read range at the survey before promising it. If it does not read well, a slim reader on the frame is the fallback.

Our super looks after several small buildings along the avenue. Can one login manage all of them?
+

Yes, if the buildings are on the same cloud platform. Each building stays a separate site with its own doors, tenants and logs, but one administrator account can see and manage them all, and the super can be given rights that stop short of deleting a building or changing who the owners are. Mixing brands across buildings makes this harder, which is one reason owners with several buildings often standardize when the oldest system is replaced.

Can the log tell us whether the street door was left unlatched overnight?
+

Only if the door has a door-position switch, which we fit as standard on street doors. The switch tells the system whether the door is actually closed, separate from whether it was unlocked. With it, the log shows every time the door was held open longer than the set time and every time it was opened without a valid credential, and the system can send an alert at the moment it happens. Without it, a log shows only who used a fob, which is not the same thing.

If the sidewalk in front of our door is rebuilt higher or lower, what happens to the door and its lock?
+

The door may stop closing properly. A new sidewalk that sits even slightly higher at the threshold can stop the door from swinging fully shut or make it drag, and a lower one can open a gap under it. Either way the latch may no longer catch the strike. After the street work is finished, have the door checked: we adjust the closer and strike, and if the threshold itself needs changing, we tell you before any work is done so you can decide.
