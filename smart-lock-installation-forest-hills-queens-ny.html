The Neighborhood

Why Forest Hills Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Looks Approved and Acts Like the Last Barrier

Forest Hills was planned twice before most of it was built, and both plans still govern its front doors. In 1906 the Cord Meyer Development Company bought farmland here and renamed it after nearby Forest Park, laying out the detached-house blocks north of what became Queens Boulevard. Two years later Margaret Olivia Sage’s foundation bought 142 acres south of the railroad from Cord Meyer, and Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. and the architect Grosvenor Atterbury designed Forest Hills Gardens around it: curving private streets meant to discourage through traffic, a town square at the station, and Tudor-style houses with stucco walls and red clay tile roofs, most of them put up between 1910 and 1917. A third layer arrived with the subway: a late-1920s zoning change allowed tall apartment buildings ahead of the line, and the Queens Boulevard stations opened in 1936. Almost everything a lock installer meets in Forest Hills belongs to one of those three plans.

The Gardens plan reached further than street layout. A Declaration of Restrictions, quoted as Declaration No. 3 in the Gardens Corporation’s own guidelines, says that no building, fence or wall may be put up and no “change or alteration made therein” until plans showing its “nature, kind, shape, height, material, color-scheme and location” have been approved in writing. More than a century later the Forest Hills Gardens Corporation still runs that review through an Architecture Committee, and its published guidelines are specific about doors: entry doors of panel type, usually wood; replacement doors that replicate the original; consistency across row and attached houses; and exterior hardware “of a type appropriate to the style of the building” with finishes that match the rest of the house. A smart lock is a small object, but on a Gardens door it is exterior hardware, and owners who skip that thought are the ones who end up taking a keypad off again.

North of the tracks the rules come from a different place. The apartment belt along Queens Boulevard, 108th Street, Yellowstone Boulevard and the streets between them is overwhelmingly co-op: one reply on r/ForestHills put it plainly, observing that most apartment buildings in the neighbourhood, especially above Yellowstone and Queens Boulevard, are co-ops. In a co-op the unit door sits on the corporation’s corridor, the proprietary lease usually requires a duplicate of any new key, and the city’s fire rules expect the door to close and latch on its own. The owner chooses the lock; the board, the house rules and the building code decide what the hallway sees and what must keep working. Our job on those doors is to make the corridor side unremarkable and the inside side do the real work.

Then there is what stands in front of those doors. QNS reported in October 2025 that a man walked in through the front door of an apartment building near 112th Street and 73rd Avenue at about a quarter past four on a Tuesday afternoon, was caught on camera sitting on a couch in the lobby, walked toward an apartment and left with a $600 phone, escaping through a courtyard toward Queens Boulevard. The same report said the 112th Precinct had recorded 114 burglaries through October 5, 2025, up 44.3 percent on the same point a year earlier. In the Gardens the shared space is the street itself: the Corporation’s nearly 4,000 members own their roads, and on more than 30 concert days in 2025 those roads carried the crowds walking to and from Forest Hills Stadium. A door in Forest Hills has to assume that the first barrier — the lobby, the courtyard, the quiet private street — is already behind whoever is standing at it.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Forest Hills’s Architecture

The same lock behaves differently in each of the neighbourhood’s building types, because the wall around the door, the people who share the space in front of it and the rules on its outside face all change from one block to the next.

Atterbury Concrete-Panel Houses

The early Gardens houses were built from precast panels set by crane. A doorway in that wall is framed by concrete, so strike reinforcement, bridge placement and any doorbell mounting are planned around concrete rather than timber.

Gardens Tudors in Brick and Stucco

Later Gardens houses in brick and stucco, often with leaded or divided-light glass. The Brownstoner listing for a 1928 Tudor on Greenway North describes leaded glass windows throughout — a reminder that glass is everywhere near the lock.

Attached and Row Groups

Groups of houses in the Gardens whose entry doors were designed to match. The guidelines call that consistency “a significant architectural feature” that “must be preserved”, so a lock on one door is judged against its neighbours.

Station Square and the Inn Apartments

The co-op apartment houses around Station Square and Continental Avenue, including the Forest Hills Inn, which opened on May 1, 1912. Unit doors on old corridors, under house rules and a board.

Pre-War Elevator Co-ops

Six-storey buildings of the early 1930s along 108th Street and the side streets — CityRealty lists 68-10 108th Street, built in 1931, with 50 apartments on 6 floors. Steel-clad or heavy wood unit doors, mortise pockets and self-closers.

Post-War Towers on the Boulevard

Large rental and co-op towers such as Parker Towers at 104-20 Queens Boulevard, which The Real Deal described in 2018 as 1,327 units in three 20-storey buildings. Hollow-metal doors, long corridors and a building-run lobby system.

Forest Hills Co-op Houses

The three towers at 108th Street and 62nd Drive whose 1972 plan was cut from 840 to 432 apartments under Mario Cuomo’s compromise, as the Queens Daily Eagle has recounted. The development’s own management rules come before any unit door change.

Cord Meyer Detached Houses

Single-family houses on the north side, where Wikipedia notes many older homes have been torn down and replaced by larger ones. One block can hold a 1920s wood door and a new oversized fibreglass entry.

Austin Street Mixed-Use

Low-rise buildings with shops on the ground floor and offices or flats above, along the mile of Austin Street between Yellowstone Boulevard and Ascan Avenue. Glass shop doors, narrow stair doors and rear service doors.

Doors set in concrete rather than timber

On a frame house, a strike is made strong by driving long screws through the jamb into the wall stud behind it. In an Atterbury house the “stud” is a cast panel. A 1996 paper in the ACSA international proceedings on Atterbury’s system describes the wall panels as hollow, with a skin of concrete on each face separated by an air space, and ribbed for strength. That changes three things. The jamb is usually fixed to the concrete with anchors rather than nails, so we check how firmly it is held before trusting any strike to it. Reinforcement goes into the jamb and, where the opening allows, into the solid edge of the panel with masonry anchors sized for hollow material — never by drilling blind into the panel face. And because the cavity is concrete, not air and drywall, radio signal from the lock to a bridge or router drops faster than owners expect, so the bridge is placed and tested before the lock is paired.

Doors that are mostly glass

The Gardens Corporation’s own guidelines note that many original entry doors in the Gardens are all glass with divided lights. On a door like that the lock rail is a narrow strip of wood between the hinges and the panes, and a thumb-turn sits within arm’s reach of anyone willing to break a small pane. We do not solve that with an inside key: on a dwelling, a lock that needs a key or a code to get out is a way to trap people in a fire, and we decline to fit one. What we do instead is choose a lock body narrow enough for the rail, consider whether the most exposed pane can be glazed with laminated glass (a glazier’s job, and a Committee question if it changes the look), set auto-lock so the bolt is thrown every time, and make the door report its state so that nobody has to wonder whether it was left on the latch.

Corridor doors in co-ops and towers

An apartment door on a shared corridor in a building of three or more apartments is meant to be self-closing under the city’s rules, and HPD’s fire-safety guidance says plainly that double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors and that apartment doors should open from inside without a key. The corridor side is the building’s face, so a board may care what a keypad looks like even when the lock itself is the shareholder’s. A motorised bolt on a door pulled shut by a strong closer has to throw against a door that is already pressed against its stop; we adjust the closer and the strike so that the bolt never fights the door, and we confirm the door still latches on its own from fully open after the lock is fitted.

Owners of Gardens houses who are planning exterior lighting or cameras at the same time should know that the guidelines treat security lighting as exterior work too; we price those alongside the lock through our Queens security camera installation team so that a single application can cover everything that will touch the facade.

Commercial Smart Locks

Business Smart Lock Installation in Forest Hills

Forest Hills’s businesses sit in a handful of corridors, each with its own kind of door and its own way of being got into. The police reports for the neighbourhood are a useful reminder that the front glass is rarely where it happens: Patch reported in April 2021 that a burglar cut a metal bar and came through the back window of Forest Hills Fishery at 71-64 Austin Street, a shop then more than fifty years in the neighbourhood, and took $2,500 in cash; ABC7 reported in March 2015 that thieves had got into a store called Rainbow on Austin Street through its skylight during a run of break-ins at businesses on Queens Boulevard and 63rd Drive.

Austin Street

The neighbourhood’s main shopping street, about a mile from Yellowstone Boulevard to Ascan Avenue. Shop entrances with glass doors and aluminium frames, upstairs offices reached by narrow street doors, and rear doors and windows onto service areas that matter as much as the front.

Station Square and 71st Avenue

The brick square beside the Long Island Rail Road station, which the Gardens Corporation treats as a no-parking, no-tolerance zone, and the shops and offices on Continental Avenue between the station and the Boulevard.

Queens Boulevard

Restaurants, banks, clinics and offices in the ground floors of the apartment belt. Many share a building entrance with residents, so a business’s staff codes must stay separate from the co-op’s own door system.

108th Street

The long commercial street running north from Queens Boulevard toward East Elmhurst, lined with shops and offices on the ground floors of apartment buildings; small premises where the owner is often the one opening and closing every day.

Metropolitan Avenue

The southern edge of the neighbourhood, which Wikipedia notes was long known for antique shops; small storefronts with rear storage rooms where an audit trail on the back door is worth more than a new front lock.

Professional Suites

Doctors, dentists, therapists and accountants in converted apartments and ground-floor suites along the Boulevard and around 71st Avenue, where a records room or a medication cupboard needs its own credential.

Rules that come with a business door

  • The way out stays mechanical. Staff and customers must be able to leave by turning a handle or pushing a bar, whatever state the electronics are in. Keypads and readers control the way in only.
  • Shared entrances belong to the building. Where a shop and the apartments above share a street door, the building owner or co-op decides how that door locks; the business gets its own credential, not the master.
  • Glass doors need a strike or a lock made for them. A residential smart deadbolt will not fit a narrow aluminium stile; those doors take an electric strike or an exit-device solution priced from our access-control lines.
  • Back doors and windows first. The local reports above all involved the rear or the roof. A door contact on the back door and a reminder that it is open at closing time will often do more than anything on the street side.

When a Forest Hills business outgrows one or two keypads — a practice with several rooms, a shop with a stock room and an office — we move it to a small controller-based system through our access control installation service in Queens, so every door shares one schedule and one log.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Forest Hills: Questions Answered

These are the questions Forest Hills owners and shareholders raise that are particular to the neighbourhood — the Gardens’ rules, concrete walls, concert evenings and co-op ownership. General questions about prices, coverage and visits are in the FAQ further down.

The Gardens

Does a keypad deadbolt count as exterior work that the Gardens Corporation’s Architecture Committee has to approve?

Treat it as exterior work unless the Corporation tells you otherwise. Its website reminds residents that all exterior work, new or restoration, requires an application, and its guidelines put door hardware in the same paragraph as mailboxes and light fixtures: it must be “of a type appropriate to the style of the building”, with finishes that match or suit the rest of the house. A keypad on the outside of a front door is visible from the street, so it falls squarely inside that language. We are not the Corporation and cannot tell you how the Committee will rule on a particular house; what we can do is make the application easy to judge.

For a Gardens job we photograph the door as it is, mark where the outside unit would sit, and give you the manufacturer’s drawing and finish name for the lock we propose, plus a second option that leaves the outside of the door exactly as it is — an inside-only retrofit over your existing deadbolt. The guidelines ask for applications to be lodged ahead of the Committee’s scheduled meeting, so it pays to start that conversation before you buy anything. If the retrofit route is chosen, the outside of the house does not change at all, which is the simplest application there is.

Our house is one of an attached group whose front doors are meant to match — can ours go keyless if the neighbours’ doors stay as they are?

Usually yes, but the matching is the point to plan around. The Corporation’s guidelines say that in row and attached houses and groups of homes, consistency in the design of entry doors is a significant architectural feature that must be preserved. A lock is not the door, yet a large black keypad on one door of a matched row changes how the group reads from the street far more than the same keypad on a detached house would.

Three approaches tend to work on attached Gardens houses. The first is the inside-only retrofit, which keeps the existing key cylinder and outside trim, so your door still matches its neighbours. The second is a keyed smart deadbolt with a small outside escutcheon in a finish close to the hardware already on the row. The third is to keep the matched front door mechanical and put the keypad on a rear or side door that the street does not see. Whichever you choose, we show the Committee the whole row in the photographs, not only your door, so the question it has to answer is plain.

Is a steel security door in front of our Gardens entrance worth it, or does the money do more in the lock and the frame?

In most Gardens houses the money does more behind the existing door. The guidelines ask homeowners not to propose heavily ornamented security doors and to choose simple designs compatible with the architecture, so an outer steel door is an application in its own right, and on a glass-panel entry it hides the very door the Committee is trying to protect. What a security door mainly adds is resistance to someone breaking glass to reach the lock.

A cheaper and less visible order of work is: make the jamb and strike hold (on a concrete-panel or masonry opening that means checking how the jamb is anchored), fit a lock whose bolt is thrown automatically every time the door closes, add a door-position sensor so the house reports an open or unlatched door, and talk to a glazier about laminated glass in the pane nearest the lock. If, after that, you still want an outer door, it can be added later without undoing anything we fitted. We will not fit an inside-keyed lock in place of any of this.

Concrete, masonry and the frame

Our door frame sits in a concrete wall with no wooden stud behind it — how does the strike get anchored?

By treating the frame and the wall as two separate jobs. First we find out how the wooden jamb is held to the concrete: on old Gardens houses it may be screwed to wood blocking cast into the opening, fixed with masonry anchors, or simply wedged and nailed to whatever was there in 1912. If the jamb moves when the door is shut hard, no strike screw will hold, so the jamb is secured to the concrete first with anchors made for the material.

Then the strike. We use a reinforced strike box and the longest screws the jamb itself will take, and, where the opening has solid concrete behind the strike rather than the hollow middle of a panel, sleeve anchors through the jamb into that solid edge. Where it is hollow, we rely on the secured jamb and a longer strike plate that spreads the load. What we never do is drill blind into the face of a hollow precast panel hoping to hit something solid. The result is checked the same way on every door: the motor has to throw the bolt fully, the bolt has to sit centred in the strike, and the door must not rattle once it is locked.

Concert season

On Forest Hills Stadium concert nights thousands of people walk past our house — what should the locks be set to do on those evenings?

Treat a show night like a night away, even if you are home. The 2025 lawsuit coverage described crowds of around 13,000 people on more than 30 concert days, private Gardens streets closed to cars, and residents who spent concert nights elsewhere; QNS reported 20 events planned for the 2026 season, from June 6 to September 29. Most of those visitors are simply going to a concert. The issue is that for a few hours the street is full of people nobody on the block knows, and a door left on the latch by someone carrying in shopping is easy to miss.

On houses near the walking routes we usually set auto-lock to a short delay so the bolt is thrown within a minute of the door closing, turn on alerts for any door left open or unlatched, and pause service codes — cleaners, dog walkers, contractors — for show evenings using the season’s published show dates. A video doorbell lets you answer anyone who comes up the path without opening the door. None of this needs a separate “concert mode”; it is a schedule you set once in the spring and switch off after the last show.

Co-op apartments on the Boulevard

When we sell our co-op apartment, does the smart lock go with us, stay for the buyer or belong to the corporation?

That is decided by your contract of sale, your proprietary lease and the building’s house rules, not by us, and boards differ. In practice, most sellers treat a lock that has been fitted to the door as something that stays, in the same way a door handle stays, and say so in the contract; others take an inside-only retrofit with them and refit the original thumb-turn. If your board asked for its own key or code when the lock went in, it will expect that arrangement to continue for the next owner.

Whatever you decide, the lock has to change hands cleanly. Before closing we can remove every code, fingerprint and phone key, take the lock out of your app account so the buyer can register it in theirs, re-key the cylinder so your old keys stop working, and leave the manual and the factory-reset steps for the buyer. If you are taking the lock with you, we put back a plain deadbolt or thumb-turn that matches the corridor, so the door you hand over is complete. Either visit is a setup-only appointment unless new hardware is needed.

Can the board or the managing agent see our lock’s activity log if the lock is our own?

Not unless you give them access. A smart lock you own reports to the account you register it to, and the entry history lives in that account and on the manufacturer’s service. Nothing in the way we set up a unit lock sends that history to the building. If the board holds a key or a code for emergencies, its use of that code will appear in your log like anyone else’s, which is one reason some shareholders prefer to give the building a code rather than a key.

Building-run systems are different. Where a co-op or a rental owner runs a lobby or unit system of its own, the building operates the account, and in New York City the Tenant Data Privacy Act limits what an owner of a multiple dwelling may collect through a smart access system and how long authentication data may be kept. That law is written about rental buildings’ systems, so if a board asks to see a log from a lock you own, read your proprietary lease and house rules, and take advice if there is a dispute behind the request. We can show you how to export or delete your own history.

Hardware Notes

What We Check Before a Lock Goes on a Forest Hills Door

The lobby is not a lock

Many Forest Hills shareholders tell us their building is safe because the lobby door locks and a neighbour is usually around. The daylight burglary QNS described in October 2025 — a man who came through a building’s front door, sat on the lobby couch and then walked toward an apartment — is the reason we plan every unit door as if the lobby were open. In practice that means a lock that throws its bolt on its own every time the door closes, an alert when it does not, and an override key that is not hanging on a hook by the door.

Signal through concrete and plaster on masonry

Concrete-panel houses, brick co-ops with plaster on masonry, and concrete towers all eat radio signal. Before pairing anything we stand at the door with a phone and check what it sees; if the bridge cannot sit within the maker’s recommended distance of the lock with a clear line, we move it, add a plug-in repeater or a Thread device, or choose a lock that does not need the internet for its everyday work. The lock’s own keypad and fingerprint reader keep working without any network at all.

Finish and shape on the street side

Many older houses in the Gardens wear dark, aged metal hardware. Few smart locks are sold in those exact finishes, so we show owners the nearest real finishes on the actual door, in daylight, before ordering. Rounded, compact outside units generally sit better on a Tudor door than tall rectangular touchscreens.

Thick doors and deep reveals

Old wood doors in masonry openings are often thicker than the range a lock is listed for, and the reveal around the door can be deep enough that a bulky inside housing hits the trim when the door swings. We measure thickness, backset and the clearance through the full swing with a paper template taped to the door, and change the model rather than shave the door.

Batteries in unheated vestibules

Some Forest Hills houses and older apartment buildings have unheated porches or vestibules, and batteries lose capacity in the cold. Schlage, for example, lists a narrower operating range for the inside part of its Encode Plus than for the outside lock body. We place inside housings in heated space where possible, set low-battery alerts to go to two phones, and leave the household with a note of which batteries the lock takes.

Four Ways to Make a Glass-Panel Gardens Door Keyless

Whichever route is chosen, two settings matter most on a glass door: auto-lock, so that the bolt is never left withdrawn after someone comes in with their hands full, and a door-position alert, so that a door resting against its stop without latching is reported rather than discovered. On many of these doors the inside thumb-turn is the only thing within reach of a broken pane; a lock that is always thrown and always reporting makes that reach far less useful, while the person inside still leaves with one turn.

We are often asked whether a deadbolt that can only be opened from inside with a key would solve the glass problem. It would create a worse one. HPD’s fire-safety guidance treats double-cylinder locks on apartment doors as illegal precisely because people must be able to get out without hunting for a key, and we apply the same principle to every house we work in.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Forest Hills

We could not read Google’s AI Overview directly from our research tools, so the claims below are the ones that cost guides, retailers and lock makers publish and that AI summaries tend to repeat. Each is set against what a Forest Hills door actually involves.

“A smart lock costs about $300 installed”

Fixr’s January 2025 guide puts a single smart lock installed at $250 to $400 nationally, with $300 as the average, and that is a fair figure for one ordinary bored door in a timber-framed house with nothing to fix and nobody to ask.

Forest Hills adds steps the national figure leaves out: a Gardens application with photos and product sheets, a jamb anchored to concrete rather than a stud, a narrow rail between glass panes, or a co-op’s contractor paperwork and permitted hours.

That is why we quote house and apartment doors flat after a free walkthrough instead of publishing a single number, and why the business-door prices we do publish are for complete keypad-and-strike entrances at the Queens rate.

“Installing one takes a screwdriver and half an hour”

Product reviews often say a retrofit or a keypad deadbolt goes on with a single screwdriver in about thirty minutes, and on a modern pre-bored door that is often true.

On a ninety-year-old door in a masonry opening, the half hour starts after the door has been made to close sweetly, the strike has been moved to meet the bolt, and the jamb has been fixed to the wall it has worked loose from.

The screwdriver part is quick. Deciding whether the door is fit for a motor, and fixing it if not, is the work that makes the lock reliable a year later.

“Any smart lock fits any door”

Retail listings stress universal fit, and most locks do fit the common American bore and backset. Forest Hills has many doors that are not common: divided-light Gardens doors with a slender lock rail, thick old slabs, and pre-war co-op doors with mortise locks in the edge.

A home-automation forum thread that has run since 2014 shows owners of metal doors with mortise locks still looking for a true drop-in smart replacement; the answers are a separate deadbolt, a cylinder change or a commercial mortise lock.

We measure first and recommend a model second. Where nothing fits well, we say so rather than cut a door to suit a box.

“Wi-Fi locks work anywhere in the house”

Marketing copy implies a lock talks to the router from any room. Lock makers’ own support pages are more careful: August asks for its Wi-Fi bridge to be within 10 to 15 feet of the lock and to have full Wi-Fi bars where it is plugged in.

Atterbury’s concrete panels, brick co-op walls and tower slabs all shorten that range. A bridge that works on the kitchen counter can fail two rooms away from a Gardens front door.

We test signal at the door and at the bridge location before pairing, and use Thread devices or a repeater where the walls demand it.

“Anyone can change the locks on their own home”

That is close to true for a detached house on the north side. It is not the whole story in the Gardens, where the Declaration and the Corporation’s guidelines reach exterior hardware, or in a co-op, where the proprietary lease and house rules govern the unit door.

In a rental, New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law lets a tenant add a lock of their own beside the owner’s lock, but requires a duplicate key on request; it does not let a tenant remove the owner’s lock.

We ask who else has a say in the door before we quote, and we put the answer in writing.

“A smart lock makes a building secure”

Summaries often present a smart lock as building security. A unit lock secures one door; it does nothing about a lobby door that a stranger can follow a resident through, a courtyard gate, or a basement package room.

One r/ForestHills post described two people coming into a building’s basement at 3:30 in the morning and taking everyone’s packages a few days before Christmas. That is a building-entrance problem, owned by the building.

Where we are asked by a board or owner, we price the shared door separately from the unit doors so that each problem is solved by the party that owns it.

“Keyless means no keys at all”

Some reviews recommend key-free models with no cylinder. In a Forest Hills co-op the board usually expects a key or a working code for emergencies, and in a house a dead battery or jammed motor still needs a way in that does not depend on electronics.

We generally fit keyed models, keep one override key in a safe place away from the door, and give the household a short written note of how to get in if the lock ever goes dark.

Key-free models are possible on some doors where the owner understands the trade-off; we explain it before fitting one, and never on a door that is the only way out.

North of the Boulevard

Rebuilt Houses and Oversized Doors on the Cord Meyer Streets

On the north side the owner sets the rules, and the doors are newer and larger. The doors that come with those rebuilds raise their own questions, none of them about approvals.

  • Tall entry doors with long pull handles. Many modern entry sets put the handle and the deadbolt in one long plate, which rules out a standard smart deadbolt. We look for a motorised option made for that hardware, or use an inside-only unit on the existing deadbolt.

Co-op Unit Doors

A Checklist for Smart Locks on Forest Hills Co-op Doors

Most of the neighbourhood’s apartment doors are co-op doors. These are the points we confirm with the shareholder, and where needed the managing agent, before a co-op unit door is touched.

  • The alteration paperwork. Whether the building treats a lock change as an alteration needing an agreement or simply a notice, and whether it wants a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the managing agent before the visit.
  • The building’s key. Proprietary leases commonly require a duplicate of any new key so the building can enter in an emergency; the Cooperator has written about a Manhattan court ordering a shareholder to hand one over. We set up the override key, or a code, for the building as the board requires.
  • The corridor face. Whether the board wants the hallway side to match other doors — which may point to an inside-only retrofit or a keyed lock with a small escutcheon.
  • The closer. Doors to interior corridors in larger residential buildings must be self-closing under the city’s Local Law 111 of 2018. We leave the closer working and adjusted, and confirm the bolt throws cleanly against it.
  • The way out. No double-cylinder or inside-coded lock on an apartment door; HPD’s guidance is that such locks are illegal on apartment doors.
  • Contractor hours and the elevator. Permitted working hours, elevator padding and where tools may be left, so the visit fits the building’s rules.
  • Peephole and chain. Whether the new inside housing clears the existing door viewer and chain guard, which stay on the door.

None of this is legal advice; it is the order in which we ask the questions so that a lock goes onto a co-op door once, and stays.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Doors Set in Concrete and Masonry

The routine our installers follow on a door hung in a concrete or masonry opening — how we work on doors like these, not an account of a particular house

We start with the door closed and a hand flat on the jamb beside the strike, while someone pulls and pushes the door from the other side. If the jamb moves, that is the job; the lock comes later.

Next we take the strike off and look at what the old screws were biting into. Short screws in crumbling wood blocking tell us the jamb has been held by habit for decades. We find solid concrete with a small pilot hole at the edge of the opening, never in the middle of a panel, and anchor the jamb before the new strike goes back.

Then the signal. A phone at the door, a phone at the nearest outlet, and the maker’s app open on both. If the bridge cannot see the lock from where it would live, we move it before we pair anything, because a lock that pairs on day one and drops in week two is the call nobody wants.

Last, the dark test: lights off, door locked, and someone who has never seen the lock opens it from inside by feel. If that takes more than one movement, we change something.
