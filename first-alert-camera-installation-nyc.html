The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need First Alert Camera Installation

New York City is a city of shared doors, and that is the first thing a camera plan here has to deal with. The 2023 New York City Housing and Vacancy Survey counted about 3.7 million homes, roughly two-thirds of them rentals, and about one million of them rent stabilized. The same survey found 829,200 units, 22 percent of the city's housing, in buildings with 100 or more apartments. In a place like that, the door you want to watch is usually not yours alone. The lobby belongs to the owner, the hallway outside your apartment is common space, the stoop of a brownstone may be shared with the tenant upstairs, and the sidewalk in front of a store is public. A First Alert camera bought with the best intentions can end up on the wrong side of a lease, a board rule or a landmark regulation if nobody asks those questions first. That is why our first conversation with a New York caller is about who controls the wall, and only then about which camera.

The second thing is age. The survey's initial findings put 2,017,000 units, 54 percent of the city's housing, in buildings built before 1947, and another 960,200, about 26 percent, in buildings from 1947 to 1974, so four homes in five are in buildings older than the early 1970s. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council describes a housing stock that "ranges from mansions in Riverdale to small homes in Midland Beach, garden apartments in Sunnyside to high-rises near Central Park, brownstones to tenements." Almost none of those buildings were designed with a camera in mind. Tenements went up fast between 1860 and 1910, according to the Architectural League, on lots usually twenty-five feet wide, with brick party walls and narrow stairs. Pre-war elevator houses have thick plaster, steel and masonry floors and riser closets packed with decades of telephone, cable and intercom wire. When a First Alert camera needs power or a network cable, every one of those materials changes the job.

The third thing is the product itself. First Alert is a name most New Yorkers know from smoke detectors, but the cameras sold under that name today are part of the First Alert VX Series, which Resideo describes as its AI video line built to work with its smart home products, covering a video doorbell and indoor and outdoor cameras. Resideo's professional page says installers can power them with AC, Ethernet, Wi-Fi or PoE and that they integrate with ProSeries and VISTA alarm panels. FirstAlert.com presents its systems as professionally installed, with cameras, video doorbells and monitoring, and points shoppers to a local expert. In other words, this is a dealer-installed line, not a box you pick up at the hardware store and stick to a window. The choice between Wi-Fi, PoE and AC power, and how the cameras talk to an alarm panel you may already own, is exactly the part an installer is for.

The fourth thing is what people are actually trying to stop. Security.org's annual package theft report found apartment residents more than three times as likely as single-family residents to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent, and noted that, depending on landlord rules, apartment residents may not be allowed to put doorbell or security cameras outside their units. The New York Attorney General's package theft tips recommend a doorbell camera or video surveillance system to deter thieves or help police identify them. Local reporting shows what that looks like on the ground. News 12 reported in December 2025 on a Morris Park resident whose package sat outside for seconds before a thief took it, all caught on his camera, and the same report mentioned a State Assembly bill that would make package theft a felony punishable by up to five years. CBS New York covered thieves cutting open packages inside a Park Slope lobby. A delivery company's blog summarized Reddit accounts of an Astoria package taken within thirty minutes of delivery and a Williamsburg resident whose footage pointed at the delivery staff. These are the moments a camera is bought for.

The fifth thing is the law around privacy, which in New York is unusually specific. New York makes it a felony to record an in-person conversation you are not part of without the consent of at least one person in it, according to the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, and Connextivity notes that nobody has cleanly settled whether a camera passively picking up hallway audio counts as eavesdropping, which is why audio is usually left off. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, puts rules on smart access systems in apartment buildings, and its definition of authentication data, as CaseGuard explains it, leaves out footage from a camera that watches an entrance without granting entry. The Landmarks Preservation Commission protects 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, and its rules govern cameras on landmarked facades. A good First Alert installation in this city is one that does its job and also keeps the owner, the tenant and the board out of trouble.

The last thing is tension between neighbors, which cameras can ease or inflame. In 2019 tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers, a 700-unit rent-stabilized complex of two brick high-rises in Brownsville, objected when the owner sought state approval for a facial-recognition entry system; the Guardian noted that the buildings already had cameras watching the grounds, doorways, elevators and halls. More recently, a West Village tenant was arrested for allegedly smashing a neighbor's doorbell camera. We take both stories as a reminder that where a lens points matters as much as what it records. When we place a First Alert camera, we show the owner exactly what it sees, we use privacy zones where the hardware supports them, and we steer away from views into a neighbor's door or window.

We also want to be clear about what our research did not turn up. We could not open live Reddit threads, Google's People Also Ask boxes, AnswerThePublic or Yelp reviews about First Alert installers specifically in New York City from our build environment, and we found no city data set of 311 complaints, HPD violations or DOB permits about cameras. Where this page talks about problems in New York buildings, it relies on the published sources named in the stamp above and on the kinds of walls, risers and doors we find in buildings of these ages generally. It does not claim a survey of your building. That is what the site visit is for.

New York City facts that shape a First Alert camera job

  • Old buildings: 54 percent of units are in buildings finished before 1947 and about four in five predate 1974, according to the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey.
  • Mostly renters: about two-thirds of the city's roughly 3.7 million homes are rentals, so a landlord's or board's consent decides much of what can be mounted and drilled.
  • Small buildings count too: one- and two-family buildings hold 26 percent of the city's units, more than any other building size, and those are the homes where an owner can usually wire a doorbell and outdoor cameras freely.
  • Big buildings concentrate risk: 829,200 units sit in buildings of 100 or more apartments, where one lobby, one mailroom and one package shelf serve hundreds of households.
  • Doors are regulated: HPD's self-closing door report for fiscal 2025 counted 2,937 self-closing door violations, so anything fixed to an apartment door or frame must leave the closer alone.
  • Landmarks are everywhere: more than 35,000 landmarked properties sit in 139 historic districts, by one architect's count, and most exterior changes there need LPC review.

What New York owners often pair with cameras. Once a First Alert doorbell or lobby camera is in, the next request in multi-family buildings is usually the entry system itself: a buzzer or intercom that actually works, because a camera that shows a stranger at the door is only half useful if the tenant upstairs cannot speak to them or refuse entry. In houses, owners often add the alarm panel side of First Alert, since the cameras are designed to work with ProSeries and VISTA panels. Tell us at booking if either is on your list so one visit covers the cable routes for both.

The First Alert Name in 2026: Smoke Alarms, VX Cameras and the Company Behind Both

Many people who call us about First Alert cameras are surprised by how the brand is put together, and the confusion causes real setup problems, so it is worth a few paragraphs. The short version: First Alert is now a Resideo brand. Its camera line is the VX Series. Its older smart smoke and carbon monoxide products run on a separate app called Onelink. Knowing which one you have, and who installed it, saves a lot of time on the day.

Who owns First Alert now

Resideo announced on February 7, 2022 that it had agreed to buy First Alert from Newell Brands, according to the press release Resideo filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission. Resideo describes itself as working with about 100,000 professional installers and taking part in more than 15 million installations a year, and it lists Honeywell Home, First Alert and BRK among its brands. On July 30, 2025, Resideo announced that it planned to spin off its distribution arm, ADI Global Distribution, as a separate public company while keeping the products business. We could not confirm from our sources whether that spin-off has been completed, so we do not say either way. For a New York customer, the practical point is that First Alert cameras, Honeywell Home thermostats and the VISTA and ProSeries alarm panels sit inside the same company, which is why the cameras are designed to talk to those panels.

What the VX Series includes

Resideo's professional page describes the VX Series as AI video built to work with its smart home products, made up of a video doorbell plus indoor and outdoor cameras. It names four power and connection options for installers: AC, Ethernet, Wi-Fi and PoE. For the VX5 indoor camera, it lists a 2-megapixel sensor with wide dynamic range, the ability to tell people from pets, and an automatic privacy shutter. We repeat only what Resideo publishes and do not add specifications for models we have not seen documented. Before we quote, we confirm the exact model you want, whether we can supply it or install hardware you already own, and how it will be powered in your building.

Onelink is not the camera app

The Onelink Home app by First Alert and BRK is the app for Onelink smart smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and the Onelink Environment Monitor, according to First Alert's support pages. On the Apple App Store it carried a 1.5-star rating from 306 reviews when our research was done. It is not the app for VX cameras. We have seen people search for help with "the First Alert app" and land on Onelink troubleshooting when their real problem is a camera, and the other way round. If you are not sure which you have, send us a photo of the device and the app's icon and we will tell you before we book.

Why the power choice matters more here than in the suburbs

In a suburban house with drywall and a garage, Wi-Fi cameras are often the simplest answer. In a city apartment building, the same choice can be fragile. Masonry walls and steel-and-arch floors weaken Wi-Fi, dozens of neighboring networks crowd the same channels, and New York authorities warned in December 2025, as Spectrum News reported, that some package thieves use Wi-Fi jammers to disrupt doorbell camera footage. A camera that loses its connection at the moment it is needed is worse than no camera, because you think you are covered. That is why, for anything watching a lobby door, a package shelf or a storefront, we lean toward PoE where the building allows a cable, and reserve Wi-Fi for indoor spots close to the router or for renters who cannot drill. We will explain the trade-off for your building plainly rather than defaulting to the cheapest option.

Decide what the camera is for

The most useful thing you can tell us is the job you want the camera to do. "Catch the face of whoever takes packages off the lobby shelf" needs a camera at face height, close to the shelf, with light on the face, which may mean a different spot from "see who rings the bell." "Know when the kids get home" is an indoor camera aimed at the entry, with the privacy shutter set for the hours you are home. "Watch the register and the front door of the store" is two views, not one. When we know the job, we can tell you whether one First Alert camera does it or whether you need two, and we can say so honestly before you spend money on the wrong position.

Think about what the lens will see that is not yours

A camera on a brownstone stoop also sees the sidewalk. A camera at the end of a hallway also sees the neighbor's door opening. A camera in a shared backyard sees the tenant downstairs having dinner. Connextivity's guidance is that hallway cameras are on firmer ground when positioned to cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open, and that a camera installed covertly inside a unit can support a felony charge under Penal Law 250.45. We aim to cover your door, your packages and your space, and we will suggest a narrower angle, a privacy zone or a different spot when a view reaches somewhere it should not.

Building Specialists

First Alert Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

The city's building types each change three things about a First Alert camera job: how the camera gets power and data, what it is fixed into, and who has to say yes. These are the types the housing survey, CHPC and the city's own guidance describe, and how we handle each one.

Brownstones and Rowhouses

Brick or brownstone fronts, plaster inside, stoop-level and garden-level entries. Old bell wiring may or may not exist. On landmarked blocks, the front facade is the hardest place to add anything, so we look at the areaway, the vestibule and the rear first.

Walk-Up Tenements

Four- to six-story walk-ups on narrow lots, the type the Architectural League dates mostly to 1860 to 1910. Brick party walls, narrow stairs, a small vestibule with mailboxes. Cable routes are tight, so a clean riser or hallway raceway path decides the plan.

Pre-War Elevator Buildings and Co-ops

Eight- to twelve-story elevator houses from the 1910s and 1920s and larger doorman buildings. Thick plaster, masonry floors, riser closets, boards with alteration rules and contractor hours.

Post-War and Large Rental Buildings

Buildings from 1947 to 1974 and newer towers, often with block corridors, drop ceilings in lobbies and an existing camera system. Integration and placement matter more than drilling.

One- and Two-Family Houses

Over a quarter of the city's units, from Midland Beach to the Bronx. Wood frame or brick, basements and attics for cable, and an owner who can approve everything, which makes doorbells and outdoor cameras straightforward.

Garden Apartments and Planned Complexes

Courtyard buildings like the Jackson Heights garden apartments and large complexes like Parkchester and Co-op City. Shared grounds, management rules, long walks between doors and the equipment room.

On brownstones and bell wiring. A video doorbell needs steady power. In a house built before electric doorbells were common, or one that has been rewired several times, the old bell wire may be missing, broken inside the wall, or connected to a transformer too weak for a camera doorbell. One New York electrician's guide notes that brownstones bring plaster walls, original wiring and a stoop-level entry that make placement and wiring trickier. We test what is there before choosing a doorbell power plan. If a new transformer or any line-voltage work is needed, that part goes to a licensed electrician and the quote says so. If the facade is landmarked, the doorbell's position and finish also need to respect LPC rules, which may push it into the vestibule or onto the door surround rather than the stone face.

On walk-ups and party walls. In a tenement, the building's vestibule is often barely wider than the door, with mailboxes on one wall and the intercom panel on the other. That is where packages land and where a camera does the most good, but there is little room and the walls are brick. We mount a camera high in the corner of the vestibule or just inside the inner door, angled to see faces coming in rather than the street, and run its cable in a surface raceway up the stair hall or through the cellar to wherever the switch can live. A city rule on intercoms, 1 RCNY §42-01, sets a push-button panel no higher than six feet and the speaking device between four and five feet from the floor; we keep cameras clear of that panel so it stays usable and compliant.

Material and method by wall type

Brick is the most common thing a New York camera is fixed into, and old brick varies. Some is hard and rings when drilled; some crumbles. We drill test holes, look at the dust, and set anchors in the face of the brick rather than the mortar joint wherever the camera base allows, because mortar holds less. Brownstone is softer still and stains easily, so we keep penetrations few and small and seal them. Plaster on masonry inside a pre-war building is fixed through into the masonry behind it; plaster on wood lath in a house is fixed into studs, located with a probe hole hidden behind the camera base. Block corridors in post-war buildings take sleeve anchors. Drywall on metal studs takes toggles rated for the weight. Outdoors, every hole that goes through a wall is sealed, because water following a camera cable into a wall is one of the slow kinds of damage that shows up years later.

Borough by Borough

Brooklyn to Staten Island: How First Alert Camera Jobs Differ Across the Five Boroughs

This is the city hub page, so it is worth saying how the work changes as you cross a bridge. On January 1, 1898, Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island joined as the City of Greater New York after a referendum that passed 64,744 to 64,467, according to the city's archaeology timeline, and the subway that opened in 1904 tied them together. But each borough kept its own building habits, and those habits show up in every camera install. Our borough price pages carry the exact figures; here is what changes on the ground.

Brooklyn

Brooklyn is rowhouse country. The Brooklyn Heights Historic District, designated on November 23, 1965, was the city's first historic district, according to the Historic Districts Council, and blocks of nineteenth-century brownstones run from there through Park Slope, Fort Greene and beyond. That means many Brooklyn doorbell and front-camera jobs start with a landmark check and a look at old bell wiring. Brooklyn also has large rental complexes such as Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville, where entry technology has been a public dispute. A Brooklyn tenant's legal question about putting a camera on an apartment door facing the hallway is one of the questions we answer further down this page. Brooklyn is our base-rate borough; see the First Alert camera installation prices for Brooklyn.

Manhattan

Manhattan concentrates the extremes: Lower East Side and East Village streets still lined with five-story pre-law and six-story Old Law tenements, and pre-war co-ops like the Dakota, built from 1880 to 1884 to Henry J. Hardenbergh's design, made a co-op in 1961 and a city landmark in 1969. What a storefront camera has to survive in the city

A camera in a shop is up against things a home camera never sees. Doors open hundreds of times a day, letting in cold and grit. Roll-down gates come down in front of the glass every night, which can block a camera mounted inside the window. Deliveries arrive at the back door at hours when nobody is watching. And bright sidewalks behind a dim shop interior make faces at the door hard to read, which is where a sensor with wide dynamic range, like the one Resideo lists for the VX5 indoor camera, helps. A property manager quoted by Brick Underground made a further point: face masks make people hard to identify on camera, so position and light matter more than resolution. We plan business cameras view by view with the owner, decide which ones are on PoE so a weak Wi-Fi signal or a jammer cannot knock them out, and schedule drilling for the hours the shop is closed.

Gates themselves are in flux. A 2009 city law required storefront roll-down gates to be at least 70 percent transparent by July 1, 2026, but NY1 reported on July 2, 2026 that the City Council had unanimously passed a bill repealing that deadline, so a compliant gate would be required only when a gate is replaced for other reasons, with the bill going to the mayor. If you are replacing a gate anyway, the camera can often be planned to see through a transparent gate at night, which is worth thinking about before both are installed.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Installer licensing. The NYC Business portal says a license from the New York State Department of State is needed to install or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and 19 NYCRR 195.2(b), as Kirschenbaum Esq. explains it, covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. A First Alert camera tied to a ProSeries or VISTA alarm panel is alarm work by any reading. Ask any installer for their license number; ours is NYS license #12000287431.
  • Facial recognition means signs. The city's biometric identifier law requires covered commercial establishments that collect biometric data, such as through facial recognition software, to post a clear sign at every customer entrance, at least 8.5 by 11 inches under the city rule. Ordinary CCTV without that kind of analysis appears to be exempt, Kennedys Law notes. If a business turns on any face-matching feature, the sign goes up the same day.
  • Low-voltage versus line-voltage. Under NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024 and effective December 21, 2025, an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits when done by a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person as the electrical code defines it. A new outlet for a camera is line-voltage work and goes to a licensed electrician with a DOB permit.
  • Landmarked storefronts. LPC rules list security cameras under Section 2-20, and the Permit Guidebook's health and safety chapter covers how they can be installed, including domes painted to match the facade. One published LPC approval covered nine ground-floor cameras, five on a west facade and three on an east facade. We check landmark status before planning any exterior camera.
  • Sharing with police is optional. 311 describes a program that lets any business with a security camera share live video with the NYPD during emergencies, and businesses can register camera locations through NewYorkCityConnect.org. Registration alone does not give police a live view. Whether to join is the owner's decision.
  • Insurers, not the city, often drive cameras. Seneca Security notes there is no general city law mandating cameras in apartment buildings, and that many commercial property insurers set camera coverage requirements for theft and vandalism claims. Ask your broker what your policy expects before you finalize positions.
Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check Before a Camera Goes on a City Door

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's building unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow at the kind of door New York is full of: a walk-up vestibule, a brownstone stoop or an apartment door off a long hallway.

We stand where the person ringing the bell would stand and look at the light, because a face lit from behind by the street is a silhouette on any camera. We open the door and watch the closer swing it shut, and we note where the closer arm, the latch and the strike plate sit, so nothing we add touches them. We check what the lens would see past the door: a neighbor's entrance, a window across the hall, the sidewalk. We run a speed test at the exact spot the camera would sit, not in the living room. We look for any old camera, intercom or bell wire that might be reused, and test it before trusting it. We ask who owns the wall in front of us and whether anyone has said yes in writing. Only then do we hold the camera up, show the owner the view on their own phone, and agree the position. In buildings this old, that ten minutes is what separates a camera that helps from one that causes an argument.

Your Questions Answered

First Alert Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Licensing and permits

Does the person putting up a First Alert camera in New York City need a state license?

Sources disagree, so here is what each says. ButterflyMX writes that a Security or Fire Alarm Installer License from the New York State Department of State is needed to install cameras legally in the city. City Smart Systems argues that alarm work needs the license but camera-only work falls under low-voltage rules. The state rule, 19 NYCRR 195.2(b), as Kirschenbaum Esq. explains it, covers a CCTV system used full-time or part-time to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft, movement, sound or fire, and the Department of State has treated even a video intercom as a small CCTV system. Because First Alert cameras are designed to integrate with ProSeries and VISTA alarm panels, a First Alert job is very often alarm work. We do it under NYS license #12000287431, and we suggest you ask any installer for their number before they start.

Is a camera job in a store or office licensed differently from one in a home?

The state license question is about what the system does, not whether the building is a home or a business; the same detection-and-monitoring test applies. What changes in commercial space is everything around the license. Landlords and managing agents more often ask for a certificate of insurance before work starts. The city's biometric identifier law adds a signage duty if the business uses facial recognition software. Commercial insurers may set camera requirements. And a business is more likely to need new power at a high spot, which is line-voltage electrical work for a licensed electrician, not part of a low-voltage camera license. We sort those pieces out on the quote so the business owner knows who does what.

Does the Buildings Department need to issue a permit before First Alert cameras go in?

We found no Department of Buildings rule specific to low-voltage cameras, and one installer's claim that every install is permitted reads as marketing rather than regulation. What we can point to is Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024 and in effect from December 21, 2025, which says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to run under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the electrical code defines it. PoE camera cabling is that kind of wiring. A new outlet or hard-wired line-voltage supply is not, and it needs a licensed electrician who files with DOB. Landmarked exteriors are a separate approval from LPC.

Apartments, hallways and landlords

I rent in Brooklyn. Can I put a camera on my own apartment door that looks out into the public hallway?

This is genuinely unsettled, and the sources split. Connextivity says no New York State or City law stops a resident from putting a camera on their own apartment door or says how it must be aimed. A JustAnswer response to a Brooklyn tenant says cameras in common areas need the landlord's permission and the landlord can remove one installed without consent. In practice, the safest route is the one several sources point to: a camera that replaces or sits on your own door hardware, such as a peephole-style unit, rather than anything fixed to the hallway wall or ceiling, plus a word with the landlord in writing. Keep the view on your doorway rather than the neighbor's, and leave audio off. We will not mount anything on common-area surfaces without the owner's consent.

What about a freestanding camera on a shelf or stand in the hallway outside my door?

An answer on AskALawyerOnCall says a tenant cannot install cameras outside the apartment because the hallway is not under the tenant's control, and suggests a peephole camera mounted on the tenant's door instead. A freestanding unit avoids drilling, but it still occupies common space, can block a corridor that must stay clear for fire safety, and is easy to knock over or take. For those reasons we do not recommend it and will not place one in a common hallway. If what you want is to see who comes to your door and what happens to deliveries, a camera on or just inside your own door does the job without arguing over the corridor.

My landlord put a camera in the hallway pointed straight at my door. Is that allowed?

Owners generally may put cameras in common areas, and Seneca Security notes there is no city law mandating them either way. The limits are about what the camera sees and how the footage is used. Connextivity's guidance is that hallway cameras are on firm ground when they cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open. In a building with a smart access system, the Tenant Data Privacy Act limits how the owner may use data the system collects, and the law may not be used to harass tenants. A camera with audio raises New York's eavesdropping law. If you think a camera looks into your home rather than the hall, raise it with the owner in writing first; a JustAnswer thread on exactly this question is a good place to see how lawyers frame it. We are installers, not lawyers, and this is not legal advice.

Can a landlord install a camera inside my rented apartment?

City Smart Systems describes landlord cameras inside a rented apartment's bedrooms or living rooms as generally illegal in the city, and Connextivity notes that a camera installed covertly inside a unit can support a charge under Penal Law 250.45, a felony. The cameras an owner can reasonably install are in common areas, not inside the home you rent. If you find a device inside your unit that you did not put there and that the landlord did not disclose, do not tamper with it; photograph it and get legal advice. We only install cameras inside an apartment for the person who lives there or, in an empty unit between tenancies, at the owner's request for that period.

Audio, privacy and smart entry

Can my First Alert doorbell or camera legally record sound in New York?

New York is a one-party-consent state for recording conversations: according to the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, recording an in-person conversation you are not part of, without the consent of at least one person in it, is a felony. A doorbell that records you talking to a courier is one thing. A camera in a hallway or lobby passively picking up neighbors' conversations is another, and Connextivity notes nobody has cleanly settled whether that counts as eavesdropping. That is why we set audio recording off on common-area and hallway cameras by default, and leave the choice to you on a camera inside your own home or at your own door where you are the one talking. Video and audio are treated separately in the law, so turning off the microphone does not turn off the camera's usefulness.

Our building lets residents in with an app and fobs. Do extra city rules apply to the cameras?

The Tenant Data Privacy Act, Local Law 63 of 2021, covers Class A multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as fobs, cards, apps or biometrics. It took effect on July 29, 2021, with owners of existing smart access buildings liable from January 1, 2023, according to Thomson Reuters Practical Law. Authentication data must be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, and HPD says owners may not use the data for anything other than granting access and monitoring entrances, exits and common areas. The important line for cameras, as CaseGuard explains it, is that authentication data does not include data from a video system that monitors an entrance without granting entry. So a First Alert camera that just watches the lobby door is outside that definition. A camera wired to unlock the door, or to recognize faces for entry, is the kind of system the law was written for, and that is a different project with its own rules.

Landmarks

Our brownstone is in a historic district. Can a First Alert camera go on the front facade at all?

Possibly, but the front is the hardest place. LPC's 2018 rules amendment proposed that cameras and similar equipment would be barred from a primary facade unless there is no workable alternative or an agency requires that location, and that installations avoid damaging significant architectural features and keep the object as small as possible. LPC's Permit Guidebook, in its chapter on health and safety equipment, covers security cameras under its rules, including domes painted to match the facade, and LPC staff can approve installations that meet the criteria without a full hearing. Most exterior changes in historic districts need LPC review, and 311 can confirm a building's status. In practice we look first at the vestibule, the areaway, the door surround and the rear facade, and only plan a primary-facade camera once LPC's route is clear.

Which power option makes sense in a brick rowhouse compared with a high-rise apartment?

In a rowhouse, the owner usually controls the cellar, so a network cable can run from the router down through the cellar ceiling and up to a rear-yard or front camera, which makes Ethernet or PoE the steady choice for outside cameras, with Wi-Fi fine for indoor cameras on the parlor floor. In a high-rise apartment, a tenant usually cannot run cable through walls or common areas, so indoor cameras on Wi-Fi or AC near the router are the realistic option, and anything covering the lobby or hallways is the building's PoE system. A Linked Security cost guide lists distance to the recorder, floors to cross and wall material as the main labor drivers, and those decide the answer as much as the camera does. We test the Wi-Fi at each spot before recommending wireless.

Packages and police

Are apartment dwellers really more likely to lose packages, and what can a camera actually do about it?

Security.org's annual report found apartment residents were more than three times as likely as single-family residents to have had a package stolen in the past three months, 10 percent against 3 percent. A camera does two things about that: it discourages the casual thief who sees it, and it records the face and time when theft happens anyway, which is what the New York Attorney General's tips point to. What a camera cannot do is stop a determined thief in a lobby with no package room. That is why, in apartment buildings, the best results come from a camera aimed at the package shelf at face height combined with the building's own measures, such as a locked package room or a working entry door. A Gowanus Houses participatory budgeting proposal asked for exactly that kind of coverage near elevators and apartment doors.

If I register my First Alert cameras with the NYPD, can police watch them live?

Not through registration alone. NewYorkCityConnect explains that registering cameras does not give the NYPD live access; it tells investigators a camera exists so they can ask you for footage, and registration is free. Separately, 311 describes a program that lets businesses with security cameras share live video with the NYPD during emergencies, which is an opt-in arrangement. A homeowner who registers keeps control of whether to hand over a clip. We can help you find and export a clip quickly when police ask, so the decision is yours rather than a technical scramble.

What moves the price of a city First Alert job

  • Power and data method. A Wi-Fi indoor camera near the router is the quickest job. Each PoE cable pulled through walls, floors or a riser adds labor, and the longer and more obstructed the route, the more it adds.
  • Distance and floors. The Linked Security cost guide names distance to the recorder and floors to cross as main drivers, and in a six-story walk-up the camera in the vestibule and the switch in the cellar can be far apart.
  • Wall material. Brick, brownstone and concrete panels take more time than drywall; every exterior penetration is sealed.
  • Reusable cable. Good existing coax or network cable can cut labor a lot, so we test what is there first.
  • Panel integration. Adding cameras alongside a ProSeries or VISTA panel is its own line of work.
  • Paperwork and access. LPC review on landmarked facades, board requirements and limited contractor hours add time, and we tell you before booking when they apply.
  • Electrical work by others. A new outlet or line-voltage supply is done by a licensed electrician and is not part of our camera price; the quote names it.

FAQ

NYC First Alert Camera Installation FAQ

What decides the price of a professionally installed First Alert camera setup in the city?
+

Mostly three things: the borough, how each camera gets power and data, and how far the cable has to travel through brick, plaster and floors. A single Wi-Fi indoor camera beside the router is the shortest job, while PoE cameras pulled up a riser to a lobby and a rear yard take longer. Manhattan carries our highest borough rate and Brooklyn and the Bronx our base rate. Each borough's First Alert price page lists the lines, and your quote names every one before work starts.

Can I have a First Alert camera in a rental without drilling into the landlord's walls?
+

Yes, in most cases. An indoor camera can sit on a shelf or furniture inside your apartment, powered from an outlet and connected over Wi-Fi, aimed at your entry. One city installer's guidance is that drilling inside a rental needs the landlord's written approval, so we keep renter installs to surfaces you control. Anything on a common-area wall or the building's exterior needs the owner's consent first.

The buzzer at my building door hasn't worked for weeks and the landlord ignores it. What can I do?
+

Report it to the landlord in writing, and if nothing happens, file a complaint with 311, which is HPD's route for maintenance issues. Buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have an intercom at the main entrance, and older buildings must add one when a majority of tenants request it in writing. A 311 complaint usually leads to an HPD inspection, and a broken intercom can be cited as a violation. A camera does not replace a working buzzer, so it is worth pursuing both.

Could a camera or doorbell on my apartment door get in the way of the building's self-closing door rules?
+

It can if it is installed carelessly. HPD requires self-closing doors in buildings with three or more apartments, and its fiscal 2025 report counted 2,937 self-closing door violations. Anything fixed to a door or frame must leave the closer, the latch and the strike alone so the door still shuts and latches by itself. We watch the door close before and after mounting, and we will not place hardware where it could hold a door open.

Is there a way to physically cover a First Alert indoor camera's lens when we are home?
+

Yes. Resideo lists an automatic privacy shutter on the VX5 indoor camera, which closes over the lens rather than relying only on a software setting. We set it up during the install and show you how it behaves, so you know exactly when the camera can and cannot see the room. For many apartment households, that shutter is the feature that makes an indoor camera acceptable to everyone living there.

Will an indoor camera alert me every time the cat walks across the room?
+

It should not if the person and pet detection is set up properly. Resideo says the VX5 indoor camera can tell people from pets, so alerts can be limited to people. We set the alert types with you during the handover and test them by walking past the camera, so your phone buzzes for the courier or the dog walker and not for every lap the cat makes.

My First Alert Onelink device keeps failing to join our Wi-Fi during setup. What is usually wrong?
+

Onelink devices work on 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi only, and setup usually fails when a dual-band router steers the device toward its 5 GHz band. Separating the two bands, or turning on a temporary 2.4 GHz network for setup, normally fixes it. Remember that Onelink is the app for First Alert's smart smoke and carbon monoxide products, not for VX cameras, so a camera problem needs a different approach.

We already have a Honeywell VISTA or ProSeries alarm panel. Can First Alert cameras work with it?
+

Resideo says the VX Series integrates with ProSeries and VISTA panels, which makes sense because Honeywell Home and First Alert are both Resideo brands. Send us a photo of your keypad and, if you can safely see it, the panel's label, and we will confirm before booking what the integration involves for your panel and whether anything else is needed.

Do we need our co-op or condo board's approval before cameras go up?
+

Often, yes, and always for anything outside your unit. Connextivity notes that some boards have a written camera policy requiring approval, some decide case by case and many have never addressed it, while The Cooperator describes alteration agreements, proof of contractor insurance and set contractor hours as common. Ask the managing agent what they need and send it to us; we provide the insurance paperwork and schedule inside the building's rules.

Is First Alert still owned by the company that made our old smoke detectors?
+

No. Resideo announced on February 7, 2022 that it had agreed to buy First Alert from Newell Brands. Resideo lists Honeywell Home, First Alert and BRK among its brands and sells the First Alert VX cameras as a professionally installed line. Your old First Alert smoke detectors and today's First Alert cameras share a name and an owner but run on different apps.

Can old coax or network cable already in our building be reused for new cameras?
+

Sometimes, and it can save a lot of labor when it works. Good existing network cable can carry PoE to a new camera, and some existing coax can be adapted, but old runs in city buildings are often unlabeled, spliced or damaged. We trace each cable, test it end to end, and only reuse what passes, so a camera does not fail months later because of a hidden break.

Can a thief knock a wireless doorbell camera offline with a jammer?
+

New York authorities warned in December 2025, as Spectrum News reported, that some package thieves use Wi-Fi jammers to disrupt doorbell camera footage. A camera powered and connected over a network cable is not affected by Wi-Fi jamming in the same way, which is one reason we recommend PoE for cameras guarding lobbies, package shelves and storefronts wherever the building allows a cable.
