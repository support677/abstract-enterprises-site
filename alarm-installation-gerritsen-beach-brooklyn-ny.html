The Neighborhood

Why Gerritsen Beach Properties Need Alarm Installation

Gerritsen Beach is the peninsula at the bottom of Gerritsen Avenue, with Sheepshead Bay across Shell Bank Creek and Knapp Street to the west, the parkland of Marine Park to the east, Avenue U to the north and the Plumb Beach Channel to the south. Wikipedia counts 4,797 residents at the 2020 census. Brooklyn Community Board 15 covers it, and the 61st Precinct patrols it from 2575 Coney Island Avenue. The name goes back to Wolphert Gerretse, a Dutch settler whose mill stood on Gerritsen Creek until it burned in 1931. For most of its history the land was meadow and marsh, with a few squatters’ shacks near the end of the avenue.

The houses arrived all at once. In 1920 a developer called Realty Associates started building a summer resort for middle-class families on the southwestern meadow: one-story bungalows with peaked roofs and, as Wikipedia puts it, no backyards. Untapped New York credits the builder William Greve with a workforce of five hundred, more than six hundred houses in two years and prices that topped out at $5,750. Within a decade there were about fifteen hundred houses. Owners winterized them one by one, added rooms and second floors, and the resort became a year-round neighborhood without ever being rebuilt from scratch. City Planning found that only about five percent of today’s buildings went up after 1983.

That history produced a building stock unlike most of Brooklyn. City Planning describes mostly detached one- and two-family homes, generally two stories, many of them former summer bungalows, on lots that average forty by forty-five feet south of the canal and thirty-five by fifty-five to the north, and twenty-four by sixty-six on the waterfront. The streets were laid out for people on foot rather than cars and measure from thirty feet down to fifteen, sidewalks included. Urban Omnibus described houses built right to the property line and second-story patios a few feet from the sidewalk. The streets south of the canal run in alphabetical order, Aster, Bevy, Celeste, Dictum, and many of them are courts: Ebony, Frank, Gain, Joval, Melba, Opal and the rest.

Put wooden houses that close together and fire becomes the first thing an alarm should watch. The neighborhood learned that early. Wikipedia records that a damaging fire on Abbey Court in 1921 showed the city’s apparatus could not reach the beach in time, residents held a mass meeting, and the Gerrittsen Beach Fire Department, spelled with the extra t, was organized in 1922. Before water mains arrived the volunteers were fighting fires at least three times a week, because families cooked and heated with oil stoves and lit their homes with kerosene. The city did not open Engine Company 321’s firehouse until October 1930. The fires have not stopped: NBC New York reported a 2018 fire on Aster Court that started in one two-story home and spread to the house next door, and Brooklyn Paper reported an August fire that burned through a wood-frame house on Ebony Court and the attached unit beside it.

Then came the water. Hurricane Sandy put almost every home on the peninsula under seawater on October 29, 2012. City Planning measured flooding up to seven feet above grade, and owners of one- and two-family homes reported average damage of $45,000 to $55,000 each to FEMA. The neighborhood had been in evacuation zone B, so nobody had been told to leave; afterwards the city moved it to zone A. A week later, State Senator Martin Golden was asking licensed electricians to volunteer because almost every home needed an inspection before Con Edison would turn power and gas back on. Many owners have since raised their houses, and Brick Underground noted that others added floors on top because there is no room to build out. Each change left old alarms cut, drowned or misplaced.

Most Gerritsen Beach callers want a doorbell camera or a camera over the back door by the end of the first conversation as well. The alarm and the cameras are best planned together, because the alarm decides that something is wrong and the clip shows what. Our Gerritsen Beach security camera installation page covers the camera side, and both can be done on one visit.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Gerritsen Beach

Each service below is described the way it usually turns out on the peninsula. A typical job combines three or four of them.

Fire-first household alarms

Smoke detectors in the sleeping areas and hallways, heat detectors where smoke detectors would false-alarm, and carbon monoxide detectors near fuel-burning equipment, all on the alarm panel and able to report to a central station. In a bungalow a few feet from its neighbors this is where we start, before door contacts.

Burglar alarms for small frame houses

Door contacts, window contacts on the openings that can be reached from a court or a yard, and one or two motion detectors. A one-story bungalow often needs fewer devices than people expect, because one well-placed detector can see most of the house.

Alarms for raised and enlarged houses

A keypad at the door the family now uses, contacts on the new entry at the top of the stairs, a decision about the enclosure at ground level, and wiring that skips the flood-prone floor entirely. We work with elevation contractors when the timing allows.

Canal-lot and dock sensors

Outdoor motion or beam sensors aimed along the dock, a contact on a gate to the bulkhead, a sensor on a gear shed, and lights that come on when something trips. Set up so passing boats and gulls do not wake the house.

Shop and office alarms on Gerritsen Avenue

Commercial panels for the one-story stores on the only commercial corridor: front glass, back door, roof access, a hold-up button, opening and closing reports, and fire detection for the back room. Some stores also have an apartment above that needs its own alarm area.

Replacing water-damaged and orphaned systems

New panels mounted on an upper floor, reuse of any old wiring that tests good, cellular communicators in place of dead landline and 3G units, and a clean removal of equipment left behind in rooms that flooded.

Fire Comes First

Why a Gerritsen Beach Alarm Starts With Smoke, Heat and Carbon Monoxide

Most alarm companies sell a burglar alarm and mention smoke detectors at the end. On the courts south of the canal we reverse that order, and the reasons are on the record. The houses are wood-frame. The lots are about forty feet wide, many houses are built close to the line, and on the waterfront some lots are only twenty-four feet across. When one house burns, the next one is at risk within minutes. The 2018 Aster Court fire reported by NBC New York began a little after five in the afternoon inside one two-story home and spread to the neighboring house; more than 130 firefighters responded, and two residents were taken to hospital in serious condition. On Ebony Court, Brooklyn Paper described heavy fire on the second and third floors of a wood-frame home and the attached house beside it, with more than 125 firefighters working for two hours. In both cases a few minutes of early warning, for the house where the fire started and for the house beside it, was what mattered most.

A burglar alarm panel can do something a set of battery smoke alarms cannot: it can call someone. When a monitored smoke or heat detector trips, the panel sends the signal through its cellular communicator to a central station, and the operator calls the fire department and then the people on the contact list. That matters when the house is empty during a long commute, when the person at home is an older parent asleep in a back bedroom, and in the days after an evacuation order when nobody is supposed to be there at all.

Placement is where small houses are tricky. In a bungalow with a kitchen a few steps from the bedrooms, an ordinary smoke detector in the hallway will catch every burned piece of toast, and a monitored detector that keeps going off for cooking quickly gets unplugged, which is worse than having none. So we put heat detectors in kitchens, in attached garages, in boiler and utility closets and in hot attics, where a rate-of-rise or fixed-temperature sensor ignores smoke from cooking but reacts to a real fire. Smoke detectors go in the bedrooms, in the hallway between the bedrooms and the living space, and at the top of every stair. In peaked ceilings we keep them out of the very top of the peak, where trapped hot air can hold smoke away, and out of the air stream of supply vents and ceiling fans.

Carbon monoxide is the third piece, and the storm history makes it a real risk here rather than a box to tick. CBS New York found nearly half of the residents still without power on November 14, 2012, more than two weeks after the storm, and long outages are when generators come out; Brick Underground’s 2022 tour of Gerritsen Beach listings included a house advertised as generator-ready with an interlock. A portable generator running too close to a window, or a boiler damaged by salt water and patched back into service, can fill a small house with gas that nobody can smell. HPD’s detector rules already require carbon monoxide detectors in one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied and have fuel-burning appliances, and smoke detectors in every apartment of a multiple dwelling. Our alarm-connected detectors are in addition to whatever the law requires; we never remove a required alarm to replace it with ours unless the new one meets the same rule.

Two practical points round this out. First, the panel itself has to survive a fire long enough to report it, so we keep it out of the kitchen and the utility closet, put it on an interior wall, and give it a cellular path that does not depend on a cable coming in from a pole. Second, fire detection on an alarm only helps if the monitoring account knows exactly where the house is. On a fifteen-foot court that delivery drivers struggle to find, we write the cross street, the side of the court and a description of the house into the station’s notes so the call to the fire department carries more than a house number.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Gerritsen Beach’s Architecture

Six kinds of building make up almost all of the peninsula, and each needs something different from an alarm.

The original one-story bungalow

Built in the 1920s for summer use, peaked roof, a handful of rooms on one floor, on a lot of about forty by forty-five feet in the old section south of the canal. Every window is at ground level. Walls were often built without insulation and later closed in, which makes fishing a wire through them easy in some places and impossible where later owners added blocking.

The bungalow that grew a second floor

Brick Underground reports that with no room to build outward, owners rebuilt after Sandy by adding floors on top. The result is a small footprint with a tall stair, bedrooms upstairs and the living space below, so the smoke detectors and a second keypad go upstairs while the door contacts stay below.

The house lifted onto a new foundation

Raised to meet the design flood elevation, which City Planning puts at about three to five feet above grade on average and five to ten feet near the water. The front door moves to the top of a flight of steps, and the ground level becomes a vented enclosure for storage, parking or access. The old alarm wiring was usually cut when the house was jacked up.

Brick homes north of the canal

Wikipedia describes the new section as traditional streets with stores, brick houses and wide sidewalks, and City Planning notes attached and semi-detached two- and three-story buildings there and on parts of Gerritsen Avenue. These behave more like the rest of south Brooklyn: shared walls, rear yards, often a basement, and hard-wired contacts that are worth testing before replacing.

Canal-front houses

Along the Gotham Avenue Canal, lots average twenty-four by sixty-six feet according to City Planning, and Urban Omnibus noted that most waterfront houses have docks and boats. The back of the house faces the water, with a bulkhead, a dock, sometimes a shed, and no street or neighbors on that side to notice anything at night.

One-story attached stores on Gerritsen Avenue

City Planning describes one-story attached commercial buildings along the avenue, the neighborhood’s only commercial corridor. Shared side walls, a glass front, a back door to a lane or yard and a flat roof shared with the store next door decide where the sensors go.

Raised After Sandy

Alarms for Gerritsen Beach Houses That Were Lifted or Built Up

Lifting a house changes almost everything an alarm depends on. The front door is no longer at the sidewalk; it is at the top of a flight of stairs, sometimes with a landing and a turn. The back door may now open onto a deck several feet above the yard. The ground floor, which used to hold a living room or a bedroom, becomes an enclosure that floodwater is allowed to pass through, with vents in the walls meant, under flood-resistant construction rules, for parking, storage and getting into the building rather than for living space. And the old system, if it survived the storm, was usually disconnected when the house was jacked up, because the wires ran down through the old floor into a basement or crawl space that no longer exists.

We plan a raised house as a new building, with the panel, communicator and battery on the living floor. Contacts go on the door at the top of the steps and on the back door to the deck. The entry delay is set for the climb: a family coming home with groceries needs time to get up the stairs, open the door and reach the keypad, so we time it on the day with the people who will use it, rather than leaving the factory setting. A second keypad or a key fob saves the most common false alarm in these houses, which is somebody fumbling with a code while standing on the landing in the rain.

The enclosure at ground level is a decision, not an automatic yes. If it holds bicycles, tools, a kayak or a freezer, a contact on its door and a motion detector set to its own zone are worth having, and we program that zone so it can be armed while the family is at home upstairs. If the enclosure is open-sided or full of flood vents, outdoor-rated sensors are the only kind that will last, and water sensors near the floor will alarm with every high tide that reaches the yard, so we leave them out. Nothing the system needs to keep working goes down there, and every cable that passes through the enclosure is run so it can be pulled out and replaced after a flood without opening the living floor.

Houses that grew upward instead of being lifted raise a different problem. A bungalow with a new second floor on top often keeps the original ground floor at its old height, which means the living room still floods in a big enough storm and the bedrooms are now upstairs. We put the panel upstairs with the bedrooms, give the ground floor its own zones so a wet sensor down there cannot stop the upstairs from arming, and make sure the smoke detector at the top of the new stair is on the system, because in these houses the stair is the only way down.

Barbara Curran told a Brooklyn College reporter in December 2025 that her family had not flooded since they elevated their house, while warning that many homes with basements still flood in heavy rain and coastal storms. If your elevation is still in the planning stage, call (347) 934-8335 before the contractor closes the walls on the new floor; running cable through open framing costs a fraction of what fishing it through finished drywall does.

The Water Side

Canal Lots, Docks and the Back of the House

The Gotham Avenue Canal cuts the peninsula in two, and Wikipedia notes that the old section beside it is a popular place to berth party and charter fishing boats. Untapped New York points out that you can see the canal between the houses on Bartlett Place and Gotham Avenue. For an alarm, a canal lot is a house with two fronts. The street side has neighbors, cars and people walking past. The water side has a bulkhead, a dock, often a boat, perhaps a shed for gear, and nobody watching after dark. A thief who arrives by water, or walks along the bulkheads from a neighboring lot, never passes a window that faces the street.

We treat the water side as its own zone. Doors and windows facing the canal get contacts like any others, and a glass-break detector covers a slider onto a deck. Outdoors, a beam or a dual-technology motion sensor aimed along the length of the dock, rather than across the water, sees a person on the boards without seeing waves, passing boats or reflections. A gate in the bulkhead fence gets an outdoor contact. A gear shed with an outboard motor or fishing equipment gets a wireless contact on its door, and if it is too far for the panel to hear reliably, a repeater on the back wall of the house brings it in range.

Outdoor sensors by salt water fail through false alarms and corrosion. We aim them low along fixed lines, choose models that need two detections, test at dusk on the dock, and use sealed housings with stainless fasteners. Fewer sensors that work beat a ring of cheap ones switched off after a week.

An outdoor zone does not have to sound the siren. For most families the right setting is a phone alert and a floodlight that switches on, with the indoor siren held back until a door or window actually opens. That way a raccoon on the dock at three in the morning costs a glance at the phone rather than a woken household and an annoyed neighbor on a lot twenty-four feet away. When cameras are part of the job, the alert carries a clip so you can see what tripped it before deciding to do anything.

Commercial Alarm Installation

City Planning calls Gerritsen Avenue the only commercial corridor on the peninsula, with a commercial overlay on six blocks, and describes its stores as mostly one-story attached buildings. One-story attached stores share three weaknesses. The glass front is the biggest opening and faces the street. The back door often leads to a yard, a lane or a parking area that nobody sees at night. And the flat roofs of neighboring stores often run together, so a hatch or a skylight is reachable from next door. We put a contact and a glass-break detector on the front, a heavy-duty contact on the back door, a contact on the roof hatch and a motion detector covering the space between them, so someone who comes through a wall or the roof is caught even if no door opens. A hold-up button behind the counter reports silently.

Gerritsen Avenue has its own reports to plan around. Brooklyn Paper recorded a July attempt to force the front door of a home on Gerritsen Avenue between Whitney and Allen Avenues at about 11:30 in the morning, stopped because the owner saw it happen; another undated item described a burglar at a home near Avenue U who came in through an unlocked back window at 4:30 a.m. and fled after knocking over a dresser; and farther up the avenue, between Avenues S and T, masked vandals threw rocks through a glass door at three in the morning. None were stores and none is recent, but together they show the daytime front-door attempt and the overnight back-window entry a system must cover.

Flood planning applies to stores as well. City Planning’s report mentions a 2013 zoning change that lets commercial owners exempt floor space used for dry floodproofing, and the Brooklyn College report from 2025 says stores on Gerritsen Avenue were promised sewer shut-offs and generators. A store with removable flood barriers across its door needs its door contact mounted where the barrier will not knock it off or hold it open, and its panel and communicator above the line the water reached in 2012.

Rules a Gerritsen Beach store alarm has to respect

  • Siren time limit. NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be able to stop, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of starting. We program sirens well inside that, and the apartments over a store will thank you for it.
  • Licensed installer. In New York State a business that installs security or fire alarms needs a Department of State license under Article 6-D of the General Business Law. Ours is #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • The building’s own fire system. Where a mixed-use building has a code fire alarm, our burglar alarm stays separate from it and does not alter it. Building fire alarm work goes through our Gerritsen Beach fire alarm installation service.
  • Apartments upstairs. HPD requires smoke detectors in each apartment of a multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detection where the rules apply. An apartment over a store gets its own alarm area, its own codes and its own detectors, so the tenant is never at the mercy of the store’s arming schedule.

Fire, smoke and the houses next door

Two houses burned together on Aster Court in 2018. Can our alarm warn us about a fire that starts in the house next door, not only in ours?

Partly, and it is worth understanding the limit. Your detectors sense smoke and heat inside your house, so a fire next door shows up when its smoke gets in through a window, a vent or a shared attic space, or when the shared wall heats up. In close-built frame houses that often happens quickly, and a smoke detector at the top of your stair or in a bedroom on that side will usually be the first thing to react. What the alarm adds over battery smoke alarms is the call: if your detector trips while you are out, the central station calls the fire department and you, which may be the first report that the house next door is on fire.

The fuller answer is a neighborly one. Two households on either side of a shared wall can each put in monitored detection on their own system and list each other as contacts, so a fire alarm in one house brings a call to the people next door. Nobody shares codes or controls; they simply agree to be called. When neighbors ask, we can set up both houses on the same visit.

Where do heat detectors make more sense than smoke detectors in a small peaked-roof bungalow?

Anywhere a smoke detector would go off for reasons that are not a fire. In a bungalow that means the kitchen, because it is a few steps from everything; the bathroom doorway, where shower steam drifts out; the attic under the peak, which gets far too hot in August for a smoke detector to last; an attached garage, where exhaust fumes set off smoke sensors; and the closet that holds the boiler or water heater.

Our kitchen is about ten feet from the bedrooms. How do we stop cooking from sending the fire department to the court?

Start with the right detector in the right spot. A heat detector in the kitchen, a photoelectric smoke detector in the hall rather than an ionization type, and a smoke detector placed outside the line of air from the stove to the bedrooms will remove most cooking alarms. Then we set up the monitoring account so the station calls the house first when a smoke detector trips while the system is disarmed, and dispatches only if nobody answers or the person who answers says it is real. Heat detectors and alarms while the house is armed away go straight to dispatch.

Our court is so narrow it feels like a driveway, and delivery drivers miss it. What should the monitoring station’s notes say so the fire engine finds us?

Everything a stranger needs, in the order they will need it. The station’s account for a Gerritsen Beach house should carry the house number and court name, the nearest cross avenue, which side of the court the house is on counting from Gerritsen Avenue, a two-word description of the house (for example a white raised house with grey stairs), and whether the door the responders should use is at the street or around the side. If the house is on a canal lot, note which end of the lot faces the street.

We type those notes in during setup and read them back to you, and we suggest reflective house numbers visible from both ends of the court.

Can the monitoring station send the Gerritsen Beach volunteer firefighters instead of waiting for FDNY?

The central station calls the public emergency system for your address, the same as you would by dialing 911, and the dispatchers decide which units go. The station does not choose fire companies, and we do not promise any particular response. What we can say is that the neighborhood is served by FDNY Engine 321 on Gerritsen Avenue and that the volunteer department, which Wikipedia describes as running an engine, two ambulances, a brush fire unit and a fly car, has a long record in the community, including evacuations and a relief center after Sandy.

We bought a portable generator after the long outage in 2012. Can the alarm warn us if its exhaust gets into the house?

Yes, with carbon monoxide detectors connected to the panel. A carbon monoxide detector on the alarm sounds in the house like any other, and if the system is monitored the station treats it as an emergency and calls for help. We put one on each sleeping floor and one near any fuel-burning appliance, and on a raised house one at the top of the stairs from the enclosure, because a generator run in or near the enclosure is the classic way exhaust gets into these homes.

Raised, rebuilt and enlarged houses

We lifted the house after Sandy, and the old living floor is now a storage enclosure with flood vents. Does the alarm need to cover it?

Only if there is something down there worth protecting or a door from it into the house. If the enclosure holds bikes, tools, a freezer or a kayak, a contact on its door and a motion detector on its own zone are worth the small cost, and the zone can be armed while you are home upstairs. If the enclosure has a door or hatch leading up into the living floor, that opening gets a contact whether or not the enclosure itself is protected, because it is a way into the house that the street cannot see.

What stays out of the enclosure is anything the system needs to work: no panel, no battery, no communicator, no keypad. Sensors that do go down there are chosen to tolerate damp and mounted high on the frame, and the cable is run so it can be pulled and replaced after a flood without opening the floor above.

Our front door is now at the top of a long staircase. Does the entry delay need to be longer than usual?

Usually yes, and it should be set on the day with the people who use the stairs. The delay starts when the door opens, so the climb itself does not count, but in raised houses the keypad is often placed for the old layout or at the far side of a new hall. We put the keypad within a step or two of the door you actually use and time the delay with you walking in carrying bags. Thirty to forty-five seconds is typical; much longer than that gives an intruder time too.

When the house was jacked up, the contractor cut every alarm wire at the old sill. Is anything left of the old system worth saving?

Sometimes more than people think. The wires inside the walls of the living floor, running from window and door contacts up into the ceiling, often survive a lift intact. They were cut where they dropped into the old basement or crawl space, which no longer exists. If the contacts on the windows still work, we can collect those wires in the ceiling or a closet, splice them to a new run that goes to a panel on the living floor, and test each one. That saves opening walls.

The old panel and anything that sat below the flood line rarely survive; salt water corrodes boards from the inside. If splicing old cable would cost more than going wireless, we say so.

Con Edison needed an electrician’s inspection before it would turn our power back on after Sandy. If the next storm floods our panel, can we just switch the alarm back on afterwards?

No, and please do not try. An alarm panel runs from a plug-in transformer and a sealed battery. If water reached either one, it can short, overheat or fail silently. After any flood that reaches alarm equipment, unplug the transformer from the outlet only if the outlet is dry and you are standing on a dry floor, otherwise leave it to an electrician. Then call us. We replace the transformer and the battery, check the board for corrosion and test every zone before the system is relied on again.

The real fix is placement. On every Gerritsen Beach job we put the panel, the battery and the communicator on the highest practical floor, so the next storm finds nothing of the alarm at water level. In 2012 the State Senate notice said almost every home needed an inspection before power and gas came back, and an alarm mounted upstairs is one less thing to inspect and replace.

Canal lots and the neighbors on the court

Our boat is tied to the dock behind the house. Can the alarm tell us if someone steps onto the dock at night?

Yes. An outdoor beam or dual-technology motion detector aimed along the dock, low and parallel to the boards, sees a person walking out to the boat without reacting to the water, passing boats or the light bouncing off the canal. We set it to send a phone alert and turn on a floodlight rather than sound the siren, unless you want the siren too. If you also want to see what tripped it, a camera aimed down the dock sends a clip with the alert.

We test it at dusk with you on the dock, walking the routes a stranger would take, and adjust the aim until it catches every one of them and ignores gulls landing on the pilings. If the dock floats and moves with the tide, we mount the detector on the fixed bulkhead rather than on the dock, so the beam does not drift.

Someone could reach our back door by boat from the canal without ever passing the street. Does the water side of the house need anything the street side does not?

It needs the same sensors with less reliance on neighbors. On the street side, a forced door at three in the morning may be heard or seen; on the water side, nobody is looking. So we put contacts on every water-facing door and every window that can be reached from the deck or the bulkhead, a glass-break detector on any slider, and a motion detector inside the room those doors open into. The outdoor dock sensor gives warning before anyone reaches the house.

On our court the houses are six feet apart and everyone knows everyone. Should the siren be outside so the neighbors hear it?

An outdoor siren or strobe is useful here for exactly that reason: on a court where people know each other, a neighbor who hears a siren and sees a strobe will often look out, call you or call 911. Indoors, the siren is mainly there to drive an intruder out and wake the household. We usually fit both, with the outdoor unit on the side facing the court and its sound limited by the panel’s siren timer.

Be considerate with the setting, though. On lots this close, an outdoor siren that goes off for a false alarm wakes half the court, and New York City requires a building burglar alarm to be able to stop within fifteen minutes anyway. We set the outdoor siren for confirmed alarms only, a door or window opening while armed, and leave outdoor sensors and fire alarms to the indoor sounders and the monitoring call.

Our Gerritsen Avenue storefront has slots for a flood barrier across the door. Will putting the barrier in interfere with the door sensor?

It can, if the contact is mounted where the barrier sits. Flood barriers slide into channels on either side of the door frame and run from the sidewalk up to two or three feet. A surface contact at the bottom of the door or on the lower frame will be knocked off or held apart. We mount the contact at the top of the door, use a recessed contact where the frame allows, and add a glass-break detector so the front is still covered while the barrier is in place.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Gerritsen Beach

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or similar answer boxes from our research environment, so nothing below is quoted from them. These are claims that commonly appear in generic answers about home alarms and on lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what we see on the peninsula.

“An alarm is a burglar alarm; smoke detection is a separate purchase”

Generic answers treat the burglar alarm as the product and smoke detectors as an accessory. In Gerritsen Beach that is backwards.

The peninsula’s own history says so: a volunteer fire department founded because of a 1921 fire the city could not reach in time, and recent fires on Aster Court and Ebony Court that spread from one wood-frame house to the next. Monitored smoke and heat detection is the part of the system most likely to save a life on these courts.

So our quotes start with fire coverage, then add door contacts and motion detectors.

“Wireless is always the easy option for an older house”

Wireless is the default answer for older homes, and often right here. Not always.

A bungalow with an accessible attic over every room can often be wired faster than people expect, and wired devices never need batteries changed on a peaked ceiling twelve feet up. A raised house, by contrast, has new framing that is open for a few weeks during the lift, which is the cheapest moment to run cable anywhere.

We choose per room. Many Gerritsen Beach jobs end up mixed: wired where the attic or open framing allows, wireless where walls are closed, and wireless outdoors where cable would cross a yard that floods.

“Put the panel near the main entrance”

Installation guides commonly suggest mounting the control panel near the front door or in the basement where the utilities come in.

On the peninsula both suggestions can fail. City Planning recorded Sandy flooding up to seven feet above grade, and a basement or ground-floor panel is exactly where seawater reached. And in a raised house the old front entrance may now be the door to a storage enclosure.

We put the panel on the living floor, on an interior wall, away from the kitchen and the boiler, with a keypad by the door the family actually uses. The panel location and the keypad location are two separate decisions.

“Professional alarm installation costs thousands plus a contract”

Lead sites often present installation as expensive and tied to a long monitoring contract. Some offers are.

Our published Brooklyn list starts at $600 for a basic system with a panel, four to six sensors, a motion detector, keypad and cellular communicator, and a small bungalow often fits close to that. A whole house with fire detection, a raised entry and a dock zone moves toward the complete-home figure of $2,500. Monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month and is optional; there is no long-term contract.

The price is fixed in writing after one visit, and sales tax is added. Jobs under $500 are paid in full; larger ones take a 50% deposit with the balance on completion.

“Outdoor motion sensors cause too many false alarms to be worth it”

Generic advice steers people away from outdoor detection because of animals and weather.

That is fair for a cheap sensor pointed across a street. On a canal lot it overlooks the one side of the house nobody can see: a dock with a boat on it and a bulkhead a thief can walk along from the next lot.

The answer is aiming and setting, not avoidance. A beam or dual-technology detector aimed along the dock, needing two detections before it reports, and sending a phone alert and a floodlight rather than the siren, gives warning without waking the court.

“The alarm keeps working in a storm as long as it has a battery”

Battery backup is presented as the answer to outages. A battery covers a power cut; it does not cover a flood.

After Sandy, nearly half of Gerritsen Beach was still without power two weeks later according to CBS New York, and the State Senate noted that almost every home needed an electrical inspection before Con Edison restored service. A panel whose battery and transformer sat in salt water is not something to switch back on.

Two things keep an alarm working through a storm here: equipment mounted above the water, and a cellular communicator that does not depend on cables from the street. We size the battery for a long outage as well.

“A camera with a siren is as good as an alarm”

Camera kits with sirens and flashing lights are sold as complete security. They are useful outdoors.

But they do not detect smoke, heat or carbon monoxide, they rarely report to a central station, and they see only what is in front of the lens. A door forced at the back of a bungalow, or a fire starting in a bedroom, is invisible to them.

We pair cameras with alarms often, as an addition to contacts and fire detection, never a replacement.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Look Up Before You Look at the Doors

This is how we survey on the peninsula, not a story about any particular house. In most of Brooklyn a technician walks in and starts counting doors and windows. In a Gerritsen Beach bungalow he looks up first. Where is the ceiling peak, and is there an attic hatch? How far is the stove from the bedroom doors? Is there a boiler closet, and what fuel does it burn? Those answers decide the fire coverage, and the fire coverage decides the panel, because not every panel supports every wireless smoke and heat detector.

Only then does he go to the doors: the entry at the top of the stairs, the door from the enclosure, the back door to the deck or the dock. On a canal lot he goes out to the bulkhead at the end and turns around to look at the house the way someone arriving by water would. And before he leaves he walks to the end of the court and back, to see what a fire crew or a dispatcher would need to find the house, and writes it down for the monitoring notes.

Our elevation contractor starts next spring. Should the alarm go in before the lift, after it, or both?
+

Plan it before and install it after. Before the lift, we walk the house with you and mark where cable should run through the new framing. While the walls are open, the contractor or we pull that cable. After the house is set down and the stairs and doors are in, we mount the panel and devices and test everything. Removing an old system before the lift avoids a damaged panel hanging off a cut wire.

Will battery sensors survive an August afternoon in an uninsulated attic or an enclosed porch?
+

Ordinary indoor sensors and smoke detectors are rated for room temperatures, and an attic under a peaked roof in midsummer can go well beyond that, which shortens battery life and can cause false alarms. In those spaces we fit high-temperature heat detectors and devices rated for the range, or we place the sensor on the cooler side of the space. We tell you which devices are outside normal conditions and check their batteries at the return visit.

How do you mount an outdoor siren or sensor on vinyl siding without letting rain in behind it?
+

We use a mounting block made for siding, which gives a flat, sealed surface over the panels, and bring any cable through the block rather than through the siding itself. The penetration is sealed and drips down and away. Where possible we mount outdoor devices under an eave or a soffit, which keeps rain off them entirely and keeps them out of reach from the court.

How do we test a monitored smoke detector without the fire department showing up?
+

Call the central station or use the test mode in the app first, so the station knows a test is coming and puts the account on test for a set time. Then press the test button on each detector and confirm the signal arrives. When you are done, take the account off test or let the timer end. We go through this with you at handover and leave the steps in writing.

Can the alarm shut off the boiler or a gas valve when a detector trips?
+

The panel has outputs that can switch other equipment when a zone trips, and some families use them to shut down a boiler or close an automatic gas valve when smoke or carbon monoxide is detected. We can provide and program the output. The connection to the boiler or gas line must be made by a licensed plumber or heating contractor, and we coordinate with yours rather than doing gas work ourselves.

Our house has solar panels with a home battery. Does the alarm still need its own backup battery?
+

Yes. The alarm’s own battery keeps the panel and communicator running regardless of how the house’s power is set up, including during the moment a home battery switches over, during maintenance on the solar system, or if the home battery is drained.
