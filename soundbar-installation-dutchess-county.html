The Neighborhood

Why Dutchess County Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Dutchess County's houses were built in three bursts, and the burst your house came from decides most of what happens on a soundbar visit. The oldest layer sits along the river: the brick and frame streets of the City of Poughkeepsie, the old mill town that became Beacon, the village centers of Rhinebeck, Fishkill and Wappingers Falls. The middle layer arrived with IBM. The Wappingers Historical Society describes how, with the population boom of the 1950s and 1960s, Wappinger took in a new wave of settlers, most of them employed by IBM in Poughkeepsie and East Fishkill, and how much of the town's farmland became suburban neighborhoods. The newest layer is the apartment buildings that have gone up around Beacon's Main Street and Fishkill Creek and on infill sites in Poughkeepsie.

Each of those layers puts something different behind the television. In a river-town Victorian it is usually plaster over wood lath, a deep baseboard, a picture rail and a chair rail, and a wall that is rarely flat across the width of a long soundbar. In an IBM-era raised ranch or split-level it is very often wood paneling in the lower-level family room, a soffit boxing in the ductwork along one wall, and a TV wall that happens to be an outside wall full of insulation. In a new Beacon apartment it is drywall on studs, a lease, and a management office that wants to know who is drilling. None of these walls is difficult. They are just different, and a fixing that suits one of them is the wrong fixing for the next.

That matters more than it first appears, because a soundbar is unusually unforgiving of a poor mount. It is long and narrow, so a quarter-inch of droop at one end shows against the straight bottom edge of the TV. It sits low enough to be knocked by a vacuum, a dog's tail or a toddler. It is often pulled on by its own cables when someone moves the cable box. And the bracket that comes in the box usually ships with a handful of plastic plugs meant for plain drywall over studs, which is precisely what a lot of Dutchess living rooms do not have.

Then there is the room itself. The IBM-boom houses were designed around a family room, often half a level down, often with a low ceiling and a long wall of built-in shelving. The Victorians have tall ceilings and hard plaster that make dialogue sound bright and echoey. Many Beacon and Poughkeepsie apartments are open-plan, with the TV wall shared by the kitchen. A soundbar placed by guesswork in any of those rooms ends up either too quiet to follow a conversation or loud enough to fill the house.

The last thing that makes Dutchess different is distance. The county runs from the Putnam line below Beacon to Columbia County above Red Hook and Pine Plains, and from the Hudson across to Connecticut at Pawling and Amenia. Wall Mount Under the TV

The bar on its own bracket a few inches beneath the screen, centered and level, fixed into whatever the wall really is: wood studs behind paneling, lath behind plaster, steel studs in a newer apartment, or masonry in a basement.

TV and Bar on One Visit

When the TV is going up too, we set its height and the bar's height together, plan a single cable route and drill once. This is how most raised-ranch family rooms and new Beacon apartments are done.

Bar on the Console or Shelf

The soundbar placed on the media cabinet, a floating shelf or the TV stand, connected and tuned, sited so it does not block the TV's remote sensor. The answer for renters, dorm-age households and anyone who would rather not drill.

Bar Hung From the TV Mount

No new wall holes: the bar is carried on the TV's own mount or its rear fixing points, and it travels with the screen whenever a full-motion arm is pulled out.

Paintable Cord Cover

A slim channel from the bar down to the outlet or the cabinet, painted to match. The usual finish where the wall cannot be opened, and where trim, paneling grooves or an outside wall make in-wall routing a poor idea.

In-Wall Concealment

A recessed outlet behind the screen, fed from the outlet below through a code-compliant kit, with HDMI alongside it in the same empty stud bay. Nothing shows between TV and bar; it suits owners with drywall-on-stud rooms.

Cable Run to a Cabinet

A longer run, up to 25 feet, to the cabinet or closet where the cable box, game console or receiver lives — common in split-levels where the equipment sits on a different wall from the TV.

eARC, CEC and Lip-Sync Setup

We find the TV's eARC or ARC socket, run HDMI from it, enable CEC so the TV remote also wakes and adjusts the bar, match speech to lips, install firmware, and make the bar the TV's default output.

Subwoofer and Rear Speakers

Fixings and methods, wall by wall

Wood paneling over studs. The tongue-and-groove or sheet paneling in a 1960s or 1970s family room is usually nailed to wood studs, sometimes over a layer of drywall, sometimes straight onto the framing or onto furring strips over a block foundation wall. The paneling itself will not hold a screw for long. We find the framing behind it, put the bracket screws into wood, and line the bracket up with the paneling's vertical grooves so the screw heads are hidden. If the wall turns out to be furring over concrete block, the fixing becomes a masonry anchor through the strip into the block.

Plaster on wood lath. Common in Poughkeepsie and Beacon houses built before the war. A soundbar is light compared with a TV, but it is knocked and pulled more than a TV is, so we do not trust a screw in lath alone. Where the bracket lines up with a stud, we use the stud; where it does not, a toggle sized for the bracket goes through the plaster so the load spreads behind it. We drill slowly, without hammer action, and tape the surface first to limit chipping. Old plaster can still crack, and you hear that from us before the first hole.

Drywall on wood studs. The upstairs living room of most raised ranches, every newer addition and most recent apartments. Lag screws into two studs where the bracket allows; rated hollow-wall anchors where the bar's position falls between studs. This is also the only wall where in-wall concealment is straightforward, as long as the cavity has no fire blocking or insulation in the way.

Light-gauge steel studs. Found in some newer apartment buildings and commercial fit-outs along Route 9. Wood screws spin out of steel, so we use fasteners made for metal studs, or toggles through the board between studs, and we check the wall for a sound-rated assembly before going near it.

Outside walls with insulation. In many split-levels and colonials the TV ends up on the exterior wall because that is where the windows are not. Bracket fixing is no different from any other stud wall. Cabling is: the cavity is packed with insulation and may carry a vapor barrier on the warm side, so we usually avoid in-wall routing there and use a painted channel or a run along the base to an interior wall instead.

Concrete block and stone in lower levels. Where a raised-ranch family room has a bare block wall, or an older house has a fieldstone or brick basement, the bracket goes into masonry with a hammer drill and masonry anchors set in the block or stone rather than the mortar joint. There is no cavity to hide cable in, so the finish is a painted surface channel.

Manufactured-home panels. Interior walls in many manufactured homes are thin finished panels on studs that can be spaced farther apart than in a site-built house. We locate the studs before planning the bar's position, keep the bracket's screws in framing, and keep the bar light; a long flagship soundbar is not the right choice for a thin panel wall.

Compliance notes for Dutchess County businesses

  • Licensing works differently here than in the counties to the south. Westchester requires home improvement contractors to hold a county licence, and Rockland requires a Home Improvement License under Chapter 286 of its county law. In Dutchess, the guidance the county's Office for the Aging circulated in May 2026, quoting the New York Department of State, is that New York State does not license home improvement contractors and that residents should check with their town, city or village. AESS works under NYS low-voltage licence #12000287431 and carries insurance either way.
  • Bars that jut into a hallway. A soundbar on a wall people walk past is a protruding object under §307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards: anything whose leading edge sits between 27 and 80 inches off the floor may project no more than 4 inches into the route. A bar plus its bracket usually exceeds that, so in a Poughkeepsie waiting room or a narrow Beacon storefront we raise it above 80 inches, recess it, or move it to a wall nobody passes.
  • Outlets are electricians' work. Running a soundbar's low-voltage cable is our trade; adding or moving a power outlet is not. If the only outlet is across the room, the options are a longer surface channel or a licensed electrician first.
  • Insurance certificates for landlords. Commercial leases along Route 9 and in Beacon commonly require contractors to be insured with the owner named. Give us the names and a certificate of insurance goes out at no charge.
  • The right jacket inside walls and ceilings. Any HDMI or speaker lead we bury in a wall needs an in-wall rating, and one crossing an air-handling ceiling needs a plenum rating. The patch lead packed in the soundbar carton has neither, so it stays outside the wall, between bar and TV.
  • Short-term rental permits are the owner's paperwork. In the Town of Rhinebeck, Local Law 2 of 2025 requires a permit for any rental of 30 consecutive days or fewer and a named contact who answers the code office within 24 hours. Nothing about a soundbar affects that permit, but owners of permitted rentals often want installs and repairs timed around guest stays, and we schedule accordingly.

Houses from the IBM years

The lower level of our Wappingers raised ranch is finished in 1970s wood paneling — can the soundbar bracket go straight onto it?

Onto it, yes; into it, no. Paneling from that era is a thin decorative skin, and a screw driven only into the paneling will hold for a while and then work loose as the bar gets bumped. What matters is what the paneling is fastened to. In most raised-ranch lower levels it is nailed to wood studs or to furring strips, and the bracket screws go through the paneling into that framing. We find it with a scanner and a fine pilot hole hidden behind where the bracket will sit, and we line the bracket up with the paneling's vertical grooves so the screw heads disappear into the shadow line.

The thing to watch for is the outside wall of a lower level. In many split-entry houses the family room's back wall is the concrete-block foundation, with furring strips fixed to the block and the paneling nailed to the strips. A strip is too shallow for a lag screw, so on that wall the fixing is a masonry anchor through the strip into the block itself, which is priced on our menu as a concrete-block wall. The front face of the paneling looks identical either way, which is why we test before we decide.

Paneling is also easy to scar and hard to patch invisibly, so we drill through painter's tape, size holes to the fixing and avoid trial-and-error holes. If you are planning to paint or remove the paneling in the next year or two, tell us; it may make sense to wait or to hang the bar from the TV mount instead, so nothing new goes into the wall you are about to change.

A ductwork soffit runs along the top of our LaGrange basement's TV wall — how do the TV and soundbar fit under it?

By measuring from the bottom of the soffit down, not from the floor up. Finished lower levels often have a boxed-in duct or beam along one wall, and if the TV is centered on the wall as though the soffit were not there, the screen ends up uncomfortably high or hard against the box. We start with the seated eye height at the couch, set the TV so its center is close to that, and then check there is still clearance above the screen for the mount's lift-off and below it for the bar and its spacing from the TV.

The soundbar is the part that usually decides it. Sonos's own guide for the Arc Ultra, for example, asks for at least 4 inches between bar and TV and about a foot of clear space at each end. In a low room that pushes the bar quite close to the console or the floor, which is fine acoustically — a bar near ear height when you are seated sounds best — as long as nobody's knees or the vacuum cleaner will hit it. If the numbers do not work, a TV-attached bracket lets the bar sit tight under the screen and saves an inch or two.

Do not fix anything into the soffit itself. It is usually light framing and drywall around a duct, and a screw that finds the duct will leak air or rattle. If the room really needs a center channel or speakers higher up, that becomes a different conversation about in-ceiling speakers in the rest of the ceiling.

The last owner left speaker wire poking out of our East Fishkill family-room wall — is any of it useful to a soundbar?

Possibly, but not in the way most people hope. A soundbar does not take speaker wire: it gets its audio over HDMI or an optical lead from the TV, and its wireless subwoofer and rear speakers each need a power outlet, not a wire from the bar. So old speaker wire cannot be plugged into the soundbar itself, and it does not save you running anything.

Where it can still earn its keep is if you decide that a bar is not enough. Wire left by a previous owner usually means someone once ran a receiver with surround speakers, and if those runs are intact they can feed a receiver-based system again — the setup our A/V receiver line on the price menu covers. Before relying on it we trace where each end goes and test it, because wire that has sat in a wall for decades is often cut, stapled through or connected to nothing at the far end.

The other useful find is an abandoned low-voltage cable that can be used as a pull string to get a new HDMI or data cable through the same path. If the old wire is unused and in the way, we can tuck it back into the wall behind a blank plate so the TV wall looks finished; we do not pull it out of closed walls.

Will the paneling in our Hyde Park family room buzz when the soundbar and subwoofer play loud?

It can, and it is one of the more common complaints we hear from paneled rooms. Thin sheet paneling nailed to studs or furring is a large, light panel, and at certain bass notes it vibrates like a drum skin; loose nails, a gap behind the sheet or a bracket screwed only into the paneling make it worse. You hear it as a buzz or rattle that comes and goes with particular scenes rather than as a fault in the bar itself.

The fixes start at the bracket. Screws go through the paneling into framing, so the bar is held by the stud rather than by the panel, and thin felt or rubber pads between bar and bracket stop the bar itself chattering against the wall. The subwoofer comes out of the corner and away from the paneled wall, and its level is set from the couch. Some bars help too: B&H's listing for the Sonos Arc Ultra wall mount notes that the bar detects when it is wall-mounted and adjusts its EQ for that position.

If one sheet still buzzes, it is usually a loose panel, and a few finish nails into the stud behind it settle it — something the homeowner or a carpenter can do, since panel repair is not on our menu.

Older river-town houses

Our Poughkeepsie apartment still has its picture rail — could the soundbar hang from it on hooks instead of going into the plaster?

We don't recommend it, even though it is a natural thought in a rental where drilling plaster is discouraged. Picture-rail hooks and cords were made to let a framed picture hang flat against the wall by its own weight. A soundbar is different: it has to sit tight and level a few inches under the TV, it has cables pulling on it from the back, and it gets nudged every time someone dusts or reaches for the TV. On cords or hooks it swings, tilts forward, and puts its whole weight on a narrow wooden molding that may be a century old and held up by nails into lath.

The rail also sits far too high. In most Victorian rooms it runs a foot or more below a ten- or eleven-foot ceiling, so a bar hung from it would be several feet above the screen and well over the heads of anyone sitting down, which is exactly where a soundbar sounds worst. Dialogue is clearest when the bar sits close under the picture at roughly seated ear height.

The no-drill options that do work in a plaster-walled rental are a TV-attached bracket that hangs the bar from a TV mount the landlord has already approved, or the bar on the console or a sturdy shelf under a TV on a stand. If the landlord will allow a few holes, a bracket fixed into the studs or with toggles through the plaster is a small, patchable repair at the end of the lease, and we describe it in writing so you can ask.

Our gallery on Main Street in Beacon wants sound for a video piece but no visible speaker — can a soundbar be hidden?

It can be made very discreet, but a soundbar cannot be sealed away completely without losing what it is for. Its drivers fire forward and, on some models, sideways and upward, so it needs an open path toward the audience. The usual gallery approaches are to mount it directly under the screen with a finish that matches the wall, to recess it into a shallow niche or plinth with an acoustically open front, or to put it on a low pedestal in front of the projection wall where it reads as part of the installation.

The other things a gallery needs are reliability and lock-down. We set the bar to come on with the display, fix the volume at a level the artist approves, and hide or disable the remote so visitors cannot change it. For a looping piece, the playback device and display schedule matter as much as the bar, and we can set those up as part of the same visit.

Where the piece needs sound that follows the image across a large room, or where nothing at all may show, a pair of small wall or ceiling speakers driven by a compact amplifier is often the better tool, and we will say so rather than force a bar into the job.

Campus, rental and country households

Can a soundbar be wall-mounted in a Vassar or Marist dorm room?

Not on the college's walls. Vassar's Office of Residential Life says that tape, hooks, putty, nails, small tacks, push pins and glue are not to be used for hanging decorations, because they severely damage walls and may mean repainting the whole room at the student's expense. Marist publishes its own housing rules and FAQs, which students should check. A screwed-on soundbar bracket is plainly outside rules like Vassar's, so we do not mount soundbars on residence-hall walls at any college.

What works instead is a small bar placed on the desk or dresser under the TV, connected to the TV's HDMI ARC or optical socket, with the volume set for a shared hall. Compact bars suit dorm rooms far better than flagship models with separate subwoofers, which are the part neighbors complain about. Students can set that up themselves; no visit needed.

Off-campus houses and apartments around Arlington and North Road are different: there the landlord decides. With the landlord's written permission we mount bars in student rentals, and when permission is not given we place and connect the bar on furniture instead.

Our bedroom TV rises out of a lift cabinet at the foot of the bed in our Millbrook house — where does the soundbar go?

Not on the TV. A lift cabinet raises and lowers the screen through a slot in the top, and anything attached to the TV has to pass through that slot and clear the mechanism every time. A TV-attached bracket that is fine on a wall mount can jam a lift or scrape the opening, so we do not hang the bar from a TV on a lift.

The bar works best fixed to the cabinet itself. The usual options are on the cabinet top in front of the lid opening, if the lid's travel leaves room, or inside the cabinet behind a fabric or slotted front panel facing the bed, so the sound has an open path to the listener. Compact bars suit both, and a bedroom rarely needs a separate subwoofer. Power and HDMI are run with a loose service loop so the cables follow the TV up and down without being pulled tight.

We set the TV to output to the bar every time it rises, switch on CEC so the lift-mounted TV's remote also controls the bar, and check volume at pillow height. Any cabinet carpentry, such as cutting a speaker opening, is for the cabinet maker; we place, connect and set up the bar.

Our place outside Rhinebeck is a permitted short-term rental — if the soundbar fails mid-stay, how fast can it be sorted before the next check-in?

Call or text as soon as the guest reports it, with the bar and TV model, and we book the next open 2-hour window, offering same-day where one is free. Many "no sound" reports from rentals are settings rather than faults: the TV has been switched back to its own speakers, an input has changed, or the bar is on Bluetooth to a guest's phone. Those can often be talked through with your on-site contact by phone before anyone drives out.

The Town of Rhinebeck's short-term rental law already requires owners to name a contact who answers the code office within 24 hours, so most permitted hosts have someone local. Having that person able to let a technician in during a turnover window is what makes a fast fix possible.

If the bar itself has died rather than a setting, the fastest route back to sound before the next arrival is usually to switch the TV to its own speakers for one stay, then reconnect a replacement or repaired bar as a setup-only visit. Keep the receipt and model number with your rental paperwork; manufacturer warranty claims move faster when the details are ready.

Homes that don't fit the usual template

Can a soundbar be mounted on the thin walls of a manufactured home in one of the county's parks?

Usually yes, provided the bracket goes into framing and the bar is a sensible size. Interior walls in manufactured homes are often thin finished panels, and the studs behind them can be spaced wider apart than in a site-built house — one thread on Reddit's manufactured-home forum asks how to mount a TV on a wall with studs spaced at 24 inches. A screw in the panel alone will not hold a soundbar that gets bumped, and hollow-wall anchors designed for half-inch drywall do not grip thin panels well.

So we locate the studs first and plan the bar's position around them. If two studs fall within the bracket's width, it goes straight into them. If not, a TV-attached bracket that hangs the bar from a TV already fixed to the framing is often the neatest answer, or a small horizontal backer fixed across two studs. Compact and mid-size bars are the right choice here; very long, heavy flagship bars put more leverage on a thin wall than it was built for.

Check your park's rules too. Some communities have rules about alterations, and if you rent the home rather than own it, the owner's permission comes first. We are happy to describe the fixing in writing for whoever needs to approve it.

Room Sound

How Dutchess Rooms Change the Way a Soundbar Sounds

The same bar can sound muffled in one Dutchess house and harsh in the next, and the room is almost always the reason. We set every bar for the room it is in rather than leaving the factory preset, and these are the three room shapes we tune for most often.

The low lower-level family room. Seven-and-a-bit-foot ceilings, carpet on a slab and paneled walls absorb treble and pile up bass, particularly in corners. A subwoofer pushed into the corner beside the TV can make every explosion boom while dialogue sounds dull. We usually bring the sub out of the corner, turn its level down from the default, raise the bar's center or voice setting slightly, and check from the couch rather than from the TV.

The tall Victorian parlor. Ten-foot ceilings, plaster walls, wood floors and big sash windows reflect sound around the room, so speech can arrive at the listener several times over and blur. Here the useful moves are keeping the bar close under the screen and aimed at ear height, turning off the widest virtual-surround modes, and using rugs, curtains and upholstered furniture you already own to soak up some of the reflections. Up-firing drivers that rely on bouncing sound off the ceiling are less effective when that ceiling is very high or ornamented.

The open-plan apartment or renovated ranch. When the TV wall is also the kitchen wall, the bar has to reach a sofa in one direction while not flooding the kitchen in the other. Narrower bars aimed straight at the seating, a moderate sub level and dialogue enhancement switched on do more here than raw power. Night or late-evening modes, which compress the difference between loud and quiet passages, help when someone is cooking or a child is asleep down the hall.

Whichever room you have, the final check is the same: we play speech, music and a film scene, stand where you sit, and adjust until voices are clear at a volume you would actually use.

Before the Visit

Getting a Dutchess Room Ready for the Installer

A few minutes of preparation saves most of the delays we see on Dutchess appointments.

  • Knock on the wall. A hollow, slightly springy sound is usually drywall or paneling over studs; a dead, solid sound is usually plaster, block or stone. Tell us which when you book.
  • Find the outlet and the boxes. Note where the nearest working outlet is, and where the cable box, streamer and any console live. If they are on another wall, that decides the cable route.
  • Have the bar, its bracket and any sub or rears on site. Boxed is fine. A missing part is the most common reason a visit cannot start.
  • Clear the space in front of the TV wall. Move fragile things off the console and give the technician about six feet of working room.
  • Renters: get the OK in writing. A text or email from the landlord naming the soundbar and the number of fixings is enough for most.
  • Second-home and rental owners: arrange access. If you will be in the city on the day, tell us who will let us in and how to reach them.

If you are not sure about any of these, send a photo of the TV wall and another of the outlet and cabinet. That is usually enough for us to plan the whole visit.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Paneled Family Room Before Drilling

General installer guidance for the kind of room we meet most in Dutchess; it describes our working method, not a particular customer's house.

Start by standing in the doorway and working out which wall is outside. In a split-entry house the lower-level back wall is very often the foundation, and that tells you before you touch it that the paneling may be on furring over block rather than on studs. Knock along the wall at bracket height; a run of identical dull knocks with no hollow spaces between is a warning.

Look up next. Find the soffit, the drop-ceiling grid or the beam, and measure from its underside, not from the floor. Then sit on the couch and look at where your eyes land on the wall; that, not the middle of the wall, is where the TV's center wants to be.

Before any bracket hole, drill one fine pilot hole where the bracket will cover it. Wood shavings mean a stud or furring strip; grey dust and a hard stop mean block; nothing at all means a void. Choose the fixing after that hole. Finally, follow the cables: find the outlet, the cable box and the router, and decide the cable route before mounting anything, because on paneling and outside walls the route is what limits the layout.

Can guests give toasts through the soundbar with a microphone at our barn or event room?
+

Usually not. Most soundbars have no microphone input, and connecting a microphone over Bluetooth adds a delay that makes speakers hear themselves echo. For toasts and announcements the right tool is a powered speaker or small PA with its own microphone input. We can set the soundbar up for the slideshow or video, and tell you where the two should sit so they don't compete.

Do you put soundbars in church halls and parish rooms?
+

Yes. Halls usually need a bar fixed high enough to clear stacked chairs and tables, a cord cover rated for a busy room, the TV set to output to the bar every time, and a volume level volunteers cannot push too far. If the room is long, we will tell you honestly whether a bar can reach the back rows or whether wall speakers would serve the congregation better.

Will my Samsung TV's own speakers keep working alongside a Samsung soundbar?
+

On supported pairs, yes. Samsung describes its Q-Symphony feature as playing sound through the soundbar and the TV's speakers at the same time. We switch it on during setup where your TV and bar both support it, and compare it with the bar alone so you can choose. Other brands have their own versions, and we set those up the same way.

I hung the soundbar myself — can you check it and finish the setup?
+

Yes. We book it as a setup-only visit at the Dutchess rate of $275 for the first hour. The technician checks what the bracket is fixed into, tells you plainly if it should be refixed, and then does the connection work: eARC or ARC, CEC, lip-sync and firmware. If refixing is needed, you get that price from our menu before any work starts.

Can the soundbar sit on a floating shelf instead of its own bracket?
+

It can, if the shelf is fixed into framing and deep enough for the bar's feet. Check that the shelf lip does not block the front drivers, and leave clear space above any up-firing drivers. A shelf also gives cables somewhere to hide behind. Placing and connecting the bar on your shelf is our $79 placed line; shelves themselves are not on our menu, so ask before buying one.

Is a wall-mounted soundbar safer than one on the stand with a toddler in the house?
+

Generally, yes. A bar on a low stand is at grabbing height and its cables make an easy handle. On the wall, fixed into framing with the cables in a channel or inside the wall, there is little for small hands to pull. Mount the TV securely too, and keep the subwoofer where it cannot be climbed on or pushed over.

Our dog chews cables — how are the soundbar wires protected?
+

Keep them off the floor and out of reach. A painted channel from the bar down to the cabinet, or in-wall concealment where the wall allows it, removes the cable from the dog's level altogether. Where a cable must cross to a subwoofer or rear speaker, we route it along the baseboard in a cover. Rear speakers are best placed near outlets so there is little cable to protect.

Can the soundbar be installed on the same visit as cameras or network work?
+

Yes, and it often saves a trip. Our Dutchess crews also install security cameras, Wi-Fi and data cabling under the same NYS licence. Tell us everything when you request the quote, and we price each part from its own menu and schedule one visit or two consecutive windows, depending on the size of the job.
