The Neighborhood

Why Williamsburg Properties Need Alarm Installation

Williamsburg sits on the East River between Greenpoint to the north and Bedford-Stuyvesant to the south, with Bushwick and East Williamsburg to the east. Wikipedia notes that it was an independent city until 1855, when it was annexed by Brooklyn and lost the final "h" in Williamsburgh, and that it is now part of Brooklyn Community District 1, covered by the ZIP codes 11206, 11211 and 11249. Most of the neighborhood is patrolled by the NYPD's 90th Precinct at 211 Union Avenue; its northernmost blocks fall under the 94th Precinct at 100 Meserole Avenue. Inside those lines the neighborhood splits into parts people name without thinking: the Northside above Grand Street, the Southside or Los Sures between Grand Street and Broadway, and South Williamsburg below Broadway and Division Avenue, home to a large Satmar Hasidic community. We cover South Williamsburg and East Williamsburg on their own pages; this page is about the Northside, the Southside, the Broadway corridor and the waterfront.

The building stock changes block by block. Older walk-ups and tenements with fire escapes on the Southside. Brick and frame row houses on the Northside streets, including the stretch of North 11th Street that Wikipedia captions as Brewers Row. Commercial buildings along Broadway, which the same article describes as primarily stores and offices, many with apartments upstairs and the elevated tracks outside the second-floor windows. Former factories converted to lofts, a process Wikipedia traces through the Smith-Gray Building with its blue cast-iron front and the old Gretsch instrument factory. And the high-rise condominiums and rentals that went up along Kent Avenue after the City Council rezoned the Northside and Greenpoint waterfront on May 11, 2005, among them The Edge and Northside Piers. At the southern end of the waterfront, the former Domino Sugar refinery, landmarked in 2007, now sits beside Domino Park, which opened in June 2018.

The burglaries reported here follow that building stock. In the summer of 2009, Brooklyn Paper counted at least 16 residences raided in one week across the Southside, East Williamsburg and Bushwick, and the 90th Precinct's community affairs officer explained most of them in one sentence: "Most of them are pretty simple, with perps going in through fire escape windows." One of those apartments was on Berry Street between Broadway and South 8th Street, entered through an unlocked window between 1:30 and 2:40 in the morning while the tenant was out with friends. Another was on South 3rd Street between Keap and Hooper, where the burglars climbed the fire escape and opened an unlocked bedroom window in the early evening. In March 2026 the target was business. News 12 Brooklyn and Williamsburg365 reported a crew tied to at least 15 commercial break-ins between February 22 and March 9, nine of them at Williamsburg businesses, with nine incidents packed into a six-hour stretch on March 4 alone. Police said the suspects pried doors with tools, broke glass doors or windows, and in some cases used keys stored outside the buildings.

None of that is unusual for a busy part of Brooklyn. What is unusual is the setting. Williamsburg is one of the few neighborhoods where an alarm has to work against constant background noise and vibration: steel trains over Broadway every few minutes, traffic off the bridge and the expressway, crowds outside the stations at all hours, and nightlife whose bass, by one neighbor's account in Greenpointers, travels through shared walls. That changes three decisions in every system we design here. Which sensors can be trusted not to react to the environment. How the alarm tells someone what is happening when a siren is easy to ignore. And what a person at home needs to know about a window opening two rooms away when they cannot hear it. The sections below take those decisions one at a time.

Williamsburg facts that shape an alarm design

  • An elevated line older than the bridge. According to Wikipedia's BMT Jamaica Line article, the Union Elevated Railroad opened its line above Broadway from Gates Avenue to Driggs Avenue on June 25, 1888, the date the Lorimer Street and Hewes Street stations also opened. The structure was rebuilt for three tracks around 1916, but the middle express track was never used as planned because engineers found the vibration of trains passing over the stations would be too severe.
  • A bridge that changed the neighborhood. The Williamsburg Bridge opened in 1903, and Wikipedia credits it with bringing many Jewish families from the Lower East Side. Wikipedia lists nine bus routes that still end at the Williamsburg Bridge plaza.
  • Two precincts. The 90th Precinct, which covers most of the neighborhood, reported 229 burglaries in 2018, and its crime across all categories fell 72.3 percent from 1990 to 2018. The 94th Precinct, covering the northernmost blocks and Greenpoint, reported 141 burglaries that year. Both figures come from Wikipedia's summary of NYPD data.
  • Two counts of people. Wikipedia gives 151,308 people in 2020 for Williamsburg, and 78,700 in 2010 for the city's two Williamsburg tabulation areas. The boundaries differ; we flag the conflict rather than pick one.
  • Landmarks among the walk-ups. The Williamsburg Houses, twenty four-story buildings on 23.3 acres designed by William Lescaze and completed in 1938, were Brooklyn's first large-scale public housing and became a city landmark on June 24, 2003. The Kings County Savings Bank building at Bedford Avenue and Broadway has been a city landmark since 1966.
  • A rezoned waterfront. The 2005 rezoning allowed high-density residential towers along the water and called for a continuous two-mile string of waterfront esplanades. The towers it produced have concrete floors, sealed or limited-opening windows, staffed lobbies and building rules about contractors, all of which change how an apartment alarm is installed.

Owners who want a picture to go with each alert often pair the alarm with a doorbell or a camera on the stoop or the shop door, which we can quote at the same visit as camera installation for a Williamsburg apartment, house or storefront.

A Neighborhood That Never Goes Quiet

Nobody Hears a Williamsburg Break-In: What the Noise Does to an Alarm

Most alarm advice assumes a quiet house. The siren is loud against silence, the neighbors notice, and the person asleep upstairs wakes up. Williamsburg rarely offers that silence. The reports we read for this page describe a neighborhood where noise is constant, and one of them describes a burglary in the next room from people who did not notice it. That is the problem an alarm here has to solve first.

Trains over Broadway since 1888

The elevated structure over Broadway is one of the oldest in the city's system. Wikipedia's article on the BMT Jamaica Line records that the Union Elevated Railroad opened its line above Broadway to Driggs Avenue on June 25, 1888, and extended it to the Broadway Ferry that July. Around 1916 the structure was rebuilt to carry three tracks, with the middle one meant for express trains, but the article says engineering studies found that the vibration of trains passing over the stations at speed would be too severe and would shake the stations apart. The J and M still cross Broadway on that structure today, stopping at Marcy Avenue, Hewes Street and Lorimer Street.

For anyone living or working beside it, the result is steady noise and movement at window height. In a 2020 first-person account for Brick Underground, a tenant who moved into a fourth-floor walk-up next to the Marcy Avenue station in September 2019 wrote that she could hear the subway from inside her apartment, that she had never seen the station not busy, and that even in the middle of the night it could look like rush hour. Within minutes of arriving on moving day, a futon left on the sidewalk was carried off by two men who had just come up from the trains, according to the store owner next door. On the r/williamsburg forum, a thread from residents whose windows face the J, M and Z at track level describes the morning noise on the walk to Marcy as brutal; we could read only the excerpts, not the full thread.

An alarm installed in a building like that faces two hazards. Sensors that react to sound or vibration can react to the trains. And people inside, already tuning out the trains, can tune out the alarm's chime or even its siren. Both are design problems, not reasons to go without an alarm.

A burglary in the next room

NBC New York reported on March 25, 2016, that a burglar climbed a fire escape on Lorimer Street and came in through an unlocked window on a Wednesday afternoon while the tenant, Tiffany Chang, was watching television with her roommate. She found him only when she walked into her bedroom and saw him halfway out the window with her laptop. She wrestled the laptop back, but he left with her phone and heirloom jewelry. She told the station she had been robbed a few times before.

Two details in that report matter for design. The window was unlocked, and the people at home did not notice it open. An alarm set to its away mode would not have helped, because nobody arms an away mode while watching television. What would have helped is a stay or home setting that keeps the fire-escape windows armed while people move freely inside, and an announcement that reaches them wherever they are sitting: a second sounder or a voice keypad in the living room, a chime loud enough for that room, and a push notification on the phone in their hand. We set those up as a matter of course in Williamsburg apartments, and we test them with the television on.

Bass through the walls

Nightlife adds a different kind of noise. In February 2023 Greenpointers reported on neighbors of the CODA Williamsburg Hotel at 160 North 12th Street, formerly the McCarren Hotel, which runs a rooftop lounge and a basement club. Residents described near-constant weekend noise into the early morning; one couple slept on an air mattress in their kitchen to get away from it. The 94th Precinct told the outlet there had been 89 calls to 311 about the hotel the previous year and 11 more by late January. The city's Department of Environmental Protection issued the hotel a summons in September 2022 for music in excess of allowable limits. A reader who commented on the article wrote that no soundproofing could guarantee immunity from bass traveling through shared walls.

NYC311 publishes the limit that applies to bars and restaurants: between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m., their music may not exceed 42 decibels measured inside a nearby residence, or 7 decibels above the surrounding sound level measured on the street 15 or more feet away. Those numbers are about sleep, not alarms, but they show how much sound and low-frequency vibration a Williamsburg apartment can be living with even when a venue is in compliance. An alarm sensor that listens for sudden sound or feels for vibration will hear that too.

Never empty, still burglarized

A crowded neighborhood is not the same as a watched one. During the 2026 pattern, nine incidents happened in a single six-hour stretch on March 4 in one of Brooklyn's busiest areas. The alarm has to report to someone whose job or whose phone makes them act, not to the street.

Sensors for a Loud Block

Choosing Sensors That Ignore the Trains, the Bass and the Traffic

The fastest way to make a Williamsburg tenant switch an alarm off for good is to let it ring for a train. Every system we design here is built in the same order, starting with the devices that cannot be fooled by noise and adding sound- and vibration-based devices only where the room allows them.

Contacts lead, everywhere

A door or window contact is a magnet and a switch. It reports when the opening moves apart, and nothing else: not a train, not a bass line, not a truck crossing the bridge. That makes contacts the foundation of every Williamsburg design, on the apartment door, on every window that opens onto a fire escape, a rear yard, a roof or an extension, on the cellar and back doors of a shop, and on the terrace door of a tower unit. The 2009 Southside burglaries Brooklyn Paper described came through fire-escape windows; the 2016 Lorimer Street burglary came through one. A contact on that window, armed in a home mode while people are inside, is the single most useful device in a Williamsburg apartment.

Windows that hold an air conditioner in summer, or that people like to leave open an inch, get the same contact plus a second magnet set at the open-a-little position, or a bracket that limits how far the sash travels. We set that up once at the walkthrough and show you how it behaves before we leave.

Glass-break detectors: only where the room is quiet enough

Acoustic glass-break detectors listen for the sound pattern of breaking glass. They are excellent in the right room and a nuisance in the wrong one. Vivint's own support page lists the common noises that can set its glass-break detector off, including slamming doors, dishes dropped into a sink, dogs barking near the device, objects falling, and loud music or television, and it says the detector can be tripped by anything that causes a sudden noise or vibration. Abode's support page warns that unusually loud sounds such as very loud music may trigger its sensor in home mode and says to mount it away from speakers, air conditioners and motors. Brinks Home lists vibration from nearby construction or loud music as a cause of false alarms.

In Williamsburg that guidance rules out a lot of rooms. We do not put a glass-break detector in a living room that shares a wall with a venue, in a front room that faces the el, or in a shop beside its own sound system. Where a storefront or a loft really needs coverage for large glass, we test the detector in place with the room's normal noise running, set the sensitivity from that test, and back it with contacts and motion so that no single sound can raise a full alarm. Where the room is simply too loud, the glass is covered by motion inside and by contacts on everything that opens.

Shock sensors: tuned, then re-checked

Shock or vibration sensors feel a blow to a window or frame. They can catch someone working on a pane before it breaks, which is useful on a ground-floor shop window. They also feel every heavy truck and, on Broadway, every train. On the few Williamsburg windows where we use them, we mount them on the frame or glass they protect, set them using the panel's own reading while a train passes, and turn on the setting most panels offer that requires repeated hits within a short time rather than a single bump. Then we look at the event history again after a few weeks of real trains.

Motion detectors: aimed away from the street

Passive-infrared motion detectors see the body heat of a person moving across a room. Sound and vibration do not bother them, which is why they do so much of the inside work in a loud neighborhood. What does bother them is heat and light: a radiator, a space heater, afternoon sun across the floor, a pet on a sofa, or a large window onto a lit street. We aim them along walls rather than at windows, choose pet-tolerant models where there is a dog or cat, and in shops with glass fronts use detectors with a second technology that must agree before they report.

Tamper and supervision

Every wireless sensor we install reports its own battery and signal on a schedule, and most have a tamper switch that reports when a cover is opened. A sensor that misses its check-in shows up in the app before it matters, not after a break-in.

Who Hears It Instead

When the Siren Gets Lost in the Street: Alerts That Reach Someone Who Acts

An indoor siren still matters. It is loud inside the apartment, it tells an intruder that the opening was noticed, and it wakes a person who is asleep in the same unit. What a siren cannot do on a Williamsburg block is summon help. So the rest of the system is built around reaching people directly.

The phone in your hand comes first

Every system we install sends push notifications to the app, and most platforms can add text messages and email. For a household, that means the person who is out at work hears about the fire-escape window at the moment it opens, with the sensor's name, and can look at a camera if one is installed. We name every sensor in plain words, so the alert says "Bedroom fire-escape window" rather than "Zone 4".

A staffed central station for when you cannot answer

Central-station monitoring adds people who are paid to respond. When an alarm comes in, the station follows the call list you set and asks police to respond when the alarm is not cancelled with the correct passcode. Monitoring from us starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract. For a business, we usually recommend it; for an apartment, it depends on how often everyone on the call list is somewhere they can act.

Inside the apartment, an announcement you can hear

For the person at home, the useful signal is an announcement in the room they are in. A voice keypad or a small second sounder in the living room, set to chime and name the opening when a door or fire-escape window opens, does the job that a single keypad by the door cannot. We set its volume with your television or music playing, because that is how you will live with it.

Light where sound fails

A strobe or a lamp that switches on in a front window is visible from a street where a siren is not heard. It also marks the right door for police at night on a block where several buildings look alike. Where building rules allow it, we can add one; inside a rental, a smart plug or bulb that the alarm switches does much of the same work without anything mounted outside.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Williamsburg's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up most of what we survey between the Greenpoint line and Broadway. They differ in where the reachable openings are, how much noise and vibration reach the inside, and what the owner or the building will let you attach.

Southside walk-ups and railroad flats

Older tenements south of Grand Street with fire escapes on the front or back, rooms in a line, and windows only at the ends. The fire-escape windows are the openings the 2009 reports describe.

Northside row houses

Brick and frame houses on the numbered North streets, some split into an owner's floors and a rental, with garden doors, rear extensions and cellars.

Broadway buildings under the el

Stores at street level and apartments above, along a street Wikipedia calls primarily commercial, with the elevated structure at second-floor height outside the front windows.

Converted factory lofts

Former industrial buildings, such as the cast-iron Smith-Gray Building and the old Gretsch factory named by Wikipedia, with large windows, steel corridor doors and freight access.

Waterfront towers

The post-2005 condominium and rental buildings on Kent Avenue and the streets behind it, including The Edge and Northside Piers: concrete, glass, staffed lobbies and building rules.

Small mixed and commercial buildings

Two- to four-story buildings with several small businesses or offices behind one street door, like the Bedford Avenue building Brooklyn Paper described in 2008.

Walk-ups and railroad flats: fire escape first, home mode always

In a railroad apartment the rooms open one into the next and the only windows are at the front and the back. We put a contact on every window that a fire escape, a roof or a yard makes reachable, a contact on the entry door, and one motion detector placed so it sees the path from the back windows toward the front without looking out of either end. The panel goes inside the apartment, not in the public hall. The home mode arms the fire-escape windows and the door while you move around, and a sounder in the front room names any opening at the back. In buildings where the trains or a venue next door can be heard, there is no glass-break detector in the front room.

Northside row houses: two households, one panel

Where an owner lives on two floors and rents the third, we set up separate areas, each with its own codes, so the tenant's comings and goings never disarm the owner's floors. Garden and cellar doors, rear extension windows and any door onto the yard get contacts; the parlor and garden floors get motion. A wood-frame house carries sound and vibration from next door more than a brick one, so we read the panel's signal and tamper reports a few weeks after installation and adjust anything that has been reacting to the neighbors.

Apartments over Broadway stores: designed for the el

On Broadway the apartment's front windows face the elevated structure. We rely on contacts and inside motion and keep shock and glass-break devices off the street side unless a test with trains running shows they are steady. The back windows, often onto a fire escape or a yard behind the stores, get the same treatment as any Southside walk-up. The siren goes on an inside wall, and the announcement for the person at home goes in the room where the noise is least. If the store downstairs has its own alarm, we keep the two systems separate.

Lofts: big glass, big rooms, shared corridors

A loft is usually one large room with a long run of windows, a steel door onto a corridor that other tenants use, and sometimes a freight elevator, a roof door or a skylight. Contacts go on every operable window and on the corridor door; motion detectors are placed for the real ceiling height and the open plan; a glass-break detector is added only after a test with the loft's normal noise. Adhesive mounts often will not hold on old brick or painted block, so we use small screws or masonry anchors and show you exactly where each one goes before drilling.

Waterfront towers: inside the unit only

Tower apartments have few reachable openings: the unit door, a terrace or balcony door, and sometimes a window near a setback roof. We install a compact wireless system that touches nothing belonging to the building, test the cellular path through the concrete before mounting the panel, and follow the building's rules for contractors, certificates of insurance and service elevators. The doorman or concierge desk is not a substitute for your own call list, because the desk cannot know what is happening inside your apartment.

Mixed buildings with several tenants

When several businesses share a street door and a stair, each tenant's space gets its own system or area, and the shared door is assigned before the quote, as the questions below explain.

Commercial Alarms

Business Alarm Installation in Williamsburg

The March 2026 reports are the clearest recent picture of how Williamsburg businesses get broken into. News 12 Brooklyn reported that a group of four struck 15 times between February 22 and March 9: nine businesses in Williamsburg, one in Greenpoint and five in Queens, with nine incidents in a six-hour span on March 4. The first was at about 1:45 a.m. on February 22, when, police said, the group used a crowbar to pry open the front of Syrena Bakery in Greenpoint and took $1,800 and an $800 check. On March 4 at 98 South 4th Street they encountered a 52-year-old man, held him at gunpoint and left without taking anything. Williamsburg365 listed Broadway, Havemeyer Street, Harrison Avenue, Hope Street and South 4th Street among the streets involved and said the suspects forced entry by prying open doors with tools, breaking glass doors or windows, or using keys stored outside the buildings, sometimes leaving with cash registers. No one was hurt.

An older report shows the other common pattern. Brooklyn Paper described a Bedford Avenue commercial building between Ross and Rodney Streets that was burglarized in May 2008: four businesses and a house of worship inside, entered after 1 p.m. on a Friday when, the paper said, many of the businesses had closed by 2 p.m. as merchants prepared for the Sabbath. The burglar took computers and phones, damaged the synagogue's lock with a crowbar but did not get in, and accounted for five of the ten Williamsburg burglaries that week. Here is how those reports translate into a business alarm.

Broadway under the el

Stores with gates and glass fronts below the tracks, back doors onto narrow yards or alleys, and apartments upstairs. The gate gets a contact, the door behind it gets a contact, and the back door is treated as the main way in. Shock sensors on the front glass are used only after a test with trains passing, and paired with inside motion.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Williamsburg: Questions Answered

Living with the trains and the noise

Our walk-up shakes every few minutes when a J or M train crosses Broadway. Over a year or two, does that constant shaking loosen sensors or wear them out, and how often should they be checked?

It can loosen them, and it is worth planning for. Constant vibration does not usually damage the electronics inside a contact or a motion detector, but it can slowly work an adhesive mount loose, shift the magnet half of a window contact a few millimeters away from its switch, or rattle a battery cover. On walls and frames that the el shakes, we fasten devices with small screws rather than tape wherever the surface allows, set the magnet gap tighter than usual, and choose sensors that report their own battery, signal and tamper status to the panel every day. That daily report is the real safeguard: a sensor that has slipped or lost contact shows up as a trouble notice in the app long before it would fail you during a break-in. We also look at the event history with you a few weeks after installation, because that is when any device that is reacting to the trains shows itself. After that, a check once a year is enough for most apartments; the panel's backup battery is usually replaced every three to five years, sooner if the app says it is weak.

Can the alarm wait until two different sensors agree before it calls anyone, so a single rattling window over Broadway doesn't send the police?

Yes, for the devices that noise and vibration can fool. Most professional panels let us group two sensors so that one of them on its own only produces a trouble notice on your phone, and a full alarm is raised only if the second one also trips within a set number of seconds. On a Broadway window the usual pair is a shock sensor on the glass and the motion detector covering that room: a train can shake the glass, but it cannot walk across the floor. We do not use the rule for door contacts or fire-escape window contacts, because an opened door or window is not ambiguous, and making it wait for a second sensor would only give an intruder more time. When we hand the system over, the app lists which sensors are paired and which raise an alarm alone, so nobody is surprised later.

On a block where sirens and car alarms sound all night, is a louder outdoor siren worth paying for?

Usually not, at least not as the main defense. A louder siren on a street full of noise is still easy for passers-by and neighbors to ignore, and in a rental an outdoor sounder needs the owner's permission and often the building's approval. Money spent on the siren does more good spent elsewhere: an indoor siren that is very loud inside the apartment, where it tells an intruder the opening was noticed; a voice keypad or second sounder so the people at home hear it; phone alerts with the sensor's name; and, for a business, central-station monitoring. If you want something visible from the street, a strobe or a lamp that switches on in a front window stands out in a way another siren does not, and it shows police which door is yours at night. Whatever is installed, the audible signal shuts off automatically within the time the city's Administrative Code allows.

We are often somewhere loud ourselves, at a show or on a train. Can alarm alerts reach a smartwatch so we feel them even when we cannot hear the phone?

Yes, on most current platforms. The alarm app sends a push notification to your phone, and if your phone is set to mirror that app's notifications to a paired watch, the alert arrives on your wrist as a vibration with the sensor's name. Nothing needs to be installed in the apartment for that; it is a setting on the phone and the watch, and we check it with you at handover by opening a window and watching the alert arrive. For alarms that must not be missed, the stronger backup is a second person on the alert list and, if you choose it, central-station monitoring, which calls people in order and does not depend on anyone noticing a single buzz.

Shops and offices after the 2026 break-ins

Police said the crew behind the 2026 break-ins sometimes used keys kept outside the businesses. We leave a key in a lockbox by our back door for the early baker. Can the alarm tell us when that box is opened?

Yes. A small wireless contact can be fitted inside most wall-mounted lockboxes, or a steel key box can be chosen that has room for one, so that every time the box opens the panel records it and can send an alert to the owner's phone. Set to report around the clock, it tells you when the box is opened at an hour the baker never arrives. The better step, where possible, is to stop leaving a key outside at all: a keypad lock or a smart lock on the back door, with a personal code for the baker that also disarms the alarm, means there is no key to find, and each opening is recorded with that person's name. Williamsburg365 reported that keys stored outside were one of three ways the 2026 suspects got in, alongside pried doors and broken glass; it is the only one of the three that costs almost nothing to remove.

Our Bedford Avenue building has several small offices behind one street door, like the building between Ross and Rodney Streets burglarized in 2008. Should there be one alarm for the building or one per tenant?

In most small multi-tenant buildings we recommend one system or one area per tenant, plus a clear agreement about the shared parts. Each business then has its own codes, its own call list and its own schedule, and a tenant who stays late never disarms a neighbor's office. The shared street door, stair and roof door are the hard part, because they belong to everyone and therefore to nobody. The cleanest arrangement is for the landlord to protect those common openings on a separate small system with alerts to the landlord and to one contact from each tenant; the next best is for one tenant's panel to cover them by written agreement. In the 2008 report, the burglar went through four businesses and tried a fifth inside the same building, which is what happens when the only protection is at the street. We sketch the floor and mark who protects which opening before we quote.

Apartments and shared doors

The street door of our walk-up is often propped open for deliveries. Can our apartment alarm let us know, even though that door belongs to the landlord?

Only with the landlord's agreement, because a sensor on the street door is a change to the owner's property. Many owners agree when a tenant offers to pay, since a contact on that door also tells the owner or the super when it has been left open. If the landlord agrees, a contact on the street door can be added to your panel as a separate zone that never sounds the siren but sends a notice when the door has stayed open longer than a few minutes. If the landlord does not agree, protect what is yours: a contact on your apartment door, armed whenever you leave and in home mode at night, and contacts on any of your windows that a fire escape or roof makes reachable. A propped street door matters most if your apartment door or those windows are not protected; once they are, the street door is a nuisance rather than a way in.

Reporting through the alarm panel, so a fire or gas problem reaches your phone and, with monitoring, the station, even when you cannot hear the detector.

Fobs for arming without a code at the door; a hidden panic button or a duress code for staff who work late in a shop.

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Williamsburg

We could not open Google's AI Overview or its related questions from our research environment for this page, so the claims below are ones we hear repeated by customers and see in general alarm advice and comparison sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is answered with what the Williamsburg reports and our own practice show.

"Glass-break sensors are the best way to protect windows."

They are a good way to protect fixed glass in a quiet room. In Williamsburg, many rooms are not quiet. The manufacturers say so themselves: Vivint's support page lists loud music or television, slammed doors and objects falling among the noises that can set its detector off, and Abode's warns that very loud music can trigger its sensor and that it should be mounted away from speakers and motors.

A front room facing the Broadway el, a living room against a bar's wall or a shop with its own sound system is exactly where that warning applies. A detector set to its default there will either false-alarm or be turned down until it no longer hears much at all.

For windows that open, contacts are better in every setting. For large fixed glass, we test a glass-break detector in place with the room's real noise and back it with motion; where the test fails, motion and contacts carry the job alone.

"A siren scares burglars off and alerts the neighbors."

Inside the apartment, a siren does tell an intruder that the opening was noticed. Outside, on a block where trains, traffic and car alarms are part of the background, it is unlikely to bring anyone to the door. The 2016 Lorimer Street tenant, watching television in the next room, did not know a burglar was in her bedroom until she walked in.

An alarm that depends on being heard by the street is designed for a different neighborhood. Here the siren is one signal among several, and the most important ones go to people's phones and, if chosen, to a monitoring station.

"A busy, well-lit neighborhood is safe enough without an alarm."

The 2026 reports are a direct answer. News 12 quoted a resident describing Williamsburg as "a little bit of a bubble" and another surprised that so many break-ins happened in an area so well trafficked and full of high-end stores. The crew still reached nine incidents in a single six-hour stretch on March 4.

Crowds on Bedford Avenue at midnight do not see a back door on a side street at 4 a.m., and the residents who live above busy stations, like the Marcy Avenue tenant in Brick Underground's account, describe a street so busy that one more noise or one more person barely registers.

"Renters can't have an alarm, and they lose it when they move."

Most Williamsburg alarms we install in rentals are wireless and sit entirely inside the apartment: a panel on a shelf or a small wall plate, contacts on the door and windows, one or two motion detectors. They come down with a few small holes to patch and go up again in the next apartment.

What a tenant does need is permission for anything that touches the landlord's building outside the unit, such as a sensor on the street door, an outdoor sounder or wiring in the hall. We put that list on the quote so it can go to the owner or the managing agent before the visit.

"An alarm system costs about the same everywhere."

National cost guides quote one broad range for the whole country. Our Williamsburg prices come from our Brooklyn list with no area surcharge, but what moves a quote here is specific: how many fire-escape, rear and roof-reachable openings there are, whether the front of the apartment faces the el, whether a loft's glass needs testing, and whether a tower's concrete calls for a cellular test or a repeater.

A two-window railroad flat on the Southside and a corner loft with twenty windows on the Northside are both "an apartment" to a national guide. They are not the same job, and they do not cost the same.

"Wireless sensors work anywhere."

Wireless sensors work in most Williamsburg buildings, but not automatically. Concrete floors in the post-2005 towers, long steel-framed lofts, and back rooms of brick buildings behind the el can all weaken a signal between a sensor and the panel, or between the panel and the cell tower.

We hold each sensor at its intended spot and read the signal at the panel before mounting it, and we test the panel's own cellular path before choosing where it goes. Where a reading is weak, a repeater or a different panel location fixes it.

In New York State, installing and servicing burglar alarms for others requires a license from the Department of State under General Business Law Article 6-D. Our license number, #12000287431, is printed on every quote and invoice, and you can check it with the state before booking.

A license is not a guarantee of good work, but it is the minimum. Ask any installer for the number, and ask who will actually come: a licensed company's own technician or a subcontractor.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Wait for the Train Before You Choose the Sensors

This is how we run a Williamsburg survey; it describes our method, not the story of one job. The technician walks the reachable openings first, starting with the windows onto the fire escape, the roof and the yard, and opens each one to see how it latches and whether an air conditioner or a gate sits in it. Then he stops and listens.

In a front room on Broadway, that means waiting for a train in each direction with a hand on the window frame and the panel's sensor reading on his phone. In a loft that shares a wall with a bar, it means asking when the music starts and, where possible, coming back at that hour. In a tower, it means standing at the panel's planned spot and reading the cellular signal through the concrete. Each room ends up on the plan as quiet, loud or shaking, and the plan follows from that: contacts everywhere that opens, motion wherever it will not see the street or a heater, glass-break only in the quiet rooms, and any shock sensor paired with a second device.

Last, he asks where everyone actually sits in the evening, and the announcement goes there, tested with the television on. The noise survey takes about ten minutes.

FAQ

Williamsburg Alarm Installation FAQ

Does an apartment whose front windows face the Broadway el cost more to alarm than one on a quiet side street?
+

Usually only a little, and sometimes not at all. The price is driven mainly by how many openings a burglar can reach, so a front room on Broadway with windows nobody can climb to adds little. Where facing the el changes the price is in the choice of sensors: a shock sensor on street-level glass is paired with a motion detector, which can add one device, and a glass-break detector is often left out, which removes one. Most Williamsburg apartments still fall inside the basic wireless range of $600 to $1,200 on our Brooklyn list, with central-station monitoring optional from $19.99 a month and city sales tax added on the written quote.

We usually get home after midnight on weekends and fumble at the door. Can the entry delay be longer late at night than in the daytime?
+

Most panels allow one entry delay per door rather than a different one for each hour, so the better fix is to make disarming easier. A key fob on the key ring disarms with one press at the door, the app can disarm the system from the corner before you reach the building, and a keypad placed just inside the door rather than down the hall saves several seconds. We set the entry delay itself long enough for whoever comes in slowest, usually between thirty and forty-five seconds for a walk-up, and no longer, because every extra second is also time for someone who forced the door. If you often come home in a group after a night out, give each person their own code so nobody is waiting for the one who knows it.

Most of the 2009 Southside burglaries came in through fire-escape windows that were simply unlocked. If we always keep ours locked, do we still need a sensor on it?
+

Yes. Locking the window is the right habit, and in 2009 the 90th Precinct's community affairs officer urged residents to do exactly that, but a lock only slows someone down and a sensor is what tells you it happened. A window latch can be forced or the glass broken in a few seconds, and windows get left unlocked on hot nights or after an air conditioner goes in. A contact on the fire-escape window reports any opening at all, armed while you are out and in home mode while you sleep or watch television, and it costs little compared with what is usually taken. Remember too that fire-escape windows must stay usable as a way out in a fire, so the sensor never locks or obstructs the window.

We sleep with earplugs or a white-noise machine because of the trains and the bars. Will we wake up if the alarm goes off at night?
+

You should, but we check rather than assume. An indoor siren in a small apartment is very loud, and we place it, or a second sounder, close to the bedroom rather than by the front door so it reaches you through earplugs and a fan. At handover we run it once with your white-noise machine on and your door closed so you can hear it for yourself. If one of you sleeps very deeply, the phone on the nightstand can be set to let alarm notifications override silent mode, and a smartwatch can add a vibration on the wrist. With central-station monitoring, the station also phones the people on your call list, so the alarm is not relying on the siren alone.
