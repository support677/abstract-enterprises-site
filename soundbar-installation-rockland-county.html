Three or four levels offset by half a storey. The family room is usually the lowest finished level, partly below grade, with an open railing or half-wall to the stairs. Sound travels up the stairwell freely, so bar and sub placement is planned for the whole house.

Raised Ranches

The front door opens to a landing between two full floors. The lower floor holds the den, often beside the garage and furnace room, with block or poured foundation wall to window height and framing above. The upstairs living room tends to be long, with the picture window on the street side.

Compliance callouts for Rockland businesses and homeowners

  • County home improvement licensing. Chapter 286 of the Laws of Rockland County requires anyone doing home improvement work on residential property in the county to hold a Home Improvement License from the Office of Consumer Protection; the current version took effect on May 14, 2026. The office's own FAQ warns that unlicensed contractors may not carry liability, disability or workers' compensation insurance, and offers an online database to check a contractor. Whether a particular soundbar job counts as home improvement is a question for that office; checking any installer's licence and insurance before work starts costs nothing.
  • Electrical work is separate. The same office licenses electricians and electrical inspection agencies. A new or moved outlet behind a TV is line-voltage electrical work for a licensed electrician; we do not add or move outlets. Bar depth plus bracket depth is what counts, so we measure both and either raise the bar above that band or keep it out of the path.

  • Volume in mixed-use buildings. Many Nyack, Suffern and Haverstraw storefronts have apartments above. Noise rules are set locally, so we set a sensible maximum volume on business bars and keep subwoofers off shared floors.

Split-levels, raised ranches and sound on half-levels

In our split-level the TV faces a half-wall that drops to the stairs — where does the sound go, and can we aim it better?

Much of it goes exactly where you'd expect: over the railing and up or down the stairwell. A soundbar fires most of its sound forward, and some bars also throw sound sideways off nearby walls to create width. When one of those side walls is replaced by an open railing, that part of the effect simply escapes into the stairwell, and the room can sound lopsided, with voices pulling toward the solid side.

Three things help. First, keep the bar centred on the screen and as close below it as the maker allows, so speech comes from the picture and not from one side. Second, if the bar's app has a room-calibration or room-size setting, run it with the furniture in place; it will usually narrow the effect to suit. Third, a rug on the landing and a full-height bookcase or curtain on the open side will do more than any setting. If the layout allows, swapping the TV to the wall that faces away from the stairs is the biggest single improvement, and we will say so at the walkthrough before anything is drilled.

The bedrooms are a half-flight above the family room — how do we keep a late movie from waking the house?

In a split-level, the family room and the bedrooms are often separated by one short run of stairs and a single floor of wood joists. Low frequencies pass through both easily, which is why the explosions reach the kids' rooms while the dialogue doesn't. Most of the fix is in how the bass is set up, not in the bar itself.

We place the subwoofer away from the wall shared with the stairwell and out of corners, where bass builds up, then set its level by ear from the seats rather than at the factory default, which is often generous. Most bars have a night or late-evening mode that compresses the loud peaks and lifts speech; we switch it on, show you where it lives in the remote or app, and, if the bar supports it, set a speech-enhancement mode so dialogue stays clear at lower volume. Before we leave we go upstairs with you and listen with the bedroom door closed. If the sub is still too present, a smaller bar without a separate subwoofer is often the more comfortable choice for a split-level household.

The lower-level TV wall is foundation block at the bottom and wood framing on top — which part carries the bracket?

This is the classic raised-ranch lower floor: concrete block or poured concrete up to about window height, then a wood-framed wall on top of the foundation. A soundbar sits low enough that it usually lands on the masonry, while a TV above it may land on the framing, so the two brackets can end up in different materials.

That's fine, as long as each is fixed into the right thing. On block we drill into the solid webs and use anchors rated for hollow masonry; on poured concrete, sleeve or wedge anchors; on the framing above, lag screws into the studs, with a plywood backer if the studs don't line up with the TV's bracket. The joint between the two materials is where cracks and moisture show up, so we keep the bracket a few inches clear of it. Because block has no cavity, the cables for both usually run down a paintable cord cover rather than inside the wall, and the masonry line on the price list ($75) applies to the bar.

Our 1960s family room still has wood paneling over furring strips — what holds a soundbar on that?

Not the paneling itself. Quarter-inch paneling is decorative, and the furring strips behind it are typically thin strips of wood nailed to block or to old studs at wide spacing. A bracket screwed only into paneling will pull out, and even a furring strip is a weak anchor for a bar that someone will lean on while dusting.

So we find out what is behind the strips. On masonry, we drill through the paneling into the block or concrete and anchor there, using spacers so the bracket doesn't crush the paneling against the wall. Over wood studs, we fix into the studs. Where neither lines up with where the bar needs to be, a painted or stained plywood backer board ($85) screwed across several solid points spreads the load and looks deliberate. If you plan to remove the paneling soon, tell us; it is far better to hang the bar after the new wall goes up than before.

There's a heating duct inside the wall behind our TV — can the cables still be hidden?

Sometimes. In many raised ranches and split-levels the furnace room backs onto the family room, and supply ducts or a return chase run up through the wall cavities on that side. A sheet-metal duct fills most of a stud bay, so an in-wall kit that needs a clear path from the TV down to the outlet may be blocked, or may only fit in the next bay over.

We scan the wall and, where the reading is unclear, make a small inspection hole where the in-wall kit's plate would go anyway. If the bay is clear, the $169 in-wall power and cable kit goes in as normal. If a duct is in the way, we shift the TV and bar a stud bay sideways where the room allows, or use a paintable cord cover instead. We never cut into or fasten through ductwork, and we don't run cables inside a duct.

Finishing, buying and selling Rockland houses

Can drilling anchor holes into a block foundation wall let water into the basement?

Not when the holes are placed sensibly. A soundbar bracket needs a few short anchors set into the inside face of the wall, a couple of inches deep, well above the floor. They do not pass through the wall to the soil side, and the exterior waterproofing or parging is untouched. Water that reaches a basement through block usually comes through cracks, the joint where the wall meets the floor slab, or the hollow cores themselves after heavy rain — not through a sealed anchor hole at TV height.

We still treat a foundation wall with respect. Before drilling we look for efflorescence, stains or cracks near the mark, and if there are signs of water we move the bracket or stop and tell you, because a wall that is already wet needs its own repair first. Anchors go into the solid webs of the block, not the hollow cores, and every hole is filled by its anchor. On a lower-level wall that has been sealed or painted with a waterproofing coating, we keep the hole count to the minimum the bracket needs.

We just bought a raised ranch with old speaker wire and a mystery wall plate behind the TV — can any of it be used?

Often some of it can. Rockland houses that were upgraded in the 1990s and 2000s frequently have speaker wire in the walls for a surround system that has since been removed, a coaxial plate from an old cable layout, and occasionally a network jack. None of that is needed by a soundbar itself, which connects to the TV with one HDMI cable, but the routes it took through the wall can be very useful.

We trace what's there with a tester, label anything that is live, and tell you what each run is good for. An existing network jack behind the TV is worth testing and reusing for a smart bar. Old speaker wire can serve as a pull line to bring a new HDMI or network cable through the same path. Coax can sometimes be used by the cable or internet provider. What we won't reuse is anything that turns out to be line-voltage; if a plate turns out to hide an outlet or an abandoned electrical box, that goes on the list for an electrician.

We're selling — should the soundbar and its bracket stay with the house?

That's a question for your contract and your agent rather than for us, but it helps to know how buyers and sellers usually see it. A TV and a soundbar are personal property; a bracket screwed into the wall is often treated as a fixture unless the contract says otherwise. Many sellers take the bar and leave the bracket, or take everything and leave the holes, which can surprise a buyer who expected a ready-to-use wall.

If you are taking the bar with you, we can take the bar and bracket down so the holes are clean and easy for a painter to fill; removing an old bracket is $45 on our list. If you are leaving it, we can make sure it is level, firmly fixed and connected to nothing that leaves with you, and write down the bar model and how it is wired for the buyer. Either way, decide before the final walkthrough.

Is a damp lower level a bad place for a soundbar and subwoofer?

A finished lower level with normal household humidity is fine. A room that smells musty, shows water marks on the block or needs a dehumidifier running all summer is less kind to electronics, wooden furniture and anything stored on the floor, and a subwoofer standing directly on a cold slab is the first thing to suffer if water ever comes in.

Check the operating conditions in your bar's manual, keep the dehumidifier running in the damp months, and get the subwoofer off the floor: a solid stand or isolation pad lifts it clear of the slab and helps the bass at the same time. We keep power bricks and connections off the floor and away from exterior block walls where condensation forms, and we won't mount anything over visible water staining until the source has been dealt with.

Low ceilings, shared walls and the network

With a seven-foot lower-level ceiling and a soffit over the TV wall, how do the TV and the bar both fit?

It takes a little planning, because height is the scarce resource in many Rockland lower levels. The comfortable viewing height for a screen is set by the seats, the bar wants to sit a few inches below the screen, and a soffit boxing in a duct or beam may take the top foot of the wall. On a short wall that can leave the TV pushed down and the bar almost on the console.

We start from the seated eye height and work outwards: TV centred close to eye level, bar just below with the gap its maker recommends (Sonos, for example, asks for at least 4 inches between an Arc Ultra and the screen), and both kept clear of the soffit's lower edge. If the numbers don't work on the wall, the bar goes on the console in front of the TV, which often sounds just as good in a low room. House Vocabulary

Rockland House Terms You'll Hear on a Soundbar Visit

Installers, inspectors and estate agents describe Rockland houses in their own shorthand. These are the terms that come up when a soundbar is being planned, in plain English.

  • Split-level. A house with three or more floors offset by half a storey, so each flight of stairs is short. The family room is usually on the lowest finished level.
  • Raised ranch (also high ranch or split entry). Two full floors with the front door on a landing between them; the upper floor holds the main living space and the lower floor, partly below grade, the den and garage.
  • Furring strips. Thin strips of wood fixed to a masonry or old stud wall to give paneling or drywall something to be nailed to. Not strong enough to hang a bracket from on their own.
  • Webs and cores. The solid walls and hollow centres of a concrete block. Anchors belong in the webs.
  • Soffit. A boxed-in section of ceiling, usually hiding a duct, pipe or beam. Common along one side of a lower-level family room, and a factor for up-firing Atmos bars.
  • Chase. A vertical space inside a wall that carries ducts, pipes or flues from floor to floor.
  • Blocking. A horizontal piece of lumber fixed between studs during construction or renovation, to give a TV or bar bracket a solid fixing point.
  • Home Improvement License. Rockland County's licence for contractors doing home improvement work on residential property, issued by the Office of Consumer Protection under Chapter 286.
  • Certificate of insurance (COI). A one-page proof of a contractor's liability and workers' compensation cover, often requested by townhouse associations and commercial landlords. Ours is free.

If a term on a listing sheet or an inspection report isn't here, send it to us with a photo. We will tell you whether it matters for the soundbar.
