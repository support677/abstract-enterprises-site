The Neighborhood

Why Flatiron Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

The blocks around the Flatiron Building have been rebuilt for new work more often than almost anywhere in Manhattan. They began as the Ladies' Mile, the carriage-trade shopping street of the late nineteenth century, with department stores along Sixth Avenue and Broadway and, later, loft buildings for the garment, toy and tabletop trades whose showrooms the district still remembers. In the 1990s the same lofts gave the district its other name, Silicon Alley, when internet and media start-ups and the venture firms backing them clustered here. Today the Flatiron NoMad Partnership counts more than 36 million square feet of commercial space and over 7,000 office tenants across its district, with tech, advertising, media and information firms the largest group.

What has changed is the pace and the size of the jumps. In 2025 office leasing in the Partnership's district passed 3.8 million square feet and vacancy fell from 15 to 12 percent. In the first quarter of 2026 the fintech Ramp expanded to 285,300 square feet at 28-40 West 23rd Street, the largest Midtown South lease of the quarter; the AI company Clay took 163,000 square feet at 11 Madison Avenue and brought the building to fully leased; Harvey AI expanded at One Madison Avenue. Brokers now describe Park Avenue South and the Madison Square edge as the centre of New York's AI office market, while the side streets toward Sixth Avenue still trade small loft floors and pre-built suites to companies that are just getting started.

For anyone wiring these buildings, that pattern matters more than any single statistic. A growing company in Flatiron rarely stays on one floor for long. It takes a suite, then the rest of the floor, then the floor above or a block of space two floors up; it sublets the part it has outgrown or hands it back to a landlord who rebuilds it for the next tenant. Each move asks the same question of the building: is there a tested, documented path from the room where the carriers land to this floor, and can it be extended to the next one without starting from nothing?

In a 1900s loft the honest answer is often "not yet." Many of these buildings were designed for freight, sewing machines and stock, not for networks, and their telephone closets and sleeves have been filled and emptied by decades of tenants. Our job is to find the path that exists, add what is missing, and lay it out so that the next jump — up a floor, across a demising wall, into a lab room — is a patch rather than a project. Teams moving into new floors often add door access and cameras at the same time, which is why our Manhattan structured cabling team usually walks the floor with us.

What We Install

Fiber Optic Installation Services in Flatiron

Most Flatiron fiber work is short and vertical, and it is rarely finished for good: a floor today, the floor above next year, a lab room or a sublet after that. These are the jobs we are asked for most between Park Avenue South and Sixth Avenue.

Entry-Room-to-Floor Links

Strands from the room where the carriers land, usually in the cellar, up the building's riser to an enclosure on your floor, labelled to your suite rather than to a company name.

Expansion-Floor Ties

A link from your existing floor to the one you have just added — above, below or two floors away — so both run as one network with one firewall.

Spec-Suite Stubs

For landlords: a small enclosure in each pre-built suite with tested strands back to a building panel, so a new tenant's provider or IT team patches in on day one.

Floor Closets on Open Lofts

Cabinets and wall enclosures placed on loft floors that have no back-of-house, with power, ventilation and a fiber feed planned around exposed brick and ceilings.

Engineering and Lab Links

Dedicated strands from desks to a server room, test bench or hardware lab, kept separate from the office network where the team needs it.

Re-Demising Splits

When a full floor becomes two suites, or two suites become one, we divide or join the fiber at the enclosure rather than pulling new cable through the new wall.

Ground-Floor Tenants

Links for restaurants, showrooms and stores along Fifth Avenue, Broadway and Sixth Avenue, from the cellar entry to the back office and the front of house.

Fusion Splicing and Termination

Pigtails fused on site, every end inspected under a scope before it is plugged in, and connector types matched to the switches your IT team has chosen.

Testing and Records

A loss reading for each strand, a trace wherever splices or length justify one, and one strand table written for both the landlord and the tenant.

Park Avenue South

Renovated prewar towers where AI and fintech tenants sign full floors. Riser capacity is controlled by the landlord, and requests usually go through the managing agent's engineer.

Madison Square Park Edge

Fifth Avenue and Broadway Core

Loft offices above flagship stores and restaurants, including 200 Fifth Avenue, the former International Toy Center, now retail, office and residential space.

Restaurants, food halls and experiential retail facing the pedestrian plazas, where back offices, kitchens and outdoor seating all want a connection.

Compliance callouts for Flatiron fiber work

  • Department of Buildings. A fiber job on its own is low-voltage work and is not normally filed with DOB. If the same project builds a new telecom room, moves walls or cuts a new opening through a rated slab, that part is handled under the building's or the fit-out contractor's own filing.
  • Historic districts. Most of the neighbourhood lies within the Ladies' Mile Historic District, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission's storefront guidance says conduit and wiring attached to a building or extended to outdoor dining need its permit. Keeping the entry and the route inside avoids the question.
  • Fire separations. Each opening we make through a rated floor or wall is closed with a listed firestop system and photographed, and old unsealed sleeves found in a loft riser are reported to the owner.
  • Jacket ratings. Cable between floors carries a riser (OFNR) rating; cable in a ceiling space used for return air carries a plenum (OFNP) rating.
  • Stairwells. Interior exit stairs may only contain what serves the stair under the city code, so no route we design uses one.
  • Noise and hours. The city's construction-noise rules set weekday hours of 7 a.m. to 6 p.m.; office landlords usually narrow that further for coring, and we follow whichever rule is stricter.
Growth Spurts

When a Flatiron Company Takes the Floor Above

The most common Flatiron fiber call is not a new building or a new tenant. It is an existing tenant that has run out of room. A team that signed for one floor two years ago now has people sitting in the kitchen, and the landlord has offered the floor above, a half floor three storeys up, or a suite in the building next door. The leases signed in 2025 and early 2026 show the pattern at the largest scale — Ramp growing through the 28-40 West 23rd Street complex, Harvey AI expanding at One Madison — but the same thing happens every month on the side streets, at a few thousand square feet at a time.

Joining two floors sounds simple and often is. When the new floor is directly above and the building's closets stack, a short riser link between two enclosures puts both floors on one network. It becomes harder when the floors are not adjacent, when the closets do not line up, or when the only sleeve between them is already full of other tenants' cable. Then the route may have to go down to the building's entry room and back up again, or the landlord may need to assign a new sleeve, and the time to ask is before the new lease is signed, not after the furniture arrives.

We therefore treat every first floor as the start of a series. When a company takes its first Flatiron space, we ask about headcount plans and any expansion rights in the lease, and we size the enclosure and strand count for at least one more floor. Spare strands in the same cable cost little compared with a second pull; a larger enclosure costs little compared with replacing a full one. If the landlord has given the tenant a right of first offer on the floor above, the proposal can show the extra link as an option, priced but not built.

Shrinking is part of the cycle too. Manhattan's sublet supply has tightened sharply since 2024, but companies still hand back or sublet part of a floor when plans change. A fiber layout that ends in one enclosure per suite, with each suite's strands labelled to the suite rather than to the company, makes it straightforward to split a floor between a tenant and a subtenant, or to return half a floor to the landlord without cutting the other half off.

The same thinking applies across a block. Some Flatiron companies spread through neighbouring buildings rather than up one stack, and private fiber cannot simply be strung across a city street. In those cases the realistic link between buildings is usually a carrier circuit, and our work is to give each building a clean, tested path from its own entry room to the company's floors, so the circuit lands somewhere tidy at both ends.

Pre-Built Suites

Spec Suites: Fiber for a Floor Built Before the Tenant Signs

Walk the side streets off Sixth Avenue and many of the listings say the same thing: pre-built, move-in ready, furniture available. Metro Manhattan's Flatiron guide notes a growing number of fully built-out spec suites "with furniture and cabling already in place so you can move in fast", and says many value-tier landlords will hand over a turnkey suite. Elsewhere in the country, brokers interviewed by LoopNet describe the same strategy: the landlord pays up front to build the space so the delivery timeline is quicker, and in return is often open to shorter leases of three to five years.

For a landlord, the fiber question in a spec suite is how to make the next tenant's connection fast without guessing at their equipment. Our usual answer is a small locked enclosure in each suite with a handful of tested strands back to a building panel near the entry room, labelled by suite number. The tenant's provider, or its own IT team, then patches in at both ends rather than asking for a new pull through someone else's ceiling. The enclosure costs far less than the delay of a missing path, and it outlasts the furniture.

For a tenant, the question is what "cabling already in place" actually covers. In most suites it means copper from a small rack or wall cabinet to the desks and access points — useful, but separate from how the internet reaches the suite. Before signing, it is worth asking three things: where the suite's network cabinet is fed from, whether that feed is fiber or copper and who owns it, and whether the landlord will let the tenant's provider use the building's riser. If the answers are vague, a short survey before the lease is cheaper than a surprise after it.

Spec suites also change shape. Landlords combine two small suites for a growing tenant, or split a floor that a large tenant has given back. If each suite has its own enclosure and its own strands, these changes happen at the patch panel. If the suites share one cabinet hidden in one of them, every change of tenant means negotiating access to somebody else's office.

Two Markets

Trophy Corridor or Side-Street Loft: How the Fiber Job Differs

Metro Manhattan's guide to the district makes a point every tenant should hear before touring: Flatiron is a premium AI corridor and a value loft district wearing one name. Savills tracked the Flatiron submarket at an average of $64.66 a square foot in late 2025 against $77.50 for Park Avenue South, and marketed rents at the Madison Square towers run higher still. The fiber work differs between the two just as sharply as the rent.

In the trophy and premium buildings, the landlord usually runs a managed riser and an entry room where carriers hand off. A tenant's fiber job there is about following rules well: submitting a route and cable details for approval, booking freight time, providing insurance certificates in the building's form, working in the hours the building allows and leaving the closets the way the chief engineer wants them. The physical path is usually clear; the paperwork decides the schedule.

In the side-street lofts, the paperwork is lighter and the physical path is the problem. The building may have one elevator, a riser sleeve shared by every tenant since the 1990s and a cellar where several generations of providers have left boxes on the wall. The landlord may be a family owner who knows the building well but has never drawn its risers. Here the job begins with tracing, clearing and labelling, and the proposal has to show what will be removed as well as what will be installed.

Between the two sit the Fifth Avenue, Broadway and Ladies' Mile loft buildings that landlords have renovated with new lobbies, mechanical systems and spec suites. These often have a better riser than the side streets and fewer rules than the towers, which makes them good places to build fiber properly once: a building panel near the entry room, assigned strands to each floor, spares, and a record kept by the managing agent.

The class label on a listing says little about any of this. As the same guide puts it, "Class describes the building. Rent tier describes what tenants pay." Neither describes the riser, and we recommend asking about it directly before a lease is signed.

Ladies' Mile

Fiber Behind 440 Protected Facades

The Ladies' Mile Historic District takes in 440 buildings on 28 blocks and parts of blocks, roughly from 15th to 24th Street and from Park Avenue South to west of Sixth Avenue. It was designated in May 1989 after a long campaign by community groups and the Historic Districts Council, and it is made up mostly of multi-storey store and loft buildings. North of 24th Street the Madison Square North Historic District continues the pattern around the park.

For fiber work, designation matters most at the edges of a building. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates work that affects the exterior, including storefronts, and its storefront guidance is explicit that conduit and wiring attached to a building or run out to sidewalk seating need a permit. Interior work that does not affect the exterior generally does not, and the Commission's staff will say whether a specific job does. In practice that means the fiber for a Ladies' Mile building should enter and travel inside: through the cellar, up existing risers and across ceilings, not up the facade or along a cast-iron storefront.

Carriers sometimes propose the quick route — a box on the front wall, a conduit up the face of the building, a cable along a cornice. In a historic district that route can trigger a permit application and a delay, and it can leave the owner responsible for equipment the Commission never approved. We plan the entry with the carrier, keep it below grade or inside, and document it so the owner can show where everything is.

Inside, many Ladies' Mile floors are what tenants come for: tall ceilings, large windows, cast columns and exposed brick. Those features make cable visible. Our routes follow the column lines and the edges of ceiling bays, use tray or J-hooks painted to match when a ceiling stays open, and keep enclosures in the core rather than on a feature wall. A route that disappears is worth a few extra feet of cable.

The Street

Plazas, Bike Lanes and a Curb With Few Places to Stop

Since 2008 the roadbed where Broadway crosses Fifth Avenue has been given over to pedestrian plazas, and the Flatiron NoMad Partnership says that in good weather upwards of 100,000 people a day stop there. In 2021 Broadway from 21st to 23rd Street became what the Partnership calls the city's largest shared street, and the Broadway Vision programme has since turned more blocks north of Madison Square into plaza and slow-street space. For a crew arriving with reels, ladders and a fusion splicer, that means the front door of many buildings is no longer a place a van can stop.

The city's own design team spells it out. In a September 2026 update to Community Board 5 on the permanent reconstruction of the Flatiron plazas and Worth Square, the Department of Design and Construction and DOT noted that the block of Broadway between 22nd and 23rd Streets carries about 70 vehicles in the morning peak and 90 in the evening, has no major property entrances, loading docks or driveways, and keeps access through Fifth Avenue and 22nd Street; DOT, the presentation says, can work with property owners to maintain critical access for repairs and move-ins. Construction of the permanent plazas is scheduled to begin in the city's 2028 fiscal year.

So we plan the arrival before the install. On the walk we note where the building's freight entrance actually is — often on a side street — which loading signs apply on that block and at what hours, and whether the managing agent has a standing arrangement for deliveries. Reels and tools come in through that entrance at the agreed time, the van moves to legal parking, and anything that would block a plaza or a bike lane stays off it. When a job needs a long piece of equipment on a plaza-fronting block, we ask the owner to raise it with DOT early.

The sidewalks are busy in their own right. The Partnership's 2022 counts showed daily visitor and employee foot traffic recovering toward 2019 levels, and by late 2025 employee visits stood at 81 percent of pre-pandemic levels. Sidewalk cellar doors are opened only with a crew member standing at them, and work that could spill onto the pavement is done early in the morning, before the lunchtime crowds around the plazas and Madison Square Park.

July 2018

The Day Fifth Avenue Closed

On July 19, 2018, a steam pipe beneath Fifth Avenue at 21st Street burst, sending a column of vapour above the Flatiron District. More than 25 nearby buildings were evacuated, officials confirmed that the pipe's insulation contained asbestos, and people caught nearby were told to bag their clothes and shower. Fifth Avenue between 19th and 22nd Streets was expected to stay closed for several days while buildings were tested and cleaned. The Flatiron NoMad Partnership later named the explosion, alongside the pandemic, as one of the crises in which it worked with city agencies to help businesses recover.

Nobody can design a fiber plant around an event like that, but it shows two things that matter in this neighbourhood. The first is that access can disappear without warning. A company whose network records live only in one person's head, or in a binder in a building that is closed for a week, cannot tell a provider or an insurer what it has. A strand table and test results held by the owner and the tenant, outside the building as well as inside it, make it possible to plan the restart before the doors reopen.

The second is that old materials are part of the building. Loft buildings of this age may contain asbestos in pipe insulation, floor tiles or old fireproofing, and the owner is responsible for knowing where. Before any coring or ceiling work, we ask whether the building has a survey, avoid disturbing suspect material, and stop and report if we find something unexpected rather than drilling through it. That is slower for an hour and much faster than the alternative.

After a closure, the first visit back is a check rather than a guess. Connectors are inspected and cleaned, every strand that matters to the business is re-tested against the original results, and any change is written down. Where the original results exist, that comparison takes very little time; where they do not, it is the first thing we create.

Ground Floors

Restaurants, Showrooms and Flagship Stores

Flatiron's ground floors are as busy as its offices. The Partnership's 2022 report counted more than 1,100 ground-floor businesses, more than 400 food and beverage establishments — over one business in three — and more than 70 furniture and design stores; it noted that nearly 40 percent of the district's retail shops specialise in furniture and interior design, and that Porcelanosa chose 202 Fifth Avenue for its US flagship showroom. Eataly's first US market opened here in 2010 and the LEGO store in 2014.

Restaurants need dependable links for point-of-sale terminals, kitchen display screens, online-order tablets, music, cameras and a guest network, often split between a front of house at street level and a kitchen or office in the cellar. Fiber earns its place when the cellar office, the dining room and an upper-floor private room are far apart or separated by heavy kitchen equipment; copper remains the right answer for short runs inside one level. Where a restaurant has outdoor seating, the Landmarks Commission's storefront guidance requires a permit for conduit or wiring run from the building to the seating area, so outdoor terminals are usually best served wirelessly from inside.

Design showrooms have a different rhythm. Product displays, screens and lighting controls move when the collection changes, and the sales floor has to look finished every day. The answer is to bring fiber to a small enclosure in the back of house, then distribute copper and wireless from there to floor boxes and ceiling points chosen with the showroom's designer, so a new layout means moving a patch cord rather than opening a wall.

Flagship and experiential retail — the Partnership lists attractions such as Harry Potter New York and The FRIENDS Experience — adds ticketing, queue screens and large video displays to the usual retail systems. Those tenants generally arrive with their own network design; what they need locally is a clean, tested path from the building's entry room to their back office, finished before opening day, and a contractor who can work inside the landlord's rules.

Engineering Floors

Fiber for Engineering Teams in Loft Buildings

Flatiron's tenant list now reads like a technology directory. The Partnership's 2022 report named IBM, Mastercard, Sony, Capital One, SeatGeek, FanDuel, Betterment and Dropbox among the companies with headquarters or major offices in the district; Metro Manhattan's 2026 guide lists Microsoft at 122 Fifth Avenue and a run of AI firms on Park Avenue South and around Madison Square. Brokers say the loft floor plates suit fast-growing engineering teams, and the tall ceilings and open floors are certainly popular.

An engineering floor loads the building's cabling differently from a sales office. There are more devices per person — laptops, docks, test phones, development boards — and some teams keep workstations or small servers in the office for jobs that are faster or cheaper to run locally. Big files move between desks and a server room many times a day. None of that needs exotic cable, but it does reward a short, clean path: fiber from the floor enclosure to a server or lab room, enough strands for a second link, and switches whose uplinks are fiber rather than copper.

Most such teams also depend heavily on the internet circuit, because their main systems run in the cloud. A faster circuit only helps if the path from the entry room to the floor and from the floor closet to the desks can carry it. We regularly find the bottleneck in between: an old copper link from the cellar, a small switch in a ceiling, or a single access point covering a loft floor through brick partitions. Fixing that path is usually the first and cheapest improvement.

Hardware companies add a further requirement. Labs, test benches and prototype rooms often need a network that is isolated from the office, either for security or because test equipment misbehaves on a busy network. Separate strands to the lab, ending in their own enclosure, let the IT team keep that network physically apart while the building only has to provide one riser.

Conversions

From Office Floors to Condominiums: The Flatiron Building and Its Neighbours

Flatiron's most famous office building is leaving the office market. The Flatiron Building at 175 Fifth Avenue, completed in 1902 and used as offices for more than a century, is being converted to condominiums. Plans filed with the state Attorney General's office and reported by The Real Deal in June 2025 describe 38 condominium units, two commercial units and 40 storage units, with the Brodsky Organization and Sorgente Group among the developers; earlier reports had suggested about 60 units. The district has done this before: the Partnership points to 212 and 225 Fifth Avenue and 1107 Broadway, now 10 Madison Square West, as earlier office-to-residential conversions, and its 2022 report counted more than 35,000 residents in the district.

A conversion changes who the fiber serves. An office building's risers were laid out for tenants who each bought their own circuits. A condominium needs a residential riser that lets several providers serve individual units, a building network for the lobby, doors, cameras and amenity rooms, and a clear division between what the condominium owns and what each unit owner owns. The time to settle those questions is during design, when walls are open and the developer still controls every space.

Two details recur in converted lofts. First, old office closets rarely sit where a residential riser needs to be, so the new path is often a fresh sleeved core beside the plumbing stacks, sealed at every floor. Second, ownership changes at turnover: the developer builds the telecom room, then hands it to a condominium board. A strand table, test results and as-built drawings delivered at that moment save the board from having to trace its own building later.

Buyers can help themselves too. Before closing on a unit in a converted building, it is reasonable to ask how service reaches the apartment, how many providers can serve the building, and who maintains the riser after the sponsor leaves. The answers belong in the building's records, not only in a sales brochure.

Fiber Optic Installation in Flatiron: Questions Answered

Questions grouped the way Flatiron jobs tend to reach us: from companies signing short leases and planning to grow, from loft floors that were never built for networks, and from the street, the plazas and the buildings turning residential.

Short leases and growing teams

We signed a three-year lease on a pre-built suite — how much fiber is worth paying for in space we may outgrow?

Pay for the path, not for a monument. A short lease justifies a tested link from the building's entry room or riser panel to a small enclosure in your suite, with a few spare strands, because that is what lets your provider and IT team work quickly and what you will want again on your next floor. It rarely justifies building a large room or pulling a big backbone that only serves you. Ask the landlord whether a building-owned stub can be installed instead: it serves you now and the next tenant later, which is often a good reason for the landlord to share the cost.

Our headcount could double within a year — can the fiber be planned now for a floor we have not leased yet?

Yes, and it is cheaper to plan it now than to retrofit later. If your lease includes an expansion option, a right of first offer or simply a landlord who has said the floor above may free up, we size the enclosure and the strand count on your current floor for one more link and price that link as a separate, optional line. Spare strands can be parked and labelled in the riser so they reach the likely floor without being terminated. Nothing is built in space you do not control, but the hard part — capacity in the right sleeve — is reserved in the drawing the managing agent approves.

We own the building and want to split a full floor into two spec suites — how should the fiber be divided?

Give each future suite its own small enclosure and its own strands from the building panel, even if one tenant might later take both. Put the enclosures where the demising wall will not trap one inside the other suite, label them by suite number, and keep the building's own systems — cameras, door access, sensors — on separate strands that do not run through either tenant's space. If the floor is later recombined, the two enclosures are joined at the panel; if it is split again, nothing has to be moved. The work is modest and it removes one of the most common reasons a suite sits unlet: nobody knows how to get service into it.

We are leaving a coworking membership for our first floor of our own — what fiber did the coworking operator handle that we now need?

Everything between the street and your desks. In a coworking space the operator owns the internet circuits, the riser link, the floor network and the Wi-Fi, and you simply connect. On your own floor you will need an internet contract, a tested path from the entry room to your floor, a network cabinet with power and ventilation, copper or wireless to the desks, and somebody to look after it. We handle the fiber path and the cabinet's fiber side, work alongside whichever IT provider you choose, and leave a record you can hand to them. Book the walk as soon as the lease is close, because carrier circuits take time to order.

Loft floors that were never built for networks

Our loft floor is open, with brick walls and an exposed ceiling — where does the network cabinet go when there is no closet?

Next to the building core, where the riser and power already are, and inside a small enclosure or partition rather than on open floor. Most loft floors have some fixed elements by the elevator and stair — a toilet block, a duct shaft, a former telephone closet — and a cabinet tucked beside them shortens the fiber run from the riser and keeps cable off feature walls. It needs a dedicated circuit, some ventilation and a lockable door. If the design calls for no visible equipment at all, a shallow closet built into a new partition near the core does the same job without spoiling the room.

Our building has one elevator for people and freight — how is a fiber pull scheduled around a single car?

By agreeing short, fixed slots with the managing agent and doing the heavy moves outside office hours. A fiber job does not need much freight: a reel, a ladder, a splicer and a few boxes. We bring those up in one booked trip early in the morning, keep them on your floor for the length of the job, and use the stairs only for hand tools. Pulls that cross several floors are scheduled so the car is not held between floors while other tenants wait. In buildings where the elevator is also the only route for residents, we confirm the slot a day before.

Our AI team runs GPU workstations in the office — does that change the fiber between our floors?

It can change how much capacity the uplinks need, not the type of glass. Engineers who move model checkpoints and data sets between workstations and a local server will notice a slow link between floors before anyone else does. We plan single-mode strands with spares, so the IT team can raise speeds by changing optics rather than cable, and we keep the path between the workstations' switch and any server room short. The heat and power those machines need are a separate question for the landlord and your electrician; fiber itself adds neither, which is one reason it suits a crowded closet.

Our hardware lab needs a network that never touches the office network — can fiber give it a separate path?

Yes. The simplest way is dedicated strands from the floor enclosure, or straight from the building panel, to an enclosure in the lab, used only by the lab's equipment. Physically separate glass is easy for an auditor or a security reviewer to understand, and it means a misbehaving test device cannot flood the office switches. We label the lab strands distinctly, document which ports they occupy at each end, and leave the decision about firewalls and routing to your IT team.

The street, the plazas and residential conversions

Our restaurant faces the Broadway plaza — can a cable be run out to terminals at our outdoor seating?

Not casually. In a historic district the Landmarks Preservation Commission's storefront guidance says conduit or wiring run from a building to an outdoor dining area at the sidewalk or roadway requires a permit, and any element attached to the storefront needs one too. Most restaurants avoid the issue by using wireless card terminals at outdoor tables, served by an access point just inside the window that is fed by cable from the back office. We install the indoor side; if you still want a fixed outdoor connection, the permit comes first.

The city plans to rebuild the Flatiron plazas from 2028 — will that work affect the fiber entering buildings along Broadway?

The plaza reconstruction is a city project in the public street, and the carriers and utilities whose cables run under it coordinate with the city directly; we cannot speak for them. What an owner can do is make sure the building's side is documented: where each provider's conduit enters, which entry room it lands in and what it serves. If street work ever disturbs a service, that record makes it quicker to show the provider what was lost. The city's September 2026 presentation also says DOT can work with property owners to keep critical access for repairs and move-ins, which is worth raising early if a job overlaps construction.

We are buying a condominium in a converted Flatiron office building — what should we ask about how internet reaches the unit?

Ask four things: how many providers can serve the building today, how their service reaches each floor, whether there is spare pathway in the residential riser, and who maintains that riser after the developer hands the building to the board. The offering plan and the sponsor's engineers should be able to answer. If the building has fiber to the units, ask for the test results; if service still arrives over older office cabling, ask whether a residential riser is planned. A clear answer before closing is worth more than a promise in a brochure.

A Connectivity Checklist for a Flatiron Lease

Most tenants tour for light, ceilings and location, and rightly so. The checklist below takes ten minutes during a second visit and saves weeks after move-in. A landlord or broker who can answer it quickly usually has a building that is easy to connect.

  • Where do the carriers land? Ask to see the entry room and which providers are already in it. A building with several providers present gives you a choice and usually a faster order.
  • How does service reach this floor? A building panel with assigned strands is best; a shared sleeve with spare space is workable; "the last tenant had something" means a survey.
  • Who controls the riser? In towers the managing agent assigns capacity and approves routes; in smaller lofts the owner may decide on the spot. Know which before you plan a date.
  • What does the suite's "cabling" include? Copper to desks, a cabinet, a fiber stub, or all three — and who owns each after you leave.
  • What happens at expansion? If the lease gives rights to more space, ask whether the riser can reach it and whether the landlord will let capacity be reserved now.
  • What must be removed at the end? Restoration clauses sometimes require a tenant to take out its cabling. Knowing that now changes what is worth installing.
  • Is the building in a historic district? In the Ladies' Mile it almost certainly is, which keeps entries and conduit off the facade.

We are happy to walk a building with a tenant or broker before a lease is signed and put the answers in writing. If the honest answer is that the space will connect easily without us, we say so.

The handover package has three parts. A drawing traces the route from the entry room to each enclosure. A strand table lists every strand by suite, floor or building system and marks the spares. The test file holds the loss reading for each strand and any OTDR traces. The owner or managing agent keeps the master copy, the paying tenant gets its own, a one-page summary is taped inside each enclosure door, and everything is emailed so a closed building never locks the records in.

When a suite changes hands, the landlord updates the table rather than calling a contractor. When a strand is reassigned, the label and the record change together. It is a small habit, and it is what stops a loft riser from filling up with another generation of unknown cable.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Flatiron Lofts

Three habits for loft buildings that change tenants often (our routine, not a report on any one address)

First, we ask about the next floor before we touch the first. A growing tenant's second link is cheapest when its strands are already in the cable and its capacity is already on the landlord's drawing. The question takes a minute on the walk.

Second, we label by suite, not by company. Companies in this neighbourhood move, merge and rename; suite numbers stay. A label that says "8 North" still makes sense after the tenant has gone.

Third, we photograph every opening we seal and every closet we leave. Loft risers are shared, and when something changes six months later, a dated photograph settles who did what faster than any conversation.

Do you also take fiber work in NoMad, Gramercy, Union Square and the blocks north of Madison Square Park?

Can our landlord's tenant-improvement allowance pay for the fiber?
+

Often it can, if the work letter allows it. Allowances usually cover improvements that stay with the space, and a tested link or enclosure serving the suite normally fits that description, while equipment you take with you may not. We can split the proposal into lines that match the work letter so the landlord's share and yours are clear. Check the wording with your broker or attorney before relying on it.

Can fiber link our floor to the building's shared amenity floor or conference centre?
+

Only with the landlord's agreement, because the amenity floor is building space. Most landlords run their own network there and give tenants guest access. Where a building wants tenants to use their own networks in bookable rooms, the cleaner design is a building-owned link from the riser panel to the amenity floor with ports the managing agent can assign, which we can install for the owner.

If our company is acquired and moves out mid-lease, can the fiber stay for the subtenant or assignee who takes over?
+

Usually, provided the lease and the landlord allow the assignment or sublease. Fiber labelled by suite rather than by company transfers easily: we re-test the strands, update the labels and the strand table, and give the incoming tenant and the landlord the same records. Anything the lease requires you to remove at the end of the term should be agreed in the transfer papers.

Can the strand map come as a spreadsheet our office-management software can import?
+

Yes. Alongside the PDF drawing and test results we can supply the strand table as a spreadsheet or CSV file, with one row per strand showing its cable, both end ports, the suite or system it serves, whether it is spare and its tested loss. Tell us the column layout your software expects and we will match it.
