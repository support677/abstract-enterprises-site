The Neighborhood

Why Silver Beach Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Silver Beach houses are small, close together, built in several different eras, and set right beside open water, and each of those facts changes how a network cable gets from the provider's line to the room where it is needed. A real estate listing for the cooperative describes Silver Beach Gardens as a community of 451 single-family homes south of the Cross Bronx Expressway. Other accounts give smaller numbers: Our Town put the community at 350 homes when it incorporated around 1972, and a former resident's memoir speaks of about three hundred houses squeezed together on the bluff. Weichert's neighborhood profile counts about 2,480 people in 952 households, though its boundary may reach beyond the gate. We do not try to reconcile those numbers, because the shape of the place is the same in all of them: a few hundred detached houses, most of them modest, packed onto a small piece of shoreline with one way in.

The history explains why so many of those houses were never planned for wiring of any kind. Wikipedia's article on Throggs Neck records that the Stephenson family's farm was sold in 1795 to Abijah Hammond, whose mansion later became the offices of the Silver Beach Garden Corporation, and that the Peters and Sorgenfrel families formed Silver Beach Garden as a summer colony of bungalows, naming most of its streets for flowers and trees on the old estate and the community itself for the color of the beach at low tide. The Bronx Times has described Silver Beach and neighboring Edgewater Park as starting out as tent colonies that became permanent homes in the 1930s. Splice Today describes the houses along Indian Trail as tiny homes originally built as summer dwellings and winterized since. A house that began as a place to spend July with the windows open was built without a telephone jack, let alone a data jack, and everything electronic it has today was added later by whoever was selling a service at the time.

That leaves layers inside the walls. A resident who moved into a bungalow on Holly Place in 1939 remembered, in a memoir posted on Bronx Board, that the walls stopped two feet short of the ceilings and that a single kerosene heater tried to warm the whole house. Since then, houses like that have been insulated, extended, re-sided and rewired, usually one project at a time. Some have been replaced outright: a listing for 74 Aster Place describes a house that was knocked down and rebuilt in 1999, and Redfin's Silver Beach results include newer houses such as one on Mullan Place built in 2018. So on a single street a cabling crew can meet an original cottage with almost no hollow space, a cottage whose attic was added to when a second room went on, and a framed house from the last twenty years with deep cavities and a closet a builder may already have prepared for wiring. Each one needs a different route, and the only way to know which one you have is to look.

Size matters more than people expect. Listings describe houses of about 900 square feet, and Our Town noted that the bungalows were built closer and closer together over the years. In a house that small, most people assume one router in the living room is enough, and sometimes it is. What usually breaks that assumption is not the size of the house but what surrounds it: the walls and floors added in different decades, a kitchen or bathroom wall full of pipes between the router and the back bedroom, and the dozens of neighboring wireless networks a few feet away on every side. A wired connection to the desk, the television and one or two access points takes most of that uncertainty out of the house. It also lets the router live somewhere sensible instead of wherever the provider's technician happened to stop.

The way Silver Beach is owned shapes the job too. The Neighborhoods newsletter explains that residents own their homes but lease the land from the cooperative, and a post on Appraisers Forum adds that the houses all share the same block and lot. A guide to New York co-op alterations by Milton Coste notes that any Buildings Department application needs authorization from the owner of the building, and in a co-op that owner is the corporation. Most indoor data cabling never reaches the Buildings Department at all, because New York City's code, as summarized by UpCodes, exempts wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data from the electrical permit requirement when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Where the work does affect something the cooperative controls, such as the outside of the house, a trench across ground the corporation owns, or a shared building, we treat the co-op office as part of the approval and confirm it before the crew arrives rather than afterward.

The utilities are worth a look as well. Splice Today noted that Silver Beach's street signs are wooden boards nailed onto the telephone poles, which tells you that much of the phone and cable plant here still runs overhead. In practice that usually means a provider's line drops from a pole to a fitting on the side of the house, enters through a hole someone drilled years ago, and ends at a box or a modem just inside. That point is where the provider's responsibility stops and the household's begins, and in many small houses it sits in exactly the wrong place: on a kitchen wall, behind a couch, or near the floor. BroadbandNow reports that fiber service reaches 84.7 percent of the Bronx through Verizon Fios and Optimum, but we found no published coverage data for Silver Beach itself, so we never promise which service a particular house can order. What we can promise is that once the line is inside, the cable from there to every room belongs to the house and can be planned properly.

Then there is the water. A National Weather Service summary of coastal flood impacts records that 200 people were evacuated from flooded areas of Edgewater Park and Silver Beach in Throgs Neck and that beach bungalows were dragged offshore. Bronx Ink reported after Hurricane Sandy that Throgs Neck, Pelham Bay and City Island suffered the most damage in the borough, and City Limits described residents shoveling a thick layer of debris off the streets after Long Island Sound came over its banks. For a network, that history has a simple meaning: the modem, router, switch and backup battery should live high on an interior wall, never in a crawlspace or on a floor, and the cable that has to pass below the living floor should be protected and easy to replace. We plan every Silver Beach house with that in mind, even on streets that have never flooded.

We also want to be honest about what we could not find. We found no Reddit threads, Patch stories or neighborhood posts about home networks or cabling in Silver Beach, and we could not see Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches or AI Overview from our build environment. We found no HPD, 311, DOB or Landmarks records specific to cabling here; Openigloo's summary of Silver Beach Gardens Corp shows it linked to 20 properties with no open violations in the city data it reviews. We found no published rules from the cooperative about contractors or alterations, so we ask each homeowner to check with the co-op office. And some sources disagree: two websites list street names and a body of water that do not match the rest of the record, so we have left those claims out. Where this page describes walls, attics and crawlspaces, it draws on the building ages and types in the public record and on what we find in houses of those kinds, not on inspections of any particular address.

Wired Jacks Went Dead, the Office Desk Dropped Off, or the Modem Took Water? Quick Help in Silver Beach

Some problems cannot wait for a planning visit: a home office desk that lost its wired connection the morning of a meeting, a television that stopped streaming after someone moved furniture, a modem and router that were sitting on the floor when water came in. Before calling, take two minutes. Is the provider's box or modem lit? Does the router show internet? Is it one jack, one room or the whole house? Where Service Enters

From the Pole to the Wall: Reading the Service Drop on a Silver Beach House

Every home network starts at the point where the provider's line becomes the household's wiring. In a big apartment building that point is a cellar room; in a Silver Beach house it is usually a fitting on the outside wall and a box just inside it. Because so much of the phone and cable plant here appears to run overhead on poles, that point tends to be wherever the line from the pole could reach most easily when it was first hung, which is not always where the household wants its equipment today. The first thing we do on any Silver Beach walk-through is stand outside and read the side of the house.

Who owns which part

The provider owns its line from the pole, its fitting on the house and usually its box or terminal. We do not move, cut or open those; if a provider's equipment needs to move, the provider moves it. Everything after the provider's equipment, meaning the router's location, the cable to each room, the enclosure, the switch and the access points, belongs to the household, and that is the part a structured cabling plan covers. Keeping those two clearly apart has a practical benefit: when a provider's technician visits for a repair, they can see where their responsibility stops, and they have no reason to drill a new hole because they cannot find the old one.

Old holes are part of the plan

A house that has had three providers in thirty years often has three or four holes through its outside wall, some still carrying wire that no longer connects to anything. Each of those holes is a path for drafts, insects and water. Before we add anything, we trace each wire with a tone generator to learn what is live and what is not. Dead wire is removed if the household agrees, and the holes it leaves are sealed from both sides. The household ends up with one documented entry point instead of a scatter of them, and a smaller chance that the next technician drills a new one.

Moving the starting point without moving the line

In many small houses the modem sits on the floor beside the kitchen door because that is where the line came in. Moving the network's starting point does not require the provider to rehang anything. We run one good cable from the provider's equipment to a small enclosure mounted high on an inside wall, usually in a closet or a hallway near the middle of the house, and the router, switch and backup battery live there. From that enclosure each room gets its own run. The provider's box stays where it is, and the household's network now starts in the right place.

Small-House Routing

Very Little Hidden Space: How Cable Moves Through a Silver Beach Cottage

In a large house, cable disappears into deep walls, basements and attics. In an original Silver Beach cottage there may be no basement, a crawlspace instead, a shallow attic or none at all, and interior walls that were never meant to hide anything. Routing in a house like that is a puzzle with only a few pieces, and the trick is to find them before anyone picks up a drill. The table below shows the spaces we look for and how each one tends to behave in a small shore house.

Reading the house in layers

The houses that started as summer bungalows were usually changed many times. A room was closed in, an attic was insulated, a kitchen was moved, siding went over the old boards. Each change can open or close a route. A wall that looks original may hide a chase left by a plumber in the 1980s; a ceiling that looks continuous may cover the joint between the original cottage and a later room. We look for those joints because they are often where a cable can cross from one part of the house to another. We also look for the opposite: places where a later project filled a cavity with insulation or blocking that will stop a fish tape cold.

Fewer, better holes

In a 900-square-foot house, every hole is visible to someone. So our plans aim for the smallest number of penetrations that does the job. One hole from the attic into the top of a closet wall can feed two rooms. One crossing under the floor can reach both bedrooms if they share a wall. Where two cables are going to the same area, they travel together. The drop plan lists each hole by location so the household knows exactly how many there will be before any drilling starts.

When the walls cannot be fished

Some interiors leave no cavity at all, whether because the walls are solid boards, because blocking fills every bay, or because the wall is full of pipes. In those rooms a slim painted raceway along the top of the baseboard or tucked into the corner where wall meets ceiling is often the neatest answer. It is visible, but only just, and it can be painted the color of the trim. We show the household the exact path on the wall with tape before installing anything, so nobody is surprised by where it ends up.

Rebuilt houses are a different job

A house rebuilt in the last few decades usually has framed walls with real cavities, an attic that someone can stand in, and sometimes a closet where a builder already ran phone and television wiring. Those houses are often easier to cable than the cottages around them, and they reward a little detective work: a builder's wiring box in a closet may already contain cable to several rooms that can be tested and reused, or at least used to pull new cable through. We test before we decide, because old cable that passes a test is worth keeping and old cable that fails is not.

Water and Weather

Keeping a Shore-Side Network Working Through Storm Season

Silver Beach sits on a bluff, but not every house sits high on it, and the record shows how far water has reached here in bad storms. The National Weather Service's summary of evacuations from Edgewater Park and Silver Beach, Bronx Ink's account of Sandy's damage along the eastern Bronx, and City Limits' description of debris shoveled off the streets all point the same way. A network can be built so that a flood costs a household a cable or two at most, rather than a modem, a router, a switch and a battery, and so that the house is back online as soon as the provider's service returns.

Below the floor, plan for replacement

If a cable has to cross under a cottage to reach a room, we assume it may one day sit in water. We keep the crossing short, run it in conduit where it is exposed, keep it off the ground on proper supports, and leave a pull string beside it so a damaged run can be replaced by pulling a new one through the same path without crawling the whole space again. The drop plan marks any run that passes below the floor so the household knows which ones to test after a storm.

High, dry and on its own circuit of care

The enclosure goes where a person can reach it standing up, ideally in a closet near the center of the house. It holds the router, a small switch if the house needs more ports, the patch panel and the battery backup, with room for one more device. Any new electrical outlet it needs is installed by a licensed electrician, and our quote says so. When the house loses power, the battery keeps the network running through short outages and gives the router a clean shutdown in long ones.

After the water goes down

If water has reached any part of the network, do not plug wet equipment back in. Cable that sat in water should be tested before it is trusted, and connectors and jacks that got wet are usually replaced rather than dried. Because every run is labeled and its original test result is on file, we can retest the affected cables and compare them to the day they were installed, which makes it obvious which ones need replacing and which ones are fine.

Original bungalows: the roof space and the floor are the whole map

An original one-floor bungalow gives a crew two real routes: over the ceiling and under the floor. If the roof space can be reached through a hatch, cable can travel along the framing above the rooms and drop into a wall or come straight through the ceiling to an access point. If it cannot, the crawlspace is the other option, and in a shore house that is the option we use least and protect most. The interior partitions in these houses were sometimes built light and low, as the Holly Place memoir describes, so a wall that looks like a good place to fish a cable may stop short of the roof framing or have nothing behind its surface. We check each one with a detector and a small inspection camera before deciding. The result is usually a plan with one route over the ceiling, short drops into two or three walls, and a single enclosure in a closet near the center of the house.

Cottages with additions: use the seam

When a Silver Beach cottage gained a bedroom or a second story, the builder joined new framing to old, and the join is often the best path in the house. A newer attic over the addition may be roomier than the original roof space; a closet built into the addition may line up with a wall below. We look for where the two structures meet, because there is often a gap or a chase there, and because the newer part of the house tends to be easier to work in. Where the two parts sit at different floor levels, the cable may need to step up or down through a short protected section, and we plan that step on paper before drilling.

Rebuilt houses: test what the builder left

A house rebuilt in 1999 or later probably has some wiring from its builder: phone lines and television coax to several rooms, often gathered in a small box in a closet. Some builders of that era ran data cable as well. We do not assume any of it is useful. We test each existing run, and anything that passes can be terminated properly, labeled and kept; anything that fails can often still serve as a pull line to bring new cable through the same path without opening a wall. These houses usually have proper attics and wall cavities, so new runs can be added neatly, and the old wiring box can sometimes be replaced with a modern enclosure in the same spot.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Silver Beach: Questions Answered

Walls, ceilings and roof spaces in a shore cottage

In our original cottage the bedroom walls stop short of the ceiling. Can a cable simply lie across the tops of the walls?

It can cross that gap, but it should not just lie there. A loose cable resting on top of a partition is easy to snag, dusty, visible from below in some rooms, and likely to be pulled when someone moves a shelf or hangs something. The memoir about a bungalow on Holly Place describes walls that stopped two feet short of the ceiling, and where a house still has that layout, the gap does make crossing from room to room easier. We use it properly: the cable is fastened along the top plate of the partition with cable-safe supports, kept out of sight from the floor, and dropped to a jack inside a wall or a short painted raceway. If the roof space above is reachable, we often prefer to go over the ceiling instead and leave the open tops of the walls alone.

The inside walls of our cottage are wood boards rather than plaster or drywall. How does a cable get into a wall like that?

Sometimes it does not, and that is fine. Board walls in early summer houses were often a single layer of wood nailed to framing, with little or no cavity behind, so there is nothing to fish a cable through. Before planning, we look at the wall from the attic or crawlspace and with an inspection camera through an existing opening to see what is really there. If there is a cavity, a cable can usually be dropped from above to a small box. If there is not, a slim raceway along the baseboard or in the corner, painted to match the wood or the trim, brings the cable to the jack without cutting the boards. We show you the route with tape first, so the choice between a hidden route and a visible one is yours.

Someone told us our cottage was partly built from salvaged lumber. Could that change how drilling goes?

It might. One neighborhood history says the founders built bungalows and cottages from repurposed materials, some taken from old buildings and ships, though we treat that source with caution and cannot say which houses it describes. Reused lumber can hide old nails, odd dimensions and metal fittings, and it is often harder and more brittle than new wood. That is a reason to slow down, not a reason to avoid the job. We scan for metal before drilling, drill a small pilot hole first, use bits suited to hard old wood, and stop to look with a camera if anything feels wrong. If a piece of framing looks structural or fragile, we route around it rather than through it.

We plan to have the attic sprayed with foam insulation this winter. Should cable go up there before the foam does?

Yes, and it is one of the best times to do it. Once the underside of the roof or the attic floor is covered in foam, running new cable through that space becomes much harder, and changing it later means cutting foam away. Before the insulation contractor arrives, we can run cable to every room that will need it, set it on supports where the foam will not bury the connections, run it to ceiling locations for access points, and leave an empty conduit or two with pull strings from the attic down toward the enclosure for anything you have not thought of yet. We coordinate with the insulation contractor so they know where the cable is and so nothing is compressed or pulled tight against framing that will be sprayed.

The roof space over our cottage gets very hot in summer. Is it safe to run network cable through it?

Running cable through a hot attic is normal; leaving equipment there is not. Network cable is made to handle a wide range of temperatures, and a short crossing through a summer attic does not hurt it, especially when it is kept off the underside of the roof deck and supported along the framing rather than lying on hot sheathing. Connections stay at the jacks in the rooms below, not in the attic. What should never go in a hot roof space is the router, switch or battery backup, which shorten their lives in heat and become hard to reach. We also keep cable clear of attic fans, recessed lights and any metal ductwork, and we check for roofing nails through the deck before working near it.

A previous owner put a dropped ceiling under the old cottage ceiling in one room. Can cable travel in the gap between them?

Usually, and it can be a handy route. The space between an original ceiling and a later dropped or furred ceiling often runs across the whole room and can connect to a wall or closet at one end. We check what else is in there first, such as old electrical wiring, a duct or insulation, and whether the lower ceiling is hung from the old one or from the framing. Cable travels on its own supports, not resting on the ceiling tiles or panels, and stays away from light fixtures. If the gap reaches a wall that leads to the enclosure, a whole room can be wired without opening anything; if it does not, we combine it with a short drop or raceway at one end.

Getting cable through the shell of the house

The cable company drilled several holes through our siding over the years. Can the new network use one of them and seal the rest?

That is exactly what we try to do. We trace each wire coming through those holes to learn which one carries the provider's current service, and that hole stays in use for the provider. Any other hole that the new plan can use, for example to bring a cable back in from an outdoor device, gets cleaned out, fitted with a proper bushing or grommet and resealed around the new cable. Holes with dead wire are emptied with your permission and sealed from inside and out so they stop letting in drafts, insects and water. If any of this involves changing the outside of the house beyond sealing old holes, we check with the co-op office first.

We just had a ductless heat pump installed, and its pipes go through the wall in a neat cover. Can our network cable go out through the same hole?

We would not put it in the same hole. The opening for a heat pump's refrigerant lines, drain and control wire was sized and sealed by the heating contractor for their equipment, and adding a cable can break that seal, crowd the lines and leave nobody clearly responsible if it leaks later. Data cable also should not be taped to refrigerant lines, which run hot and cold. If the outside cover already runs where your cable needs to go, the heating contractor may agree to a separate channel or a second, small cover beside it, with its own sealed entry. That keeps the look tidy while each system keeps its own hole and its own warranty.

Our house was winterized to keep the cold out. Will drilling holes for cable make it drafty again?

Not if every hole is sealed, which ours are. A cable passing through a floor into the crawlspace or through a ceiling into the roof space creates a small path for air, and in a house by the water in January, small paths add up. Each penetration we make is fitted snugly to the cable and sealed with a suitable sealant on the warm side, and anything passing through a floor or ceiling assembly gets a fire-rated seal as well. Where several cables pass through one spot, they go through one sealed opening rather than several. The drop plan lists every penetration, and the handover file includes a photo of each seal, so you can see that the house is as tight as it was before.

Mice get into the crawlspace under our cottage now and then. Will they chew the network cable?

They can, which is why cable in a crawlspace should not be left bare. Rodents chew whatever is in their path, and a loose cable on the ground is an easy target. Any run that crosses a crawlspace goes inside conduit, or in a jacket made to resist chewing where conduit is impractical, and it is supported off the ground along the framing. The points where the cable enters the living space from below are sealed tightly, which also closes off a route mice could use to get into the walls. If you already have signs of animals in the crawlspace, we will mention it, because it affects where we route and how much protection the cable needs, but dealing with the animals themselves is a job for pest control.

Small houses, crowded airwaves

Our cottage is under 1,000 square feet. Why would a house that small need wired connections at all?

Many small houses do fine on Wi-Fi alone, and we will tell you if yours is one of them. The ones that do not usually have one of three problems: the router sits at one end of the house because that is where the line came in; a wall full of pipes, a bathroom or an old chimney sits between the router and the rooms that need a signal; or the household relies on a few devices that simply work better on a wire, like a work computer, a television that streams in high resolution, or a game console. In those cases a handful of tested cables, plus one access point in the middle of the house, solves the problem permanently. In a house of about 900 square feet that is often three or four runs, not a dozen.

The houses here are only a few feet apart. Do our neighbors' Wi-Fi networks slow ours down?

They can. Wi-Fi networks that share the same channels take turns using the air, and in a place where houses are as close as they are in Silver Beach, a router may hear many neighboring networks through its walls. Each one adds to the competition, especially in the evening when everyone is streaming. A wired connection takes the devices that matter most off the air entirely, which leaves more room for phones and tablets that have to use Wi-Fi. A properly placed access point in the middle of your house, cabled back to the router, also helps, because your devices hear your network more strongly than your neighbors'. Turning the router's power up as high as it goes usually makes things worse, not better.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Silver Beach

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask boxes for Silver Beach from our build environment, so we are not quoting them. These are claims that turn up often in quick online answers about home networks and cabling, tested against the record for Silver Beach and against how we work. Marketplaces and cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; Fixr, for example, asks whether installing Cat6 is worth it. They are useful for a sense of scale, but none of them has crawled under a Silver Beach cottage.

"Structured cabling is a commercial service for offices, not for houses."

The phrase comes from offices and data centers, but the method is just as useful at home: cable from one central point to each place a device lives, tested, labeled and documented. In a house it might be four runs instead of four hundred.

The difference in Silver Beach is the house itself. An original bungalow has little hidden space, a rebuilt house has proper cavities, and both sit where water has reached before. A structured approach decides where the equipment goes and how each cable travels instead of leaving it to chance.

What the office version does not need, a house does not either. Nobody in a 900-square-foot cottage needs a full rack. A small enclosure, a few jacks and an access point are structured cabling at the right size.

"Every cabling job in New York City needs an electrical permit."

Not under the current code. UpCodes summarizes the New York City rule that no electrical permit is required for wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person, and doineedapermit.org dates the current code to December 21, 2025.

That does not mean anything goes. Fire alarm and other life-safety wiring is outside the exemption, new outlets and circuits are electrical work, and in Silver Beach the cooperative is the owner of the land and has its own say over anything outside the house.

So the honest answer is that indoor data cabling in a Silver Beach house usually needs no permit, but it does need a qualified installer, and anything touching the outside of the house or shared ground should be confirmed with the co-op office first.

"If you have fiber internet, the wiring inside your house is fiber too."

Fiber service usually ends at a box on or inside the house that converts the signal for the router. From there, every wired connection in a home is copper network cable, and every wireless one starts at a router or access point.

BroadbandNow reports that fiber reaches 84.7 percent of the Bronx through Verizon Fios and Optimum, but we found no data specific to Silver Beach. Whatever arrives at the wall, the cable from the provider's box to the rooms is the household's to plan.

That is good news, because it means the inside network does not depend on the provider. If a house switches services, a well-built inside network usually needs nothing more than a new connection at the enclosure.

"Mesh Wi-Fi has made running cable inside a home pointless."

Mesh systems help, but each unit has to pass traffic to the next over the air unless it is wired, and that air is shared. In a neighborhood where houses sit a few feet apart, many neighboring networks compete for the same channels.

A mesh unit wired back to the router behaves far better than one relaying wirelessly, and the devices that matter most, a work computer or a streaming television, do best on their own cable.

In practice, the best small-house setups we see combine the two: a few wired jacks where devices stay put, and one or two access points cabled back to the enclosure for everything that moves.

"Whatever cable a house already has is good enough for today's internet."

Some of it is. Much of it is not. A Silver Beach house that was rebuilt around 1999, like the one on Aster Place, may have phone wire and coax from its builder and possibly early data cable, and an original cottage may have wire stapled in by several providers over decades.

The only way to know is to test it. Cable that passes a modern test can be terminated properly and kept; cable that fails can often still be used as a pull line to bring new cable through the same path.

What we never do is assume. A run that looks fine but tests poorly will cause the kind of slow, intermittent problems that waste hours on the phone with a provider who can see nothing wrong.

"Any outdoor-rated network cable can be buried or laid along the ground."

Cable made for outdoor use is not necessarily made to be buried. Burial-rated cable, conduit and the depth and route of a trench are separate questions, and a cable laid on the ground will be stepped on, mowed over, chewed and soaked.

In Silver Beach there is another question first: the land belongs to the cooperative, so any trench or run across the ground needs the co-op office's agreement before anyone digs.

Where an outdoor run is approved, we use cable rated for the conditions, protect it in conduit where needed, and bring it into the house through a sealed fitting below an overhang so water runs away from the hole.

"Staples are a perfectly good way to hold network cable."

Hammered staples are the most common way network cable gets damaged in houses. A staple driven too tight crushes the pairs inside, and the run may still work but fail a test or slow down under load.

We support cable with fasteners made for it, loose enough not to deform the jacket, and in open spaces we use hooks or straps along the framing rather than pinning the cable flat.

When we take over a house where previous installers stapled cable along trim or joists, we test those runs before deciding whether to keep them. A crushed run is usually cheaper to replace than to keep troubleshooting.

FAQ

Silver Beach Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Our house was rebuilt around 1999 and has a builder's box in a closet with phone and cable splitters. Can it become the center of the new network?
+

Often, yes. A builder's wiring box is usually in a sensible central closet, and the cables that run from it to the rooms already follow hidden paths. We test each existing run first. Data cable that passes can be terminated on a new patch panel; phone wire and coax that cannot carry a modern network can often be used to pull new cable through the same path. The old box is either cleaned out and reused or replaced with a modern enclosure in the same spot, with power for the router, switch and battery added by an electrician if the closet has no outlet.

Our cottage still has an old brick chimney from the original heater that we no longer use. Can network cable run up inside it?
+

We almost never use a chimney. Even an unused flue can hold soot, moisture and debris, may still be connected to something, and is hard to seal properly where cable leaves it. In a one-floor cottage it rarely leads anywhere useful anyway, since the route we need is across the house rather than up through the roof. If the chimney sits between the router and the rooms that need a signal, the more useful fix is usually a cable around it through the roof space and an access point on the far side, where the masonry is no longer in the way.

The only closet with space for the network box also holds our electrical panel. Can they share the closet?
+

They can share the closet but not the same spot. Electrical code requires clear working space in front of an electrical panel, so nothing can be mounted where it would block access to the breakers. The network enclosure goes beside the panel or on another wall of the closet, with its own mounting and its own outlet installed by an electrician if one is needed. Data cable never runs inside the electrical panel, and where it travels near power wiring it keeps its distance and crosses at right angles. A closet like this is often a good choice, because it is usually indoors, dry and easy to reach.

Can a weatherproof Wi-Fi point on our deck be powered through its network cable, so we do not need an outdoor outlet?
+

Yes. Outdoor access points made for that purpose take their power over the same cable that carries their data, supplied by a switch or injector inside the enclosure. That means one cable from inside the house, no new outdoor outlet, and a device that shuts off with the rest of the network if the battery runs down. We mount the access point under an overhang, bring the cable in from below through a sealed fitting so water runs away from the hole, and use cable rated for outdoor exposure. If the mounting or route changes the outside of the house, we confirm it with the co-op office first.

Our ceilings are low. Is there a Wi-Fi access point that mounts on a wall jack instead of the ceiling?
+

Yes. Wall-plate access points fit over a standard jack box, take their power over the cable, and often add a couple of wired ports for a television or console in the same room. They suit small rooms, low ceilings, and houses where there is no roof space above to reach a ceiling. Their coverage is a little different from a ceiling unit, so we pick the wall and height during the walk-through. In a small cottage one well-placed wall-plate access point near the center of the house can do the job that a router at one end could not.

Does a house our size need a rack, or is there something smaller?
+

Almost no Silver Beach house needs a rack. A small wall-mounted enclosure, about the size of a shallow cabinet, holds a patch panel for a handful of runs, a compact switch, the router or the provider's equipment, and a battery backup, with room for one more device. It mounts high on an inside wall, ideally in a closet near the middle of the house, and closes so the equipment is out of sight and out of reach of children and pets. If the house later needs more, the enclosure is chosen with a little spare space.

Our cottage has electric baseboard heaters along most walls. Can cable run behind or beside them?
+

Not behind them. Baseboard heaters get hot along their whole length, and cable pressed against them or run through the space behind them will be exposed to heat it was not meant for. We keep cable away from heaters, route along walls or stretches without them, and place jacks so cords from devices do not have to drape over a heater either. Where a heater fills the only wall that makes sense for a jack, we come down inside the wall above it, or bring the cable to an adjacent wall, and say exactly where in the drop plan.

If a cable comes up a little short inside the wall, can you just join two pieces together?
+

We do not splice cable inside walls. Each run goes in as one continuous piece from the patch panel to the jack, which is what lets it pass a full test and keep performing for years. A hidden joint is a weak point that can loosen, corrode in a damp house or simply fail a test. If a run comes up short, we pull a new, longer cable. That is why we measure routes during the walk-through and leave a little service slack at each end, so a jack or the enclosure can be moved slightly later without new cable.

Every wall in our small cottage seems to have a pipe or a wire in it. How do you avoid drilling into one?
+

We look before we drill, every time. Each spot is scanned with a detector for metal, live wiring and studs, and where possible we look into the cavity from the roof space, the crawlspace or an existing opening with a small inspection camera. We note where the kitchen and bathroom plumbing runs and keep clear of those walls. Holes start small, and we stop if anything feels wrong. In a house where every wall is crowded, we may choose a surface raceway rather than risk a pipe. The drop plan shows each location, so you know where the holes will be.

We are repainting the inside of the cottage this year. Should the cabling happen before or after the painters?
+

Before, if you can. Any small holes we make, any raceway we install and any patching around a new box can then be painted along with the rest of the room, so nothing looks added afterward. We can pull and route cable, set the boxes and patch, then come back after the painters to terminate the jacks and fit the faceplates, which keeps them clean of paint. If the painting has already happened, the work is still neat, but small touch-ups or a raceway painted to match may be needed.
