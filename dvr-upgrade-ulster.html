The Neighborhood

Why Ulster County Properties Need a DVR Upgrade

Most places we work, a dead recorder gets noticed within a day because somebody walks past the monitor. Ulster is different because so much of it is empty for part of the week. The Census Bureau's 2023 five-year estimates count 86,178 housing units in the county; 13,073 are vacant at any time, and half of those — 6,545 — are classed as held for seasonal, recreational or occasional use. That is roughly one Ulster home in thirteen. Up the Route 28 corridor the share climbs steeply: 886 of Shandaken's 2,675 homes, 813 of Woodstock's 4,289, 237 of Denning's 432 and 204 of Hardenburgh's 292. In the City of Kingston, by contrast, it is barely one in a hundred. So the typical Ulster recorder call is a weekend owner who drove up from Brooklyn or New Jersey on a Friday, opened the app, and found every tile grey — or found live pictures but no recordings for the three weeks they actually wanted to see.

Owners are already improvising. When someone on r/hudsonvalley asked second-home owners in early October 2026 who looks after their houses, the answers were caretakers, neighbours and cameras: one family relies on Ring cameras and a contractor friend who checks after storms, another simply wrote “me via Blink cameras”, and one commenter described a part-time neighbour with “extensive cameras and a full house battery backup, combined with cellular fallback on their Internet package.” Another warned that insurers look hard at claims when nobody was checking on a house, and described pipes that burst and froze while a place sat empty. Short-term rentals add to the count: the Ulster County Comptroller's September 2025 report found 2,827 of them in May 2025, 3.28% of all county housing, 98% let as entire homes and 36% owned by people with mailing addresses outside the county.

The second reason recorders fail here is power. Central Hudson reported more than 19,000 homes and businesses out after the slow-moving nor'easter of late September 2026, and named Woodstock, Shawangunk, Plattekill and Hurley in Ulster as the hardest hit, with restoration running into the weekend. Each outage is a hard shutdown for a recorder that has no battery behind it. Older boxes do not always come back cleanly: some wait for someone to press the power button, some come up with the clock reset and record under the wrong date, and a drive that was already tired can fail on the restart. Add Ulster's lightning — Earth Networks counted 182,760 pulses and 49 thunder days in the county in 2020 — and a coax run that crosses a lawn to a barn becomes a path for a surge straight into the back of the recorder.

Nothing about this needs alarm, but it does need a recorder chosen and set up for absence. When the Sheriff's Office warned Town of Esopus residents in January 2025 after a residential burglary and several thefts from cars around Union Center Road, its advice included installing a security system and motion lighting; Saugerties police were asking for help after someone walked into a detached garage near Old Kings Highway on a November 2025 afternoon and left with a dirt bike. Footage only helps in cases like those if the recorder was actually saving that day, and if someone can get a clip off it quickly. Most of the Ulster upgrades we do are therefore less about megapixels than about three plain questions: is it recording, will I hear about it when it stops, and will it survive the next storm without me there.

Came Up for the Weekend and Every Camera Was Blank?

Call (845) 640-3835. We book the next open two-hour window anywhere in the county, and most systems are recording again within one to two hours of arrival. Before you call, these checks tell us a lot — and sometimes fix it.

Five things to look at when you walk in

  1. Is the recorder on at all? No lights on the front after an outage usually means it is waiting for the power button, or its plug-in supply has died. Press the button once and give it three minutes.
  2. Is the date right? Open the recorder's menu or the app's playback page. If the clock jumped back to a factory year, it lost time during the outage, and recordings since then are filed under the wrong date.
  3. Is there a disk warning? A beep, a red light, or a storage page that says “error”, “abnormal” or “no disk” means the cameras are live but nothing is being kept.
  4. Are the cameras dark or the internet down? If the monitor in the house shows video but the app does not, the recorder is fine and the router, modem or satellite dish is the problem.
  5. Did something get hit? If only the barn or gate camera is black after a thunderstorm, note it before touching anything; a surge on a long run often takes one channel and leaves the rest.

Write down what you saw and take a photo of the recorder's screen. If it is a disk fault, do not keep power-cycling the recorder — that is the fastest way to lose whatever the drive still holds.

A four-season routine for a recorder in an Ulster house

Recorders need very little attention, but a house that is empty for long stretches benefits from a short routine tied to the calendar. This is the one we hand to owners and caretakers after an upgrade; none of it takes more than a few minutes.

  • Before winter. Scrub back through a week of playback to confirm the drive is saving. Check that the battery backup still holds by unplugging it for a minute with the recorder running. Brush leaves and wasp nests off the outdoor cameras, and make sure nothing will be stacked in front of the recorder closet when the house is shut up.
  • After the first big snow. Look at every camera from the app. A camera that shows only white has snow on the housing or the lens; one that shows a soft blur may have ice. If a camera under an open sky keeps disappearing every storm, it is worth moving it under an eave.
  • In spring. Walk the long runs to the barn, the studio and the gate and look for cable chewed by rodents, pulled loose by frost heave or crushed by a plow. Wipe the lenses and check that the infrared lights still glow at dusk.
  • In midsummer, before the storm season. Confirm the surge protectors on outbuilding runs are still connected to ground, that the phone alerts still arrive by unplugging one camera for a moment, and that the recorder's firmware is current.
  • Whenever someone new gets a key. Give them their own account rather than a shared password, limited to the cameras they need, and remove it when they stop coming.

Owners who would rather not do any of this can book it as a seasonal visit; the same checks are part of our camera maintenance service in Ulster County.

How each building type changes the upgrade

Stone houses. The cost in a stone house is not the recorder, it is the holes. Drilling a two-foot fieldstone wall is slow, noisy and permanent, and on an eighteenth-century house it can disturb mortar that has been repointed by hand. So we begin by mapping the openings the last installer already used — a gap in a window frame, a cable through the sill beam, a run along the underside of the porch roof — and we test the cable in each before deciding anything. If a run tests clean, a modern HD-over-coax camera goes on the end of it and the wall is never touched. If a new path is unavoidable, we look first at the wooden parts of the building: a sill plate, a door casing, a frieze board. A core drill through stone is the last option and gets discussed with you before it happens. Camera bases on masonry go into mortar joints with sleeves, never into the face of the stone, so they can come off later without a scar.

Cabins and weekend houses. A cabin recorder usually sits wherever the original installer found an outlet: a bedroom closet, a utility nook beside the water heater, sometimes a crawlspace hatch. Two things decide where we put the new one. It must stay above any water that can collect — snowmelt and failed pumps put more recorders out of action in the western towns than burglars do — and it must be on the same battery backup as the modem, because a recorder that survives an outage while the internet does not can record but cannot tell you anything. Where a house is winterized and the heat turned down, we keep the recorder in the warmest interior closet, not the unheated garage or the porch.

Farmhouses with outbuildings. The farm call is mostly about distance. A barn camera on old coax a few hundred feet from the house was often installed when analog cameras gave a soft picture at any distance, so nobody noticed the cable was marginal. HD-over-coax cameras are far more forgiving of long runs — the main hybrid recorder families publish distances measured in hundreds of metres — but a corroded connector at the barn end or a splice in a junction box will still ruin the picture. We test from both ends, re-terminate, and add a surge protector where the run enters the house. Where a barn has never had a cable and the ground between is ledge, a pair of small wireless bridge radios is usually cheaper than a trench.

Brick buildings in Kingston and Saugerties. These recorders tend to live in cellars that have taken water. In the Rondout, where the creek's tidal flooding is serious enough that the city won a federal grant to raise East Strand and North Street, a recorder belongs on an upper floor or at least well up a wall. The cable already comes up from the cellar, so we extend it rather than re-run it. In a building with a shop below and a flat above, we separate the shop's cameras from the stair and the flat's door in the user permissions, so a part-time clerk does not watch the tenant coming and going.

Studios and outbuildings. A studio camera is frequently the only camera that justifies the whole system, and the only one on a separate building with its own power. We check whether the studio's circuit is fed from the house panel or a separate meter, because a camera on a separate supply can still be dark after the house power is back. Where it makes sense, the studio camera gets power over the same cable from the house, so it lives and dies with the recorder and the battery backup.

Postwar and 1970s houses. These are the easiest Ulster upgrades and the ones most likely to be done in a morning. The coax usually runs through a finished basement ceiling or an attic, the recorder sits near the electrical panel, and the main changes are the drive, the outage behaviour, the alerts and closing whatever port the original installer opened on the router.

Rules, credits and paperwork for Ulster camera work

  • Kingston landmark districts. In the Stockade, Rondout–West Strand, Chestnut Street and Fair Street districts, exterior work that makes a material change visible from a street or alley needs a certificate of appropriateness from the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission. The city code exempts ordinary maintenance and repair that does not change design, materials, color or outward appearance, and the commission's desk reviews are generally turned around within a week.
  • Short-term rental platforms. Airbnb has banned indoor cameras in all listings since April 30, 2024, and requires hosts to disclose outdoor cameras and their general location before guests book. Cameras may not watch indoor spaces or private outdoor areas such as an enclosed outdoor shower or sauna.
  • Electrical work. Ulster County's electrical licensing code covers wiring for signaling, communication, alarm and data. A recorder swap on existing low-voltage cable is our work under the NYS low-voltage license; a new outlet, a generator inlet or a dedicated circuit for a recorder closet is done by a county-licensed electrician, and we coordinate with one when it is needed.
  • Neighbors and backyards. New York Civil Rights Law § 52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a claim against someone who installs a camera on adjoining property to record their backyard without written consent. We aim yard cameras at your land and use privacy masks where a view crosses the line.
  • Sound. New York is a one-party consent state for conversations under Penal Law § 250.05, but a camera microphone at a shop counter or on a rental porch records people who are not part of any conversation with you. We leave audio off unless you ask, and explain why.
  • Retailers may get some of it back. A state credit introduced with the FY2025 budget pays up to $3,000 per retail location to firms of 50 or fewer staff whose spending on security, cameras among it, passes a set threshold. The certificate application is due by October 31 each year; your accountant can say whether a recorder upgrade counts.
Your Questions Answered

DVR Upgrades in Ulster County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Ulster owners actually bring to us, grouped by the reason the recorder matters. Each answer is written for this county's houses, power and internet.

Central Hudson Outages, Credits and Lightning

Central Hudson credits customers after long outages — does that cover a recorder that died in the storm?

No. Central Hudson's outage credits apply to widespread outages lasting at least 72 hours: residential customers get a $25 bill credit for each further 24 hours without service, and residential and small commercial customers can claim for spoiled food and medicine with proof. Electronics damaged when the power dropped or came back are not part of that scheme.

A recorder or camera lost to a surge is usually a homeowner's insurance question, and the paperwork goes better with a dated photo of the dead unit and a note of the outage dates. The cheaper answer is prevention: a battery backup between the wall and the recorder absorbs the drop and the return, and surge protection on long camera runs covers the other way surges arrive.

Central Hudson's map shows our road is out — can we tell from the city whether the cameras are running on the battery?

Yes, if the modem is on the battery too, and that is the main reason we put it there. While the battery is carrying the recorder, the modem and the camera power, the system stays online, so the app still shows live pictures; that is your sign the battery is doing its job. When the battery runs down, the recorder's offline alert arrives on your phone, and when Central Hudson restores the line, the recorder comes back by itself and the app shows it online again.

If you want more than that, a battery backup with its own network connection can send an email the moment it switches to battery and again when mains power returns, which gives you the outage times to the minute. On houses with satellite internet, remember that the dish and its router need power too; if they are not on a battery, the cameras will keep recording locally during an outage but you will not be able to see them until power is back.

Should there be surge protection where the barn camera's cable comes into the house?

Yes. A long cable from an outbuilding behaves like an antenna for nearby lightning, and Ulster sees plenty: Earth Networks counted 49 thunder days in the county in 2020. When a strike lands near the barn, the surge travels along the coax or network cable to whatever is at the other end — usually the recorder's input for that channel, sometimes the whole board. Recorders that lose one channel after a storm, or die outright, often have exactly this history.

We fit a protector for that cable where it enters the house, and we bond the protector to the building's electrical ground so the surge has somewhere to go other than the recorder. Where the far camera has its own power at the barn, that end gets protection too. If you have whole-house surge protection on the electrical panel, that helps the power side; it does nothing for a coax run that brings the surge in on the video cable.

Stone Walls, Metal Roofs and Barns

Our house has two-foot stone walls — how does a new cable get from the recorder to a camera outside?

Through the parts of the house that are not stone, wherever possible. In Hurley, on Huguenot Street in New Paltz or around the Stockade, the most useful paths are already there: a window frame with a gap behind the trim, a sill beam where the frame sits on the foundation, the cavity under a porch roof, or the opening an old phone or oil line once used. A thin cable can usually pass through one of those without new holes in the masonry.

When there is no such path, we plan the hole with you. A single small core hole through a mortar joint, sleeved and sealed, does far less harm than several holes through stone faces, and it can take every new cable at once. On the outside, cameras are fixed to timber or into mortar joints so the stone itself is never drilled, and the cable is run in the shadow of a downspout or trim rather than across the face of the wall.

Snow slides off our metal roof in sheets — where can a camera go so it isn't torn off?

Not under the drip edge of the metal slopes. Snow and ice leaving a standing-seam roof come off with real force, and a camera on the fascia below takes the full load. The safe spots are gable ends, where nothing slides, under a porch roof that has snow guards, or on the wall well below the eave line, angled up slightly to keep the view.

If the view you need only works from under a sliding slope, a short steel bracket with a hood, or a set of snow guards fitted above that section by a roofer, protects it. We would rather move a camera a few feet to a gable than refit it every February.

Kingston's Old Districts and Street Cameras

Does swapping the recorder and reusing the existing brackets on our Stockade house count as a change the preservation commission reviews?

Swapping the recorder does not touch the exterior at all, so it is not a matter for the commission. The question is the cameras. Kingston's code requires a certificate of appropriateness from the Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission for a material change in appearance visible from a street or alley in a landmark district, and it exempts ordinary maintenance and repair that changes nothing about design, materials, color or outward appearance. Replacing an old camera with a new one of a similar size and color on the same bracket is the kind of like-for-like work that exemption describes.

A noticeably bigger camera, a new position on the street front, or new visible cable is a change in appearance, and the commission's desk reviews are generally turned around within about a week. When a job sits near that line, we give you a photo and a short description to send to the city's preservation staff before work starts, and we keep visible changes to the back of the house where we can.

Our Midtown Kingston storefront has a pressed-tin ceiling — can the new domes go in the same spots?

Usually yes, and that is the best outcome for an old ceiling. The existing holes are where the cable already comes through, and most modern dome cameras will cover a smaller base with a mounting plate or a short adapter ring. We match the plate to the old footprint so the tin around it is not cut or bent further.

Where a new camera's base is smaller than the old hole, a painted trim ring covers the gap. Where it is larger, we use an adapter rather than enlarging the hole. If a camera needs to move to see the register or the door better, we look for a seam, a beam or a wall position before making a new hole in the tin, because a pressed-tin panel cannot be patched invisibly.

Kingston police run their own street cameras — does that make a camera on our Uptown shop unnecessary?

No. The city's 71 public-safety cameras and seven plate readers, which police described to a Common Council committee in August 2026, watch streets and intersections, mostly in Midtown and Uptown. They do not see inside your shop, your back door or the alley behind it, and you cannot pull a clip from them yourself.

Your own recorder covers the register, the stockroom door and the rear lot, and you control who sees it and for how long it is kept. If police ever ask for footage after an incident on your block, you decide what to export and hand over.

Internet at the Edge of the Map

Our only internet is a satellite dish — can the phone app reach a recorder behind it?

Yes, but not the old way. Many satellite and cellular services do not give your house its own public internet address, so the classic method of opening a port on the router and pointing the app at it simply cannot work. That is not a loss: open ports were also how botnets found and took over cheap recorders — FortiGuard Labs issued an outbreak alert in June 2025 about one family of DVRs being enlisted that way.

Instead, the recorder makes an outgoing connection to its maker's relay service, and your phone meets it there. That works through satellite, cellular and fixed-wireless connections, needs no router changes and leaves nothing open to the internet. Live viewing on a slow link uses the cameras' lower-resolution sub-stream, while the full-quality footage stays on the drive in the house for when you need it. Where the relay is too slow or you want extra privacy, a small VPN at the house is the alternative.

Fiber is coming to our road under the county broadband program — should we wait to upgrade the recorder?

No. The recorder records to its own drive whatever the internet is doing, so a failing recorder is failing now, and the new internet connection only changes how easily you can watch from away. Ulster's ConnectALL award covers nearly 1,300 unserved locations, and the county announced in May 2026 that all 1,293 will get fiber under the federal BEAD programme; construction on a given road can still be some time off.

What is worth planning is the handoff. When fiber arrives, the provider's terminal will be wherever the line comes into the house. If the recorder lives near there, or can be cabled to the new router, the switch takes a few minutes. We set the recorder up so moving it from the old connection to the new one is just a matter of plugging into the new router.

Rentals: Short-Term Guests and Student Leases

We rent the house to short-term guests some weekends — where can cameras go, and what do guests have to be told?

Outside only, and disclosed before they book, if you list on Airbnb. Since April 30, 2024 Airbnb has banned indoor cameras in all listings, including ones that are switched off, and requires hosts to state the presence and general location of outdoor cameras in the listing. Outdoor cameras may not look into the house or cover private outdoor spaces such as an enclosed outdoor shower or sauna. Other platforms have their own rules, so check yours.

In practice we set up a rental house with cameras on the driveway, the entrances from outside and outbuildings, aimed away from windows, with a hot tub or outdoor shower masked out entirely. Audio stays off. Indoor cameras that you use when the house is yours are physically removed or unplugged and put away for rental stays, not merely turned off in the app. A separate manager or cleaner account sees only the outdoor cameras.

Our New Paltz rental turns over every May — what should change on the recorder between tenant groups?

Every account that belonged to the outgoing tenants should go, and nothing else on the system should change. If you gave last year's tenants access to the front-door or driveway camera, delete their user accounts — do not just change a shared password, because a shared password is how access leaks between years. Then create fresh accounts for the incoming group, limited to the cameras they need.

It is also the right moment for a quick health check: confirm the drive is still recording, update the recorder's firmware, and make sure the admin password is one only you hold. With SUNY New Paltz enrolling more than 7,000 students, rental turnover in the village is a fixed date in the calendar, and putting the recorder on the same checklist as the smoke detectors and keys keeps it from being forgotten.

Farms, Stands and Seasonal Shops

Can a camera watch the self-serve cash box at our farm stand from the recorder in the house?

Yes, and it is one of the more useful farm cameras. A stand at the road is often a few hundred feet from the house, so the camera either runs on coax or network cable along the lane or links back with a pair of wireless bridge radios where a cable would be impractical. A zoom-lens camera at the house can sometimes see the stand directly, which avoids any cable at all.

Aim it at the cash box and the counter, not at the road, so it records the hands and the box rather than passing traffic. A second, wider view of the pull-off catches vehicles. Set the recorder to tag motion at the stand as events, and the end-of-day check becomes a short list of clips rather than hours of footage.

Our Woodstock shop closes for part of the winter — can the recorder alert us only when the shop is shut?

Yes. Recorders let you schedule alerts by camera and by time, separately from recording. The cameras record around the clock, but notifications for the shop floor and back door only reach your phone outside opening hours, and for the whole of the closed season if you choose. That way you get a handful of meaningful alerts rather than one every time a customer walks in.

For the closed months we add a second layer: person detection on the doors, so a falling icicle or a passing car does not trigger anything, and a disk and video-loss alert so you know the system itself is still alive. When you reopen in spring, switching back to the business-hours schedule is one setting.

Keep the coax with a hybrid, or go fully networked? Three tests we apply in Ulster

Distance and cable come first. On a property with runs of a few hundred feet to outbuildings, coax that still tests clean is an asset. HD-over-coax cameras on that cable give a big jump in picture quality without a trench or a new hole in a stone wall, and the hybrid recorder still accepts network cameras later. If most runs fail, if the old cable is thin premade stuff stapled along fences, or if walls are open for a renovation anyway, new network cable and a network recorder become the better buy.

Power comes second. Network cameras usually take their power over the network cable from a switch at the recorder, which makes the battery backup at the recorder cover every camera. Coax cameras can be powered from the recorder end too, with the right supply. Either way, a system that takes all its power from one closet survives outages much better than one with an adapter plugged in at every camera.

How you use it comes third. A weekend house with five to eight cameras and an owner who wants to know it is recording is almost always a hybrid job. A farm with a dozen cameras spread over several buildings, or a business that wants advanced analytics at the door, may justify planning a staged move to network cameras. When the two options are close, we price both, and you choose.

How a wired recorder stacks up against ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe on a rural property

Should the old recorder simply go, replaced by a few consumer cameras? It is a fair question for a weekend house. Ring and SimpliSafe cameras are mostly Wi-Fi or battery devices that keep their history in the company's cloud on a subscription, which means everything depends on the house's internet and Wi-Fi reaching the barn — two weak points on many Ulster properties. Batteries in outdoor cameras also struggle through a hard winter. ADT-style systems are built around a monitoring contract, with cameras as an add-on. An upgraded wired recorder keeps recording to a drive in the house whatever the internet does, costs nothing per month, and reuses the coax already run to the outbuildings. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About DVR Upgrades in Ulster County

A live Google AI Overview was not reachable when this page was researched. The seven claims below are instead lifted from the cost guides, product pages and forum answers that such summaries draw on for DVR upgrade searches, and each one is set against what actually happens on farms, cabins and Main Street shops in this county.

“Recorders are $100 to $350 at retail, so that's the job's cost”

Cost guides quote the box. HomeGuide, for example, puts a new DVR or NVR at $100 to $350 when the current one cannot take more cameras, and Security.org, citing HomeAdvisor, puts professional installation of two cameras at $100 to $200 on average. In an installers' forum, a homeowner planning a do-it-yourself swap reckoned on $500 for eight cameras and a recorder.

The hardware is the smallest part of an Ulster upgrade. The time goes into testing a 400-foot run to the barn, re-terminating connectors that have spent fifteen winters outdoors, fitting surge protection, setting outage restarts and alerts, and confirming every one of them works before we leave a house that may then sit empty for weeks.

That is why our Ulster figures, with the county's pricing factor applied to the silo base, start at $689 for a four-channel swap. If the only goal is a box on a shelf, a retail kit is cheaper; if the goal is knowing the cameras are recording when nobody is there, the setup is the job.

“That old cable won't do HD, so budget for all-new wiring”

This is repeated in forums and installer sales pitches. It confuses the old analog signal with the cable that carried it. The cable is often fine; the analog format was what limited the picture.

Retailer CCTV Camera World says plainly that if you already have good coax runs from a roll of solid copper cable, there is no reason to switch to an Ethernet camera system, and hybrid recorder data sheets list HD-over-coax distances of hundreds of metres.

On a farm or a stone house, rewiring is the expensive and destructive option. Testing the existing runs first and replacing only the ones that fail saves trenches through lawns and holes through foundation walls.

“A UPS is enough to keep cameras running through an outage”

Product pages for battery backups make it sound as if one box solves outages. A small battery carries a recorder, a modem and a few cameras for minutes to perhaps an hour, depending on the load and the battery.

Ulster outages are often longer: Central Hudson's restoration after the late-September 2026 nor'easter carried into a second day. No affordable battery covers that. What the battery does is ride through the short drops and generator transfers, and give the recorder a clean shutdown so its drive and clock survive.

The part people skip is the restart. A recorder that does not come back by itself when the power returns turns a twelve-hour outage into a two-week gap in a house nobody visits, which is why we test auto-restart on every job.

“Just open a port on the router to watch from your phone”

Older setup guides and forum answers still recommend port forwarding. On many Ulster connections it does not work at all, because satellite and cellular services often do not give the house its own public address.

Where it does work, it is the opening attackers look for. FortiGuard Labs issued an outbreak alert in June 2025 after botnets exploited a flaw in one family of DVRs for which, it said, no vendor patch was available; NIST's vulnerability database shows a Hikvision recorder flaw from 2021 on the U.S. government's list of known exploited vulnerabilities.

The maker's relay service or a small VPN at the house works over any connection and leaves nothing exposed. Faster internet is on the way for the last unserved Ulster roads under the county's fiber programme, but the safe setup does not depend on it.

“Cloud cameras are simpler for a second home”

Simpler to buy, yes. The second-home owners who answered the r/hudsonvalley thread in October 2026 mentioned Ring and Blink cameras alongside caretakers and neighbours, and those work well at the door of a house with steady Wi-Fi.

They rely on the internet to keep any history, on Wi-Fi reaching every camera, and on batteries or adapters at each camera through outages. On a property with a barn, a long drive and satellite internet, each of those is a gap. The commenter whose neighbour had “a full house battery backup, combined with cellular fallback” was describing what it takes to make cloud cameras reliable out here.

A wired recorder sidesteps most of it: it records locally, takes its camera power from one backed-up closet, and only needs the internet when you want to look.

“Put cameras wherever you like inside your rental”

Some host forums still treat indoor cameras in shared spaces as fine if guests are told. On Airbnb that changed: indoor cameras have been banned in all listings since April 30, 2024, even when switched off.

Outdoor cameras must be disclosed before booking and may not view indoor spaces or private outdoor areas such as an enclosed outdoor shower or sauna. With 2,827 short-term rentals in Ulster in May 2025, this is a live issue for many of the owners we work for.

We set rental properties up outside-only from the start, with privacy masks over windows and hot tubs, audio off, and owners' indoor cameras physically removed for guest stays.

“Higher resolution means better footage”

Retail listings sell megapixels. On an Ulster property, resolution is rarely what is missing from a bad clip. Missing footage is — a drive that failed, a recorder that did not restart after an outage, a camera knocked out by a surge.

After that, the problems are aim and light: a barn camera pointed at the sky, a driveway camera blinded by headlights, a camera at the road with nothing to light the plate at night. More pixels fix none of them.

We spend the budget in that order: recording reliability first, then placement, lighting and lens, and only then resolution. A four-megapixel camera aimed well at the stand or the gate beats an eight-megapixel one aimed at the treeline.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Pull the Plug Before You Leave

The last thing I do on any recorder job at a house that sits empty is cut its power. Not the switch on the front — the plug, or the breaker if the closet has its own. Then I stand there with the owner and watch. Does it come back on its own? How long does it take? Does it start recording without anyone logging in? Is the date still right? Does the offline alert reach the owner's phone, and does the “back online” message follow it?

This is general practice rather than one particular job, but it comes from seeing the same thing many times: a recorder that worked perfectly while someone was in the house and then sat dark for weeks after the first storm, because it needed a button pressed. In a full-time house that is a nuisance. In a weekend house or a rental it is the whole system gone, and nobody knows until they need it.

So the test is part of the job, not an extra. If a recorder fails it, it does not stay. And the owner leaves knowing what the alerts look like, which matters more than any picture on the monitor.

FAQ

Ulster County DVR Upgrade FAQ

Our old farmhouse still has some original wiring — is it safe to plug the recorder into it?
+

Possibly not, and it is worth finding out before a box that runs day and night is left on it in an empty house. Older circuits often have no ground and may be shared with half the house. We look at the outlet the recorder will use, and if it is ungrounded, overloaded or suspect, we ask a county-licensed electrician to add a proper grounded outlet for the recorder closet. A grounded outlet also matters for the surge protection on the camera runs, which needs a ground to work.

Our barn is booked as a seasonal event venue — can cameras cover the parking field without filming guests inside?
+

Yes. Cameras on the parking field, the lane and the barn doors from outside cover what owners usually care about at events: vehicles, arrivals, and anyone wandering where they should not. Inside the venue we leave cameras out, or mask them during events, and audio stays off. A short notice at the entrance that the grounds are recorded is good practice, and if you have a separate events manager, they can get an account limited to the outdoor views.

A contractor is renovating while we're in the city — can the cameras show progress without recording their conversations?
+

Yes. Video-only cameras on the work areas and the driveway let you see when crews arrive and leave and how the work is going, without recording what anyone says; we leave audio off. Tell the contractor in writing that the site is on camera. During the renovation itself, ask us before walls close up: it is the cheapest moment to run a new cable to a camera position you will want later.

Hunters keep crossing our posted land in the fall — can a camera on the boundary help?
+

It can record who crosses, which helps when you report trespass to the Sheriff or a state environmental conservation officer. A boundary may be far from power, so the usual answer is a camera on a building or post near the line, with a zoom lens, or a link back to the recorder by wireless bridge. Aim it along your own land and the posted line, not at a neighbor's house or yard.

Can the camera at our front door show which code was used on our smart lock?
+

Usually not on the same screen: most smart locks keep their own log of which code opened the door and when, in their own app. What the recorder adds is the picture. Matching the lock's log entry to the door camera's clip at the same minute shows who used a cleaner's or guest's code. Some recorder and lock combinations can link the two, and we tell you if yours can before you buy anything.

Thick stone walls kill our Wi-Fi — can a Wi-Fi camera on the porch still reach the recorder?
+

Often not reliably. Two feet of stone absorbs most of a Wi-Fi signal, so a porch camera that works on a summer afternoon can drop out in wet weather. A wired camera on the existing coax, or a cable passed through a window frame or sill, is the dependable fix. Where a wireless camera is the only option, a small outdoor access point mounted outside the stone, wired back to the router, gives it a strong signal.

Can the inside cameras be switched off while we're at the house?
+

Yes. Most recorders let you pause recording and live view on chosen cameras on a schedule or with one tap in the app, and some cameras have a privacy mode that physically turns the lens away. A common setup is outdoor cameras recording all the time and indoor cameras recording only when the system is set to away. We set the switch up so any adult in the family can use it, not only the person who holds the admin password.

Can a camera be mounted on a log or board-and-batten wall without leaving damage?
+

Yes. On log walls we fix the camera base to a small mounting block screwed into the log, so the screws go into wood that can be filled and stained later, and we keep the cable in the shadow of a log's lower edge. On board-and-batten siding we mount on a batten or a trim board rather than across a seam, so the boards can still move with the seasons without pulling against the camera.

We arrived on Friday night and one camera shows a black tile — what should we check before calling?
+

Look at whether that camera's infrared lights glow faintly red in the dark; if they do, the camera has power and the fault is more likely the cable or its connectors. If the camera has its own plug-in adapter, check that it is still in the socket and the socket has power. Then look at the recorder's screen: a 'video loss' message on just that channel points to the run, while a whole row of black tiles points to a shared power supply. Tell us what you found when you call.
