The Neighborhood

Why Soundview Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

The buildings are now old enough to be on their second or third generation of detection. A tower completed in the mid-1970s, like the nine Mitchell-Lama buildings of Stevenson Commons with their 948 apartments, has been through decades of battery alarms, hardwired replacements, panel upgrades and tenant turnover. A walk-up from the early 1950s may have hallway equipment that nobody has tested in years. At the same time, Soundview has a newer layer that changes the picture: Story Avenue East and West opened in 2019 on part of the Lafayette-Boynton campus, Casa Celina opened in 2024 with 204 apartments for seniors on the Sotomayor campus, the Soundview Homes co-ops were financed on NYCHA's Soundview campus, and the first phase of Stevenson Square was completed in 2026. New buildings and old towers now sit side by side, often on the same block, with different owners and different rules.

There is a fourth pattern that never makes the news but shapes our work just as much. When an alarm sounds too often for no reason, people stop trusting it. A summer 2025 dispatch summary described an automatic fire alarm on Soundview Avenue, between St. Lawrence and Lafayette Avenues, as a problematic alarm with multiple devices sounding. A building whose system sends firefighters for nothing teaches its residents to wait, and the 2016 Department of Investigation findings at NYCHA, where some residents said they took down detectors or removed batteries after inspections, show how quickly protection disappears when people see it as a nuisance. So a good fire alarm plan in Soundview has to do two things at once: reach the places where fire actually starts and travels, and stay quiet enough the rest of the time that people still believe it when it sounds.

The Mitchell-Lama tower: a big system, a board and an approval process

A Mitchell-Lama tower is the largest kind of fire alarm job in Soundview and the one with the most paperwork around it. The building usually has a panel in the lobby or a service room, devices in halls, stairs and service spaces, and household alarms inside each apartment that the company maintains. Before we price anything, we find out which parts the company wants addressed now: the building system, the apartment alarms, or both. Then we survey the way the work will actually be done, floor by floor and line by line, because a tower of several hundred apartments cannot be closed for a week. Mitchell-Lama companies also sign contracts under HPD's rules, which call for the lowest cost compatible with quality, competitive bids and HPD's written approval for larger contracts, and a clause that no work begins until HPD approves the agreement. That makes the shape of the proposal matter: we write the scope so a board and a managing agent can compare it line by line with other bids, with devices counted by floor, cable routes described, and anything that depends on a design professional's drawings separated from the work we can price outright.

The co-op and condo high-rise: halls belong to the board, apartments to the owners

In a co-op like 825 Morrison Avenue, fire detection is split in a way residents often do not realize. The board answers for the building system in the halls, stairs, lobby and service rooms. Inside each apartment, smoke and carbon monoxide alarms may be maintained by the co-op or left to the shareholder, depending on the proprietary lease and house rules, and condo buildings usually put them on the owner. When an apartment fire like the one on the fourth floor at Morrison Avenue in January 2026 happens, both halves matter: the alarm inside the unit wakes the family, and the hall system warns the floor and calls for help. Our plans for these buildings start with a written split of responsibility, then deal with each half. For the board, we survey the existing panel, every hall and stair device and the service spaces. For owners, we offer a building-wide program where every unit is done to the same standard, with a list of each unit's alarm models and replacement dates that the board can keep. Where a building reaches the height at which the DOB's bulletin on existing buildings calls for one-way voice communication, that question goes to the design professional; a tower of nineteen floors is the kind of building where it is likely to come up.

The postwar low-rise and walk-up: the top floor and the roof above it

In a four- to six-story building, the floors are usually concrete or masonry, but the top floor is different: its ceiling hangs below a roof, and the space between them is often open from one end of the building to the other. That is the cockloft the FDNY found burning at 1466 Watson Avenue in 2019, and the space involved in the 2023 fire at a NYCHA building on the same avenue. A smoke alarm inside a top-floor apartment watches the apartment, not the space above it. So in low-rise surveys we spend time at the top: we look for the hatch or bulkhead, check whether the space can be entered or only viewed, look for old penetrations and abandoned cable, and ask whether anything up there is powered, such as exhaust fans or roof equipment. Where detection belongs in that space, heat detection is normally the right device, because dust, cold and summer heat make smoke detectors unreliable there. On a filed system, the design professional sets the layout; on an existing one, adding devices to a zone may be a modification the FDNY wants to see. We sort that out before anyone climbs a ladder with tools.

The new infill building: modern systems, unfamiliar owners

The buildings that went up on former parking lots and campus land since 2019 arrived with modern fire alarm systems designed, filed and inspected as part of construction. That does not mean they need nothing. New owners and new boards, especially in first-time homeownership co-ops like the 58 apartments of Stevenson Square's first phase, often take over a building without anyone explaining what the panel does, who holds the monitoring account or when the next inspection is due. WXY describes those co-ops as single-stair buildings with two apartments per floor, eight stair volumes serving 58 homes, and that layout matters for detection: each stair is the only exit for the households above it. Our work in these buildings is usually not installation but a documented walk-through: we match each device to the as-built drawings where the board has them, confirm the monitoring account and call list, explain the panel's messages to the board and the super, and flag anything that a developer's warranty should still cover. When a board later wants changes, such as converting a storage room or adding a bike room, we tell them whether the change is a repair or something that goes back to the FDNY.

The senior residence: more warning, more time, and the hours when staff go home

Casa Celina is managed by JASA and houses seniors on 16 floors; the Stevenson Square senior residence, as WXY describes it, places services and laundry near the lobby and its common rooms along the pedestrian path. Buildings like these change the alarm conversation in three ways. First, residents may need longer to move, may sleep through ordinary sounds, or may use hearing aids they take out at night. The FDNY told residents at a Soundview fire-safety session after an attic fire that a low-frequency tone alarm is an alternative for people who are hard of hearing, and that idea extends to the whole building: notification has to reach the person, not just the room. Second, staff schedules matter. A service office that is busy at noon is empty at midnight, so the plan has to say who learns of a signal after hours and how. Third, these buildings already have modern systems designed by professionals, so our role is usually supporting the owner and the manager: in-apartment alarms that suit the residents, records of every replacement, testing with residents warned in advance, and clear explanations for the people who will hear the building sound.

The attached and two-family house: household alarms that reach every bedroom

The attached and two-family houses on Soundview's side streets are mostly the same age as the towers around them, and their alarms tell the same story of piecemeal replacement: a hardwired alarm from one decade in the hall, battery units from another in the bedrooms, nothing in the basement, and a carbon monoxide alarm sitting in a drawer. The Housing Maintenance Code requires landlords to provide and install smoke detecting devices in each apartment, and the Rent Guidelines Board's summary notes that smoke detectors must now use a non-removable, non-replaceable battery that lasts at least ten years. In a two-family with a tenant, that means the owner's obligation covers the rental unit. Our plan treats the house as one building: smoke alarms on every level, outside each sleeping area and in bedrooms, CO alarms on levels with sleeping rooms and near the boiler and water heater, and all of it linked so a fire in the basement sounds in both apartments. Where walls are plaster on masonry and opening them would be destructive, sealed ten-year alarms with wireless interconnect do the job without new cable. Where the household wants hardwired alarms, we test the circuit before connecting anything and bring in an electrician if a new circuit is needed.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Soundview Building From the Roof Hatch Down

Start where the reported fires started. On a low-rise or walk-up survey, the technician goes to the top first: the bulkhead door, the roof hatch, and the space above the top-floor ceilings. A light and a camera on a pole show whether that space is open end to end, whether old cable or ductwork crosses it, and whether anything up there runs on power. The photos go to the owner whether we get the job or not.

Then the top floor, before the lobby. We knock on a top-floor door or two with the super, look at the ceiling and the alarms, and ask whether residents have ever smelled smoke or seen water stains from above. Top-floor tenants usually know their ceilings better than anyone.

Walk every stair, every landing. In towers and in single-stair co-ops alike, the stair is the way out. We note each door, whether it closes and latches on its own, what is stored on the landings, and whether anyone charges bikes or scooters there, because the June 2025 Fteley Avenue fire is a reminder of what batteries can do.

Find the service rooms. Compactor rooms, boiler rooms, bike rooms, storage cages and laundry rooms are where fires grow unseen. Each gets noted with what is in it and what detection is there now.

Read the panel, then read the history. The panel's screen, event log and battery dates get photographed, the monitoring account identified, and the super asked how often the system has sounded in the last year and whether the fire department came. A building with repeated activations gets a separate list of suspect devices. Only after all of that do we start a drawing or a price.

Compliance notes for Soundview buildings and businesses

  • Apartments need alarms whatever else the building has. The Housing Maintenance Code requires landlords to provide and install smoke detecting devices in every apartment, and Built Engineers' summary notes smoke and carbon monoxide alarms are required in almost all residential occupancies even where no building system is.
  • Battery alarms must be the sealed kind. The Rent Guidelines Board's FAQ says smoke detectors must use a non-removable, non-replaceable battery that powers the alarm for at least ten years.
  • Subsidized buildings follow NSPIRE. HPD's November 2024 bulletin says that from December 29, 2024, smoke alarms in properties under the NSPIRE standard must be hardwired or have sealed ten-year batteries, with at least one working alarm on every level, outside every sleeping area and inside each bedroom.
  • NYCHA apartments have their own rule. City Limits reports that city law requires every NYCHA apartment to have a hardwired smoke detector with battery backup and a self-closing door to the hall. NYCHA arranges that work in its own buildings.
  • Filed systems start with approved plans. Fire alarm design and installation documents go to the FDNY for review and approval before installation, plans are approved by a New York State registered architect or professional engineer, the work involves a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder, and the FDNY issues a Project Authorization before work begins. We install, label and pre-test; we are not the design professional.
  • Complete before the inspection. The FDNY's Fire Alarm Inspection Unit inspects a filed system once it is 100 percent complete and pre-tested.
  • A repair is not a redesign. Replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair; moving or replacing a control panel with something different needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Tall buildings may need voice. A SPARC summary of the DOB bulletin on existing buildings says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor.
  • Mitchell-Lama contracts need HPD. HPD's rules for housing companies require competitive bids and written HPD approval above a set contract size, and allow HPD to review smaller contracts too.
  • Records and monitoring. Owners keep inspection and maintenance records for at least three years, and monitoring that reports to the FDNY must come from an FDNY-approved central station.
  • No landmark district found. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission district or individual city landmark in Soundview. The synagogue at 1115 Ward Avenue is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, which is a separate federal listing.
The Space Under the Roof

Watson Avenue, Twice: Why Soundview's Low-Rise Buildings Need a Plan for Their Top Floor

Two fires a few years apart on the same avenue make the case better than any code section. In July 2019 the fire at 1466 Watson Avenue went to four alarms, displaced at least 20 families and sent smoke high enough to be seen across the borough. FDNY Chief of Operations Richard Thomas told ABC7 that firefighters arriving at the building found fire on the top floor "in what we call the cockloft space, which is the space between the ceiling and the roof." Residents told CBS New York the smoke detectors stayed silent and that neighbors pounding on doors were the only warning, and the FDNY said it was looking into whether working detectors were in the building. The same building had a fire in 2017 that was ruled electrical. In 2023, according to Hoodline's later account, a fire on the top floor and cockloft of a NYCHA building on Watson Avenue injured two firefighters. We have no information about the detection in either building beyond those reports, and we draw no conclusion about why anyone did or did not hear an alarm. What both fires share is location.

A fire above the ceiling is invisible from below. The cockloft of a low-rise is usually dark, unheated, uninhabited and continuous. A fire that starts in it, from old wiring, roof equipment or a fire that climbs from a top-floor apartment, can run its length before anything inside the apartments senses it, because smoke and heat have to come down through the ceiling first. An apartment smoke alarm is designed for the apartment. It is the right device for the bedroom; it is not a roof-space detector.

What goes up there, and when it is a filing. Where an owner or a design professional decides the roof space should be watched, heat detection is usually the device, chosen for the temperature range that space actually reaches, from winter cold to the heat that builds under a summer roof. Those devices connect to a building panel, which makes them part of a fire alarm system. If the building already has a filed system, adding a new zone may need the FDNY to see it; if it has none, the first question is whether a system is required or simply wanted. Either way, the design professional and the FDNY process come first, and we say so before quoting an installation.

What a top-floor household can do now. Nothing in the roof space replaces working alarms inside the apartments, and top-floor tenants are the first to be threatened by a cockloft fire that breaks through. So the immediate work is the same as everywhere else, done with extra care at the top: sealed ten-year or hardwired alarms in every bedroom and outside sleeping areas, a carbon monoxide alarm where the rules call for one, alarms tested with the bedroom door closed, and residents told what to do if they smell smoke but hear nothing. In our experience, top-floor residents who have smelled smoke and seen stains or heat marks on their ceilings are worth listening to on the survey.

Keeping the path for cable from becoming a path for fire. Every opening we make between the top floor and the roof space, and every abandoned opening we find, is noted. Ours are sealed with fire-rated material before we leave. Openings made by others are photographed and reported to the owner, so the trade that made them can close them properly.

Fires in Tall Buildings

Morrison Avenue, Lafayette Avenue and the Fifth Floor: How Detection Works When Fire Is Between Floors

Two layers, two jobs. In a typical Soundview tower, the alarm inside an apartment warns that household, and the building system in the halls and service spaces warns the floor and, where monitored, calls for a response. Residents often expect one to do the other's job. A smoke alarm in a fourth-floor bedroom does not sound on the fifth floor, and a hall detector does not sense a fire behind a closed apartment door until smoke leaks into the hall. Both layers need to work, and residents need to know which is which.

Doors matter as much as devices. City Limits notes that city law pairs NYCHA's hardwired detectors with a self-closing door to the hall, and the same logic applies in private towers: a door that closes on its own keeps smoke in the apartment of origin long enough for the hall and stairs to stay usable. When we survey a tower, propped apartment and stair doors, broken closers and missing latches go on the report even though they are not fire alarm devices, because detection buys time and doors protect it.

Where a fire spreads past the floor of origin. When a fire reaches three floors, as the Lafayette Avenue report described, the floors above become the most dangerous places in the building. Detection on those floors' halls and stair landings, and alarms inside their apartments that work with doors closed, are what reach the people above the fire. In buildings tall enough for voice communication, the design professional's plan for messages is what tells those residents what to do.

Batteries in the apartments. At 1260 Fteley Avenue in June 2025, the FDNY confirmed crews found lithium-ion batteries at the scene, and Firehouse.com reported that the FDNY said battery-caused fires citywide were up about 70 percent in 2026. In a tower, a battery fire in one apartment becomes everyone's problem within minutes. Owners who know where residents store and charge bikes and scooters can place detection to suit, and boards that set aside a bike room give those batteries a watched home away from apartments and stairs.

Who Answers for the Alarm

NYCHA, HPD-Supervised, Subsidized or Private: The Rule Depends on the Building

Few neighborhoods in the city mix ownership types as thoroughly as Soundview. On a single walk down Lafayette Avenue you can pass NYCHA buildings, Mitchell-Lama towers, a former Mitchell-Lama complex that became co-ops, new affordable rentals with HPD financing, senior buildings and private houses. The question of who has to put alarms in, which kind and to what standard has a different answer for each, and getting it wrong wastes money or leaves people unprotected.

Public housing. NYCHA apartments are required by city law to have hardwired smoke detectors with battery backup. NYCHA owns and manages its developments and arranges its own fire alarm work, including at the nine developments in Soundview. In 2016 the Department of Investigation found NYCHA workers had falsified detector inspections, and some residents said they removed detectors or batteries after inspections; those findings were citywide. NYCHA's Sotomayor Houses had a federal NSPIRE inspection scheduled in October, according to NYCHA Journal. We did not research NYCHA's internal procedures and do not take work inside NYCHA buildings without NYCHA arranging it.

Subsidized private buildings. For properties in HPD's subsidized portfolio that fall under the federal NSPIRE standard, HPD told owners that from December 29, 2024, every smoke alarm must be hardwired or have a sealed ten-year battery, with at least one working alarm on every level, outside every sleeping area and inside each bedroom. Inside-the-bedroom placement is the requirement older buildings most often miss, because many were set up with one alarm in the hall outside the bedrooms. Owners preparing for inspection need an apartment-by-apartment count, not a guess.

Mitchell-Lama companies. The housing companies running Soundview's Mitchell-Lama towers fall under HPD supervision, and HPD's contract rules decide how fire alarm work gets bought: competitive bids and HPD approval above a set size, a clause that no work starts until HPD approves the agreement, and HPD inspection before final payment. A board that starts planning early, with a scope that can be bid, avoids the most common delay, which is an approved contract that does not match what the building actually needs.

Market-rate rentals, co-ops, condos and houses. Here the Housing Maintenance Code and the Building Code set the floor: landlords install smoke detecting devices in every apartment, sealed ten-year batteries where alarms are battery powered, CO alarms where the rules require them, and a building system where the building's size and occupancy call for one. Co-ops and condos add their own documents on who maintains what. In owner-occupied houses, the owner decides how far above the minimum to go, and we usually recommend linked alarms through the whole house.

Electrification and carbon monoxide. The Bronx Times reported that 1471 Watson Avenue, part of the Sotomayor Houses, was set to become NYCHA's first all-electric building, and that the pilot showed a 43 percent drop in daily carbon monoxide exposure. That is a reminder that CO comes from fuel-burning equipment: gas ranges, boilers, water heaters and dryers. Where a building still burns fuel, CO alarms belong where the rules require them and near that equipment; where it has stopped, the plan should be reviewed rather than assumed.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Soundview: Questions Answered

These are the situations that boards, managing agents, landlords, homeowners and business operators in Soundview bring to us. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Top floors and the space under the roof

We live on the top floor of a six-story building, and the fires we keep reading about in Soundview started near the roof. Does a top-floor apartment need anything the floors below don't?

Inside the apartment, the requirements are the same as on any floor: working smoke alarms outside sleeping areas and, in most rental and subsidized buildings, inside each bedroom, plus carbon monoxide alarms where the rules call for them. What changes on the top floor is the building around you. Your ceiling is the floor of the roof space, so a fire up there reaches you first, and a fire in your apartment can reach the roof space quickly. That makes three things worth asking for. First, alarms that are actually working, sealed or hardwired, and tested with the bedroom door closed, because you may be the first to be woken. Second, a look at the roof space during any survey of the building, including whether old penetrations above your ceiling have been sealed. Third, a clear plan for what you should do if you smell smoke but hear nothing: leave, close the door behind you, warn your neighbors and call the fire department, rather than waiting for an alarm. If you rent, the detection above the ceiling is the owner's decision and may involve a design professional; you can ask the owner whether it has been considered, and if you own a co-op apartment, you can ask the board the same question.

If heat detection goes into the low space under our flat roof, how does anyone test it later when you can't stand up in there?

The access route for testing has to be planned at the same time as the device, not discovered at the first inspection. Every detector in a building system has to be reachable for testing and maintenance, and a device that can only be reached by crawling across ceiling joists is a device that will not be tested properly. When we plan detection for a roof space, we choose locations that can be reached from the hatch, from a planned access panel in a common hall ceiling, or from a walkway, and we mark each access point on the device list and the drawings. Some heat detectors can be tested with tools on extension poles from a short distance; others need the technician beside them. We pick the type with the testing method in mind, and we note on the records which ones need a ladder at the hatch and which need someone inside the space. Where a design professional is laying out the system, we raise access before the layout is fixed. For owners, the practical result is a written route to every device above the top floor, so whoever services the system next year does not have to guess or skip it.

Towers, co-ops and Mitchell-Lama buildings

Our Mitchell-Lama building's contracts need HPD sign-off. How should a fire alarm proposal be written so competing bids can actually be compared?

Every bidder should be pricing the same scope, described the same way, and the board should be able to see which parts are fixed prices and which depend on drawings that do not exist yet. HPD's rules ask housing companies for the lowest cost compatible with quality, and that comparison only works if quality is defined. We write proposals for Mitchell-Lama buildings with devices counted by floor and by space, each device type named, cable routes described for each riser or stair, testing and documentation listed as their own items, and any work that depends on a registered design professional's plans separated out so it can be priced after the design. We also state what is excluded, such as patching beyond small openings or fire-stopping existing penetrations made by others, so a low bid that leaves those out does not look cheaper than it is. If the board's managing agent has a bid form, we use it. The contract itself has to carry the clauses the rules require, including that no work starts until HPD approves the agreement, and those are for the company's counsel and agent to confirm, not for us to interpret.

Our tower is built of concrete block walls and precast plank floors. How does new fire alarm cable get from the riser to each floor when there's no hollow ceiling to fish through?

In a block-and-plank building there is usually no space above the ceiling to pull cable through, and the walls are solid or grouted block, so the routes have to be designed rather than found. The usual options are existing chases and risers that have spare room and can legally carry fire alarm cable, new surface raceway or conduit along hall ceilings and walls, and short drilled penetrations through block from a stair or riser into a corridor, each sealed with fire-rated material afterward. In the halls of a mid-century tower, a neat, painted surface raceway along the line where wall meets ceiling is often the least disruptive route, and the board can choose its color. Inside apartments, where we are installing household alarms, sealed ten-year alarms with wireless interconnect avoid cable entirely. Drilling into precast plank is treated with care: we do not core through structural plank without the design professional's or the building engineer's approval, and we avoid it wherever a surface route exists. We confirm the construction on site before planning routes, since even within one complex buildings were sometimes built differently.

Shareholders in our co-op keep renovating kitchens and moving walls. Who makes sure the smoke alarms still cover each apartment's new layout?

The co-op's alteration process is the right place to catch it, and the simplest fix is to make alarm placement a line in the alteration agreement and the sign-off. When a shareholder moves a wall, a bedroom can end up without an alarm, an alarm can end up inside a kitchen where it sounds every time someone cooks, or a hardwired alarm can be disconnected and left on the ceiling as decoration. None of those is obvious from the hall. We suggest boards require three things for any renovation that changes rooms: a drawing showing where smoke and carbon monoxide alarms will be in the new layout, confirmation that hardwired alarms were reconnected or replaced and linked to the rest of the apartment, and a short test at the end, witnessed by the super or by us. If the renovation touches a building system device, such as a heat detector a building panel watches, it is building work and goes through the board's contractor. We can write a one-page alarm checklist for the board's alteration package and do the final checks for shareholders who want an outside sign-off, at our service rate.

Subsidized buildings and senior housing

Our HPD-subsidized building has an NSPIRE inspection coming. What should we fix first for the smoke alarms?

Start with an apartment-by-apartment count against the three placement rules in HPD's bulletin, because that is where older buildings fall short. Under NSPIRE, every level needs at least one working smoke alarm, every sleeping area needs one outside it, and each bedroom needs one inside it, and every alarm must be hardwired or have a sealed ten-year battery. Many Soundview buildings were set up decades ago with one alarm in each apartment's hall and none in the bedrooms, and some still have alarms with replaceable batteries that tenants have taken out. So the first job is a survey: every apartment, every bedroom, every alarm, its power type and its date. From that list we can tell you how many alarms are missing, how many are the wrong type and how many are past their manufacturer's replacement date, and price the fix per unit. The second job is access, since apartments you cannot enter cannot be counted; give tenants notice and plan return visits. The third is records: a list matched to each apartment that shows what is there and when it went in. We do not conduct NSPIRE inspections and cannot tell you what an inspector will cite, but this is the work that puts a building in a position to pass.

Our senior residence has a social-services office near the lobby that closes in the evening. After hours, who should a fire alarm signal reach?

The answer has to be written down before it is needed, and it should never be the empty office. In a modern senior building with a filed system, the building alarm reports to an FDNY-approved central station, which notifies the fire department, so the response to a building alarm does not depend on staff. What staff hours change is everything around that: who meets firefighters at the door, who knows which residents need help to leave, who checks on residents after an alarm, and who is called when the panel shows trouble rather than fire. For evenings and weekends, those calls should go to an on-call super or manager whose number the central station and the building both hold. Inside apartments, where household alarms sound only in the unit, residents who might not hear or respond need alarms suited to them, such as low-frequency tone devices or strobes, and staff need a current list of those residents to share with responders. We help managers put that list, the call sheet and the after-hours routine in one binder at the panel. The design of the building system itself remains the design professional's.

Batteries, bike rooms and alarms that sound for nothing

New buildings nearby have bike rooms, and our older building wants to turn a cellar storage room into one. What detection does that room need?

A bike room in a cellar becomes a room full of batteries, so plan it as a fire risk from the first day, not as storage. The newer Soundview buildings that include bike rooms had them designed with the building; an older building creating one is changing the use of a space, and that has consequences. If the building has a fire alarm system, adding detection in the room means adding devices to the system, and depending on the building and the change, the FDNY may need to see plans. If it does not, the room still needs detection that will alert someone, because a cellar room is usually empty and closed. Heat detection is often suited to a room where charging equipment is used, with smoke detection where the air is clean and a design calls for it. Just as important are the decisions that are not ours: whether residents may charge in the room or only park, where chargers plug in, and how the door closes. Write rules for the room before it opens, keep it clear of stored furniture and cartons, and do not let it become the place every household puts the battery they would rather not keep upstairs. We can survey the room, tell you which category of work it falls into and price the detection.

The fire department keeps coming to our building for alarms with no fire. How do you find which device is causing it?

We start with the panel's memory and the building's own record, then go to the devices. Most panels keep an event history that shows which zone or address activated, when, and how often. Matched to the super's notes on dates and times, that usually points to a few suspects: a hall detector beside a stair door where cold air and dust rush in, a smoke detector at the top of a stair that catches steam or cooking smoke, a device in a compactor or boiler room that should have been heat, an old detector past its life that has become oversensitive, or a wiring fault that looks to the panel like an alarm. We inspect each suspect, clean or test where appropriate, and check its age and type against where it sits. The fix is to replace a failing device like for like, correct a wiring fault, or, where a device is simply the wrong type for its location, raise it with the design professional, since changing device types on a filed system is a design question. What we do not do is disable a device to stop the calls. A building whose alarm sounds for nothing is training its residents to stay inside, which is the most dangerous outcome of all.

Attached and two-family houses

Our house has hardwired linked alarms on two floors, but the top floor was redone with wireless alarms from another brand. Can the two kinds talk to each other?

Usually not directly, and mixing them is the reason many houses end up with alarms that each work alone but do not warn the rest of the house. Hardwired interconnect and wireless interconnect are generally specific to one manufacturer, and often to one product family. Some manufacturers make a bridge device that connects their own hardwired alarms to their own wireless ones, but not to another brand's. So the first step is to identify every alarm in the house: brand, model, date and power type. If the hardwired alarms are from a family that offers a bridge, the wireless ones may be replaced with matching wireless units that join the hardwired circuit. If not, the cleanest answer is often to replace one set so the whole house is on one system. In many Soundview houses, where hardwired alarms are reaching their replacement date anyway, replacing everything with one family of sealed ten-year wireless alarms, or with one family of hardwired alarms plus wireless where cable cannot reach, costs less than trying to make two systems cooperate. We test the result by setting off the alarm farthest from the bedrooms and listening in every room.

Businesses and congregations

Our business on Soundview Avenue keeps one worker in the back room overnight. Will an alarm on the sales floor reach that person?

Not reliably, and a lone worker is exactly the person a plan has to reach, because nobody else is there to shout a warning. A back room or office with the door closed, a radio on or machines running can block the sound of a device on the sales floor. We plan for a lone overnight worker in three ways. First, notification where they actually are: a sounder in the back room, and a strobe if the room is loud, so a fire anywhere in the store reaches them. Second, a signal that leaves the building, so if the worker is the one in trouble, someone outside knows; central station monitoring through our partners at $19.99 a month does that for a store not required to have a filed system. Third, a clear way out: the back exit unlocked from inside, unblocked by stock, and known to the worker. If the store is below apartments, the same plan should warn the households above, since the worker may be the only person awake in the building. Whether a store needs a filed system depends on its occupancy and size, which a design professional decides, and if it does, notification placement is part of that design.

Our congregation's 1930s building is listed on the National Register of Historic Places, like the synagogue on Ward Avenue. Does that listing limit where fire alarm devices can go?

The National Register is a federal list, and it is separate from the city's landmarks system. In Soundview we found no Landmarks Preservation Commission district or individual city landmark, and Landmarks review applies to designated city landmarks, not to buildings simply because they are on the National Register. National Register listing generally matters for work involving federal funding or federal approvals, so if your project uses a preservation grant or other public money, ask the funder what review applies before work starts. Separately from any rule, a historic house of worship deserves care: decorative ceilings, woodwork and original finishes should not be drilled just because it is convenient. We plan device locations that meet spacing requirements while avoiding decorative surfaces, run cable through concealed routes or carefully placed raceway, and show the congregation exactly where each device will go before anything is mounted. Whether the building needs a full system depends on how many people gather, which a registered design professional determines from its occupancy and size. We can survey it, tell you what is there and price what it needs.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Soundview

These are claims that come up again and again in generic online answers and national guides, paraphrased, each followed by what Soundview's buildings and the published rules actually show.

"In public housing, keeping a smoke detector working is the tenant's responsibility"

General advice about rentals often tells tenants to buy and maintain their own alarms, and that advice gets applied to every kind of building.

City law, as City Limits reported, requires every NYCHA apartment to have a hardwired smoke detector with battery backup, and the Housing Maintenance Code puts the duty to provide and install smoke detecting devices on landlords in private buildings. Tenants have duties too, such as not removing or disabling alarms, but the installation belongs to the owner.

In a neighborhood with nine NYCHA developments and many subsidized buildings, telling a tenant to fix it themselves sends the job to the wrong person. The right first step is telling the owner, in writing.

"A battery smoke alarm is fine as long as you change the battery once a year"

The annual battery change is the most repeated piece of fire safety advice there is, and generic answers still present it as the standard for any home.

The Rent Guidelines Board's summary says smoke detectors in New York City must now use a non-removable, non-replaceable battery lasting at least ten years, and HPD's NSPIRE rule for subsidized buildings allows only hardwired alarms or sealed ten-year batteries.

A replaceable-battery alarm is also the kind that gets its battery borrowed for a remote, which is what the 2016 DOI findings described at NYCHA. In Soundview rentals, the sealed alarm is the rule, and in houses it is the better choice.

"Smoke alarms in the top-floor apartments will catch a fire in the roof"

Answers about attic and roof fires often assume that alarms on the top floor cover whatever is above them.

At 1466 Watson Avenue the FDNY found the fire in the cockloft, which its chief of operations described as the space between the ceiling and the roof, and a 2023 fire on Watson Avenue also involved a top floor and cockloft. A fire there has to come through the ceiling before an apartment alarm senses it.

Detection in that space, usually heat detection, is a building decision made by the owner and, on a filed system, the design professional. Apartment alarms remain essential, but they are not the roof's protection.

"Concrete high-rises keep a fire inside the apartment where it starts"

Generic answers about fireproof construction often suggest that a tower's concrete floors make spread unlikely.

The December 2025 fire at 1965 Lafayette Avenue spread to three floors, and the fourth-floor fire at 825 Morrison Avenue in January 2026 sent two people to the hospital. Fire and smoke move through open doors, windows, shafts and penetrations, whatever the floors are made of.

That is why a tower needs both layers working, the alarm in the apartment and the building system in the halls, along with doors that close on their own. Construction slows a fire; it does not replace warning.

"Fire alarm installation costs about what the national averages say"

National cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish averages from many places and building types, and summaries repeat them as if they apply to any address.

An average cannot see a block-and-plank tower with no hollow ceilings, a subsidized building that needs an alarm in every bedroom before an inspection, or a Mitchell-Lama contract that needs HPD approval. Those details decide device count, cable route and schedule.

Our standard Bronx list starts at $600 for a basic residential system, runs $400 to $800 per unit for landlords, and $2,000 to $6,000 for a custom hardwired system, written per building after a survey. Filed systems are priced after the design.

"Upgrading an old building's fire alarm just means installing a new panel"

Some answers treat panel replacement as a simple swap, like changing an appliance.

ASM Integrators' summary of the rules draws a clear line: replacing a panel with an identical one and no design change is a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel with different equipment needs new plans filed with the FDNY. In a Mitchell-Lama building, the contract also has to clear HPD.

For an owner in Soundview, the right first question is which side of that line the job is on. We answer it before quoting, so a board does not approve a swap that turns out to be a filing.

"A fire alarm that goes off too often is just an annoyance"

Consumer answers often treat nuisance alarms as a matter of comfort, solved by moving or switching off the device.

A 2025 dispatch summary described an automatic alarm on Soundview Avenue as problematic, with multiple devices sounding. Repeated activations send firefighters away from real emergencies and teach residents to ignore the sound, and switching a device off can leave a space with no protection at all.

The fix is diagnosis: the panel's history, the device's age and type, and whether it suits its location, with any change of device type on a filed system taken to the design professional. A trusted alarm is the one people leave for.
