The Neighborhood

Why Schuylerville's Storefront Buildings, Walk-Ups and New Bruckner Projects Need Fire Alarm Work Done to the Letter

Schuylerville is a small, low-rise piece of the East Bronx, about 0.68 square miles tucked between the Bruckner Expressway and the Hutchinson River Parkway in Community District 10, with ZIP codes 10461 and 10465. Wikipedia describes its housing as mostly single-family and two-family houses, notes the nickname "Skyville," and puts Saint Raymond's Cemetery in its southwestern corner. Officially it is counted as part of Throggs Neck, though people tie it more closely to Pelham Bay and Country Club. That naming question matters more than you might expect, and we come back to it below.

A neighborhood of houses still has plenty of buildings that need a real fire alarm system. Homes.com's property data for Schuylerville breaks the built square footage down this way: townhouses about 28 percent, detached homes about 20 percent, apartments about 18 percent, industrial space about 17 percent, retail about 9 percent, condos and co-ops about 4 percent and offices about 3 percent. The same page puts the median year built at 1930 and says about 61 percent of households rent. Put plainly, a third of the floor area is apartments, shops, offices and industrial space, much of it in buildings close to a century old.

Those buildings sit mostly on the edges. East Tremont Avenue and Crosby Avenue are the shopping streets, lined with pizzerias, diners, a Puerto Rican restaurant and bar with late hours, cafes, markets and service businesses, many of them under apartments. The Bruckner Boulevard service road carries a supermarket, a firehouse and an elementary school, and on the western edge Herbert H. Lehman High School spans the Hutchinson River Parkway.

The Bruckner edge is also where the neighborhood is changing. In October 2022 the City Council unanimously approved the Bruckner Sites rezoning: four mixed-use apartment buildings, about 348 or 349 homes in all depending on the report, on parcels bounded by the expressway, Crosby Avenue, Balcom Avenue and Meyers Street (CityLand; Bronx Times). Press coverage calls the project Throggs Neck, but those blocks sit north of the expressway, inside the Schuylerville lines Wikipedia draws. A lawsuit against the rezoning was dismissed in August 2023, and in January 2025 Foodtown's management told News 12 it would not build over its supermarket. Each building that does go up brings its own fire alarm filing, and the plans show neatly how the code decides what a building needs.

The problem we see most often: owners of small mixed-use buildings and walk-ups assume the rules for houses cover them. Sometimes they do. Sometimes the building crossed a line years ago, when a store became a restaurant, a basement became a gym or an attic became an apartment, and nobody checked whether a building fire alarm came with that change. And sometimes a system exists but nobody has opened the panel since the last owner. Our job starts by answering a plain question for each building: what does the code and the Certificate of Occupancy require here, and does what is on the wall match it?

This page covers commercial, mixed-use and multifamily fire alarm systems designed under NFPA 72 and the NYC Building Code: building systems with a control panel, devices on monitored circuits and a connection to a central station. It does not cover alarms inside one- and two-family houses, which are household smoke and carbon monoxide alarms under different rules.

Schuylerville at a glance

  • Where: between the Bruckner Expressway and the Hutchinson River Parkway in the East Bronx, Community District 10, ZIP codes 10461 and 10465; officially part of Throggs Neck (Wikipedia).
  • Building stock: mostly one- and two-family houses, with rowhouses, small apartment buildings, a prewar co-op, stores and some industrial space; median year built 1930 (Wikipedia; Homes.com).
  • Shopping streets: East Tremont Avenue and Crosby Avenue, with restaurants, markets and everyday services (Homes.com).
  • Largest building: the Herbert H. Lehman High School campus at 3000 East Tremont Avenue, designed by Eggers and Higgins and opened in 1972, with a classroom wing over the Hutchinson River Parkway (Historic Districts Council).
  • What is changing: the 2022 Bruckner Sites rezoning for four apartment buildings between the expressway and Meyers Street; the supermarket site's building was called off in January 2025 (CityLand; Bronx Times; News 12).
  • Fire protection next door: FDNY Engine 89 and Ladder 50 at 2924 Bruckner Boulevard, a firehouse the Bronx Times dates to 1926 (NYC Open Data firehouse listing; Bronx Times).

If the fire alarm panel in a Schuylerville storefront building, co-op, restaurant or office is beeping, showing a trouble light or sounding with no fire, call (646) 490-0629 and we book the visit within 2 hours. Give us the address, where the panel hangs, the exact words on its screen and who holds the central station account. In a mixed-use building, tell us whether the store or the landlord controls the panel, because that decides who can let us in at night.

Before anyone arrives, take a photo of the display, call the central station so they know the signal is being worked on, and press reset once at most. Do not pull the batteries, unplug the panel or take a detector off the ceiling to quiet a beep. When a required fire alarm in New York City is out of service, the FDNY must be notified and a fire watch kept until protection is restored, and a silenced panel helps nobody.

What we tend to find follows the buildings. A fire alarm system not communicating usually means a phone line the building dropped when the owner switched carriers, so the fire alarm communication failure repair is really a communicator replacement. Fire alarm battery trouble repair starts with a battery load test, because standby batteries age out quietly. Fire alarm intermittent trouble, the worst kind, needs patience and a log of when it happens.

Building Types

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Schuylerville's Building Types, From 1930s Brick to the Bruckner Sites

Before the building types, the basics. What is a commercial fire alarm system? It is a building-wide network of initiating devices (smoke and heat detectors, manual pull stations and sprinkler switches), notification appliances (horns, strobes and speakers), modules and a fire alarm control panel that watches all of it and reports to a central station. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? A device changes state, the panel decides whether it is an alarm, a supervisory signal or a trouble, sounds and flashes what the approved design calls for, sends the signal off-site and runs whatever the building is supposed to do next: release door holders, shut down fans or recall elevators. When does a building need a fire alarm system? When the NYC Building Code, read for that building's use and size, says it does; the design professional makes that call from the Certificate of Occupancy. Here is how that plays out in Schuylerville.

Two- and three-story storefront buildings

Earlier research for our other Schuylerville pages found a typical example at 3054 East Tremont Avenue, listed on StreetEasy as a three-story mixed-use building with five units, built in 1931. A building that size usually does not trigger a system on the apartment side alone, but the stores can: a large dining room, cellar storage or a second floor turned into a studio all shift the answer. Mixed use building fire alarm installation in buildings like this is mostly about drawing the line clearly: which spaces the building's panel protects, which belong to a tenant, and how a fire in the store reaches the people sleeping upstairs.

Prewar co-ops and rental walk-ups

Homes.com mentions a prewar co-op building in the neighborhood, and research for our other Schuylerville pages found a co-op walk-up listed at 1131 Revere Avenue. Under NYC Building Code section 907.2.9, an apartment building needs a fire alarm system where any apartment sits three or more stories above the lowest level of exit discharge, more than one story below the highest, or where the building has more than 16 dwelling units. In those buildings the code calls for a system "without alarm notification appliances": smoke detectors in the public halls and service rooms report to the panel and on to a central station, while the household smoke alarms inside each apartment do the waking up. A board that inherits a 1960s or 1970s panel in that kind of building is usually looking at an outdated fire alarm system replacement sooner or later, because parts for obsolete panels get scarce.

New mid-rise apartment buildings on the Bruckner

The rezoned Bruckner Sites, as CityLand described them at the 2022 Council hearing, are the clearest local example of what the code asks of new buildings. Site B at Bruckner and Edison Avenue was planned at five stories with 102 apartments and enclosed parking for 75 cars; Site C at Bruckner and Revere Avenue at 99 apartments for seniors with a live-in superintendent and 29 parking spaces; Site D at Bruckner and Balcom Avenue as a three-story, 22-unit building for veterans with counseling and job services on site. All three have more than 16 homes, so each falls under section 907.2.9 and gets its own filing. Enclosed parking adds carbon monoxide detection and, depending on the design, heat or smoke detection in the garage. The veterans' building adds office and counseling space, a different use on the same panel.

Senior housing

A building for older tenants is still an apartment building to the code, but the design choices change. Residents may hear less and move slowly, so the design professional weighs strobe coverage in common rooms, fire alarm ADA strobe installation in units set aside for residents with hearing loss, and an annunciator the live-in superintendent and firefighters both read. Site C's partner was the Jewish Association Serving the Aging, per CityLand.

The Lehman campus

The Historic Districts Council describes Herbert H. Lehman High School at 3000 East Tremont Avenue as a 1972 Eggers and Higgins design that straddles the Hutchinson River Parkway: a rectangular main academic building linked to a circular 1,000-seat auditorium by a three-story classroom wing built on the air rights over the road, planned for 1,700 students. Wikipedia lists Renaissance High School for Musical Theater and Technology, Westchester Square Academy, Pelham Lab High School, Schuylerville Preparatory High School and Bronx River High School as co-located on its upper floors. Public schools are handled through the city's school agencies, and we make no claim to work there; we mention it as the far end of the local scale: six schools, an auditorium and a wing over a highway in one building.

Industrial and storage space

Homes.com counts roughly 17 percent of Schuylerville's built floor area as industrial. We found no public list of those buildings, so we name none. In practice, industrial fire alarm installation in a neighborhood like this means garages, workshops and storage buildings near the expressway rather than factories. Industrial fire alarm systems for that kind of space lean on heat detection, sprinkler monitoring and, in a tall open room, beam smoke detector installation or projected beam smoke detector installation across the bay. Warehouse fire alarm installation, warehouse smoke detection installation, storage facility fire alarm installation, industrial building fire alarm installation and manufacturing facility fire alarm installation all follow the same FDNY path, and an industrial fire alarm contractor plans industrial fire detection system installation around dust, vehicles and stock.

Restaurants and bars

A pizzeria with a dozen seats and a restaurant and bar that stays open late are very different buildings to a fire alarm designer. Assembly occupancies cross into a required system by occupant load, so the seat count, the standing room at the bar and any party room upstairs all matter. Either way, a kitchen with a hood needs its own suppression system, and where the building has a fire alarm, the alarm watches that system and reports when it discharges.

Business Fire Alarm Installation on East Tremont Avenue, Crosby Avenue and the Bruckner Service Road

Business fire alarm installation in Schuylerville happens on three strips. Business fire detection systems on each of them start with the same question: what is the space used for today, and does the Certificate of Occupancy say the same thing?

East Tremont Avenue

East Tremont Avenue runs from the Lehman campus at the parkway east toward the Bruckner and carries most of the neighborhood's sit-down restaurants. Homes.com names the Tremont Diner, Havana Café for Cuban food and cocktails, and Made in Puerto Rico, a restaurant and bar with late-night hours. The Bx40 and Bx42 buses run along it. Here the work is restaurant and bar fire alarms in the ground floors of mixed-use buildings, plus the occasional office or clinic upstairs. The detail that matters most is the shared stair: when a dining room and the apartments above use the same exit, smoke from the store must not reach the stair unnoticed.

Crosby Avenue

Crosby Avenue is the other shopping street. Homes.com calls Louie and Ernie's Pizza a local staple and lists Sundays Off Cafe, Metro Acres Market and A-Z Grocery and Deli among the everyday stops. These are small stores, and the fire alarm work tends to be small too: a duct smoke detector on a rooftop unit, a commercial smoke detector replacement in a cellar, a horn strobe added when a back room becomes a seating area. Small jobs still go through the design professional and, if devices are added or moved, through the FDNY.

Bruckner Boulevard service road

Along the expressway the buildings are bigger and lower. The Super Foodtown fills a block at 2945–65 Bruckner Boulevard at Crosby Avenue, according to Produce Business; CityLand reported in 2022 that Site B of the rezoning was then home to batting cages; P.S. 14 Senator John Calandra is at 3047 Bruckner Boulevard, per the city's school directory; and FDNY Engine 89 and Ladder 50 sit at 2924 Bruckner Boulevard. A supermarket is a mercantile occupancy that crosses into a required system by size. A large grocery's fire alarm usually includes sprinkler monitoring, duct detectors on the rooftop air handlers and, where the store has loading docks, carbon monoxide detection under the FDNY's rules.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Certificate of Occupancy Before You Read the Panel

In a neighborhood of older mixed-use buildings, our survey begins with paper. We pull the Certificate of Occupancy, or the building's records if it predates one, and compare each floor's legal use with what is actually there. The gap between the two is where most fire alarm surprises come from: a store listed as a shop that now serves food to forty people, an apartment that the records call office space, a cellar that is legally storage but has a gym in it.

Only then do we open the panel, read its event history, test the batteries and count the devices on each circuit. Where drawings are missing, the panel's labels are the only map left, so the report lists every device and compares the records, the real use and what the alarm covers.

One local habit is worth knowing. Plenty of people here do not call the neighborhood Schuylerville at all; a 50-year resident wrote on Reddit's r/bronx that "No one who lives there ever calls it Schuylerville," and that it is "always pelham bay." The FDNY does not care what the neighborhood is called. What it cares about is the address, block and lot on the filing, and the Certificate of Occupancy behind it. Older paperwork for the same building may say Throggs Neck, Pelham Bay or Schuylerville, so we search by address, not by neighborhood name.

Your Questions Answered

Schuylerville Fire Alarm Questions From Store Owners, Boards, Builders and Managers

Storefront buildings and restaurants

Our East Tremont Avenue building has one front stair that serves both the restaurant's rear exit and the apartments upstairs. What does the fire alarm need to watch in that stair?

That stair is the only way out for the people upstairs, so smoke from the restaurant must not reach it unnoticed. The design professional usually looks at detection at the top of the stair and near any door from the restaurant into it, and at doors that close and latch on their own. Where the building has a required system, those devices report to the panel and the central station; where it has none, a change of use is the moment to ask whether it now needs one.

I own my building and live over my own store on Crosby Avenue. Can the fire alarm just call my cell phone instead of a central station?

Not if the system is required. A required fire alarm in New York City reports to a central station approved to receive fire signals and pass them to the FDNY, and the FDNY's tip sheet expects that connection to be active before the acceptance test is even scheduled. You can and should be on the central station's call list, and many panels can also text you, but that comes on top of monitoring, not instead of it.

Our building went up in 1931 and still has old BX cable in the walls. Can a new fire alarm reuse it?

Not as general wiring. Fire alarm circuits use cable listed for fire alarm service and installed to the approved drawings, and armored cable left over from the building's lighting circuits is not that. What can sometimes be reused is existing fire alarm cable or raceway, if it tests clean, is the right type and suits the new design. The survey sorts out which is which before anyone opens a wall.

Co-ops and walk-ups

A board member found a used fire alarm panel for sale from a store that closed. Can a co-op install a second-hand panel to save money?

It is rarely a saving. The panel has to match the approved design, be listed for the devices it will run, be programmed with the maker's software and have parts and support for years. A used panel with an unknown history, old firmware or a locked program can fail on every one of those points. The engineer specifies the equipment, and the proposal should price new equipment that the maker still supports.

Our co-op's 1960s panel has a handwritten zone chart taped inside its door. Is that enough for firefighters and inspectors?

It is better than nothing and well short of what is expected today. Firefighters need to see where a signal is coming from at a glance, which is why new systems put a labeled display or annunciator at the entrance and keep as-built drawings with the panel. A chart that no longer matches the building can send people to the wrong floor. A survey checks each zone against the chart, and an upgrade or replacement fixes it properly.

Plenty of Schuylerville buildings have a finished cellar. If a walk-up turns its cellar into a legal apartment, can that change the building's fire alarm requirement?

It can. Section 907.2.9 of the NYC Building Code counts apartments more than one story below the highest level of exit discharge, and adding a unit also raises the total count toward the 16-unit line. A legal conversion goes through an architect or engineer, who checks whether the building now crosses a fire alarm threshold along with every other code requirement for the new home.

The Bruckner rezoning buildings

Site B was planned with enclosed parking for 75 cars under its apartments. Why does a garage level usually get heat and carbon monoxide detection rather than ordinary smoke detectors?

Exhaust, dust and cold air make ordinary smoke detectors in a garage a recipe for false alarms, so designers usually rely on sprinklers, the waterflow signal and heat detection there. Carbon monoxide is a separate concern: the FDNY's guidance for businesses ties CO detection to enclosed parking in many occupancies protected by a fire alarm, and the design professional decides how that applies under apartments. The garage normally gets its own zone so firefighters know the problem is below the homes.

The senior building planned at Bruckner and Revere Avenue includes a live-in superintendent. With someone living on site, does the fire alarm still need off-site monitoring?

Yes. Under section 907.2.9, an apartment building's system sends its signals to a central supervising station or a constantly attended location, and one superintendent who sleeps, works and takes days off is not a constantly attended location. The super belongs on the call list and should be able to read the annunciator, but the central station is what makes sure the fire department is called.

The veterans' building planned at Bruckner and Balcom Avenue is only three stories with 22 apartments. Why would a building that low fall under the apartment-building fire alarm rule?

Because height is only one of the triggers. Section 907.2.9 also applies when a building has more than 16 dwelling units, and 22 is more than 16. The counseling and job-services rooms in the plan add a non-residential use, which the design professional classifies and covers on the same drawings.

Site A was designed to rebuild the supermarket under six stories of apartments before the plan was dropped. When homes are built over an existing store, does the store's fire alarm get folded into the new building's?

Usually the store's old system does not survive as it was. The new building is designed as a whole, and the design professional decides whether the store keeps its own panel tied to the building's or becomes part of one system. Either way the design is filed with the FDNY, and while construction goes on the store's existing protection has to keep working or be covered by a fire watch.

Older buildings, paperwork and the Lehman campus

Our building's old records call the area Throggs Neck, the deed says Bronx and the neighbors say Pelham Bay. Which name goes on an FDNY fire alarm filing?

None of them matters by itself. The filing is tied to the street address, borough, block and lot, and to the Certificate of Occupancy or other occupancy document for that property. Use exactly what the city records show for the building, and make sure the floor numbers on the drawings match the Certificate of Occupancy, which the FDNY's own guidance asks for.

Lehman High School's classroom wing sits on air rights over the Hutchinson River Parkway. How does a fire alarm treat a part of a building that bridges a highway?

As part of the same building, with the bridge section designed carefully. Cable and pathways crossing the span are protected and supported like any other run, devices in the wing report to the same system, and the labels must tell firefighters on either side of the parkway exactly where a signal starts. School systems are handled through the city's school agencies; we make no claim to work there and use the building only as an example.

Our 1930 building on a side street has never had a fire alarm system. What usually changes when a prewar building gets one for the first time?

Usually more than the alarm itself. A first system brings cable routes through plaster and brick, a panel location with its own circuit, a central station connection, and often door closers, labeled doors and cleared exits that the design or the FDNY review turns up. The design professional starts from the Certificate of Occupancy and the real layout, and the installer plans routes through closets, chases and hallway ceilings to keep patching small.

Equipment

Types of Commercial Fire Alarm Systems We Install in Schuylerville

A five-unit building over a pizzeria, a 99-apartment senior building and a supermarket do not need the same system. These are the types the design professional chooses among.

Addressable

What is an addressable fire alarm system? Each detector, pull station and module has its own address, so the panel reports "smoke, third-floor hall, north" instead of "zone 2." Addressable fire alarm installation and addressable fire alarm device installation are the normal choice for new apartment buildings.

Conventional

What is a conventional fire alarm system? Devices share zone circuits, and the panel reports which zone tripped. Conventional fire alarm installation, conventional fire alarm system installation and conventional fire alarm panel installation still suit a single store, a small office or a cafe with a few detectors and one pull station.

Apartment-building systems

Systems built for section 907.2.9: hall and service-room smoke detection reporting to a central station without building-wide sounders, plus manual stations where the design calls for them.

Voice evacuation

Fire alarm voice evacuation installation and fire alarm emergency voice communication replace tones with spoken instructions. They suit a large assembly room or school and are rare in Schuylerville's low buildings.

Intelligent detection

Intelligent fire alarm installation uses multi-sensor detectors that weigh smoke, heat and other signals. An intelligent fire detection system helps in a restaurant building where steam and cooking fumes fool simple smoke heads.

Monitoring connection

Fire alarm monitoring system installation, fire alarm communicator installation and fire alarm central station communicator installation send signals to the central station over cellular or IP paths, replacing copper phone lines many buildings no longer have.

"A small apartment building only needs smoke alarms"

For a two-family house, that is close to true. For an apartment building, section 907.2.9 of the NYC Building Code turns on three things: more than 16 dwelling units, any apartment three or more stories above the lowest exit level, or any apartment more than one story below the highest exit level.

Can a store's fire alarm panel run off the store's own breaker panel, or does it need its own circuit?
+

It needs a dedicated branch circuit, arranged and marked so it cannot be switched off by accident, plus standby batteries sized by calculation. Plugging a panel into a store outlet or sharing a lighting circuit is not acceptable. The design professional shows the power source on the drawings, and the electrician of record installs it.

How can an owner tell whether a fire alarm quote really includes the FDNY filing and the acceptance test?
+

Read the lines. A complete proposal names who prepares and seals the drawings, who files the TM-1 and how the government fees are handled, who installs and programs, who pre-tests, who requests the acceptance test on form B-45 and who attends it with the right FDNY certificate. Anything left out, such as the design fee, central station setup or patching, should be listed as an exclusion so it does not surface later.

Is the bell on the front wall of a sprinklered building the same thing as the fire alarm?
+

Not necessarily. In many older buildings that bell is driven by water moving in the sprinkler pipe or by a flow switch, and it can ring whether or not the building has a fire alarm panel. Where a fire alarm exists, the flow switch usually reports to it too, and the panel sends the signal to the central station. A survey shows which arrangement your building has.

Can a restaurant's natural gas detector report to the building fire alarm?
+

Sometimes, if the design professional includes it and the equipment is listed to work together, but gas detection is a separate safety system with its own rules. Some buildings tie a gas detector to an automatic gas shutoff and send a supervisory or trouble signal to the panel; others keep them apart. No one should connect it to the panel unless the change is designed and recorded.

How many more devices can an old conventional panel take before it has to be replaced?
+

Each zone and each notification circuit has limits on devices, current and wire length, and an older panel may have no spare zones left at all. Adding devices means checking those limits and the standby battery calculation, then handling the change through the design professional. When a panel is at its limit or its maker no longer supports it, replacement usually makes more sense than squeezing in another circuit.

Can a fire alarm company check or program our panel remotely?
+

Some newer panels and communicators allow remote status checks and, with proper security, some programming. Many older panels in Schuylerville buildings do not. Remote access never replaces the on-site testing NFPA 72 requires, and any programming change to a filed system still has to match the approved design and be tested.

We already pay a burglar alarm company. Can it also monitor the building's fire alarm?
+

Only if its central station is approved to receive fire alarm signals in New York City and the account is set up as a fire account. Many security companies do both, but a burglar alarm contract does not cover fire signals automatically. Ask for the central station's fire listing in writing before you sign anything that moves the fire alarm account.

Our building has no sprinklers, yet the panel shows a supervisory signal. What else can a fire alarm supervise?
+

A supervisory signal means something that keeps part of the fire protection from working has changed, not that there is a fire. Without sprinklers it can come from a kitchen hood suppression system, a duct detector the design set to supervise rather than alarm, or other equipment the design ties in. The panel's event log names the point, and a technician traces what that point is before resetting anything.

Is a heat detector the same thing as a sprinkler head?
+

No. A heat detector sends a signal to the fire alarm panel when it senses heat; a sprinkler head opens and sprays water. A building can have either or both. Where both exist, the sprinkler system's flow switch also signals the panel, so the alarm sounds whether the heat detector or the sprinkler reacts first.

Can a co-op board read the panel's event history to see how often the system goes into trouble?
+

Yes, most addressable panels and many conventional ones keep an event log that a technician can print or export. Reviewing it at each inspection shows patterns, such as the same detector tripping every winter or a communicator dropping its signal at night. Ask your fire alarm company to include a short event summary in each inspection report.
