The Neighborhood

Why Park Slope Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Park Slope is a neighborhood of front doors. Most of its housing is row houses, many of them brownstones and limestones divided over the past century into two, three, four or more flats, plus six- to eight-story apartment buildings near the park and a wave of newer rentals along Fourth Avenue. In nearly every one of these buildings, several households share a single street door, a small vestibule and a hall or lobby where deliveries pile up. That shared doorway is where the camera problems in the local news keep starting. It is also the part of the building that the city's preservation rules care most about, because the stoop, the door surround and the facade are what make a Park Slope block look the way it does.

The thefts that made the news follow a short, repeatable script. Late on December 15, 2020, a man broke the front-door lock of a building on St. Marks Place between Fifth and Fourth Avenues, took a package from the lobby and put it in his book bag. On December 26, at about 1:30 in the morning, he did the same at a building on Sixth Street near Fifth Avenue and rode off toward Fifth Avenue on a black bicycle. Patch reported that he was caught on surveillance video both times, and police released stills from the footage. In August 2019, CBS New York aired video from another Park Slope building in which three men cut open boxes in the lobby, one apparently holding a knife, and shoved a building worker in the hall on the way out. A resident told the station that management had asked tenants to keep track of who was coming in and out. That is the job a lobby camera can do without asking neighbors to stand watch.

House burglaries here have tended to be quick, daytime and through the door. Between December 2019 and January 2020, police linked six break-ins in Park Slope and the blocks around it to one man. At a house on Seventh Avenue he damaged the lock and walked in mid-afternoon; on President Street he broke a door open around noon; near Seventh Avenue and Seventh Street he forced the front door. Patch reported that police had surveillance video only from the last location, and that it was grainy. A burglary pattern of six homes, and one usable clip, is the clearest local argument for what this page keeps returning to: a camera is worth what its picture of a face is worth. Other cases came in higher up. In 2017 a burglar climbed in through a fire escape at a building off Sterling Place, and in May 2016 a man on an outside ledge at a 19th-century townhouse on Eighth Avenue near Carroll Street damaged a Tiffany stained-glass window that DNAinfo valued at $50,000 before he fell and was arrested.

The avenues have their own record. In March 2016 the 78th Precinct told its community council that recently obtained surveillance video was helping it track the people behind break-ins at Aji Sushi, Postmark Cafe and Acme Wine and Spirits on Fifth Avenue, one of them hit twice in a week. In late December 2025, News 12 reported that four masked men robbed a family news stand on Seventh Avenue and took about $2,000 from two separate registers, and that surveillance footage showed them at both. "They brandished a weapon, and they told him ‘We have a gun,’" Zack Shabain, whose family has run the store for more than 60 years, told the station.

None of this makes Park Slope unusually dangerous. The incidents here cluster at shared doors, lobbies, rear windows and registers, and the footage from them was sometimes good and sometimes too poor to help. This page is about closing that gap. Many owners pair the cameras with a fob reader on the inner vestibule door, and we plan both at once.

The Street Door

The Lock Breaks Before the Box Goes Missing: Camera Coverage for a Park Slope Front Door

Read the December 2020 reports closely and the order of events matters. The man did not wait for someone to hold the door. He broke the front-door lock, stepped into the lobby, took a package and left. A camera aimed only at the package shelf records the last ten seconds of that. A camera that also sees the lock side of the door records the moment of entry, the tool or the shove, and usually the clearest face, because a person forcing a lock has to stand still and face the door. On a Park Slope building, that one decision changes what the footage is good for. Below is how we break a typical shared entrance into the places a camera has to cover, from the sidewalk to the hall.

The stoop and the top step

Most brownstones and limestones here are entered up a stoop to a parlor-floor door, with a second door under the stoop to the garden floor. The top step is where a visitor stops, rings and waits, so it is where the first face picture comes from. A camera mounted above and slightly to one side of the door, looking down the last few steps, gets that face without staring across the street. A wide camera set high on the facade, by contrast, shows the whole stoop and a face the size of a fingernail. On a landmarked front, the camera also has to sit where the Commission will accept it, which on most row houses means under the door hood, beneath a band course or inside the vestibule looking out, not on the carved surround. We cover how those rules work further down.

The lock side of the street door

The lock side is the edge of the door with the latch and strike. Whoever forces the door, or slips a card or a tool into the gap, has to put their hands and face right there. We aim a camera at that edge from inside the vestibule when we can, so it sees through the door glass at a person working the lock, or from the hall looking back at the inner side of the latch when the outer door is solid. On double doors, which many Park Slope houses still have, the active leaf is the one with the latch, and the camera favors that leaf. The goal is a picture in which the latch, the hands and a face are all in frame at the same time.

The vestibule

The vestibule is the small room between the outer and inner doors, often with mailboxes on one wall and, on many blocks, a stroller parked against the other. It is also where a lot of packages end up when there is no package room. A small camera high in the corner over the inner door sees everyone who comes through the outer door, at face height, as they step in from the light. That last part is the hard part. Coming in from a sunny stoop into a dim vestibule is the worst lighting a camera faces, and a cheap camera turns the person into a dark shape against a white rectangle. We choose cameras with real wide dynamic range for that spot, and on a south-facing stoop we sometimes move the camera so it is not looking straight out at the sky.

The lobby, the hall and the package shelf

Inside, the 2019 footage CBS aired showed two of the men walking down the hall while one went through boxes. A lobby camera should see the package shelf or table and the path from it back to the door, because the path out is where the second face picture comes from. In a brownstone split into flats, the hall is often narrow and runs back toward the stairs; one camera at the rear of the hall looking toward the street door covers the shelf, the hall and everyone leaving. In a larger walk-up or co-op lobby, we put a second camera at the mailboxes or package room door. We do not frame hall cameras on apartment doors.

The garden-floor door under the stoop

The door under the stoop is easy to forget and easy to work on unseen, because it sits below sidewalk level behind an iron gate in the areaway. On a house where the garden floor is its own flat, that door is someone's front door. A compact camera under the stoop, aimed at the gate and the door, closes the gap. We use a weather-rated camera there and seal the cable entry carefully.

Footage That Helps

Six Break-Ins, One Grainy Clip: Making Park Slope Door Footage Good Enough to Identify Someone

The 2019–2020 pattern is the most useful local lesson we know of for camera buyers. Police tied six burglaries in and around Park Slope to one man, most of them through damaged or forced doors, and according to Patch they had video from only the final location, described as grainy. Two problems hide in that sentence. Five homes had no usable footage at all, and the one that did produced a picture too poor to do much with. Both are fixable, and neither is fixed by buying the camera with the biggest number on the box.

Pixels on the face, not megapixels on the box

What makes a face identifiable is how many pixels land on it, which depends on the camera's resolution, the lens angle and the distance. A 4K camera with a very wide lens, mounted high and far from the door, can put fewer pixels on a face than a modest camera with a narrower lens placed close to the step. On a Park Slope stoop the distances are short, which helps, but owners often want one camera to see the whole stoop, the sidewalk and the street. We usually split that job: one camera framed tightly on the door and top step for identification, and a second, wider view for the overall scene. The written quote says which camera is for faces and which is for context, so there is no confusion later about what each one can show.

Daylight is not easy light

Most of the 2019–2020 break-ins happened around midday or in the afternoon: the Seventh Avenue house at about 3:30 p.m., the President Street apartment around noon, the Fifth Avenue apartment at 3 p.m. People assume daytime footage will be clear. On a Park Slope stoop it often is not, because the camera is looking from a shaded doorway toward bright sky or a sunlit facade across the street, and the face it needs is in shadow under a hood or brim. Cameras with good wide dynamic range, set up for the actual light at that door, solve most of it. We check every door camera at midday as well as after dark before we call the job finished.

Night pictures without washing out the face

At night the problem flips. A camera's infrared lights the nearest surface brightest, so a face close to a camera under a stoop can turn into a white blur while the steps behind it look fine. Glass makes it worse: a camera inside the vestibule shining infrared through the door glass mostly films its own reflection. We avoid that by keeping the infrared off and relying on the porch light where the light is good enough, by using cameras that do color at low light, or by moving the camera so it does not fire straight through glass. Each choice is tested in the dark on the day of installation.

Five homes with nothing

The bigger loss in that pattern was the five homes with no usable video. Part of that is simply not having cameras. Part of it, in our experience, is cameras that were present but not recording: a Wi-Fi camera that had dropped off the network, a cloud subscription that lapsed, a recorder whose drive had failed months earlier with nobody told. Every system we install sends a health alert when a camera goes offline or a drive stops recording, and we set those alerts to reach someone who will act on them, not just the person who signed the paperwork years ago.

Single- and two-family brownstones: the whole house is yours to wire

A brownstone that one family owns and lives in is the simplest Park Slope camera job, because every route through the house is available. The front cameras usually feed down to the cellar through the vestibule ceiling or a closet behind the parlor-floor door; the rear yard camera goes out through the back wall of the garden floor; and the recorder sits in the cellar or a top-floor closet near the internet service. Parlor floors often have original plaster and woodwork, so we route around closets and the old dumbwaiter or chase, and drill the masonry from the inside wherever we can.

Brownstones divided into flats: one door, many households

This is the building behind most of Park Slope's lobby package stories. Four or five households share the street door, the vestibule and the hall, deliveries land on a radiator cover or a shelf, and no one person is responsible for the door. Cameras here do two jobs. They protect the shared entrance for everyone, and, if the owner chooses, they let each flat see who is at the door without the owner replacing the old buzzer. Brownstoner's forum has an owner asking exactly that: upper-floor apartments cannot see who is at the front door, two of them share an old buzzer, and a wired video quote was too high. A shared door camera on each tenant's phone, with access limited to the door and the vestibule, is often the cheapest way to give the upstairs flats eyes on the stoop, and it can be added to later with a proper video intercom.

Routing is the hard part. The original dumbwaiter shaft, a stack of closets or an old chimney flue that is no longer in use can carry cable from the cellar to the top floor. Where none of those exist, a cable may run up the rear of the building in a painted raceway, which is usually acceptable on the back of a district house in a way it would not be on the front. Tenants are told before the hall cameras go in.

Limestone and Romanesque rows: let the stone decide

Park Slope's limestone and rough-faced brownstone rows are beautiful and unforgiving. Carved stone, decorative lintels and cast-iron details are exactly the surfaces the Landmarks guidebook says equipment must not be attached through, and they are also surfaces that cannot be repaired invisibly if a drill bit wanders. We fix front cameras into mortar joints or plain masonry only, usually under the door hood or beneath a band course, and bring the cable straight back through the wall behind the mount so no conduit shows.

Walk-ups over shops: two owners, one facade

The walk-ups along Fifth and Seventh Avenues usually have a store at street level, a side door to the apartments and often a fire escape. The store's cameras and the building's cameras end up on the same facade and sometimes the same wall, and they may belong to different people. We find out early who owns what, so the shop's cameras are aimed at the shop's door and register and the building's cameras at the residential door, stairs and roof. Cable for the residential side normally runs up the stair hall to a recorder in a locked closet or the cellar, away from the store.

Prewar elevator co-ops: the board, the super and the riser

The six- to eight-story buildings near Prospect Park West, and similar prewar apartment houses on the cross streets, behave like small institutions. There is a lobby, often a service entrance, an elevator, a laundry room and a cellar storage area, and every one of them is a camera location the board will want to discuss. Cable runs in the building's risers or the service stair, with fire-stopping restored wherever it passes through a floor. These jobs start with the managing agent and the alteration rules, and the most important document we produce is a camera schedule: each camera's location, what it sees, how long it records and which board members or staff can view it.

Fourth Avenue and new construction: work with what is there

Newer buildings along Fourth Avenue, and the 2026 buildings such as Paseo on Fifth and the 144-apartment building on the former Grand Prospect Hall site, usually arrive with an entry panel, a package room and sometimes a garage already wired by the developer. The camera work there is mostly about filling gaps the original system left, such as the garage ramp, the bike room, the roof deck or the loading door, and about getting the owner or board control of the recorder rather than a vendor login.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Park Slope

License. AESS installs under NYS license #12000287431 and carries general liability insurance. We do not do electrical work; where a camera needs a new outlet, a licensed electrician does that part.

Landmarks. A storefront inside the Park Slope Historic District or its extensions falls under the same Landmarks Preservation Commission rules as the rest of the building. Cameras on the front need a permit, and cut sheets for each camera go with the application. Interior cameras do not.

Privacy. We install shop cameras with audio switched off unless the owner asks otherwise, and cameras never go in restrooms or changing areas.

Cameras Inside the Park Slope Historic District: Working to the Commission's Rules

Park Slope's historic district is one of the city's largest. The original 1973 designation covered 1,948 buildings, the 2012 extension along the southwest edge added 613, and Extension II in 2016 added roughly 300 more, counted at 292 by New York YIMBY and 296 in Village Preservation's designation list. If your house is in any of them, cameras on the outside need a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. That sounds like a barrier, and owners sometimes give up on front cameras because of it. In practice, the Commission's staff can approve cameras that meet a published set of criteria, and most well-planned Park Slope fronts can meet them.

What the guidebook asks for

The criteria are in chapter 11 of the Commission's permit guidebook. Cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance." Equipment should not go on or in front of a primary facade unless there is no other option, and when it must, it has to sit somewhere discreet, the guidebook's examples being beneath a band course or behind plantings. For small residential and commercial buildings, which covers most Park Slope row houses, the guidebook allows no more than two cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage; larger residential, commercial and institutional buildings may have up to three per 25 feet. Equipment must be fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork or the concrete of the areaway or sidewalk, and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." Conduit should be concealed by going through the wall behind the camera; where that is impossible, staff may approve roughly ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match.

How that plays out on a Park Slope row house

A row house with 25 feet of frontage or less gets the two-camera allowance for the front, and the tape measure, not a guess, decides that on the walk-through. In practice two is enough when the two cameras are chosen well: one face camera at the parlor-floor door and one at the garden door or the areaway gate. The overall street view that owners often ask for is usually better done from inside, looking out through a parlor window, which is not a facade installation at all. The cameras that do the most security work on these houses, at the rear yard, fire escape and roof, are on walls the public rarely sees, and we plan them in the same application so the whole house is approved at once.

Paperwork we prepare

The application needs manufacturer cut sheets for each camera, photographs of the facade and a marked-up photo showing where each camera and its cable go. We prepare the camera side of that package, including the cut sheets and a mounting detail showing the mortar-joint fixing and the concealed cable path, and the owner or their architect files it.

The back of the house is not exempt

Owners are sometimes told that only the front matters. The Commission's chapter on yards says it requires a permit for most work in yards and areaways, and rear-yard work is not on the list of exemptions, so we treat rear cameras on a district house as part of the application rather than assuming they can go up unseen. The practical difference is that a camera on a plain brick rear wall, with its cable through the wall, is the easiest kind of installation for staff to approve.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Starting a Park Slope Walk-Through at the Latch, Not the Sidewalk

This describes how we approach buildings here in general, not one particular job. On a Park Slope walk-through we start at the street door with the owner and ask a simple question: if someone forced this door tonight, where would they stand and which way would they face? Then we close the door and look at the latch from both sides. We check whether the strike is on the leaf people actually use, whether there is glass in the door, and how the vestibule light compares with the light on the stoop at that hour. Then we do the same at the garden door and in the hall.

Then we go up. On a brownstone in flats we ask to see the roof hatch, because it is the one door in the house the residents rarely look at, and on a walk-up we look at the fire escape from the yard. In the cellar we look for the old dumbwaiter, a disused flue or a stack of closets, because that route decides whether the job takes one day or two. Last, we stand on the sidewalk across the street and look at the front the way a Landmarks reviewer would. Only then do we write down how many cameras the house needs, and it is usually fewer at the front than the owner expected and more at the back and top.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Park Slope: Questions Answered

Shared doors and lobbies

Our landlord lives out of state and the brownstone has no super. Who should be the administrator on the vestibule camera so someone can actually pull a clip on a Sunday?

The owner should hold the master administrator account, because the system is the owner's property and the owner answers for it. But a recorder that only one person far away can export from is a recorder that will not help when a package goes missing on a weekend. We set up a second account with export rights, limited to the street door, vestibule and hall cameras, for a person the owner names, often a long-term tenant or a local managing agent, and we write down who has which account.

A tenant upstairs wants to stick a battery doorbell camera on our shared street door. Should the owner allow it, or put up one camera for every flat?

One camera for the whole building is almost always the better answer. A tenant's doorbell on a shared door records every other household's comings and goings into one tenant's private account, which tends to cause arguments, and on a landmarked front it is also equipment on the facade that needs a permit. A single door camera owned by the building, with a viewing account for each flat limited to the door and vestibule, gives every household the same picture, keeps the footage in one place and puts one device on the front instead of several.

After the 2019 lobby theft, a resident said management had asked tenants to keep track of who came in and out. Can a lobby camera do that job without anyone sitting at a screen?

Yes, and it should, because asking tenants to watch strangers is uncomfortable and unreliable. A lobby camera set to record continuously and to mark every time someone passes through the door gives management a timeline it can scroll in minutes: who came in after the courier, who left with a box. We do not install face recognition or any system that builds a list of named people; the camera records the hall, and people look at the footage only when something has happened.

When faces are covered

The four men in the December 2025 Seventh Avenue news stand robbery wore masks. What can a store's cameras still record that helps when faces are covered?

A great deal. A well-placed door camera records height against the door frame, build, clothing, shoes, gloves, bags and the way each person moves, and a camera with a view of the sidewalk records which way they went and whether a car or scooter was waiting. Register cameras record what was touched, which matters for fingerprints, and exactly how much was taken. A mask does not make the cameras pointless; it makes the clarity of everything below the face more important, which is another reason we avoid a single wide camera that sees the whole store at low detail.

Above the parlor floor

In 2016 a would-be burglar on Eighth Avenue near Carroll Street reached a third-floor window from an outside ledge. Is it worth putting cameras on the upper floors of a row house?

On some houses, yes. Where a ledge, a cornice, a bay roof or a neighbor's lower roof gives someone a way to an upper window, a camera high on the rear wall or under the roof edge, looking along the windows, records the approach. Where nothing leads to the upper floors, the money is better spent at the doors, the yard and the roof hatch. We decide that on the walk-through by looking at what a person could actually climb.

The roofs along our row connect, and our roof hatch only has a hook latch. Can a camera watch the hatch from inside?

Yes, and it is one of the cheapest cameras on a Park Slope house. A small camera inside the bulkhead or on the top-floor ceiling, aimed at the underside of the hatch and the ladder, records anyone coming down and can alert you the moment the hatch opens at an hour it should not. Because it is inside, it needs no Landmarks permit and no weatherproof housing. We also tell owners plainly that the hook latch is the real problem; the camera records what happens, and a proper lock on the hatch is what stops it.

Our vestibule fills with strollers during the school run, and the camera sends alerts all morning. Can it ignore that and still warn us about a stranger?

Yes. Community District 6 has a large share of children, and on many blocks the morning door traffic is parents, strollers and scooters for an hour or more. Rather than switching alerts off, we set the door camera to record everything but to send alerts only during the hours the house should be quiet, such as late evening to early morning and the middle of a weekday when everyone is out. You still have every minute of footage if a stroller goes missing.

Avenue shops

Our Fifth Avenue restaurant takes deliveries through a steel cellar hatch in the sidewalk. Can a camera cover the hatch?

Yes. Sidewalk cellar doors are a common weak point on avenue storefronts because they open straight into the basement, often next to the stock. A camera on the inside of the cellar, aimed up the stairs at the underside of the hatch, records anyone who comes down, and a second, small camera at the front of the store can watch the hatch from above at sidewalk level. The inside camera is usually the more useful one, because it gets a face lit by the open hatch and it cannot be reached from the street. We run the cellar camera's cable along the basement ceiling to the recorder, clear of the delivery path.

Who Sees the Footage

Tenants, Landlords, Detectives and the House Next Door: Who Gets to See a Park Slope Camera

A camera on a Park Slope building rarely records only its owner. It records tenants, delivery workers, neighbors on the stoop next door and, from the back, parts of other people's yards. Deciding who may see the footage is as much a part of the plan as deciding where the cameras go.

Tenants and landlords

In a rental building, the cameras in the shared spaces normally belong to the owner, and so does the footage. Brick Underground put the legal position bluntly in a 2023 column about a package thief: "Unfortunately, your landlord isn't obligated to share surveillance footage with you," outside a police request or a court order. The same column suggested a tenants' association could press the owner for a better lock or a package locker. For owners, the practical lesson is that a clear, written policy on who can request a clip, and how quickly it is pulled, prevents most disputes. We can set up a limited account that lets a named person export door footage without seeing anything else.

Detectives from the 78th Precinct

After a burglary or a robbery nearby, detectives usually ask in person or by phone for footage from a time window. You decide whether to share it, unless a court orders it. If you do share, export just the window they ask for, from the camera they need, at full quality, and keep your own copy. Do not hand over the recorder itself unless there is no other way, because it stops recording your building while it is gone.

Co-op and condo boards

In a co-op or condo, the lobby, halls, mail room, laundry and service doors are common property, and the board decides who sees what. Often the managing agent and one or two officers can export, and the super sees live views. We set that up as separate accounts and write it into the camera schedule the board approves, so there is a record if anyone asks later who could see what.

Neighbors and their yards

Park Slope's rear yards are long and narrow and sit side by side, so a camera on the back of a row house can see into several gardens unless it is aimed with care. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets an owner or tenant sue over a camera placed on an adjoining property to record recreational activities in their backyard without written consent, where it was put there to harass, annoy or alarm. Our cameras are on your own house, and we aim them at your own yard, windows and doors, and use privacy masks to black out a neighbor's garden or windows that fall in the frame.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Park Slope

These claims show up in AI-written answers and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each has a grain of truth, and each misses something about Park Slope.

"A video doorbell covers the front of the house."

On a single-family house with one door, a doorbell can be a reasonable start. Most Park Slope row houses have two front entrances, the parlor-floor door up the stoop and the garden door beneath it, and many are divided into flats that share the upper door. A doorbell sees one of those doors from one height and records into one person's account.

The local thefts we have described began at the lock and ended in the lobby. A doorbell beside the door frame sees the person ringing; it often does not see someone working the latch from a step below, and it never sees the hall where the packages were.

For a building with shared entrances, the front of the house means the top step, the lock side, the vestibule, the garden door and the hall. That is usually three or four cameras, and only one of them looks anything like a doorbell.

"Any 4K camera will capture a clear face."

Resolution matters, but it is not the deciding factor. A 4K camera with a very wide lens, mounted high on a facade to see the whole stoop and the street, can put less detail on a face than a 1080p camera with a narrow lens a few feet from the door.

Park Slope adds lighting problems that resolution does not fix: a shaded doorway looking toward bright sky at midday, a dim vestibule entered from a sunny stoop, and infrared bouncing off door glass at night. The grainy clip in the 2019–2020 burglary pattern is the kind of result those conditions produce.

What decides a usable face is the lens, the distance, the angle, the dynamic range and a test at the actual times of day that matter. We check door cameras at midday and after dark before we finish, and we keep the main stream recording so the detail is there when it is exported.

"You can't put cameras on a landmarked building."

You can. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's own guidebook sets out criteria under which its staff can approve security cameras, including spacing, a limit of two per 25 feet of frontage on small buildings, fixing at mortar joints or plain masonry and concealed conduit.

The opposite mistake is just as common: assuming that because a camera is small, nobody will mind. Equipment on the outside of a building in the historic district or its extensions needs a permit, rear yards included, and a camera drilled through a carved surround or cast iron is the kind of work that leads to trouble.

In practice, most Park Slope fronts can carry the two cameras they need under staff-level review, and the rear and interior cameras are easier still.

"Wireless cameras are just as good as wired, and much easier."

Easier to put up, yes. Brownstone walls are thick masonry, and Wi-Fi from a router on one floor often struggles to reach a camera on the stoop, under the stoop or on the rear wall. Battery cameras also need recharging, which on a busy shared door means often.

The bigger point is what happens when the connection drops. A wireless camera that has fallen off the network records nothing, and nobody notices until something happens and the owner opens the app to find a gap where the evening should be.

A cable carries power and picture together, needs no outlet at the camera and records to a recorder in the house whether or not the internet is working. On a Park Slope building the route for that cable usually already exists in a cellar, a shaft or a stack of closets.

"Crime is rising, so every house needs cameras now."

Not by the latest 78th Precinct figures. CompStat for the week of November 2–8, 2026 shows burglary up 2.9% for the year to date, 107 against 104, but down 16.4% over two years and 84.2% over 33 years, and grand larceny down 11.3% for the year.

The more honest reason to install cameras here is not that crime is surging but that the incidents that do happen cluster at shared doors, lobbies and registers, and that footage often decides whether anyone is identified. The two December 2020 lobby thefts were caught on video; most of the six homes in the 2019–2020 pattern were not.

So we plan cameras around the doors and rooms where something is most likely to happen, and we say so when a requested camera would not add much.

"Camera installation costs about the same everywhere."

National guides give national averages, and those averages assume houses where cable runs through an open attic or drywall. A Park Slope brownstone has masonry walls, plaster finishes, a cellar, a stoop and often a landmarked front, and the cable route is usually the biggest part of the labor.

That cuts both ways: a one-family brownstone with a usable dumbwaiter shaft can be quick to wire, while a house in flats with no chase and a front that needs permit paperwork takes longer.

Our Brooklyn packages start at $850 for four cameras installed, with the recorder and two weeks of recording included, and the written quote for your building shows exactly what the route, the walls and any Landmarks work add before anything is ordered.

"If you have cameras, police will just pull the footage when they need it."

There is no automatic link from a home recorder to the precinct. Detectives ask owners for footage, and owners decide whether to share it unless a court orders otherwise. That means the quality and the export are the owner's job.

The common failures are practical: a recorder clock that is off by an hour, so the time on the clip does not match the incident; an export made from a phone's low-quality stream; or an owner who cannot remember the password when the detective is at the door.

We set the clock to a time server, keep full-quality recording on the door cameras and have every owner practice an export at handover. That is the step that turns a camera into evidence.

FAQ

Park Slope Security Camera FAQ

How hard is it to get power to a new camera on the second floor of a brownstone front?
+

Usually not hard at all, because most of the cameras we install take their power through the same cable that carries the picture, from a switch or recorder inside the house. That means no new outlet on the facade and no electrician for the camera itself. The real work is the cable route from the camera to the cellar or a closet, which we plan through the inside of the house so the front stays clean. A floodlight camera that needs household power is different; that part needs a licensed electrician, and on a landmarked front the fixture also needs a permit.

A locksmith told us a doorbell camera that goes on with two screws is enough for a Park Slope front door. Is it?
+

It is a reasonable start for one door in a one-family house, and it is better than nothing. It is rarely enough for a brownstone in flats or a walk-up, because it watches one door from one height, records into one person's account, and usually misses the lock side, the vestibule and the hall where packages go missing. On a landmarked front it is also equipment that needs a permit. We often keep an owner's doorbell for answering visitors and add a proper door camera and a vestibule camera for recording.

Why does wiring cameras in a brownstone split into flats often take longer than in a house with one owner?
+

Because the easy routes run through other people's homes. In a one-family brownstone we can use any closet, shaft or ceiling, but in a house of flats every apartment is occupied, so the cable has to stay in the cellar, the common hall, an old dumbwaiter shaft or a raceway on the rear wall, and we need access dates agreed with tenants. The cameras cost the same; the time on the cable route is what changes, and the written quote shows it as its own line.

Our block's street trees fill in every spring. Will they block a front camera that looked fine in winter?
+

They can, mostly for cameras aimed out at the sidewalk or the street. Leaves block the view and moving branches set off motion alerts. That is one more reason we frame front cameras tightly on the door and the top step, which trees rarely cover, rather than on the street. If a wider street view matters to you, we check where the canopy will be in summer during the walk-through and set motion zones that ignore the tree.

Can the camera work and a fob reader on the inner vestibule door be done in the same visit?
+

Yes, and it often saves time, because both use low-voltage cable through the same route from the vestibule to the cellar. Many Park Slope owners pair a vestibule camera with a reader on the inner door, so that even if the outer door is forced, the hall and the packages stay behind a second locked door. We quote the two as separate lines so you can see what each costs, and the camera can record every time the inner door is opened.

Can someone in a top-floor flat speak to a delivery driver through the stoop camera?
+
