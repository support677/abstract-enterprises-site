The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Bay Properties Need Access Control

Pelham Bay sits in the northeast Bronx between the Hutchinson River Parkway and the New England Thruway, with Pelham Parkway to the north and the Bruckner Expressway to the south, according to the neighborhood’s Wikipedia entry. It is part of Community District 10 and the 10461 ZIP code, and it is patrolled by the 45th Precinct. The Department of City Planning drew its own study area slightly differently, from Pelham Parkway South down to Middletown Road, and other neighborhood guides draw it differently again; on this page Pelham Bay means the residential blocks around Westchester, Buhre, Crosby and Middletown Road, with the park and Country Club named separately.

City Planning’s rezoning study explains why the place looks the way it does. The arrival of the IRT in the early twentieth century, the study says, spurred development around a new rail terminal; the Pelham Bay Park station, still the end of the 6, opened on December 20, 1920. What was built then and afterwards is mostly houses. In the 2005 study, detached homes made up about 72 percent of the housing stock and semi-detached homes another 17 percent, with attached houses and apartment buildings a small minority along the wider avenues. The Council adopted the rezoning on March 22, 2006, largely to stop sound detached houses from being replaced by rows of attached housing.

Houses like these change hands slowly. Across Community District 10, the NYU Furman Center puts the homeownership rate at 44.7 percent in 2024, against 32.7 percent citywide, and the share of residents aged 65 and over at 19.2 percent; it was 23.9 percent in 2019. More than a third of households, 37.8 percent, are one person living alone. Those district figures include Co-op City and Throgs Neck as well as Pelham Bay, so they describe the area rather than these exact blocks, but they match what we see at the door: parents who bought decades ago, still in the house, often with a tenant or an adult child on the other floor.

That is the access problem we are asked to solve here most often. It is rarely a stranger kicking in a door. It is a parent who can no longer get downstairs quickly to answer the bell, a son in New Jersey who wants to know the aide arrived, a daughter who holds the only spare key and lives forty minutes away, a door that has grown too heavy to pull open, a code that three former aides still know. A door system cannot solve all of that, but the right one makes it easier: a call that reaches the family as well as the parent, a credential an older hand can use, a log the family can check without hovering, and codes that end when a job ends.

There is also a harder reason families ask. In early 2011 two men posing as Con Edison workers talked their way into the homes of older Bronx residents, asking to read a water meter or fix a leak, and robbed them; CBS New York reported six such cases since New Year’s Eve with victims between 69 and 93, and WABC named Pelham Bay among the neighborhoods hit. One Pelham Bay resident told WABC he chased two such men off his doorstep because he saw no Con Ed truck. The lesson the utility gave then still holds: check who is at the door before opening it, and call to confirm an employee’s identity. A door station that lets a parent see and speak to a caller, and lets a relative join that call from elsewhere, puts that advice into practice.

Repairs

Parent Locked Out or a Keypad Dead in Pelham Bay?

If a parent is locked out, a keypad has gone dark, or an electric strike will not release and someone is waiting on the stoop, call the Pelham Bay line at (646) 490-0629 and say so first. When someone in the house cannot easily get to the door, or cannot be left alone without a working lock, we treat the visit as urgent and give the family a time window by phone.

Most failures we find in houses are small. Batteries in a keypad deadbolt run down without anyone noticing the warning chirp; a door has dropped on its hinges so the bolt drags and the motor gives up; a storm door was closed over a keypad and the wiring pinched; an app stopped working because the account was on a phone that was replaced. We fix what we can on the first visit, and if a part has to be ordered we leave the door lockable by key or by hand before we go.

We work on systems other installers put in, and on locks the family bought online. The first thing we do is find out who holds the administrator account. In a family house it is often a grandchild who set it up years ago, or nobody at all, and getting that settled is part of the repair.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Pelham Bay’s Architecture

Pelham Bay’s houses were mostly built in the decades after the 6 train reached the terminal in 1920, and City Planning’s study describes large one- and two-family detached homes on the inner blocks and apartment buildings along the avenues. The building types below are the ones we plan for. We describe them in general; each house is surveyed before anything is chosen.

Detached two-family houses

The most common case. One household on each floor, often a separate stair or a shared front hall, a basement door and sometimes a driveway. The decision is which doors belong to which household and where the doorbell for each one rings.

Semi-detached pairs

Two houses sharing one wall, about one home in six in City Planning’s count. Front doors can sit a few feet from the neighbor’s, so camera views and keypad placement need care, and cable can only run on your own side of the party wall.

Larger detached houses on the inner blocks

City Planning pointed to the large detached homes on Mulford Avenue as the character it wanted to protect. Bigger houses mean longer cable runs from basement to door, and often a side or back door used every day.

Small apartment buildings on the avenues

Walk-ups and elevator buildings along Westchester, Crosby and Buhre, in the R6 and R7-1 corridors. A lobby door, a vestibule door, a cellar door and sometimes a rear yard gate, each with its own rules for residents and staff.

Mixed-use buildings under the el

Shops at street level along Westchester Avenue with apartments above. The apartment door sits between storefronts, the train runs overhead, and the shop and the residents need separate access on one wall.

Senior and supportive housing

Buildings where most residents are older. Middletown Plaza on Middletown Road is the best known; residents there complained in 2016 about faulty automatic doors and keys. Lobby doors, power operators and credentials have to suit residents with walkers and wheelchairs.

How we adapt the hardware to an older house

  • Fix the door first. A door that sticks, sags or needs a shoulder to close will defeat any electric lock. Hinges, closers and strike plates are adjusted before anything electronic goes on.
  • Measure the opening force. If an older owner has to pull hard to open the door, the closer is turned down or replaced, and the lock is chosen to latch reliably at the lighter setting.
  • Keep a key. Every keypad or reader we fit to a house door keeps a mechanical key override, so a dead battery never locks anyone out.
  • Put controls where the person sits. Indoor screens, release buttons and chimes go where the parent spends the day, not only beside the door.
  • Keep cable inside the house. Runs go through the basement ceiling and up inside walls or closets where possible, so nothing outside can be cut or pulled.
  • Write it down. Each door, its lock, its battery date and who can open it goes on one page that stays in the house.

The Two-Family Household

Two Generations, One Two-Family House

The Pelham Bay two-family usually works like this: one floor belongs to the people who bought the house, the other to a tenant or to their grown children, and the two households share a front walk, sometimes a hallway, often a basement. Over twenty or thirty years the arrangement shifts. The tenant leaves and a daughter moves in; the parents move down to avoid the stairs; a grandchild takes the top floor; one parent dies and the other lives alone below a family that works long hours. The doors stay the same while everything about who uses them changes.

An entry system for that house has to follow the people, not the floor plan. We start by naming each household and each person in it, then decide which doors each person needs and who should hear when they use them. A parent on the ground floor might have a keypad set to a short, familiar code, an indoor screen beside the armchair and a call that also rings two children; the family upstairs might have their own front-door code, their own doorbell and no view of the parent’s visitors at all, because the parent asked for that. Each choice is written down, so when the household changes again the system is changed on purpose, not patched.

Three rules keep the arrangement fair. First, each household controls its own doorbell and its own visitors; nobody downstairs should be buzzing in strangers for the people upstairs. Second, the person who lives behind a door decides who can see its camera and its log, unless they have asked someone else to decide for them. Third, nothing we install should ever stop anyone getting out: every door opens from inside with an ordinary handle, whatever the power or the batteries are doing.

Families sometimes ask whether a two-family needs the same paperwork as an apartment building. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law covers smart-access systems in multiple dwellings, which in city housing law generally means three or more households, and the Housing Maintenance Code’s lock and intercom rules are written for the same class of building. A one- or two-family house is usually outside both, but we still treat a tenant’s entry data as theirs: a tenant’s code is not shared, their entries are not shown to the landlord household unless they agree, and their credential is deleted the day they leave. If a basement or attic apartment would make the house a third unit, that is a building question for an architect before it is an access question for us.

Aging in Place

Doors for Older Hands, Eyes and Ears

Most hardware is designed for someone standing at the door with a phone in one hand. Many of the people we install for in Pelham Bay are sitting down when the bell goes, cannot read small print on a screen, or find it hard to turn a key in a stiff cylinder. The equipment is the same; how it is chosen and set up is not.

For hands, the federal accessibility guide on doors is a useful yardstick even in a private house: hardware should work with one hand, without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist, and with no more than five pounds of force. That points away from small round knobs and toward levers, push-button keypads with raised keys, and readers that open with a tap of a fob on a key ring. It also means the door itself must be light to open. We check the closer before we add a lock, and when a closer has been set to slam the door shut, we adjust or replace it and choose a strike that holds at the lighter setting.

For eyes, we use keypads with backlit, high-contrast keys and indoor screens large enough to recognize a face at arm’s length, mounted at seated height where the person spends the day. For ears, a door call can be made louder, repeated on a second chime in the kitchen or bedroom, or shown as a flashing light; and where the parent wears hearing aids, a phone call to the parent’s own mobile can be easier to hear than a speaker on the wall.

For memory, the simplest credential usually wins. A short code chosen by the person who has to remember it, or a single fob on the key ring they already carry, beats an app that needs updates. We also set the door to relock itself after a short delay, so a forgotten turn of the key is not a problem, and we send a quiet notice to the family if the front door has been left unlocked for longer than they choose.

Transitions

When the Household Changes: Aides, Moves and the Family Home

Access in a family house changes at predictable moments: when an aide or a cleaner starts or stops, when a parent goes into hospital or moves in with a child, when the house is put up for sale, and when it is settled after a death. Each of those moments has a door task attached, and a system that makes the task easy is worth more than one with more features.

When an aide starts, they get their own code, valid on their working days and hours, ending on the date the agency gives. When they stop, their code is deleted that day, not when someone remembers. When a parent is away for a few weeks, codes that are only needed when they are home are paused and the family can see any entry. When the house is to be sold, the system is either left for the buyer with administrator access transferred at closing, or removed and the door made good; we explain the trade-offs below.

Empty houses deserve particular care. In December 2005 an off-duty police officer who rented a brick two-family in Pelham Bay heard glass breaking and found two men breaking into his neighbor’s vacant house; he was shot and killed, and the corner of Westchester Avenue and Arnow Place was renamed Detective Daniel Enchautegui Way the following August. A door system cannot prevent a crime like that, and we do not suggest it can. What it can do for a house that is temporarily empty is tell the family at once when a door opens, keep a record of who used a code, and make sure that the people who still have legitimate reasons to go in are the only ones whose codes work.

Senior Housing

Senior Buildings: What Middletown Plaza Shows

Middletown Plaza, at 3033 Middletown Road, is a 15-story NYCHA building for older residents, designed by Paul Rudolph and completed in 1973, according to the Paul Rudolph Institute; NYCHA says it was built between 1970 and 1973. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in August 2024. The Bronx Times counted 179 apartments; the Rudolph Institute lists 177 apartments and two elevators. We are not involved in its renovation and mention it only because its public record is a clear lesson for any building whose residents are mostly older.

In 2016, residents complained publicly about faulty automatic doors, bathrooms, keys and the lobby ceiling, as the Bronx Times later summarized; in 2013 the Daily News reported tenants demanding action on delayed security measures, including cameras. NYCHA’s 2017 needs assessment put the building’s repairs at more than $21 million. In September 2024 Middletown Plaza converted to Project-Based Section 8 under NYCHA’s PACT program, with Wavecrest as property manager and renovations expected to run until early 2027, while NYCHA keeps ownership of the land and buildings.

The lesson is not about one building. For residents who walk with a cane or a walker, the automatic door and the lock are one machine: if the operator swings the door before the strike has released, or the strike relocks while a slow walker is still in the opening, the door becomes an obstacle. In buildings like that we time the release, the operator and the relock together, add a second push plate at seated height inside, and make key and fob replacement a short, written process at the office rather than a wait.

  • Release before swing: the lock releases first, the operator opens the door second, and the door relocks only after it has fully closed
  • Longer hold-open for residents who need it: some systems let selected fobs hold the door open longer than standard ones
  • Credentials that are hard to lose: fobs on a key ring, cards in a lanyard holder or wristband tags, with a spare policy agreed in writing
  • Panel call by name or number, with large type, and a directory that can show apartment numbers instead of names
  • A plain record of which fob belongs to which apartment, so a lost or unreturned fob is switched off the same day

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Pelham Bay

Pelham Bay’s shops line Westchester Avenue under the el, Buhre Avenue and Middletown Road near their stations, and Crosby Avenue, where Middletown Road crosses it at one of the neighborhood’s main corners. In January 2014, the Pelham Bay Merchants Association gave Safe Haven decals to 45 businesses along Crosby Avenue and Westchester Square, so that children who felt unsafe could step inside until a guardian or the police came. Many of those shops are family businesses with a few long-serving staff, and their door problems are practical ones.

Westchester Avenue under the 6

Storefronts and apartment doors share walls beneath the elevated tracks between Middletown Road, Buhre Avenue and the Pelham Bay Park terminal. A shop’s front door, the apartment door beside it and the shop’s cellar hatch often need three different sets of people.

Crosby Avenue

The neighborhood’s other main shopping street, and the street where the Safe Haven decals went up. Family-run shops here usually need a back door code for each worker and a front door that the owner can open remotely for a delivery.

Buhre Avenue

Shops near the Buhre Avenue station with apartments above. In the 2006 rezoning City Planning changed the commercial overlays along Westchester and Buhre and cut several of them from 150 to 100 feet deep, to keep shops from spreading into the residential mid-blocks.

Middletown Road

Shops, the Pelham Bay branch library at number 3060 and Middletown Plaza share this corridor. Any office or shop on this stretch that serves older customers needs a door that stays open long enough for someone who walks slowly, and a reader or bell they can reach.

Offices above the shops

Second-floor professional offices reached by a street door between storefronts. Clients come by appointment; the door needs a call button that reaches the office and a way to buzz people in without someone walking downstairs.

Elevation of a generic mixed-use building beside the elevated line, drawn by AESS; not any building on Westchester Avenue.

Rules that shape business doors here

  • Free exit: the city Building Code requires exit doors in businesses to open from inside without a key or special knowledge, so readers go on the outside and ordinary handles or push bars stay on the inside.
  • Landlord consent: most shop leases require written permission for alterations to doors and frames; we provide a description and drawing for the landlord.
  • Recording: New York’s eavesdropping law makes it a crime to record a conversation you are not part of without the consent of at least one participant, so we leave audio recording off on shop cameras unless the owner has taken advice.
  • Employees’ data: a code per employee gives a useful record of who opened up; we keep that record to the owner and the employee concerned, and delete a former worker’s code on their last day.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Pelham Bay: Questions Answered

These are the questions Pelham Bay families and owners ask us about doors in houses where a parent is getting older, in two-families shared by generations, in senior buildings and in family shops. Each answer is how we would handle it; where a law or a code is involved, we say which and suggest when to check with a professional.

Two Households and a Parent on the Ground Floor

Our parents are moving down from the top-floor apartment of our two-family, and we are moving up. What should change on the doors when the floors swap?

Treat it as two new households, not a rearrangement. Every code, fob and phone credential tied to a floor should be reissued to the people who now live there, and every doorbell button, indoor screen and app account should be moved so a caller for your parents rings your parents. The most common mistake we see after a swap is a doorbell that still rings the old floor for months.

Hardware usually stays where it is, but the downstairs door now needs to suit your parents: a lighter closer, a keypad they find easy, an indoor screen where they sit. If the upstairs apartment gets its own door at the top of a shared stair, that door gets your family’s credential and nothing of your parents’. Budget a single visit to re-enroll everyone and to change the record kept in the house.

My mother opens the door to anyone who rings. Can calls from her front door reach my brother and me at the same moment, so one of us can check first?

Yes. Most video door stations can ring an indoor screen and several phones at once, or ring the phones first and the indoor screen a few seconds later. The family can then see and speak to the caller, tell them to wait, and either call your mother or unlock the door for a known visitor.

Two cautions. Agree with your mother how it will work, because a system that answers for her without her knowing can feel like being managed; and decide what happens when neither of you answers, since the call will then reach her anyway. For callers who claim to be from a utility, the advice Con Edison gave after the 2011 impostor robberies still applies: check the person’s photo identification on the screen and call the company to confirm it before anyone opens the door.

Dad lives alone on the ground floor. Can the system tell us if his door has not opened by midday, without putting a camera inside the house?

Yes. A contact on the front door, or the lock itself, can report each time the door opens or locks. The system can then send a message to the people you choose if there has been no activity by a set time, for example noon. It sees nothing inside the house; it only knows the door has not been used.

It is a prompt to call, not a medical alarm. Some people stay in all day, so set the time with your father and choose a fallback: the tenant upstairs, a neighbor, or a relative who can stop by. If he has a medical alert pendant, keep it; the door message is a second line of reassurance, not a replacement.

The closer on our front door slams it so hard that Mom can barely pull it open. Can it be eased without the electric lock failing to latch?

Usually, yes. A closer that has been turned up to make sure the door latches against a sticky frame or a draft is often hiding a door problem. We adjust the hinges and the strike so the door latches with little force, then set the closer to a gentle speed with a final latching push in the last few inches.

If an electric strike is fitted, we choose one that holds a door that closes softly and does not need a slam to engage. For reference, the federal accessibility guide sets five pounds as the maximum force for operating door hardware in public buildings; we use the same idea at home, measured with a simple gauge, so the person who lives there can open the door with one hand.

Family, Consent and Paperwork

My sister wants to see Mom’s door camera from her house in Westchester. Should Mom agree first, and what should the family be able to see?

Yes, ask her first, and let her decide who sees what. A door camera shows who comes to see her, when she goes out and when she comes home, which is personal information about her life. Some parents are glad for a child to watch; others want help answering the door but not a record of every visit.

Most systems can separate these: one relative can answer live calls but not view recordings; another can receive only the “door left unlocked” and battery messages. We set each relative up with their own login, never a shared one, so access can be removed for one person without affecting the others. If your mother is no longer able to decide, follow whoever is legally authorized to decide for her.

Mom owns the house, but I am arranging the work under a power of attorney. Who has to sign the agreement, and what do you need from us?

The agreement should be signed by the owner, or by the person legally authorized to act for the owner in property matters. If you sign as her agent under a power of attorney, we will ask to see the document, note it on the agreement, and keep a copy with the job file. If the house has more than one owner, we ask for the co-owners’ agreement too.

Separately from who signs, we will explain the system to your mother in person and leave her a copy of the paper record. Even when someone else is paying and arranging, the person who lives behind the door should know who can open it.

Dad will probably move in with us next year and the house will be sold. Is it worth installing anything now?

Think in two parts. Things that make his life easier this year (a lighter door, an easier lock, a door station that calls you) are worth doing whatever happens to the house, and battery keypad locks and wireless door stations can be taken off and reused, with the old lock put back. Hard-wired systems with cable in the walls are better left for the next owner, or removed with the holes patched.

If you leave a system for a buyer, plan the handover: administrator accounts transferred at closing, all family codes deleted, the paper record and manuals left in a kitchen drawer. A smart lock still registered to your phone after the sale is the most common problem new owners call us about.

Senior Buildings

A resident of our senior building died last week. How quickly should her fob be switched off, and who should tell the office?

The fob should be switched off as soon as the office knows, ideally the same day, and the family should be told it has been done so they are not surprised if they find it in her bag. Relatives who still need to clear the apartment can be given a temporary fob or code that expires on the date agreed with management.

The building should not rely on families to call. A simple rule works: whoever learns of a death or a move to a nursing home (a neighbor, a social worker, the super) tells the office, and the office checks the fob list that day. We set the system so that switching one fob off takes a minute and is recorded with the date and the name of the staff member.

Several residents of our building keep their keys on a coil around the wrist. Would a wristband credential be a good idea for them?

It can be. Silicone wristband tags use the same technology as fobs and cards, are hard to drop, and do not need to be found in a bag at the door. They suit residents who use a walker, since both hands stay on the handles until the last moment, and they can be held up to a reader at seated height for wheelchair users.

Two cautions: choose tags that work with the readers you already have, and keep a written record of which tag belongs to which apartment, because wristbands all look alike. Offer them as a choice, not a rule; some residents prefer a fob on the key ring they have carried for decades.

Our entry panel shows every resident’s name. Some older residents do not want their names on display. What are the options?

Most modern panels can show apartment numbers only, show a name only after a visitor types part of it, or show a short code instead of a surname. A resident can also ask to be left off the directory and give callers their apartment number directly.

The trade-off is for visitors such as home-care workers who come for the first time, who may not know the apartment number. Give the agency the number and the building’s calling instructions in writing, and the panel can stay private.

Keys, Neighbors and Running Costs

Mom keeps a spare key with a neighbor and another under a flowerpot. What replaces those once there is a keypad?

A code. The neighbor gets a personal code that works at any time, or only when your mother is away, and every use is recorded. The flowerpot key should simply go; once a door has a keypad there is no reason for a hidden key, and anyone who has watched the house knows where flowerpot keys are.

Keep one mechanical key per household for emergencies, held by a relative who does not live there. And if the lock has not been changed since the house was bought, have the cylinder rekeyed when the keypad goes on, because nobody knows how many copies of the old key are out there.

The house next door has been empty for months since the owner went into care. Should anything change on our side?

Your own doors and windows on that side deserve a look, because an empty house next door gives anyone a quiet place to stand. Make sure your side and basement doors close and latch properly, that a light covers the path between the houses, and that any camera is aimed at your property, not into the neighbor’s yard or windows.

You cannot put anything on the neighbor’s house, but if you know the family, offer to tell them if something looks wrong. If a vacant house is left open, report it through 311. In December 2005 the burglary that led to an off-duty officer’s death on Arnow Place began at a vacant house next door, a reminder that neighbors are often the first to notice.

How much will a door system add to our electricity bill?

Very little. A keypad deadbolt runs on batteries and uses no household power at all. A door station, a strike and a small controller with its battery backup usually draw a few watts continuously, similar to a phone charger or a router that is always on, which is a small amount over a year at typical residential rates.

The cost that matters more is batteries. Keypad locks typically need new batteries every several months to a year, depending on how often the door is used, and we put the date on the paper record. Rechargeable systems avoid that but need someone to remember to charge them; for a parent living alone, plain batteries changed on a schedule are usually safer.

Our family has run the same shop on Crosby Avenue for decades, and nobody knows how many copies of the back-door key are out there. Where do we start?

Start with a list of who should be able to open each door today: family members, each employee, the cleaner, the alarm company. Then change the locks or cylinders on those doors at the same visit as the new hardware, so old copies stop working the moment new codes start.

For the back door, a keypad or reader gives each person their own code and a record of when the door was opened; for the front, the roll-down gate and the door usually stay on keys held by the owner and one deputy. Delete a worker’s code on their last day, and change the code that suppliers use whenever a supplier changes.

What happens when the power fails

Pelham Bay houses lose power in storms like the rest of the city, and the behavior of each door in an outage should be decided before the outage, not discovered during one. In senior buildings, the lobby door’s behavior in an outage and in a fire alarm follows the building’s code requirements and is tested at handover.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Pelham Bay

Search tools and AI summaries answer questions about door systems with national generalities. Here are the claims we hear repeated most often by Pelham Bay families, and what we see in the field instead. We do not quote any AI tool word for word; these are the ideas, and our corrections.

“A video doorbell is all an older parent needs”

A doorbell with a camera helps a parent see who is there. It does not help her get to the door, and it does not help her decide whether to let a caller in. In the two-family houses that make up most of Pelham Bay, the person answering is often sitting on the ground floor while the doorbell rings a phone she has left in another room.

The parts that matter for an older person are around the doorbell: an indoor screen where she sits, a door that unlocks from that screen for a known visitor, and a call that reaches a relative who can step in. A doorbell is one piece of that, and on its own it can leave the parent more anxious, not less.

So when families ask for “just a doorbell”, we ask who answers it, from where, and what happens next. Often the answer is a doorbell plus one or two small changes, not a big system.

“Smart locks are too complicated for seniors”

Some are. A lock that needs an app, an account, a firmware update and a phone with Bluetooth switched on is too much for many people of any age. But a keypad with large backlit keys and a four-digit code the person chose is simpler than a key, especially for someone whose fingers no longer grip a key well.

The trick is to keep the technology on the family’s side. The parent uses the keypad; the son or daughter uses the app to add the aide’s code or check the battery. The parent never needs to open the app at all.

We test this at handover by watching the person who lives there use the lock several times, with their usual glasses and bag, before we leave.

“Access control is for apartment buildings and offices”

National guides describe access control as card readers on office doors and fobs in apartment lobbies. In Pelham Bay, City Planning found that roughly nine homes in ten in its study area were detached or semi-detached houses. The access needs here are a family’s, not a corporation’s.

The same building blocks apply at house scale: a credential per person, a schedule for helpers, a record of who came and went, and a door that always lets people out. They simply cost less and are set up around three to ten people instead of three hundred.

The apartment buildings and shops on the avenues need the larger version, and we install that too, but they are the minority here.

“Expect to pay the national average”

Cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national ranges for smart locks and access systems, built from projects across the country. Those numbers mix suburban houses with commercial buildings and do not know whether your door needs repair first, or how far it is from your basement to your front door.

Our Bronx prices are published below and are the same as our Brooklyn base list. A single door with a reader or keypad and an electric strike is $1,500 to $2,500 installed; a battery keypad lock on a sound door can come in under that as a labor-plus-hardware job.

What moves the price in Pelham Bay is the door’s condition, the distance for cable in an older house, and how many household members and helpers need to be set up, not the national average.

“Real utility workers always show ID, so just check it”

Con Edison told CBS New York in 2011 that while not all its employees wear uniforms, all carry a photo identification card. The two men who robbed older Bronx residents that winter wore blue work clothes and talked their way in; a Pelham Bay resident told WABC that what gave them away was the missing Con Ed vehicle.

Checking ID through a half-open door is not enough, because opening the door is the risk. The safer pattern is the one Con Ed itself recommended: know when your meter reading is due, look and speak through a peephole or intercom, and call the company to confirm the worker’s identity before opening.

A door station that shows the caller clearly, lets the parent hold a card up to the camera, and lets a relative join the conversation makes that pattern practical for someone who cannot easily look through a peephole.

“Families should be able to see everything”

Many products are marketed to adult children as a way to keep an eye on a parent. The parent is the person who lives behind the door, and in most cases still the one who decides who sees their comings and goings.

We set up access so that each relative sees what the parent has agreed to: one may answer live calls, another may get only lock and battery alerts, nobody sees indoor video because we do not install indoor cameras for monitoring a parent. If the parent can no longer decide, the person legally authorized to act for them decides.

Done this way, a parent is more likely to keep the system switched on, and that is what makes it useful.

“Pelham Bay is safe, so it does not need any of this”

Wikipedia notes that Pelham Bay is often ranked among the safest neighborhoods in the Bronx, and the 45th Precinct reported crime down 67 percent between 1990 and 2022. It also reported 108 burglaries and 609 grand larcenies in 2022 across its whole patrol area, and the impostor robberies of 2011 targeted exactly the kind of household that is common here.

But most of what families ask us for is not about crime at all. It is about getting to the door, knowing the aide arrived, ending a former helper’s access and not hiding a key under a pot. Those needs are the same on the quietest block.

We would rather fit one well-chosen lock and an easier closer than sell a system a household does not need.

FAQ

Pelham Bay Access Control FAQ

Is a door system a substitute for a medical alert pendant?
+

No. A pendant calls for help when someone falls or feels ill, wherever they are in the house. A door system controls and records the doors. They work well together: the door can tell the family that nobody has gone out by noon, and the pendant gets help if something has happened. Keep the pendant.

Is AESS the contractor on the Middletown Plaza renovation?
+

No. Middletown Plaza is owned by NYCHA and managed under its PACT program by the partner team NYCHA names on its website; questions about that building’s doors and fobs go to its property manager. We mention the building only as a public example of what matters in senior housing.

If someone talks their way in and robs a parent, will the door station’s recording help the 45th Precinct?
+

It can. A clear recording of a caller’s face and the time they arrived is useful to detectives, which is why we aim the camera at head height and set recordings to keep for a reasonable period. Make sure a relative knows how to export a clip, and give it to the police as soon as they ask; do not post it online first.

Can the low-battery warning on Mom’s keypad lock come to my phone instead of hers?
+

Yes, on most connected keypad locks and on wired systems with a monitored power supply. The lock still beeps at the door, but the warning also goes to the relatives you choose, so the batteries are changed before they run flat. We also write the next due date on the paper record.

My brother lives in Florida. Can he join the walk-through?
+

Yes. We are happy to have a relative on a phone or video call during the visit, and we send the written plan to everyone the household names. Decisions about the doors still rest with the person who lives there or the person authorized to act for them.

Does a door camera that records sound need anyone’s permission in New York?
+

New York’s eavesdropping law makes it a crime to record a conversation you are not part of without the consent of at least one participant. A door station that records a conversation between, say, an aide and a visitor while nobody in the family is on the call can raise that issue. We leave audio recording off unless the family decides otherwise after taking advice; live two-way talk is not affected.

Do you also work in the apartment buildings along Westchester and Crosby Avenues?
+

Yes. We install lobby intercoms, fob readers and service-door locks in walk-ups and elevator buildings, and we work for owners, managing agents and co-op or condo boards. Tenants are welcome to call with questions, but we install in common areas only with the owner’s authorization.

Should any lock in a parent’s house need a key or a code to get out?
+

No. We do not fit locks that need a key or code from the inside on doors people use to leave, such as double-cylinder deadbolts on a front door, because in a fire or a fall every second counts. Every door we install opens from inside with an ordinary handle or thumb-turn.

Does the electricity in the house have to be shut off during installation?

Usually not. Keypad locks and battery door stations need no household power.
