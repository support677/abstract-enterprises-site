The Neighborhood

Why Tottenville Properties Need Sonos Installation

Tottenville is the last neighbourhood in New York in every direction that counts. Wikipedia places it as the southernmost neighbourhood in both the city and the state and the westernmost in the city, with the Arthur Kill along its western and northern edges and Raritan Bay and the New York Bight to the south. Water on three sides, about two and a half square miles of land, and a single ZIP code, 10307. The Staten Island Railway has ended here since June 2, 1860, and Wikipedia gives the run to St. George as fourteen miles and about forty-two minutes. Across the Kill, the Outerbridge Crossing has tied the neighbourhood to Perth Amboy and New Jersey since 1928. People who live here tend to describe it as a small town at the end of a long line, and the housing bears that out.

The numbers explain why a Sonos job here looks so different from one in a Manhattan co-op or a St. George rental. American Community Survey estimates summarised by Point2 count roughly 5,412 homes in Tottenville, with 51.6 percent of them detached single-family houses, 24.4 percent in two-unit buildings and 19.8 percent single-family attached. About 82.9 percent are owner-occupied. The median home was built in 1989. In practice that means the person who calls us is usually the person who owns the walls, the yard, the deck and the basement, and the conversation starts with how far the music should go rather than who has to approve a bracket.

The neighbourhood was built in two bursts more than a century apart. The first was the village of oystermen and shipbuilders. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's history of Main Street calls Tottenville "the Town the Oyster Built" and says the oyster trade reached its golden age by the 1880s; shipbuilding began in the 1840s and the last yard closed in 1930. That era left Greek Revival, Gothic Revival, Italianate, Second Empire, Queen Anne and Colonial Revival houses on and around Main Street, Amboy Road, Bentley Street, Hopping Avenue, Satterlee Street and Yetman Avenue, many of them later wrapped in aluminium or vinyl siding. Point2's figures put about 20.4 percent of the housing in the "built 1939 or earlier" bracket. The second burst came in the 1990s, when, by Wikipedia's account, the land south-east of Hylan Boulevard, until then nearly uninhabited, filled with new houses. Point2 counts 17.2 percent of homes from the 1980s, 22.9 percent from the 1990s and 15.2 percent from the 2000s. A 2006 Cooperator article quoted a local broker describing narrow bungalows being replaced by "Florida Style" centre-hall colonials with stucco and brick fronts. Between the two, mid-century ranches and split-levels fill the streets further inland, and newer two-family and multi-family houses have gone up on former lots along Amboy Road.

For a Sonos installer those are two very different sets of walls. A village house usually means plaster on wood lath, deep floor framing, tall rooms, a cellar with low clearance and an attic reachable through a hatch, and an owner who wants music without the house looking modernised. A 1990s or 2000s colonial means drywall, roomy attics, an attached garage, a basement that has often been finished, a family room open to the kitchen and a router wherever the cable company happened to bring the line in. Both are owner-occupied, both have yards, and both usually end up with more Sonos zones than a city apartment ever would. The planning question is the same in each: how does the music get from the room where the TV sits to the deck, the basement and the bedrooms without a single speaker losing its connection?

Then there is the water. Time magazine's account of the weeks after Hurricane Sandy described Tottenville as flanked by water on three sides and battered by the storm, and the Staten Island Advance reported that a gigantic wave tore through the end of Yetman Avenue near Billop Avenue. City testimony to the Council lists Tottenville among the Build It Back neighbourhoods; citywide that programme rebuilt and elevated almost 1,400 homes to current flood-protection rules, and its designs often moved mechanical systems up and out of harm's way. Living Breakwaters, completed offshore in September 2024, now places eight partially submerged breakwaters along about 2,400 feet of shoreline to cut the waves reaching buildings and roads to below three feet. None of that removes the need to think about height. In a raised or rebuilt house, the level that floods is the last place for a Sonos Amp, a Port, a router or a network switch, and we plan every shore-block job around that before we discuss speakers.

Finally, the rhythm of the week. A forty-two-minute train to the ferry, an express bus to Manhattan or a drive over the Outerbridge into New Jersey means many households are out early and home late. They want music that starts when they press play, survives the internet provider swapping the router, and does not need a phone call every time someone moves a speaker. That is why we lean on wired connections wherever a house allows, write down every setting we change, and keep the number of things that can drift to a minimum.

The list says what most of this page goes on to explain: Tottenville is a neighbourhood of houses with land around them, built in two very different eras, sitting beside water that has already shown what it can do. A good Sonos system here covers more ground than a city flat, needs a stronger network behind it, and keeps its most expensive boxes at a sensible height.

A Sonos visit here often turns into a network visit as well. In a long colonial with the router at one end and the family room at the other, or a village house with brick chimneys in the middle, the honest first step is sometimes a wired access point or a cable run to one Sonos product, and the same licensed crew handles both on the same day.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Tottenville's Architecture

Walk from the station up Main Street, along Amboy Road and then south-east across Hylan Boulevard and you pass through most of Tottenville's building history in about twenty minutes. The house descriptions below come from Forgotten New York, the Landmarks Preservation Commission, Wikipedia, the Cooperator and real-estate surveys; the Sonos notes are our working practice, not claims about any particular address.

Greek Revival and Italianate Village Houses

Mid-nineteenth-century houses built for oystermen, ship joiners and shipbuilders, some on deep lots set back from the street. Expect plaster on lath, solid timber framing, small rooms off a centre or side hall and a cellar with stone or brick foundations. Wireless speakers and a console soundbar are usually the first move.

Gothic Revival Cottages

Steep-gabled cottages of the 1880s like those Forgotten New York found on Hopping Avenue. Sloped upper ceilings and small attics limit in-ceiling speakers upstairs; downstairs rooms with a cellar beneath are easier to reach from below.

Second Empire and Queen Anne Houses

Mansard roofs, towers, bays and wraparound porches from the late oyster era. Curved and angled walls make placement tricky and porches invite outdoor music; most of these houses are privately owned and not landmarked, so the porch question is the owner's alone.

Colonial Revival and Early 1900s Frame Houses

Simpler, squarer houses of the early twentieth century, often with a full attic and basement. These are the friendliest of the old houses for an Amp in the basement and ceiling speakers on the floor below the attic.

Mid-Century Ranches and Split-Levels

Inland houses from the postwar decades, single-storey or stepped. Ranches give attic access to every ceiling; split-levels put the den half below ground, where Wi-Fi is weakest and a wired product pays off.

1980s and 1990s Colonials

The houses that filled the land south-east of Hylan Boulevard: drywall on wood studs, open attics, attached garages, family rooms off the kitchen, often a two-storey entry. The natural home for a full Sonos system, because almost every ceiling and wall can be reached.

"Florida Style" Stucco and Brick Colonials

Centre-hall houses with stucco or brick fronts that replaced narrow bungalows. Inside they behave like any newer colonial; outside, the front facade is a poor place for speakers and the rear deck or patio is the usual outdoor zone.

Two-Families and Townhouse Communities

Newer two-family houses on former lots along Amboy Road and condominium communities such as Surfside Village and The Captain's Quarters. Separate households, separate networks and sometimes an association that controls the outside of the building.

Raised and Rebuilt Shore Houses

Houses on the streets nearest the water that were lifted or replaced after Sandy, with living space a storey up, parking and storage below and mechanical systems moved higher. Equipment height is the first design decision, not the last.

Plaster, drywall, stucco and stilts: how the work changes by house

Old plaster on lath. In the village houses we treat every ceiling as fragile until proven otherwise. Sonos's own in-ceiling manual asks installers to run an obstruction survey with a stud finder before cutting, looking for joists, conduit, pipes, ducts and air returns, and to drill a small pilot hole and probe with a length of wire if in doubt. On plaster that survey matters twice over, because a stud finder reads lath and old repairs as well as framing. We score the plaster before the saw goes in and choose a speaker size the ceiling can take: the six-inch Sonos ceiling speaker needs an opening about 8.25 inches across and about 4.73 inches of depth, the eight-inch about 10.125 inches across and 5.32 inches deep, and the mounting dogs grip ceilings up to roughly 1-1/4 inches thick with their caps on or 1-7/8 inches with them off. Thick old plaster on lath can push that limit, which is one reason we look before we promise.

Drywall in the newer colonials. A 1990s house is the opposite case. Ceilings are thin, joists are regular, and an open attic above the second floor makes bedroom speakers straightforward. The first-floor ceilings sit under the upstairs floor, so cable usually travels through the basement and up inside an interior wall or a closet. We check for recessed lights, bath fans and ductwork in the joist bays before marking a speaker position, and we keep a stereo pair between about six and ten feet apart, the spacing Sonos gives in its manual, so the sound images properly over the kitchen island or the dining table.

Stucco, brick and siding outside. Outdoor speakers want solid fixing and shelter. CertainTeed's installation guidance for vinyl siding says external fixtures must be attached to solid backing such as exterior-grade plywood and never directly to the siding, which has to stay free to expand and contract. On stucco and brick we fix into the structure behind with anchors suited to the material, and we prefer an underside of an eave or deck roof to a bare wall in the sun and spray. The older houses wrapped in aluminium siding get the same treatment as vinyl: find the framing, add a mounting block, keep the siding free.

Houses on stilts. In a raised house the living floors behave like any other house, but the run from a deck or a parking level back to the equipment has to stay above the level that floods, and nothing belongs in a breakaway wall or near a flood vent. We look at where the builder moved the electrical panel and mechanicals and follow the same logic: equipment up, cable routes high, nothing on the ground level that would not survive getting wet.

The Whole Lot

Planning Sonos Across a Tottenville Lot, Not Just a Living Room

In a city apartment, a Sonos system is usually one room, maybe two. In Tottenville it is often six or seven zones spread across two storeys, a basement, a deck and a yard, sometimes a detached garage or a pool area. That is a different design problem. Each zone has to have a reliable path back to the network, a sensible place for its equipment, and volume that suits the people around it, from the bedrooms upstairs to the house next door. Here is how we think about the pieces.

Start with the network, because everything rides on it

Sonos publishes a short list of network setups it will not work with, and three of them come up in detached houses all the time: wireless range extenders, guest networks and Ethernet over power lines. It also says that when a mesh system is used with a wired Sonos setup, the Sonos product must be cabled to the primary mesh unit. In a long colonial where the router sits by the front door or in the garage and the family room, deck and kitchen are at the back, the reliable answer is nearly always one Sonos product cabled to the router or to the main mesh node, and sometimes a wired access point at the far end. We check the signal in every room that will get a speaker before we sell anyone a speaker.

Building materials matter too. Sonos's own guide to wireless interference rates wood, glass and synthetic materials as low obstructions, brick and water as medium, plaster and concrete as high and metal as very high. A Tottenville village house with plaster walls and a brick chimney stack in the middle can be harder for Wi-Fi than a bigger, newer house with drywall. A raised house with a concrete or block ground level and a steel beam under the floor can block signal between the deck and the living room. The same guide asks for Sonos products to sit at least 18 inches from other electronics, and names cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras and microwaves in use among the common interference sources.

Decide where the boxes live before choosing speakers

Every passive speaker in a ceiling, wall or yard needs an Amp, and every Amp needs a home with power, ventilation and a cable path to the speakers. In the newer colonials the basement utility area or a closet near the router is the usual answer. In the village houses the cellar is only an option if it stays dry and has headroom; otherwise a ground-floor closet takes the job. In raised houses the equipment goes on the first living floor. Sonos's guide for the Port, the box that feeds Sonos into an existing stereo, asks for at least two inches of clear space around it on a shelf and for it to be kept away from heat, and the Amp deserves the same respect. A closed cabinet beside a boiler is not a home.

Think about where the sound goes next

A detached house solves the party-wall problem that dominates apartment work, but it brings others. Bedrooms over a family room hear a Sub through the floor. A deck faces somebody else's yard, and in the newer streets south-east of Hylan the houses are close enough for that to matter on a summer evening. A two-family house has one household above the other. We place Subs on the ground floor away from the stair, set volume limits on outdoor zones and bedroom speakers, and aim deck speakers at the seating rather than across the fence. These are settings that cost nothing on the day and save a lot of awkwardness later.

Leave room for the next zone

Owners rarely do the whole lot at once. The common pattern is a soundbar first, ceiling speakers in the kitchen a year later, and the deck the summer after that. So the first visit is where we decide where the Amp location will be, which closet the future cable will come down, and whether a run to the back of the house is worth pulling while the basement ceiling is still open. A system planned that way grows in single visits rather than reworks. Sonos's system requirements allow up to 32 products in one system, which is more than any Tottenville house we can imagine will need; the limit is always the network and the routes, never the count.

One more point on the yard. Not every outdoor space needs a fixed speaker. A battery speaker such as a Move 2 or Roam 2 can carry music to the far end of a yard, a beach towel or a neighbour's barbecue and come back inside when the evening ends. For many Tottenville households a fixed pair on the deck plus one portable is the most useful combination, and it keeps the number of holes in the house to a minimum.

Shore Blocks

Sonos in Houses Raised and Rebuilt After Sandy

On the streets closest to Raritan Bay and the Arthur Kill, many houses are not the ones that stood there in 2012. Some were lifted onto new foundations; some were demolished and rebuilt to current flood-protection rules. A photojournalist who walked the area seven months after the storm described boarded-up houses and empty lots on the streets closest to the water and quoted a resident from the corner of Rockaway Street and Billop Avenue. City testimony lists Tottenville among the neighbourhoods served by Build It Back, and an architecture firm that worked on the programme notes that its designs often relocated mechanical systems to safeguard them. A Sonos system in one of these houses has to respect the same thinking.

Nothing on the flood-prone level

In a raised house the ground level, whether open, enclosed for parking or used for storage, is the part of the building designed to take water. We never put an Amp, a Port, a router, a switch or a power strip for any of them down there, and we do not run speaker or network cable along its walls if there is a route higher up. If the internet line arrives at ground level, the router goes upstairs and the provider's cable comes up to it. That is the same logic the builders used for the electrical panel and the heating plant, and it is our working rule on every shore-block job, not a judgment on any particular house.

Getting music to a deck that sits over parking

Raised houses often have a deck at the first living floor, sometimes over the parking space. Cable from an Amp inside to speakers under that deck's roof can usually travel inside the floor framing of the living level, emerge through a sealed opening close to the speaker, and never touch the ground floor. Where that is not possible, we look at the soffits and the underside of the deck structure itself, staying clear of anything designed to break away or let water through. If the only route crosses the flood-prone level, we say so and suggest a battery speaker for that part of the deck instead.

Wi-Fi through concrete, block and steel

The new foundations, block walls and steel beams that hold a raised house up are exactly the materials Sonos's interference guide rates as high or very high obstructions. A deck on the far side of a block wall from the router can be a dead zone even when the living room is fine. We test it before we promise anything. Often the cleanest fix is a wired access point or a cabled Sonos product near the deck door, so the outdoor zone does not depend on signal crossing the structure.

Power cuts and storm season

Every Sonos product needs mains power and the network to play together. When the power goes, so does the system, and when it returns the products have to find the router again. We set the house up so that recovery is simple: the router and the wired Sonos product on the same protected circuit or surge strip, the Amp in a ventilated spot that comes back cleanly, and room names and groups written on the handover sheet so anyone in the house can check that everything has reappeared. Whether a particular house needs whole-house surge protection or a dedicated circuit is a question for its electrician, and we say so in the quote.

Landmarks

Individual Landmarks, No District: What That Means for Sonos

Tottenville has a strong preservation culture and a long list of admired houses, but the way the city protects them here is different from neighbourhoods such as St. George or Park Slope. As Forgotten New York put it after a 2025 walk, Tottenville has no landmarked residential districts. What it has are individually designated buildings: the James L. Bedell House on Amboy Road, a Second Empire house designated in 2005 over its owner's objection; the De Hart House at 134 Main Street, built in 1849; the Rutan-Journeay House, the first on Amboy Road at number 7647; the George Cunningham Store at 173 Main Street; the Tottenville branch library at 7430 Amboy Road, designated in 1995; and the Conference House, with much of Conference House Park also covered by its own designations, including the Aakawaxung Munahanung Archaeological Site. The Preservation League of Staten Island has also given awards to several private houses on Bentley Street, Brighton Street, Wood Avenue and Amboy Road, which is an honour rather than a regulation.

So for the great majority of Tottenville owners, Landmarks rules simply do not apply to a Sonos job. The house is not designated, there is no district, and the owner decides what goes inside and outside. For the handful of owners of an individually designated house, the Commission's own permits page says an LPC permit is needed for interior work only when the work requires a permit from the Department of Buildings, when interior work affects the exterior, or when the interior itself has been designated. The Commission's permit-types page gives HVAC louvres and vents as examples of interior work that affects the exterior. Ceiling and wall speakers wired at low voltage inside the house sit comfortably on the interior side of that line in our reading; anything fixed to the outside, and any cable that passes through an exterior wall, is the kind of work that would need the owner to apply first.

Two cautions. First, a real-estate blog describes a "Historic Tottenville" district with conservation guidelines. We could find no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district for Tottenville, and we do not treat that claim as a rule; if you are unsure about your own house, the Commission's online designation map settles it in a minute and we will check it with you on the phone. Second, we are installers, not preservation consultants. If you own one of the designated buildings and want speakers on a porch or wall outside, the application comes from you, and we supply product details, photographs and a cable route to go with it.

The low-voltage rule helps in every house, landmarked or not. NYC Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7 says no electrical permit is required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signalling, communication, alarm and data circuits when it is done by a licensed electrician, special electrician or qualified person. Speaker cable from a Sonos Amp and network cable to a Sonos product are low-voltage. New outlets and circuits are not, and those always go to a licensed electrician.

Commercial Sonos Installation

Business Sonos Installation in Tottenville

Tottenville's businesses sit in two places that grew a century apart. Until the 2000s, Wikipedia notes, commerce was largely confined to the Main Street corridor in the heart of the old village; since then a second core has grown at the north end along Page Avenue and west of Amboy Road. The customers are neighbours, commuters coming off the train and drivers coming off the Outerbridge, and the music a business plays has to suit short visits, families and staff who hear the same playlist all day. Rules that apply to music in a Tottenville business

  • Low-voltage work without an electrical permit. Under Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, speaker and data wiring below 50 volts needs no electrical permit when done by a qualified person. New outlets for an Amp shelf or a ceiling equipment location are line-voltage work for a licensed electrician, and we say so in writing.
  • Landmarked storefronts. The George Cunningham Store and the De Hart House on Main Street are individually designated. Anything fixed to their exteriors goes through the building owner and the Landmarks Preservation Commission; interior low-voltage work does not, in our reading of the Commission's rules.
  • Night-time music limits. NYC311's guidance on noise from bars, clubs and restaurants says that between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. their music may not exceed 42 decibels measured inside nearby homes, nor 7 decibels above the background level measured on the street at least 15 feet away. In storefronts with apartments above, a Sub belongs off the shared floor and volume limits matter.
  • Landlord approval and insurance. Strip-centre and mixed-use landlords normally require approval and a certificate of insurance naming them before work begins. We send ours with the booking confirmation.
  • Plenum ceilings. Where a drop ceiling is used as an air return, cable must be rated for that space. We check and specify it in the quote rather than discover it on the day.
  • Clear paths and fixtures. Speakers and brackets in public areas go high on walls or in ceilings, out of walkways and away from exit signage, so they never become an obstruction.

Choosing and buying

A property listing described our street as part of "Historic Tottenville." Is there a landmark district here, and would it limit where speakers can go?

We could not find one. The Landmarks Preservation Commission protects individual buildings in Tottenville, such as the Bedell House, the De Hart House, the Rutan-Journeay House, the Cunningham Store and the library, and Forgotten New York noted in 2026 that the neighbourhood has no landmarked residential district. The "Historic Tottenville" wording appears on a real-estate blog and does not match any Commission designation we could find. If your own house is not individually designated, Landmarks rules do not touch a Sonos job inside or outside it. We check the city's designation map with you during the first call so there is no doubt.

Do Sonos in-ceiling speakers need a Sonos Amp, or do they power themselves?

They need an amplifier. Sonos describes its architectural ceiling speakers as passive, 8-ohm speakers optimised for use with the Sonos Amp, and says one Amp reliably powers up to three pairs, six speakers in all, when wired in parallel. The speakers themselves have no power, no Wi-Fi and no controls; the Amp provides all three and joins them to the rest of your system. That is why every ceiling or deck zone on a Tottenville quote shows an Amp location as well as speaker positions, and why the Amp needs a ventilated home with an outlet near where the speaker cables can gather.

We already bought our Sonos at a big-box store and the app walks you through setup. What does an installer add?

For a single speaker on a shelf, often nothing, and we will tell you so. The value shows up in the parts the app cannot do: fixing a soundbar to the right framing, cutting ceiling openings without damaging plaster, getting cable from a basement Amp to a deck, deciding which product should be cabled to the router so a seven-zone house stays stable, and tuning each room with Trueplay, which Sonos says requires an iPhone or iPad for most of its products. Sonos's own guides say in-ceiling speakers require special installation and often a professional installer. In a Tottenville house with a yard and a basement, those jobs tend to come with the system.

Sorting out what is already there

Our basement has an old stereo, a Sonos, two routers and a nest of cables nobody understands. Can it be untangled?

Yes, and it is one of the more satisfying visits we do. We trace every cable with a tone generator and probe, label what each one does, remove what is dead, and decide which router stays; two routers on one network is a common cause of Sonos products losing each other. The old stereo can stay and play Sonos through a Port, which Sonos lists with analogue and digital outputs and a 12-volt trigger to wake an amplifier, or it can go. A Staten Island Yelp reviewer described exactly this kind of basement tangle being solved, and it usually fits inside a $225 first-hour setup visit plus whatever time the cables demand.

Living with the system

Can the Sonos play music while we are away so the house sounds lived-in?

Sonos alarms can start a chosen room or group with a set playlist or station at a set time and volume on chosen days, so a radio station in the kitchen from six to nine in the evening is easy to arrange and easy to switch off in the app. Treat it as one small part of making a house look occupied, not as security. CBS New York reported a Tottenville burglary near George and Stephen Streets in which the suspects got into the garage using an opener left in an unlocked car in the driveway; no speaker schedule would have changed that. Locks, lighting and an alarm do the real work, and we can talk about those separately.

Our deck looks straight out over Raritan Bay and takes the full wind. How do outdoor speakers get fixed so they stay put?

Into the structure, under shelter, with the manufacturer's bracket. We fix brackets through mounting blocks or solid backing into framing, never into vinyl or aluminium siding alone, following siding makers' guidance that fixtures need solid backing. We prefer the underside of an eave or deck roof to a bare wall, because it cuts both wind-driven rain and direct sun, and we angle speakers down toward the seating so they can be played at lower volume. After a big storm, check that the brackets have not shifted; if one has, it is a short visit to re-fix, not a new installation.

For a backyard movie night with a projector, can the sound come through Sonos?

It can, with a caveat about timing. Sonos plays TV sound in sync through its soundbars, which take audio from the TV over HDMI or optical; a projector or streaming stick sending audio to a Sonos speaker over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth can drift out of step with the picture. The tidy outdoor setup is a soundbar or a portable speaker that the projector's source feeds directly, placed near the screen, with the deck speakers left for music. We test the lip sync on the night of the install and leave notes on the delay setting, if the source device has one.

We want music loud enough for workouts in our basement home gym. Which Sonos product, and where should it go?

For a typical finished basement, a pair of Era speakers on wall brackets or a pair of ceiling speakers on an Amp, placed away from the treadmill and weights so nothing drops on them, and paired as stereo. The Era 300's Sonos wall mount is rated for stud mounting only, not plain drywall, so we fix it into framing. Basements are where Wi-Fi is weakest, so one product cabled to the network is worth it here. And because a basement gym sits under living space, a volume limit and keeping the Sub, if any, off the wall shared with the stairwell will keep early workouts from waking the house.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Tottenville

"Mount the outdoor speakers anywhere on the outside wall"

Siding manufacturers are explicit that fixtures must go on solid backing such as exterior-grade plywood and never directly onto vinyl siding, because the siding moves with temperature. A speaker bracket screwed straight into siding will eventually work loose, and on a deck that takes the wind off the bay it will do so sooner.

The better rule is structure first, shelter second. We find the framing, add a mounting block where siding is involved, and choose the underside of an eave or a deck roof over a bare wall wherever the layout allows.

"A mesh kit or a booster will cure the dead rooms"

This is the most repeated tip for weak Wi-Fi, and half of it is wrong for Sonos. Sonos lists wireless range extenders and Ethernet over power lines among the setups it does not support, alongside guest networks.

Mesh systems can work, but Sonos says that when one Sonos product is wired, it must be cabled to the primary mesh unit, not a satellite. In a long colonial whose router sits in the garage, the satellite in the family room is exactly where people want to plug the soundbar in.

In Tottenville's village houses the problem is often the walls themselves; Sonos's own guide rates plaster as a high obstruction and brick as medium. A cable to one product, or a wired access point, solves what no booster can.

"Put the equipment in the basement, out of the way"

Generic home-audio advice sends the Amp, the receiver and the router to the basement as a matter of course. In a Tottenville colonial with a dry, finished basement that is often right.

In a house on the streets nearest the water, it can be the worst possible choice. Build It Back designs often moved mechanical systems up to protect them, and the same logic applies to a Sonos Amp, a Port and the network gear they depend on. A flood-prone ground level is no place for any of them.

Even in the old village, a cellar with stone walls and a history of damp is a poor home for electronics. We look at the space before we decide, and we say so plainly if a closet upstairs is the better answer.

"Tottenville's historic district has design rules for your house"

"Ceiling speakers mean tearing up the ceilings"

The fear is understandable, and in some buildings it is justified. In most Tottenville houses it is not.

Ranches and colonials have open attics over the top floor, so bedroom and upper-floor speakers are cut from below with cable dropped from above. First-floor ceilings are reached from the basement and through interior walls or closets. The opening for the speaker is often the only opening, and the speaker covers it.

The village houses take more care: plaster on lath, solid framing and fewer open voids. Sonos's own manual calls for an obstruction survey and a probe through a pilot hole when in doubt, and we follow it. Where the route cannot be found without damage, we say so and recommend Era speakers instead.

"Staten Island jobs come with a travel or island surcharge"

"Sonos runs on any Wi-Fi, guest network included"

Many households put new gadgets on a guest network to keep them apart from laptops, and some advice encourages it. Sonos lists guest networks and networks with a log-in page among its unsupported setups.

It also requires the phone running the app to be on the same network as the speakers, and says routers that only provide 5 GHz are not supported, since all its products support 2.4 GHz. A two-family house where the upstairs tenant uses a guest network on the owner's router is a common Tottenville trap.

The fix is usually simple: one main network for the Sonos system and the phones that control it, and a separate router, not a guest network, for a second household.

FAQ

Tottenville Sonos Installation FAQ

Our family-room TV is only 50 inches. Will an Arc Ultra look too wide underneath it?
+

It may sit a little wider than the screen. Sonos gives the Arc Ultra a width of about 46.38 inches, and a 50-inch TV measured diagonally is usually around 44 inches across, so the bar can overhang slightly on each side. Many owners do not mind; others prefer the Beam, which is about 25.63 inches wide and suits a smaller screen better. If you choose the Arc Ultra, Sonos asks for at least four inches between the bar and the TV above it, and we plan the wall-mount height around that gap.

One of us works from home. Will the internet be down while you set up the Sonos?
+

Only briefly, if at all. Setting up Sonos products does not take the internet away from the rest of the house. The interruptions come when we cable a product to the router, move the router to a better spot or replace it, and each of those usually means a few minutes offline. We ask about video calls when we book and schedule any router work around them, and we tell you before anything is unplugged.

Can an Era 100 sit on top of the kitchen cabinets, in the gap under the ceiling?
+

It can, with two checks. The speaker needs an outlet within reach of its cable, which above cabinets often means a new receptacle from an electrician. And it needs air around it and distance from heat, so a spot above the oven or a range hood is a poor choice. Sound from up there spreads across the room well, but the buttons are out of reach, so you will control it from the app or by voice. Keep it well away from the microwave too.

If a summer shower catches the Move 2 out on the deck, is it ruined?
+

A passing shower should not harm it. Sonos rates the Move 2 at IP56, which covers dust and strong jets of water, and the smaller Roam 2 at IP67, which covers brief immersion. Neither rating means the speaker should live outdoors, so bring it in after the rain, let it dry before charging and do not leave it standing in a puddle. For music that stays out all season, fixed outdoor speakers on an Amp are the better tool.

Does the Sub have to sit right beside the soundbar, or can it go across the room?
+

It does not have to be beside the bar. The Sub connects wirelessly, and Sonos's own guide allows a corner, a wall or a spot behind, under or next to furniture, as long as the front opening stays clear. Low bass is hard to locate by ear, so a Sub on the far side of the room often sounds just as good. Trueplay sets the Sub's phase automatically after it moves. In a colonial, the one spot we avoid is directly below a bedroom.

Our kitchen speaker sits near the microwave. Could that be why the music cuts out at dinner time?
+

It could. Sonos lists microwaves in use among the common sources of wireless interference and asks for its products to sit at least 18 inches from other electronics. A microwave running can upset the Wi-Fi a nearby speaker depends on, which matches dropouts that only happen while food is heating. Moving the speaker a few feet away, or cabling one Sonos product elsewhere in the house so the system leans less on that signal, usually ends it.

The base for our cordless phone sits right beside the router. Can it upset the Sonos?
+

It can. Sonos names cordless and DECT phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras and smart-home hubs among the devices that interfere with its products, and the router is the busiest point in the network. Giving the phone base its own spot a few feet away from the router and any wired Sonos product costs nothing and is one of the first things we try when a system drops out without an obvious cause.

Should the deck speakers be switched off for the winter, or can they stay connected?
+

They can stay connected. Outdoor speakers on a Sonos Amp have no power of their own; the Amp lives indoors, and the speakers are simply silent when nothing is playing to them. If the Amp powers only the deck and you would rather it disappear from the app until spring, unplug the Amp and the room vanishes until it is plugged back in. Battery speakers are different and should come indoors for the cold months.

Can the music follow us from the kitchen to the family room to the deck by itself?
+

Not automatically. Sonos does not track where people are in the house and move the music with them. What works instead is grouping the rooms you move between so the same music plays in all of them, which takes a tap in the app, or carrying a Move 2 or Roam 2 from room to room. For most households a saved group covering the kitchen, family room and deck does the job.

Can the Sonos speakers tell us when the garage door opens?
+

Not on their own. A Sonos system has no sensors and does not know what a garage door is doing. Some smart-home platforms that support Sonos can play an alert through a speaker when a connected garage controller or door sensor reports a change, but that depends entirely on the platform and the devices you already have. We can tell you on the phone whether your setup supports it; we do not install garage door controllers.

We bought a Ray for the bedroom TV. Can it play music too, or only the TV's sound?
+

It plays music as well. Every Sonos soundbar is also a full Sonos speaker, so the Ray can stream your music services from the app, join a group with the kitchen or the deck, and run an alarm in the morning. When the TV turns on, it switches to the TV's sound. Setting it up as part of the whole system is covered by the $125 setup line.
