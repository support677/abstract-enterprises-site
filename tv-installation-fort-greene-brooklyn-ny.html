The Neighborhood

Why Fort Greene Properties Need TV Installation

Fort Greene is small, a little over half a square mile between Flushing Avenue and Atlantic Avenue, Flatbush Avenue Extension and Vanderbilt Avenue, but for anyone putting a TV on a wall it behaves like two neighborhoods stacked on top of each other. Wikipedia's history of the area dates its Italianate brick and brownstone row houses to the 1850s and 1860s and says construction had "virtually ended by the 1870s." The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fort Greene and BAM Historic Districts in 1978, which is why so many of those houses still have their stoops, their parlor mantels and, behind the paint, their original plaster. Then, starting in the 2000s, the western and southern edges filled with towers: 300 Ashland Place at 32 stories, 250 Ashland Place at 52, 80 DeKalb Avenue with 369 apartments on 34 floors, Eighty Nine DeKalb at 30 stories, Forte at 230 Ashland Place, and the two 32-story buildings at 240 Willoughby Street across from the park. A TV installer who walks from a South Portland Avenue parlor to a 30th-floor rental on DeKalb in the same afternoon is drilling into completely different materials, held together by completely different rules.

We pulled the city's MapPLUTO land-use records for the ten census tracts that make up Fort Greene to put numbers on that split. They list 2,126 lots, of which 1,771 are residential, holding 19,739 apartments. Of those residential lots, 895 were built before 1900 and another 518 between 1900 and 1939, so roughly four out of five buildings went up before the Second World War. But those old buildings are small, and they hold only about 8,000 of the apartments between them. The 107 lots built since 2005 hold 7,986 apartments, and just 32 towers of fifty or more units built since 2005 account for 7,629 of them, about four in ten of all the homes in the neighborhood. Another 2,996 apartments are in buildings from 1940 to 1979, most of them in the public housing along Myrtle Avenue. So a TV request from Fort Greene is close to an even bet between a 160-year-old wall and a wall that is younger than the TV standard it is about to carry.

Those two kinds of wall fail in opposite ways. Old Brooklyn plaster is hard on the face and weak underneath, and in a row house it may sit on wood lath, on metal mesh or directly on brick. Brownstoner's forum has years of threads from owners who discovered this the hard way: one described party-wall fireplaces with "no studs, just plaster over brick" and noted that short anchors held his shelves for a few weeks while long lead shields into the brick lasted about forty years; another found that his stud finder gave nothing but false readings on the street wall between two parlor windows. A tower partition fails differently. It looks like ordinary drywall, but behind it may be steel studs, a concrete shear wall, a sprinkler branch or the neighbor's bedroom, and the building office will usually have written rules about drilling, working hours and insurance before it lets a contractor up. The fixing that suits one kind of wall can be exactly wrong for the other, which is why we read the wall first and choose hardware second.

The households inside those walls shape the work as much as the materials do. Many of the row houses are no longer single homes. Brownstoner has profiled Fort Greene brownstones split into three apartments with their own internal stairs, and owners frequently live on the garden and parlor floors with a rental above, which puts an owner, a tenant and sometimes a condo board in the conversation about one wall. The public housing is large: Wikipedia counts the Walt Whitman Houses at fifteen buildings of 6 and 13 stories with 1,659 apartments, and NYCHA finished exterior brick and facade work at Ingersoll and Whitman in the summer of 2025. And the towers are run by management companies with concierges, live-in supers and package rooms. CityRealty describes 300 Ashland Place's 24-hour concierge and live-in superintendent, and New York YIMBY describes Eighty Nine DeKalb's "24/7 doorman-attended lobby." In all three settings the drilling is the easy part. Knowing who has to say yes, and when the crew is allowed through the door, is what keeps the job from being postponed on the day.

Then there is what people actually do with a TV here. Fort Greene is a cultural district as much as a residential one: the Downtown Brooklyn Partnership lists BAM, BRIC, the Mark Morris Dance Center, the Center for Fiction and the L10 arts building along Lafayette Avenue, Ashland Place and Fulton Street. Its restaurants and bars line DeKalb, Myrtle and Fulton. Some of the townhouses have whole-house audio and video systems put in by AV integrators during gut renovations; Resolution AV NYC, for example, lists a "Fort Greene Townhouse" project with centralized video, Control4 and a hi-fi stereo. So a Fort Greene TV call might be a simple 50-inch screen in a rental bedroom, a parlor TV that has to look at home under a plaster cornice, a screen that has to plug into somebody else's equipment rack in the cellar, or a pair of displays in a bar that fills up for big matches. We price all of them from the same list, and we plan each one around the building it is in.

Seven Fort Greene facts that change a TV job before we pick a bracket

  • Four in five buildings are prewar. PLUTO dates 1,413 of Fort Greene's 1,771 residential lots before 1940. Plaster is the wall we expect first in those houses, and it carries the +$45 plaster line on our list because it needs slower drilling, different bits and anchors that reach past it.
  • Four in ten apartments are in towers finished since 2005. The 32 large towers built since 2005 hold 7,629 apartments. Their TV walls are newer partitions, often over steel or against concrete, and their offices usually ask for a certificate of insurance and set working hours.
  • Most of the historic houses are landmarked. Our PLUTO count puts 686 residential lots inside the Fort Greene Historic District and 83 inside the BAM Historic District. Landmarks rules mostly concern what is visible outside, which matters for a dish, an antenna or a cable run on the facade, not for a bracket on an inside wall.
  • The party wall is often the longest wall. In an 18- to 20-foot-wide row house the unbroken wall in the parlor is usually the one shared with the next house. It is commonly plaster laid straight onto brick, which holds a TV very well but takes a hammer drill and masonry anchors, and it is the brick line on our list, not the plaster line.
  • Mantels sit on those party walls. In many Fort Greene parlors the fireplace breast is on the party wall too, so a TV over the mantel usually means the above-fireplace line plus the brick line, and a conversation about height before anything else.
  • Old houses often have lead paint. HPD's lead-safe work practices apply to housing built before 1978 when work disturbs two square feet or more of paint in a room. A bracket and a pilot hole disturb far less than that, but an in-wall kit or a wall that is being stripped can get closer, and we treat old paint with care either way.
  • A good share of homes are in public housing. The Whitman and Ingersoll Houses together began as the 35-building Fort Greene Houses of 1941 to 1944. Their walls are brick and masonry, and their residents work within NYCHA's management office, which we plan around rather than work around.

Same-Day Repair in Fort Greene

Need Repair in Fort Greene? A Screen Tipping Off a Party Wall, a Bracket Spinning in Tower Drywall, or a Cord Pushed Through the Plaster

Urgent TV calls from Fort Greene tend to come in three shapes, and each one points back to a mismatch between the fixing and the wall. The first is a row-house TV that has started to lean forward or drop on one side. Almost always, someone used anchors made for drywall in old plaster: plastic plugs or small toggles that bite into the hard skin of the plaster and nothing behind it. Every time the screen is pulled out on an arm or bumped while dusting, the crumbly brown coat behind that skin gives a little more, until the bracket is held by friction and paint. The second is a tower TV whose bracket screws have started to spin. That usually means a lag screw went into a thin steel stud, or into nothing at all between studs, and the threads have stripped. The third is the leftover problem: a previous tenant or owner pushed the TV's power cord into a hole above the screen and out of another near the floor, which the electrical code does not allow, and now the new owner wants it done properly.

If a screen is tipping, do not try to push it back up or tighten the screws while it hangs there. If two people can lift it safely, take the TV off the bracket and lay it face up on a blanket, unplug it, and call (347) 934-8335. On our Brooklyn list, taking down an old TV is $85. If the same screen goes back up properly on the same wall in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type: $179.99 for drywall on wood studs, with $45 added for plaster or $65 for brick, and the take-down added on top. Where a cord has been hidden illegally inside the wall, we pull it out and replace it with an in-wall power and cable kit ($169) on a framed wall, or with a paintable cord cover ($59) on masonry. If the trouble turns out to be the wall itself, a plaster patch that has let go from its lath or a soft section of mortar, we say so plainly and stop, so you can bring in a plasterer, a mason or the landlord before anything heavy goes back on it.

One more repair is worth mentioning because it is so common in the old houses here: the TV that went up over a parlor mantel years ago, far too high, with a stiff neck to show for it. Lowering it is not always possible, because the mantel shelf and the chimney breast fix the lowest safe point. But it is often possible to bring it down a few inches, change to a slimmer bracket, or move the screen to another wall entirely, and we can tell from photos which of those your room allows before you book.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Fort Greene's Architecture

Seven kinds of building cover almost every Fort Greene TV call. They differ in three ways that matter more than any brand of TV: what the walls are made of, how far a large screen has to be carried and through what, and who besides you gets a say in what is drilled.

The single-family row house

Italianate, Greek Revival, Second Empire and Neo-Grec houses on the streets around the park, mostly from the 1850s and 1860s: parlor floors with high ceilings, mantels on the party walls and plaster everywhere.

The divided row house

The same houses split into two, three or four apartments or condos, each with its own floor or duplex, an owner often living on the lower floors, and one front door shared by everyone.

The rare wood-frame house

A handful of frame houses survive among the brick rows, including an 1870 house on a landmarked block near the park that 6sqft wrote about. Their walls are framed, which changes the anchors entirely.

The prewar elevator building

Apartment buildings from the early 1900s on Waverly Avenue, Greene Avenue and nearby, and One Hanson Place, the 1929 Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower converted to condos in 2005 to 2007.

The new tower

Buildings finished since 2005 along Ashland Place, Rockwell Place, Flatbush Avenue Extension, DeKalb Avenue and Willoughby Street, with doormen or virtual doormen, package rooms and building rules about contractors.

The public housing campus

The Walt Whitman and Ingersoll Houses along Myrtle Avenue, brick buildings of six to thirteen stories from 1941 to 1944, where the management office is part of every plan.

The shop with apartments above

Three- and four-story buildings on Myrtle, Fulton and DeKalb with a restaurant, bar or store at street level and rentals upstairs, where the TV may be for the business or the tenant.

The single-family row house: a parlor built for a mirror, now asked to hold a screen

The classic Fort Greene parlor was designed around a fireplace, a pier mirror between the front windows and a pair of pocket doors to the back parlor. None of those features leaves an obvious place for a 65-inch TV, and each wall in the room has a different construction. The front wall facing the street is the outside of the house: brownstone or brick on the outside, with plaster inside that may be on furring strips, on lath over rough framing, or applied straight to the masonry. The wall that runs from front to back on one side is the party wall, usually solid brick with plaster laid directly on it, and it often carries the chimney breasts for the parlor and the floors above. The wall on the other side runs beside the stair hall, typically framed and plastered on wood lath. And the back wall is interrupted by the pocket doors. So the question "where should the TV go" in a single-family row house is really a question about which of four walls you are choosing, and the price and the method follow from that choice.

The party wall is the strongest choice if the room works around it. Plaster on brick holds a TV for decades when the anchors are set into the brick, not the mortar, and are long enough to pass through the plaster coat, which can be thick in a house of this age. It is the brick line on our list ($65 on top of the $179.99 base for a screen up to 55 inches). The hall-side wall is plaster on lath over wood framing, which is the plaster line ($45) and is the only wall in most parlors where an in-wall power and cable kit can go, because the kit needs a hollow framed cavity between its two openings. The front wall between the windows is the one Brownstoner posters warn about, because a stud finder reads it badly and its construction varies from house to house. We do not guess on that wall. A single pilot hole behind where the bracket plate will sit tells us what is there.

The divided row house: one wall, several households, and sometimes a condo declaration

Many Fort Greene brownstones are now two, three or four homes. A common layout puts the owner's duplex on the garden and parlor floors and a rental or two above, each with its own kitchen and its own living room squeezed into a floor that was built as two bedrooms. In those apartments the TV wall is often a partition that was added during the conversion, which may be ordinary drywall on wood studs and the simplest wall in the house, or it may be the old party wall again. Either way, the apartment is narrower than the parlor below it, the ceiling is lower, and the windows are smaller, so the screen size that looks right in the owner's parlor can overwhelm a top-floor living room.

The harder part is permission. If the house is a rental with an owner downstairs, the owner decides what goes on the walls, and the lease's alteration clause is what counts. If the house is a small condo, the condo's bylaws may treat opening a wall differently from hanging a bracket, and a party wall shared with the next building may be a common element rather than part of your unit. We cannot read your declaration for you, but we can tell you exactly what each option involves, two small openings and a box for an in-wall kit, four or six bracket holes, or none at all with the TV on its stand, so you can ask the right person the right question before the visit.

The rare wood-frame house: framed walls among brick neighbors

Fort Greene still has a few wood-frame houses that predate or survived the brick rows, and owners are sometimes surprised that their walls behave nothing like their neighbors'. A frame house has studs in its exterior walls and its side walls, so there is no masonry to anchor into anywhere, and plaster on lath is the norm on every wall. That makes the plaster line the likely add-on, and it means every fixing must find the framing, which in a house built before standard stud spacing can be irregular. It also means an in-wall power and cable kit is possible on more walls than in a brick house. The one caution is the outside walls: in an old frame house they may hold insulation added later, old knob-and-tube wiring or blocking between studs, so we scan carefully and keep the kit to interior walls where we can.

The prewar elevator building and One Hanson Place: solid walls and tall ceilings

Fort Greene's prewar apartment buildings, mostly from the early twentieth century, usually have masonry outside walls and interior partitions of plaster on gypsum or clay tile block. A StreetEasy thread about mounting a TV in exactly this kind of building described the problem well: a poster who could hang the screen but not "drill into the cement-like wall and hide the wires," and replies explaining that trenching those walls is messy, that a paintable cord cover is "not ideal but it isn't bad," and that power cords cannot be buried anyway. That matches what we find. These walls hold anchors well, but they cannot take an in-wall kit, so a cord cover, a carefully planned console position or a cable run along a baseboard is the honest answer.

One Hanson Place, the former Williamsburgh Savings Bank Tower at the corner of Ashland Place and Hanson Place, deserves its own note. Built from 1927 to 1929 and converted to condominiums between 2005 and 2007, it is an individual landmark whose exterior was designated in 1977 and some of whose interior spaces were designated in 1996. Corcoran's building listing describes beamed ceilings of at least 10.5 feet, with some floors reaching 11 to 14 feet. Tall ceilings tempt people to hang a TV high; in practice the right height is set by your eyes when seated, not by the ceiling, and the beams can actually help by giving a cable a route across the room that does not involve opening a wall. The condo's board and managing agent set the rules for contractors, and we send whatever paperwork they ask for before the visit.

The new tower: drywall that hides concrete, steel and the neighbor

The towers that now line Ashland Place, Rockwell Place and DeKalb Avenue look the simplest from the inside: smooth painted walls, wide windows, a single long wall facing the sofa. The construction behind that paint is the question. A partition between rooms may be drywall on steel studs; the wall between your apartment and the next may be a heavier assembly built for fire and sound separation; and some walls are the face of a concrete core or column with drywall fixed directly over it. A lag screw into a thin steel stud strips; a toggle into the wrong cavity can puncture a sound assembly; and a drill bit that suddenly stops dead in concrete may have found reinforcing steel. Our list's base price assumes drywall on wood studs, so for tower walls we confirm the construction from your photos and the building's information before booking, and tell you the total for that wall before we put you on the schedule.

The other half of a tower job is logistics. CityRealty describes 300 Ashland Place's 24-hour concierge, valet and live-in superintendent; New York YIMBY describes Eighty Nine DeKalb's doorman-attended lobby and app-based keyless entry; StreetEasy lists The Garrison at 151 Carlton Avenue, finished in 2025, with a virtual doorman and a package room. In buildings like these, the office typically wants a certificate of insurance naming the building and its managing agent, a service elevator booking, and work inside set hours. Brick Underground's guide to alteration agreements notes that they "may also dictate the days and hours in which contractors can work in your apartment" and can require contractor liability insurance. We ask for the building's contractor rules when you book and send our certificate before the visit, so nobody is turned away at the desk.

The public housing campus: masonry walls and a management office in the loop

The Walt Whitman and Ingersoll Houses began as a single 35-building complex, the Fort Greene Houses, built between 1941 and 1944 and split into two developments in 1957 and 1958. Their apartments have masonry walls, which hold a bracket very well with the right anchors and a hammer drill, and they often have limited outlets, so where the TV can go is usually decided by where the outlet and the cable come in. NYCHA residents work through the development's management office for permission to alter walls, and we ask that you confirm what the office allows before booking. Where a wall mount is not allowed or the answer has not come back yet, a TV on its own stand is a full job on our list at $119.99, with the devices connected and the cords tidied, and the wall mount can always follow later.

The shop with apartments above: two kinds of customer, one building

Myrtle Avenue, Fulton Street and DeKalb Avenue are lined with three- and four-story buildings that have a business at street level and apartments above. For a TV installer that means two quite different jobs in the same walls. Downstairs, a bar or restaurant wants screens that can be seen from the door and every seat, mounted where staff can reach the controls, and installed before opening or after closing. Upstairs, a tenant wants a living-room TV in an apartment whose walls are often brick on the sides and partitions inside, with a landlord who also owns the shop downstairs. We schedule the commercial work around the business's hours and the residential work around the tenant's, and we keep the two quotes separate so each party pays for its own screen.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Work In When a Fort Greene Parlor Has Four Different Walls

These notes describe how our technicians approach a row-house parlor of the kind Fort Greene is full of. They are our standing practice, not a story about any one house.

Find the party wall first. Before the scanner comes out, the technician works out which wall is shared with the next house. In a row house it is the long side wall without windows, usually the one with the chimney breasts. That wall is almost certainly masonry under its plaster, so it changes the anchors, the drill and the price line before anything else is decided. Knowing which wall it is also tells us which wall has the neighbors' bedroom on the far side, which matters for drilling noise and, later, for where a soundbar should point.

Look for the services. Next comes everything that might be inside the walls: the bathroom above (cast iron pipes run down from it), the radiator risers, the old gas lighting pipes some of these houses still have, outlets in surface metal boxes that mean conduit, and the spot where the cable or fiber comes in. Apartment Therapy's piece on stud finders in old buildings, quoting the Brownstone Boys, makes the same point about cast iron pipes: check for a bathroom above before you trust any wall.

Scan, knock, then one pilot hole. A deep scanner helps on a framed wall and is nearly useless on plaster laid on brick or metal mesh. A Brownstoner poster put it well: "Knocking on plaster is a bit decieving as it is so hard." So the deciding test is a single small hole drilled where the bracket plate will cover it. White powder followed by wood means lath; white powder and then red grit means brick; a sudden drop means a void.

Tape, then sit. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen on the wall, and we ask you to sit where you actually watch. In a parlor with eleven- or twelve-foot ceilings, the eye wants to put the TV high; the neck wants it low. Moving tape costs nothing.

Drill once, clean as we go. Masonry drilling makes fine red dust. We hold a vacuum at the bit, put drop cloths on the floor and over the mantel, and do all the noisy work in one short stretch, after warning anyone in the house who is sleeping or working.

Wall by Wall

Plaster on Lath, Plaster on Brick, Tower Drywall: What Holds a TV in a Fort Greene Wall and What It Costs

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only, with the bracket sold separately. Very little in a nineteenth-century Fort Greene house is drywall on wood studs, which is why the wall add-ons are the first thing we talk about. Each one exists because that wall needs different tools, different fixings and more time, and each is listed in advance so the total is never a surprise.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). Strips of wood nailed across the studs, then two or three coats of plaster that can build up to an inch thick. It is strong when you press on it and weak when you pull on it. A screw into lath alone, or a plug in plaster alone, can feel tight on the day and slowly work loose over months. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into the stud behind it, with the pilot hole drilled slowly and without hammer action so the plaster does not crack outward. Where the bracket's holes do not line up with studs, we use fixings rated for hollow plaster walls and spread the load across more of them.

Plaster laid on brick (priced as brick). On party walls and many front walls, the plaster goes straight onto the masonry with no gap. Once the pilot hole tells us that, we price the wall by what holds the anchor, which is the brick. The Brownstoner thread on mounting over a fireplace describes the failure mode clearly: short anchors that only reached the plaster held "for a few weeks," while long anchors set into the brick held for about forty years. We use anchors long enough to pass the full plaster thickness and seat into sound brick.

Solid brick or stone (+$65). Exposed brick in a renovated garden floor, a stripped party wall or a stone accent wall. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor sits in the brick and not the mortar joint, which in a house of this age can be soft enough to scratch away with a key. We use a hammer drill, keep each hole clear of the edges of the brick, and on softer brick use more fixings carrying less load each.

Drywall on wood studs (base price). Partitions added when a row house was divided, renovated rooms, and some newer houses. The simplest wall, and the one where an in-wall power and cable kit goes in most easily.

Tower walls. Drywall on steel studs, drywall over concrete, and heavier assemblies between apartments are common in the buildings finished since 2005. Our published list does not set a separate line for every one of these, so we confirm the wall from your photos and the building's information and give you the total before booking, rather than guess on the day.

Hiding the power and cables without breaking the electrical code

After "put it on the wall," the most common request is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right way and a wrong way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and pull it out of another hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow that. As EC&M's explanation of Section 400.12 puts it, flexible cords "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings," may not be run through holes in walls, and may not stand in for the building's fixed wiring. A cord buried in a wall can be crushed by a fixing, can overheat where it is bundled, and is invisible to the next person who drills there.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit. It has a recessed inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives you a recessed outlet behind the TV, with a separate pass-through for HDMI and other low-voltage lines. On our list it is $169 including the kit. It needs a hollow framed cavity between the two openings, so it suits plaster on lath and drywall, and it does not suit plaster on brick, solid brick, block or concrete. On those walls the choice is a paintable cord cover at $59, which runs neatly from the screen to the outlet and can be painted the wall color, or a console placed so the cables drop straight behind it. Low-voltage cables such as HDMI and speaker wire are a different matter from power: they may run inside a wall, and the StreetEasy thread on prewar walls makes the same distinction.

In a landmarked house, none of this needs Landmarks approval as long as it stays inside. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's own permit page says an interior permit is only needed when the work requires a Buildings Department permit, when interior work affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark, and that ordinary repairs and maintenance need no permit at all. The Buildings Department lists minor alterations and ordinary repairs, including painting and plastering, as work that does not need a permit, and city law excludes low-voltage wiring under fifty volts, the category HDMI and network cable fall into, from electrical permit requirements. What we will not do is add a new electrical outlet or circuit; that is work for a licensed electrician, and the in-wall kit exists precisely so that a TV can have power behind it without one.

Permission First

Landlords, Boards, Tower Offices and Landmarks: Who Gets a Say in a Fort Greene TV Wall

Fort Greene has every kind of ownership a TV installer meets in New York: owner-occupied houses, owners renting out floors, small condos carved from brownstones, co-ops, managed rental towers, condo towers and public housing. The permission question changes with each one. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is what we have learned from the public guidance and from doing the work, so you can ask the right person before booking.

If you rent, start with the lease. Brick Underground put this question to a New York real estate attorney in its "Realty Bites" column, and the answer began: "You should check the alteration provision in your lease first." The same column quoted a property manager who said a nail-sized hole is generally fine but a larger hole "is considered damage," and that a tenant who takes down a mounted TV and leaves significant damage must fix it or pay for it. A TV bracket leaves four to six holes larger than a nail hole, and an in-wall kit leaves two openings. All of it can be patched, but it is the kind of thing worth agreeing in writing. A landlord who knows the work is by a licensed, insured installer, and exactly what will be left behind, is much more likely to say yes.

If the owner lives downstairs. In a divided Fort Greene brownstone, the owner on the parlor floor may hear the drill and may well be the person whose answer counts. A short text before the visit, naming the wall, the size of the holes and where the wires will go, avoids most problems. If you are the owner setting up a rental, one well-placed bracket and a proper in-wall kit, installed between tenants, saves the wall from a new set of holes every lease.

If you own in a co-op or condo. Boards and managing agents often treat hanging a bracket as minor work and opening a wall as an alteration. Brick Underground's guide to alteration agreements notes that they protect building-wide structures, can require contractor liability insurance, and "may also dictate the days and hours in which contractors can work in your apartment." Send us the requirements and we send the certificate of insurance and a written description of the work.

If you rent in a tower. Management companies in the newer buildings usually publish contractor rules: a certificate of insurance naming the owner and agent, a service elevator reservation, work during weekday hours, and sometimes a ban on drilling certain walls. We ask for those rules when you book, and if a wall is off-limits, we work out the best TV position on the walls that are allowed, or set the screen on its stand.

If you live in the Whitman or Ingersoll Houses. Wall alterations in NYCHA apartments go through the development's management office. We ask you to confirm what the office allows before we book a wall mount, and we can set the TV on its stand in the meantime.

If your house is landmarked. A bracket and an in-wall kit on an inside wall are not Landmarks matters. The outside is different. The Commission's rules for equipment on buildings in historic districts call for "no visible conduit or, if this is not feasible, a maximum of approximately ten (10) inches of visible conduit," kept off decorative surfaces, and its guidance on rooftop equipment asks that it be non-visible or, at most, minimally visible. So a new cable line run up the facade, an antenna or a dish needs thought, and often a call to the Commission, before it goes up. The FCC's guide to antennas and dishes says its federal rule covers dishes up to one meter in areas you have exclusive use of, such as a terrace or balcony, not common areas like the roof or outside walls of an apartment building, and that restrictions are allowed where a historic site is involved.

Lead paint in old houses. HPD's safe work practice rules apply in housing built before 1978 when work will disturb two square feet or more of paint in a room, and since August 9, 2025, pre-1960 units must be tested by an EPA-certified inspector under the city's lead law. A bracket's pilot hole and anchors disturb a tiny fraction of that, but we still treat old paint carefully: dust captured at the bit, drop cloths down, and a vacuum before we leave. If a family has young children and the paint has never been tested, it is worth asking the owner what the lead records show before any larger opening is cut.

Your Questions Answered

These are the situations particular to Fort Greene's mix of landmark houses, converted towers, new high-rises and busy commercial streets. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Landmark designations, now and possibly later

Our block is inside the area people have proposed adding to the Fort Greene Historic District. If the extension is approved, would a TV and cord cover we put in now have to come out?

No. The Historic Districts Council's material on the proposed extension describes adding stretches such as Willoughby Avenue between Carlton and Clermont and part of Fulton Street, and a designation of that kind governs future work on the outside of buildings. A bracket, an in-wall kit or a cord cover inside a room is not exterior work and does not need a Landmarks permit even in an existing district, so the same is true after an extension. What could change is anything on the facade or the roof: a cable run up the front of the house, a dish or an antenna would be reviewed under the district's rules once designated. If you are planning one of those, it is worth doing the interior work first and checking with the Commission before anything goes on the outside.

Some of the interiors at One Hanson Place are designated landmarks. Does that designation reach inside our condo apartment where we want the TV?

The designated interiors at the former Williamsburgh Savings Bank are the building's public spaces from its banking days, which the Commission designated in 1996, not the private apartments created in the 2005 to 2007 conversion. The Commission's own guidance says an interior permit is only needed when the interior itself is a designated interior landmark, when the work needs a Buildings Department permit, or when it affects the exterior. A TV on a wall inside your unit is none of those. Your condo board and managing agent are the ones whose rules apply, so the paperwork you need is theirs: usually a certificate of insurance and an agreed working time. Send us their contractor requirements and we take care of the rest.

Inherited wiring and equipment

The previous owners of our renovated townhouse left an HDMI wall plate behind the TV spot that runs to an equipment closet two floors down. Will our new 4K TV actually work over it?

Maybe, and the only way to know is to test it. HDMI over long in-wall runs depends on the cable's grade and length, on whether there is an extender or a balun at either end, and on whether anything in the closet still powers it. A run installed for an older 1080p system may pass a picture but drop out or refuse higher resolutions and HDR. During the visit we connect a source to the closet end, check what arrives at the wall plate at your TV's full resolution, and tell you what works. If the old run is not good enough, the usual answer is to move the streaming device to the TV and use the closet line only for network, or to replace the HDMI path with a newer extender pair. Our setup-only visit at $195 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks, covers that troubleshooting if it is not part of a mount.

Our house came with a whole-house control system from the last owner's AV company, and nobody has the passwords. Can a new TV still be installed and used?

Yes. A TV does not need the house's control system to work. We can mount and connect the new screen so it runs on its own remote with its own streaming device, independent of whatever is in the equipment closet, which is often the most practical first step. Re-integrating the TV into an existing control platform is a separate job that usually needs the original integrator or a dealer for that brand to recover the programming, and we will tell you honestly if that is what your system needs. What we will not do is unplug or rewire someone else's control equipment without knowing what else in the house depends on it, because lighting, shades and audio in other rooms may run through the same system.

Tower rules and tower walls

Our building's house rules say residents may not drill into concrete walls at all. Where can the TV go?

A rule like that is common in newer towers and it is enforceable through your lease or condo rules, so we work inside it rather than around it. Most apartments still have at least one framed partition, such as the wall between the living room and a bedroom, and a bracket can go there with fixings chosen for the stud type; we confirm which walls are framed from your floor plan, a scan and a pilot hole the plate will cover. If the only wall that faces the sofa is concrete, the answer is the TV on its own stand ($119.99) or a floor-standing TV mount that needs no wall at all. We will not drill a wall the building has told you is off-limits, even with your permission, because the building's permission is the one that counts.

The window wall in our high-floor DeKalb Avenue apartment faces the park, and the only solid wall faces the windows. Won't the reflections ruin the picture?

They can, especially with a glossy screen and afternoon light off the park. Before we drill, we hold a phone flashlight or tape a sheet of paper where the screen will be and check from your seat at the time you usually watch, which shows where the bright reflections land. A tilting bracket helps when the reflections come from above; when they come straight across from a window, the fix is usually lateral, moving the screen a couple of feet along the wall, or a fixed bracket a little lower with the window shades doing the rest. Matte-finish screens help, and so does picking the screen before the bracket if you have not bought it yet. We would rather spend ten minutes on this with tape than put the bracket in the one spot that catches the sun.

Businesses, screens and the hours they keep

Our restaurant on DeKalb only wants a TV for weekend matches and doesn't want a screen staring at diners the rest of the week. Can it be hidden when it's off?

Yes, in a few ways, and the right one depends on the wall and your budget. The simplest is a fixed bracket on a wall that already has a cabinet or shelving built around it, so a pair of doors or a curtain can close in front of the screen. Another is a full-motion arm that folds the screen flat into an alcove facing the wall when not in use, which works well beside a bar back. A screen that shows art or a static image when it is not showing a game is a third choice, though it is still visibly a screen. Whatever you pick, we keep the power and cables hidden so the wall looks finished with the TV off, and we schedule the install before you open.

Our dance company rehearses in a studio near Lafayette Avenue and wants a screen dancers can see from across the room for video playback, then roll out of the way for performances. Is that a wall job?

It can be either, and the choice turns on the room. A studio with mirrored walls and a sprung floor rarely has a good wall: mirrors cannot take a bracket, and the remaining walls may be booked for barres or sound treatment. In that case a screen on a heavy rolling stand, set up and connected so it only needs plugging in, gives you the playback without any holes. Where there is a solid end wall the dancers face, a high fixed mount with the cables in a cover works well, and the screen size follows from how far back the dancers stand. We look at photos of the room with the mirrors and barres in frame before suggesting either, and we schedule around your rehearsal calendar.

Privacy and the new TV

We have heard that smart TVs record what you watch for advertisers. Can you turn that off when you set ours up?

Most smart TVs ship with a viewing-data feature, often called automatic content recognition, switched on, along with advertising and voice-recognition settings. During setup we walk through the privacy menus with you and switch off the ones you do not want, and we show you where they live so you can check them after a software update. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Fort Greene

These are claims that turn up in generic online answers, AI summaries and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, paraphrased, each followed by what Fort Greene's buildings actually show.

"Use a stud finder, mark two studs and screw the bracket in"

Almost every generic guide starts here. Find the studs, drive lag bolts into two of them, and the TV is safe.

In a Fort Greene row house the strongest wall in the parlor usually has no studs at all. It is the party wall, plaster laid straight onto brick, and a stud finder either reads nothing or reads everything. The Brownstone Boys, quoted by Apartment Therapy, put it bluntly: on a shared townhouse wall "your stud finder will only find a solid brick wall!" The street wall between the windows is often worse, because its construction varies.

What actually works is one pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it, read by the dust and the feel of the bit. Then the fixing matches the wall: through plaster and lath into framing, or long masonry anchors seated in brick. The stud finder is a helper, not the decision.

"Mounting a TV costs about the same everywhere"

National cost pages give a single average or range, and AI answers repeat it as if it applied to any wall in any city.

Those figures blend new drywall houses with everything else. A national average cannot tell you whether your wall is plaster on lath, plaster on brick or a tower partition over steel, and those three take different tools, time and anchors. TaskRabbit's own cost guide concedes that fireplace mounting costs more because of "heat, height, wall material."

That is why our Brooklyn list prices the wall rather than averaging it: $179.99 for the base, +$45 for plaster, +$65 for brick or stone, +$130 above a fireplace, each known before you book. A Fort Greene parlor job is priced by what is behind its paint, not by a national number.

"Just run the power cord through the wall"

Plenty of how-to videos and some AI summaries suggest cutting a hole behind the TV and one near the outlet and feeding the TV's cord down inside.

The National Electrical Code forbids it. Section 400.12 says flexible cords may not be concealed by walls, floors or ceilings or run through holes in walls. In an old Fort Greene house, where the wall may already hide old gas pipes, radiator risers and decades of wiring changes, a buried cord is exactly the kind of hidden hazard nobody finds until something goes wrong.

The legal routes are an in-wall power kit with its own rated cable on a framed wall, or a paintable cord cover on masonry. HDMI and network cable are low-voltage and may go inside a wall; the power cord may not.

"Over the fireplace is the natural place for a TV"

AI room-planning answers often treat the space above a mantel as the obvious focal point for a screen.

In a Fort Greene parlor the mantel shelf usually sits at chest height under a ceiling of eleven or twelve feet, so a screen above it lands far above seated eye level. A Brownstoner poster warned that mounting over a mantel "invites a sore neck," and another that heat from a working fireplace "won't be good for the tv." The chimney breast is also usually on the party wall, so the fixing is a masonry job.

It can still be the right answer, especially over a fireplace that is no longer used, and our list prices it as the above-fireplace add-on. But we tape the position and have you sit down first, and we often suggest the hall-side wall instead.

"Landmarked houses can't have TVs mounted"

Owners in historic districts sometimes hear, from AI answers or from neighbors, that any change to a landmarked house needs Commission approval.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's own permit page says otherwise for interiors: a permit is needed only when interior work needs a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or is in a designated interior landmark. Hanging a bracket and hiding cables inside a room in the Fort Greene or BAM Historic District is none of those.

The outside is where the rules bite. A new cable on the facade, a dish or an antenna is reviewed, and the Commission's rules allow only about ten inches of visible conduit. We keep the work inside, and we tell you before anything would need to touch the exterior.

"New buildings are easy: it's all drywall"

Generic advice tends to assume that a new apartment means ordinary drywall on wood studs, the easiest wall to mount on.

Fort Greene's towers finished since 2005 hold roughly four in ten of its apartments, and their walls are rarely that simple. Partitions may be on steel studs that strip a lag screw, demising walls may be acoustic or fire-rated assemblies, and some walls are drywall fixed over concrete with reinforcing steel inside. Many buildings also restrict which walls can be drilled at all.

So in a tower we ask for the floor plan and the building's contractor rules before booking, confirm the wall with a scan and a pilot hole, and choose fixings for what is actually there. Smooth paint tells you nothing about what holds the screen.

"A renter can mount a TV without asking"

Some online answers suggest that small holes are normal wear and that tenants rarely need permission for a TV bracket.

Brick Underground's attorney source said the first step is the alteration provision in the lease, and its property-manager source said holes larger than a nail are treated as damage that a departing tenant must repair. In a divided Fort Greene brownstone the owner may live one floor down and hear the drill; in a tower the building office may have its own rules on top of the lease.

Asking takes a minute. We suggest a short written note naming the wall, the hole sizes and the cable route, and if the answer is no, a TV on its stand at $119.99 gives you the same tidy setup with nothing to patch when you leave.

Fort Greene TV Installation FAQ

What does it come to if an old TV has to come down before a new 55-inch screen goes over the mantel on our party wall?
+

On our Brooklyn list that is $179.99 for the mount, $130 for above the fireplace, $65 for the brick behind the plaster and $85 to take the old TV down, so $459.99 in labor. The bracket is extra unless you already own a rated one, and a cord cover or soundbar is added at its own line.

We rent our garden apartment to short-stay guests. Can the TV be set up so guests use their own streaming accounts without seeing ours?
+

Yes. During setup we can leave your own accounts off the TV, set up a separate profile or a guest-friendly streaming device, and show you how guests sign in and how you sign them out between stays. If this is all you need on a TV that is already mounted, it is a setup-only visit at $195 for the first hour.

Our son plays fast games on a new console. Will you set the TV up for gaming, not just for movies?
+

Yes. Many TVs have only one or two inputs that support the console's highest frame rates, and a game mode that cuts input lag. We connect the console to the right input, switch on the correct mode and check the picture and sound with the console running before we leave.

Our home music room has acoustic panels glued across the wall where the TV should go. Do they have to come off?
+

Usually only where the bracket plate sits. Acoustic panels cannot carry any weight, so the bracket must be fixed to the wall behind them, which means removing or cutting a panel at the plate and fitting it back around the bracket. We look at photos first, because some panels come off cleanly and others take paint with them.

Our interior designer has drawn exactly where the TV and soundbar should go. Will your technician follow the drawing?
+

Yes, as long as the wall allows it. Send the drawing with your request. If the pilot hole or the scan shows a pipe, a flue or a void right where the drawing puts a fixing, we stop, tell you and the designer what we found, and agree the nearest safe position before drilling again.

If the old in-wall HDMI run in our house fails your test, what are we paying for?
+

Only the time spent testing and setting up the alternative. On a mount visit the testing is part of the setup; as a stand-alone job it is billed as a setup-only visit at $195 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks. We tell you what failed and what the working alternative is before you decide.

When we leave our rental, can the in-wall power kit come out and go with us to the next apartment?
+

Usually not in a way worth doing. Once a kit is removed, its two wall openings need patching and painting, and the cable inside is cut to that wall's height. Most people leave it in place with the landlord's agreement or patch the openings, and fit a new kit in the next home at its own line.

What does a parlor TV on the plaster wall by the stair hall, with the wires inside the wall and a soundbar under it, add up to?
+

For a screen up to 55 inches it is $179.99 plus $45 for plaster, $169 for the in-wall power and cable kit and $129 for the wall-mounted soundbar, or $522.99 in labor. The bracket and the soundbar bracket are extra unless you supply them.

The new TV in our bedroom has a bright standby light and a glowing soundbar display. Can those be turned off?
+

Often, yes. Many TVs and soundbars have settings to turn off or dim the standby light and front display, and we look for them during setup. Where a light cannot be switched off, a small dimming sticker over it is the simple fix, and we can show you which ones are safe to cover.

Do we get the total in writing before the visit is booked?
+

Yes. Once we have your photos and details, you get the lines that apply and the total in writing before you book. It changes only if the pilot hole shows a different wall from the one we priced, and then the technician tells you the difference before going any further.
