The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Start with the fires. On the morning of December 21, 2023, a fire broke out on the second floor of a multi-family building on Ninth Street between Second and Third Avenues, climbed to the third floor and spread into the building next door. ABC7 reported that about 140 firefighters responded and that the residents of several apartments got out on their own. The FDNY said the building where it started was more than 100 years old and a wooden structure, which may have helped fuel the flames, and the Red Cross relocated 11 adults from ten apartments. Fifteen months later, on the night of March 5, 2025, a fire started on the second floor of a three-story home a block east, on Ninth Street between Third and Fourth Avenues. News 12 Brooklyn reported two people with minor injuries, an hour to bring it under control, and a full vacate order from the Department of Buildings. A neighbor, Liesa Witt, told the station: "It's horrible how fast a fire can break out." Neither report says anything about the alarms in those buildings, and we will not guess. What they do show is the building type: old, attached, partly wooden, with bedrooms stacked above the floor where the fire began.

Then look at the new buildings. The City Council approved the Gowanus rezoning in November 2021, covering 82 blocks and opening the way for about 8,500 new apartments, roughly 3,000 of them affordable, with towers allowed up to 30 stories near the canal and 17 on Fourth Avenue, as 6sqft reported at the time. Lightstone's two buildings at 363 and 365 Bond Street, about 700 apartments over a 193-space garage, were built without a cellar because the site floods. Newer buildings around Gowanus Wharf, Union Channel and 251 Douglass Street have added hundreds more homes. Every one of them carries a fire alarm system designed by an engineer and accepted by the FDNY. But the city's Building Code describes the apartment-building version of that system in a way that surprises most residents: in a Group R-2 building, it is a fire alarm system "without alarm notification appliances," and the code says the actuation of its smoke detectors "shall not initiate a signal to alarm notification appliances." Those detectors sit in electrical rooms, mechanical rooms, elevator machine rooms and elevator lobbies, and they report to a central station or a constantly attended location. The alarm that wakes a family is the smoke alarm inside the apartment. We explain the difference further down, because it decides what an owner, a manager and a tenant each have to look after.

Then the working buildings. The Gowanus Industrial Business Zone, which NYC Planning's 2021 vision plan described as nearly 300 businesses and about 4,000 private jobs, still runs south from around Third Street between Hamilton Avenue and Third Avenue to the canal. Its buildings hold auto body shops, big-box stores, construction yards, artist studios and makers, many of them in factories built long before anyone thought about smoke detection. One of those former factories, at 130 Third Street beside the canal, became a 400-bed shelter in April 2024. THE CITY reported that its fire alarm was "not currently up to fire code" when it opened, that the temporary certificate of occupancy had leaned on an FDNY alarm inspection from August 2022, "when the building was a mostly empty factory," and that the FDNY allowed the opening only because the operator agreed to a round-the-clock fire guard while the system was fixed. That is the clearest local lesson we know about converted buildings: an alarm approved for one use is not automatically right for the next one.

And then the public housing. Gowanus Houses, home to more than 2,600 residents, is waiting on its own upgrade: in May 2023 Representative Dan Goldman asked for $1,500,000 for NYCHA to replace fire detection components there "to bring the residences up to New York City Fire Code." That work belongs to NYCHA and its contractors, and residents of Gowanus Houses and Wyckoff Gardens should call NYCHA about their apartments.

Fire Alarm Installation for Gowanus's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost all the fire alarm work in Gowanus. What sets them apart is less brick versus frame than three other things: whether people sleep there, how many floors sit above the street door, and whether an engineer's filed system is already in place.

Wood-frame and brick walk-ups

Three- and four-story buildings on the side streets, many over a century old, with an apartment or two per floor and a cellar below.

Row houses and two-families

Single-family and owner-plus-tenant houses on the blocks near Fourth Avenue and the Park Slope edge, attached on both sides.

Rezoning-era apartment buildings

Mid-rise and high-rise rentals and condos by the canal and on Fourth Avenue, with engineered systems, monitoring and, in some cases, no cellar.

Converted factories and studio buildings

Masonry shells with timber or steel inside, split into artist studios, workshops and offices, where nobody is supposed to sleep.

One- and two-story industrial buildings

Block and steel buildings in the industrial zone toward Hamilton Avenue holding body shops, trades, storage and vehicles.

Venues and mixed-use buildings

Converted warehouses used as music halls, and avenue buildings with a bar or shop below and apartments above.

Wood-frame and brick walk-ups: the fire starts low and the bedrooms are above it

Both of the Ninth Street fires we described began on a second floor. That is typical of how walk-ups and attached houses burn: the fire starts where people cook, heat, charge things or store things, and the people who need the most warning are on the floor above, asleep with a door between them and the stair. In a building of this kind, the city's rules are clear about what the owner provides. HPD requires smoke detectors in every multiple dwelling and carbon monoxide detectors in multiple dwellings with fuel-burning equipment, each within fifteen feet of the main entrance to every room used for sleeping, and natural gas detectors in buildings with gas piping starting January 1, 2027. Battery-powered smoke alarms installed in existing dwellings have to be the sealed, ten-year type that meets UL 217, under the Department of Buildings' bulletin on the subject.

What the rules do not spell out is how to make an alarm in one apartment matter to the people in the next. In a three-story building with one apartment per floor, a sealed alarm in the second-floor hall does its job for that family and nobody else. Linking alarms across apartments in a multiple dwelling is a design question, not a quick add-on, and in a building above certain sizes the code expects a building system instead. So we start by counting: how many apartments, how many floors above the street door, whether the cellar has a boiler and a meter bank, and whether a store sits on the ground floor. From that count we tell the owner which of three things the building needs: apartment alarms only, apartment alarms plus detection in the cellar and common spaces, or a design professional and a filed system.

The physical work in these buildings is mostly about old plaster, old lath and old wiring. Sealed alarms need no cable at all, which is why they suit tenant-occupied walk-ups where opening ceilings means moving families out. Where an owner wants hardwired alarms during a renovation, the Department of Buildings' affidavit for smoke and CO alarm installation and testing calls for a licensed electrician, registered architect or professional engineer on hardwired work, so we coordinate with the electrician rather than work around them. In cellars we look first at the boiler, the gas meters and anything stored near them, because a fire or a CO leak that begins there reaches the stair long before it reaches a bedroom.

Row houses and two-families: one family's stair is the other family's way out

Gowanus row houses sit mostly on the blocks closest to Fourth Avenue and Park Slope, and a good share of them are owner-occupied with a rental floor. That matters for the rules. HPD's detector page treats a one- or two-family house differently depending on whether the owner lives there: carbon monoxide detectors are required in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy where there is fuel-burning equipment, and the gas detector requirement that starts in 2027 reaches the same kinds of homes where there is gas piping. Reporting on the 2027 date described the gas rule as covering residential buildings other than owner-occupied single-family homes. A tenant in a two-family can be reimbursed $25 per CO or gas detector the owner installs.

The design question in a row house is simple to state: when a fire starts on one floor, will everyone in the house hear it in time? A single alarm per floor with nothing linking them leaves a top-floor bedroom relying on the sound carrying up two flights with doors closed. Linked alarms sound together, so the alarm in the garden-floor kitchen wakes the family on the top floor. In a two-family, linking across both households is the strongest arrangement, and we discuss it with the owner and the tenant before we install it, because both will hear every false alarm too. Our complete home protection price of $2,500 covers a house from the cellar to the top floor.

Rezoning-era apartment buildings: the building's detectors are not the ones that wake you

This is the building type where we spend the most time explaining rather than installing. A new rental or condo near the canal with more than sixteen apartments, or with apartments three or more stories above the lowest exit, has the fire alarm system that Building Code section 907.2.9 describes for Group R-2 buildings: smoke detectors in each mechanical, electrical, transformer and telephone equipment room, in air distribution systems, in elevator machine rooms and in elevator lobbies, reporting to a central supervising station or a constantly attended location, and not wired to sound horns in the corridors. Taller buildings add more. The city's rules call for one-way voice communication in buildings over 125 feet, and the rezoning allows towers well past that height by the canal.

Inside each apartment are the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, usually hardwired with a backup battery, and those are what actually wake the people inside. They are not the same equipment as the building system, and the FDNY warns that detectors connected to a building fire alarm system are not stand-alone units and that turning them into single-station alarms can lead to enforcement. So when a manager asks us to replace a unit's alarms, the first thing we confirm is which devices on that ceiling belong to the apartment and which belong to the building. We work on the apartment alarms. Changes to the building system go through the engineer of record and the company that services it.

Where new buildings do need us is in the gaps. Units change hands, renovations move bedrooms, tenants pull alarms down while cooking and forget to put them back, and a hardwired unit alarm that fails on a Sunday still needs replacing that week. Managers of buildings with hundreds of apartments also need a dated list of every unit alarm, so that when HPD or an insurer asks, the answer is a document and not a guess.

Converted factories and studio buildings: timber floors, many tenants, one panel

The factories along Second, Third and Fourth Avenues and on the streets running down to the canal were built for manufacturing, and many of them now hold artist studios, woodshops, ceramics rooms, photo stages and small offices. Hyperallergic, covering the 2022 Gowanus Open Studios, described studios in former factories on all three avenues and a ceramics studio, Third Avenue Clay, in a former ironworks. The structure is usually masonry outside and timber or steel inside, and the FDNY's description of the Red Hook warehouse that burned in 2025 explains why that matters: once heavy timber members start burning, in Assistant Chief Woods's words, "the fire takes over rather quickly."

Detection in these buildings has three jobs. It has to reach the places fire would start unseen, such as top floors, roof spaces, shafts, storage rooms and the studios that are locked most of the week. It has to ignore the ordinary smoke, dust and heat of the work, which means choosing heat detection for kiln rooms and woodshops and keeping smoke detection for corridors, stairs and quiet rooms. And it has to tell someone when the building is empty, which in a studio building is most nights. That usually means a monitored panel owned by the landlord, with each studio's devices labeled to the panel so a signal at 3 a.m. names a room.

The hardest part is the sleeping question. A studio building is commercial, and the shelter at 130 Third Street showed what happens when a factory's alarm is asked to protect people who sleep there: the FDNY was not confident the existing system met the criteria for that use, and a round-the-clock fire guard filled the gap. If anyone sleeps in your building, tell us at the start. It changes the rules the building answers to, and it is not something an installer can fix with a few extra detectors.

One- and two-story industrial buildings: long rooms, big doors, nobody at night

South of the rezoned blocks, the industrial zone holds the buildings Gowanus was built around: body shops, contractors' yards, supply houses, storage and fleet garages, in block, brick and steel. Detection here is about the work and the hours. A body shop's paint and solvents, a fleet garage's exhaust and a welder's sparks all call for heat detection and carbon monoxide detection placed for the room, not smoke detectors that would sound every morning. Most of these buildings are empty from evening to morning, so the alarm is only as good as the signal that leaves the building: we connect panels to central station monitoring through our partners and set up the call list with the owner before the system goes live.

The cable work is different too. Open block walls and steel roofs mean exposed runs that get hit by ladders and forklifts, so we run alarm cable in conduit where it can be damaged and keep it clear of tenants' power and data cable.

Venues and mixed-use buildings: crowds at night, families above the bar

Gowanus has well-known rooms for music and comedy. The Bell House on Seventh Street occupies a converted 1920s warehouse with a main room under 25-foot wooden arched ceilings, and Littlefield at 635 Sackett Street has run as an independent venue since 2009. Rooms like these are assembly spaces, and their fire alarm work follows approved drawings and FDNY rules, with notification that has to be seen and heard over a band, a crowd and haze. We install and test to the design professional's drawings. We do not design or file those systems ourselves.

The more common mixed-use building is smaller: a bar, café or shop on the ground floor of a three- or four-story building on Third or Fourth Avenue, with apartments upstairs. The business closes at night and the families above go to sleep over a room with cooking equipment, stock and nobody watching. We give the apartments their own alarms under HPD's rules, put detection in the store and the cellar that suits the business, and, where the owner agrees, arrange for a fire in the closed store to be heard upstairs as well as reported to a central station.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Sleeps There, Then Ask When the Alarm Was Last Checked Against the Building

In Gowanus the two questions that change a job most are simple ones, and we ask them on the first call. The first is who sleeps in the building tonight. A studio building where nobody sleeps, a walk-up with a family on every floor and a factory that now houses people are three different jobs even when the floor plans look alike, and the answer is not always on the certificate of occupancy. The second is when the alarm was last checked against the building as it is used today, not as it was used when the system went in. A panel accepted when a building was a warehouse, a unit alarm placed for a bedroom that has since been moved, and a cellar detector installed before a tenant started charging tools down there all pass a button test and still miss the point.

So before we price anything we ask for three things: the certificate of occupancy or whatever the owner has instead, the date and use the building had at its last FDNY inspection if it has a filed system, and a walk of every floor with someone who knows where people actually sleep, cook and work. That walk is where most real findings come from, and we never skip it.

New Buildings by the Canal

What the Code Puts in a New Gowanus Apartment Building, and What It Leaves to the Owner and the Tenant

The rezoning is turning Gowanus into a neighborhood of large new apartment buildings, and the fire protection in those buildings is mostly designed before anyone moves in. That is a good thing. It also means owners, managers, condo boards and renters inherit a system they did not choose and often do not understand. Here is how the pieces fit, in the order a resident usually runs into them.

The apartment alarms. Every unit has smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms placed by the rules for sleeping rooms, usually hardwired with a battery backup in new construction. They sound inside the apartment only, unless the design links them to something else. When one chirps, fails or gets painted over, it is the owner's equipment to fix under HPD's rules, and in a rental the tenant's job is to test it monthly, replace batteries where the model has replaceable ones and never cover it. These are the devices we replace and relocate most often in new buildings.

The building detectors. Section 907.2.9 of the city's Building Code puts smoke detectors in the rooms where a fire could start out of sight of residents: mechanical rooms, electrical and transformer rooms, telephone equipment rooms, elevator machine rooms and elevator lobbies, plus the air distribution system. When one of them trips, the code says it signals a central supervising station or a constantly attended location, such as a 24-hour lobby desk where the building has one. Union Channel at Gowanus Wharf, for example, was described in coverage of its refinancing as having a 24-hour attended lobby. The code also says those detectors must not set off horns in the halls, so a trip in the electrical room can happen with no sound on the residential floors at all.

The extras on taller buildings. Towers above 125 feet need one-way voice communication, and larger buildings add sprinklers, elevator recall and fire command equipment, all designed by the engineer of record and serviced under the building's own arrangement.

The flood line. Gowanus sits low beside a canal that overflowed into streets and basements during Sandy. Several new buildings were designed without cellars for that reason, and the city's electrical code requires panels, disconnects and breakers at or above the design flood elevation in flood hazard areas. For fire alarm work that means control panels, power supplies and batteries belong above that line, and the engineer's drawings usually say where. When a manager asks us to add a power supply or move a panel, we check that elevation first.

The move-in months. Large buildings often open in stages, with a temporary certificate of occupancy covering some floors while work continues on others. The Department of Buildings told THE CITY in 2024 that it can issue a temporary certificate when temporary safety measures are in place, and that the city's codes allow occupancy with fire guards while a fire alarm is being serviced. In practice, the occupied floors need working apartment alarms from the first night, the building system serving those floors needs to be in service and monitored, and construction dust and hot work upstairs need to be kept from setting off, or being hidden from, the detectors below. That coordination belongs to the general contractor and the engineer, but the unit alarms are often the last thing installed and the first thing to go wrong, and that is where we come in.

What Local Cases Show

Ninth Street, Nevins Street and Third Street: What Gowanus Fires and Alarm Cases Teach an Owner

None of the reports below says a detector failed or worked, and we are careful not to read that into them. What they show, taken together, is the shape of fire risk in Gowanus: old attached buildings where fire spreads sideways and upward, small fires found by smell before anything else, and converted buildings whose alarms were approved for a use they no longer have.

Ninth Street, December 2023: a wooden building and the one next to it

The fire between Second and Third Avenues started on a second floor, reached the third and then the neighboring building, ABC7 and News 12 reported. The FDNY's comment that the original building was more than a hundred years old and wooden is the part an owner should take away. In a frame building, the time between a fire starting and the stair becoming impassable is short, so the alarm on the floor above the fire, and the link between floors, matter more than anything else on the ceiling. Vicenta Martinez, who had lived in one of the buildings for twenty years, came back the next day to salvage what she could, News 12 reported. Detection cannot save a wooden building, but it can give the people inside the minutes they need to leave it.

Ninth Street, March 2025: a three-story home, a vacate order and three families

A block east, between Third and Fourth Avenues, a fire started on the second floor of a three-story home at about 10:30 at night. Firefighters worked from ladders to keep it from spreading, two people had minor injuries, and the Department of Buildings ordered the home vacated, News 12 reported. Three families needed emergency help from the Red Cross. A late-evening fire in a three-story home is exactly the case for linked alarms: some people are still awake on one floor while others are asleep on another, and the sound has to reach both.

Nevins Street, July 2026: smoke at 4:30 in the morning

Citizen's log of FDNY radio traffic records a 911 report of smoke at 325 Nevins Street near President Street at 4:33 a.m. on July 24, 2026, and a small fire on the first floor, brought under control in about an hour with no injuries reported. The dispatcher's description of the buildings on either side as three-story multiple dwellings is a fair picture of the blocks around it. A first-floor fire before dawn, discovered as smoke, is the kind of event where an alarm in the cellar and on the first floor, heard on the floors above, buys the most time.

Third Street, April 2024: an old inspection, a new use

Compliance notes for Gowanus businesses and mixed-use buildings

Who designs, who files, who installs. A building fire alarm system in New York City starts with plans approved by a New York State registered design professional, a professional engineer or registered architect, and an FDNY application that carries a $585 fee, according to the city's business portal. The FDNY's guide to approval says the process from plan submission to final approval runs about twenty to twenty-four weeks, and that once plans are approved the installation must be done by a New York City licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate. At inspection, the as-built riser diagram must carry a Functionality Statement signed by a professional engineer, registered architect, licensed electrician or authorized installer licensed by the state. Ask any company, us included, to show in writing which of those roles it fills on your job.

Repairs versus changes. Replacing a detector, a pull station or a module with an identical part is a repair. Moving or replacing a control panel, adding devices to a filed system or changing how it works generally means new plans. We tell you which side of that line a job falls on before we start, and when it is a change, the design professional comes first.

Professional certification. Since May 1, 2023, the FDNY has let licensed professionals request professional certification of certain fire alarm designs and installations instead of FDNY plan examination and inspection, through the FDNY Business portal. Whether a particular fit-out qualifies is the design professional's call.

Monitoring before inspection. The FDNY guide asks for the central station connection to be active before the inspection is scheduled. Shops and studios that leave monitoring to the last week often lose their inspection date. We set up monitoring through our partners early so it is never the item holding up a filing.

Wireless systems. Wireless building fire alarm systems in New York City need FDNY approval before installation and cannot use unlicensed radio frequencies. Household sealed alarms that link wirelessly are a different product and are not part of a filed system.

Landmarks. The Coignet Building at Third Avenue and Third Street is an individual city landmark. Interior fire alarm work in most Gowanus buildings does not involve the Landmarks Preservation Commission, but anything on a landmark's exterior, such as a strobe or a monitoring antenna, should be raised with the Commission before it goes up.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Gowanus: Questions Answered

New buildings from the rezoning

Residents are moving into the lower floors of our new building near the canal while crews are still finishing the floors above. How is the fire alarm handled in a building that is half home and half job site?

In layers, and with one person in charge of each layer. The floors people live on need working apartment smoke and CO alarms from the first night, and the building system serving those floors needs to be in service and reporting to its monitoring point. The Department of Buildings told THE CITY in 2024 that it can issue a temporary certificate of occupancy when temporary safety measures are in place, and that the codes allow occupancy with fire guards while a fire alarm is being serviced, so a staged opening is normal. What is not normal is leaving the coordination to chance.

The general contractor and the engineer of record decide how the unfinished floors are separated from the occupied ones and what the building system does on each. Our part is usually the apartments: installing or replacing unit alarms as each floor is released, testing them with the doors closed, and keeping a dated list floor by floor so the manager can show which units were protected on which date. We also flag the two problems we see most in staged buildings: construction dust drifting down a stair and setting off alarms in occupied units, and covers left on detectors after painting. Both are easy to prevent if someone walks the occupied floors at the end of every work week, and both are serious if nobody does.

A smoke detector in our new building's electrical room went off last week, and nothing sounded in the halls. Was the system broken?

Probably not. In a Group R-2 apartment building with more than sixteen units, or with apartments three or more stories above the lowest exit, Building Code section 907.2.9 calls for exactly that arrangement. The detectors in electrical, mechanical, transformer and telephone rooms, in elevator machine rooms and elevator lobbies and in the air distribution system report to a central supervising station or a constantly attended location, and the code says their actuation "shall not initiate a signal to alarm notification appliances." In plain terms, the building is told and the residents are not, because a detector in a room nobody occupies is meant to bring staff and the fire department, not to empty forty floors.

What you should check is that the signal actually arrived somewhere. Ask the manager whether the central station or the desk logged it, who was called, and how long it took. If nobody can answer, that is the real problem, and it belongs to the company that services the building system. It is also a good moment to make sure your own apartment's alarms work, because they are the ones designed to wake you.

Former factories, studios and the industrial zone

Our former factory near the canal passed an FDNY fire alarm inspection a few years ago, when it was mostly empty. People will soon be living in it. Does that inspection still count?

Not for the new use, and Gowanus has a public example of why. When 130 Third Street opened as a 400-bed shelter in April 2024, THE CITY reported that the temporary certificate of occupancy relied on an FDNY inspection of the alarm from August 2022, when the building was a mostly empty factory. According to a department source quoted in the story, the FDNY was not confident the existing system met the criteria for a shelter, gave the operator a list of fixes, and allowed the opening only because a fire guard would be on duty around the clock while the system was brought up to code.

An inspection approves a system for the building as it was designed and used at the time. When people start sleeping in a building that was a factory, the occupancy changes, and with it the detection the code expects: apartment or sleeping-room alarms, building detection and notification appropriate to the new use, and filings by a registered design professional. Start with the architect or engineer handling the conversion, and bring the old approval and inspection paperwork to the first meeting. We can survey what is installed, label it and tell the design professional which parts look reusable, but the decision about what the new use needs is theirs and the FDNY's.

Our studio building has heavy timber floors like the Red Hook warehouse that burned in 2025. What should the owner look at first in the fire alarm?

Three things, in this order. First, whether detection reaches the places a fire would grow unseen: the top floor, any roof space, shafts and stairs, storage rooms, and studios that are locked most of the week. In the 481 Van Brunt Street fire, Brooklyn Paper reported, flames engulfed the top floors and spread to the roof, the roof collapsed and part of the fourth floor fell into the third. A system that covers the busy ground floor and not the quiet top floor protects the wrong end of the building.

Second, whether the alarm reaches anyone when the building is empty, which in a studio building is most nights. That means monitoring, a current call list and a panel that names rooms, not just zones. Third, whether the detectors suit the rooms: heat detection in kiln rooms, woodshops and anywhere dust or fumes would cause false alarms, and smoke detection in corridors, stairs and quiet rooms where it gives earlier warning. We would add one more check after any nearby fire: walk the building with the tenants and write down what has changed since the alarm was last tested, because studio buildings change faster than their device lists.

Our building is inside the Gowanus industrial business zone. Does the zoning change what kind of fire alarm we need?

Not directly. Zoning decides what a lot may be used for and how big a building can be. The fire alarm requirements come from the Building Code and the Fire Code, and they follow the building's occupancy classification, its height, its area and how many people use it. A woodshop inside the industrial zone and a woodshop on Fourth Avenue are treated the same way for fire alarm purposes if the buildings are the same. What the industrial zone does change is the mix of uses: more vehicles, hot work, storage and buildings empty at night, which shape the detection and monitoring we recommend.

The place where zoning and fire alarm meet is a change of use. If a building inside the zone is converted to something with more people in it, or people who sleep there, the occupancy changes and the fire alarm requirements change with it. Your design professional sorts out both questions at once, and it is worth asking them before you sign a lease for a new kind of use.

State environmental officials want to test our building for soil vapor from the old gas plant sites. Can our carbon monoxide or natural gas detectors warn us about those vapors?

No, and it is worth knowing why. THE CITY reported in 2024 that the state Department of Environmental Conservation had put about 300 sites around the Gowanus on a list for possible soil vapor intrusion testing, because contaminants found in the soil of a former gas manufacturing plant might have migrated. A carbon monoxide alarm is listed to sense carbon monoxide. A natural gas detector of the kind the city's 2027 rule requires is listed under UL 1484 to sense natural gas near gas-burning appliances. Neither is designed or certified to measure the contaminants DEC tests for, and a quiet alarm says nothing about them either way.

So treat the two as separate jobs. Let DEC, or an environmental consultant working with the owner, do the vapor testing and any mitigation it calls for. Keep the smoke, CO and gas detectors placed where the city's rules put them, for the hazards they were built for. If mitigation adds a fan or other equipment to the building, tell your design professional and the company that services any building fire alarm, in case the equipment room it lands in needs detection.

Walk-ups, row houses and older apartment buildings

My apartment in a 1910 building had no smoke or CO detector, so I bought one and put it up myself. Does that let the owner off the hook?

No. Putting up your own alarm was the right thing to do that night, but in a multiple dwelling the owner is still the one HPD holds responsible for installing approved smoke detectors within fifteen feet of each sleeping room's main entrance, carbon monoxide detectors where the building has fuel-burning equipment, and, starting January 1, 2027, natural gas detectors where there is gas piping. The owner also has to replace a detector that is missing or inoperable before a new tenant moves in. A tenant-bought alarm does not count toward any of that, and HPD's rules on reimbursement assume the owner supplied the device.

Write to the owner or managing agent, say which rooms have no detector, and keep a copy. If nothing happens, a 311 complaint brings HPD. As an owner, the fix is simple: install the required devices, give the tenant the notice, and keep a dated record. In a Class A building, the tenant can be asked to pay back $25 per smoke, CO or gas detector, $50 for a two-in-one device and $75 for a three-in-one, within a year of installation. Leave the tenant's own alarm up if they want it; extra alarms do no harm.

Our building's front door is on the street, but the back of the cellar opens at ground level onto a lower yard. Which floor counts when the code asks how many stories sit above the exit?

The code measures from the lowest level of exit discharge, the level where an exit leads directly outside. Section 907.2.9 is triggered when any apartment is three or more stories above that level, or more than one story below the highest level of exit discharge, or when the building has more than sixteen apartments. If your building really does have exits at two grades, the count can come out differently from what you see on the street side, and a building that looks like a three-story walk-up from the front can count as taller.

This is a design professional's call, not ours or yours, and it depends on whether that rear door is a required exit and what it leads to. If you are buying, renovating or converting a building where the grade falls from front to back, ask the architect to settle the exit discharge question early. It decides whether the building needs a filed system or only apartment alarms, and that is the biggest single difference in what a Gowanus fire alarm job costs.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Gowanus

Search results and AI summaries about fire alarm installation pull from national cost guides, sellers' blogs and forum posts. Some of it is fine. Some of it is wrong for New York City, and some is wrong for Gowanus in particular. These are the claims we hear repeated most often, with what we see on the ground.

"A basic fire alarm system costs $0.50 to $1.00 per square foot"

Per-square-foot figures like this one, and the "$1 to $5 per square foot" and "$5 to $13 per square foot" ranges in other guides, are averages from places where most jobs are simple commercial systems in one-story buildings. They tell a Gowanus owner almost nothing, because the biggest cost question here is not size. It is which kind of job the building is.

A 4,000-square-foot row house that needs sealed smoke and CO alarms is a household job: on our Brooklyn list, complete home protection is $2,500 and a single apartment starts from $600. A 4,000-square-foot studio floor in a converted factory that needs a building system is a design, filing and inspection job, with a registered design professional's fee, a $585 FDNY application and months of review on top of the installation itself.

Settle the building's category first, then ask for a price by device and room, not by area.

"Conventional systems run $3,000 to $10,000 for small buildings"

That range appears in commercial cost guides, and for a small filed system in some cities it may be fair. In Gowanus it misleads in both directions. For a three-story walk-up that only needs apartment alarms and cellar detection, the honest price is far lower: our landlord rate is $400 to $800 per unit.

For a studio building or venue that needs a filed system, the equipment and installation may fall in that range, but the design, the filing fee, the time to approval and the work of meeting an FDNY inspection are separate. The FDNY's own guide puts plan submission to final approval at about twenty to twenty-four weeks.

A quote that gives one number for "a conventional system" without saying whether it includes design, filing and inspection support is not a quote you can compare. Ask what is in it.

"Building code in NYC requires all smoke and CO detectors to be hardwired"

This one comes up in forum answers and gets repeated by AI tools. It is true for many new buildings and gut renovations, where the code calls for alarms on building power with battery backup. It is not true for existing apartments and houses, which is most of Gowanus's older stock.

The Department of Buildings' bulletin on battery alarms says battery-powered smoke alarms installed in existing dwellings must be the sealed type that meets UL 217, with a non-removable battery lasting at least ten years. New York State has required that type for new residential smoke alarm sales since April 1, 2019.

For a tenant-occupied walk-up on the side streets, sealed alarms are legal, quick to install and kinder to old plaster. For a renovation that opens the ceilings, hardwired alarms are the better choice, and the electrician on the job handles the circuit.

"In a new apartment building, any detector that trips sounds the alarm everywhere"

Many people assume the building detectors in a new rental work like a school's fire alarm. For Group R-2 apartment buildings, the city's Building Code says the opposite: section 907.2.9 describes a fire alarm system "without alarm notification appliances," and the actuation of its smoke detectors "shall not initiate a signal to alarm notification appliances."

Those detectors sit in equipment rooms and elevator lobbies and report to a central station or a constantly attended location. Taller buildings add voice communication and other features, but the basic idea stays: the building is told, and the fire department decides who moves.

The practical point for residents of the new canal-side buildings is that the alarms inside their apartment are what wake them. Keep them working, and never take one down because the building "has a system."

"If the FDNY inspected the alarm, the building is covered"

An FDNY inspection approves a system for the building as it was being used at the time. The 2024 shelter at 130 Third Street showed what happens when the use changes afterward: the temporary certificate of occupancy relied on an inspection from August 2022, when the building was a mostly empty factory, and the FDNY was reported as not confident the system met the needs of a 400-bed residence.

Converted buildings are common in Gowanus, and their uses drift: a studio becomes a rehearsal room, a warehouse floor an event space.

The useful question is not whether the alarm was inspected. It is what the building was used for when it was inspected, and whether that is still true.

"Old masonry factories are fireproof, so they need less detection"

Brick walls do not make a building fireproof. Many Gowanus factories have masonry outside and timber floors, beams and roofs inside, the construction the FDNY described after the Red Hook studio warehouse fire in 2025, when Assistant Chief Kevin Woods said that once heavy timber members start going, "the fire takes over rather quickly."

The wood inside, plus decades of stored materials, finishes and tenant partitions, can give a fire plenty to burn. And because these buildings are often empty at night, a fire can grow for a long time before anyone outside notices.

If anything, a converted factory needs more thought about detection than a modern building, especially on the top floors and in rooms nobody visits, and it needs monitoring that reaches someone at 3 a.m.

"Any smoke detector you buy online will do for a studio or shop"

A household smoke alarm from a store is fine in a bedroom. In a kiln room, a woodshop, a body shop or a venue with haze, it will either sound every day or get its battery pulled, and an alarm with no battery is worse than none because everyone assumes it works.

Commercial spaces need devices chosen for the room, usually heat detection where the work makes smoke or dust, and in a building with a filed system, every device has to be compatible with the panel and shown on the approved drawings. The FDNY also warns that detectors connected to a building system are not stand-alone units, and that swapping them for single-station alarms can lead to enforcement.

The cheapest detector is the one that is right for the room the first time. We pick devices after we have seen what goes on in each space, not before.

Gowanus Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Is the cable that carries a fire alarm the same as the network cable in your job photos?
+

No. The photos on this page come from our network jobs and show the same care with routes, labels and testing that alarm work needs, but fire alarm circuits use cable listed for fire alarm use under the electrical code, commonly marked FPL, FPLR or FPLP depending on where it runs. Data cable is not a substitute. We keep the two on separate supports and label them separately.

Does the FDNY publish a list of companies approved to inspect, test and service fire alarm systems?
+

Yes. The FDNY posts an approved companies list for fire alarm system inspection, testing and service on its website. Companies are recognized after an FDNY review, and the department's letter of approval is valid for one year, so check the current list rather than an old certificate on a wall. Owners of filed systems should use a company on that list for the required testing.

What is a fire guard, and when can one stand in for a fire alarm?
+

A fire guard is a trained person who patrols a building and raises the alarm when a fire protection system is out of service or incomplete. The Department of Buildings told THE CITY in 2024 that the city's codes allow occupancy with fire guards while a fire alarm is being serviced, which is how the Third Street shelter opened. It is a temporary measure set by the FDNY's requirements, not a replacement for fixing the system.

How do you keep dust off artwork, stock and finished pieces in a studio while devices go in?
+

Where a studio holds wet work or finished pieces that cannot be covered, we schedule that room for a day the tenant chooses. Everything is wiped down before we leave.

Is FDNY plan approval the same as final acceptance of a fire alarm?
+

No. Plan approval means the FDNY has accepted the design professional's drawings, and only then can installation begin. A building with approved plans and no acceptance is not finished.

Our project has stores below and apartments above. Why does the FDNY's guide say the apartment part of the fire alarm is filed on its own?
+

The FDNY's tips for fire alarm approval note that systems for R-2 apartment occupancies are filed separately. The apartment portion follows its own section of the Building Code, with detectors that report to a monitoring point rather than sounding in the halls, while a store or restaurant below may need something different. Our new building is redoing its lobby. Can the interior contractor move the smoke detector in the elevator lobby?
+

Not on their own. Elevator lobby detectors in an apartment building are part of the building's filed fire alarm system, so moving one is a change to that system rather than decoration. The engineer of record and the company that services the system should be involved before the ceiling comes down, and the detector has to be back in service, tested and documented before the lobby reopens.
