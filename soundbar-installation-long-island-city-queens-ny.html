The Neighborhood

Why Long Island City Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Long Island City was rebuilt as a place to live within living memory, and its walls show it. A rezoning in 2001 opened the Court Square area — until then, in the New York Times' description, mostly low warehouses and auto repair shops around the Citigroup tower — to apartment buildings, and the waterfront at Hunters Point followed. What went up is overwhelmingly concrete: flat concrete floor plates, concrete columns and shear walls, metal-stud partitions between rooms, and glass curtain walls facing Manhattan. That combination changes almost every decision in a soundbar install. The wall you want may be concrete with steel reinforcing a couple of inches in; the one beside it may be gypsum board on light steel framing; the third is glass. Each needs a different fixing, and one of them takes none at all.

The second difference is who else has a say. Very few people in LIC's towers own the building around them. Renters answer to a management company and a lease rider; condo owners answer to a board, a managing agent and an alteration agreement. Before anyone drills, those offices commonly want a certificate of insurance, a description of the work, sometimes a product sheet for the bracket, and a booking for the service elevator. None of that is difficult, but it decides whether the installer gets past the lobby on the day, so we treat it as part of the job rather than your homework.

Third, the neighbours are closer than they look. A tower apartment has people on both sides, above and below, often sharing a wall built of steel studs and board rather than masonry. Residents on the neighbourhood's own forum say so plainly: one r/longislandcity thread asks whether a new building's “walls are just very thin” because a neighbour's television always seems too loud, and in another a resident writes that they “find the new luxury buildings to be very noisy.” A soundbar that is placed and set up without that in mind is the easiest way to become the neighbour in somebody else's thread.

And the rooms themselves are unusual. Open-plan living rooms run straight into the kitchen; a window wall or a sliding balcony door takes one whole side; ceilings in many newer units and in the factory conversions are bare concrete, with no hollow space above them to hide a wire or a speaker. Lofts in the old Eagle Electric plant on Queens Street were marketed with 12- to 14-foot exposed concrete ceilings and floor-to-ceiling columns. Those are handsome rooms, and difficult ones for sound: hard surfaces everywhere, few places to run cable, and long distances between the TV and the outlet.

Not all of Long Island City is new, either. On 45th Avenue between 21st and 23rd Streets, the Hunters Point Historic District preserves a row of 1870s stone-fronted houses; Dutch Kills, north of Queens Plaza, still has lines of modest early-1900s rowhouses; and Queensbridge Houses, opened in 1939 beside the bridge, remains the largest public housing development in North America. A soundbar visit in one of those homes looks nothing like one on the fortieth floor of a Jackson Avenue tower — which is why this page exists separately from our Queens soundbar installation page.

Long Island City facts that shape a soundbar job

  • 2001: the year the city rezoned the Court Square area for housing, which the New York Times later described as having been mostly low-lying warehouses and auto repair shops. By 2012 roughly a dozen developments with up to 4,700 apartments, most of them rentals, were planned there.
  • 5,000 homes expected under the city's redevelopment of Hunter's Point South, including two TF Cornerstone towers of 56 and 46 storeys on Center Boulevard with 1,194 apartments between them (6sqft, 2021).
  • 14,700 homes allowed under the OneLIC Neighborhood Plan, which the City Council approved in November 2025 for 54 largely industrial blocks, with 4,350 of them permanently affordable (6sqft). More concrete towers, and more first-time TV walls, are on the way.
  • About 70 storeys: The Orchard, at Orchard Street and Jackson Avenue, now the tallest building in Queens; nearby Lumen LIC rises 66 storeys with 938 apartments (6sqft).
  • 3,142 apartments and nearly 7,000 residents at Queensbridge Houses, opened in 1939 (LIC Post, cited by Wikipedia).
  • High-risk flood zones: much of the Hunters Point area lies in FEMA's high-risk zones, according to the New York Times, and the Hunters Point Parks Conservancy notes the neighbourhood was heavily affected by Hurricane Sandy — worth remembering for anything installed at street level or in a storage cage.

Put together, that is the brief for a Long Island City soundbar: mostly concrete-and-glass apartments run by managers, a growing number of lofts, a small and precious stock of older houses, lots of near neighbours, and a waterfront that floods. Everything below is organised around those facts.

The post-2001 rental and condo towers around Jackson Avenue, Crescent Street, 23rd and 24th Streets and Queens Plaza — Linc LIC with its 709 units, the 5Pointz pair of 47 and 41 storeys, Lumen and The Orchard. Concrete frames, steel-stud partitions, a managing agent and a lot of near neighbours.

What holds a soundbar in each Long Island City wall

Poured concrete in a tower. Shear walls, column faces and the occasional exposed slab edge are reinforced concrete. We scan the area first so the drill avoids rebar and any conduit buried in the pour, then use a hammer drill with a carbide bit, clear the hole with a vacuum and set wedge or sleeve anchors sized for the bracket. Concrete is the strongest fixing in the neighbourhood and the noisiest to make, so we drill inside the building's permitted hours and warn the neighbours through management when the rules ask for it. Nothing hides inside a concrete wall, so cable goes in a painted raceway or straight down behind the furniture.

Steel studs and gypsum board. Most interior walls in LIC towers are light-gauge steel framing with one or two layers of board. Steel does not grip a wood screw, so we locate the stud flanges, use self-drilling fasteners or toggles made for steel framing, and keep the load spread across the bracket. These partitions are usually hollow, which makes them the best candidates for hiding the bar's power and HDMI with an in-wall kit, subject to the building's alteration rules and to what the wall is for: a rated wall to the corridor or a party wall to the next apartment is treated very differently from a closet partition.

Concrete columns and original masonry in lofts. Factory columns are massive and often finished rough or sealed; old brick and block perimeter walls may be the building's original shell. Both take masonry anchors well. The difficulty in a loft is distance: the TV wall may be thirty feet from the nearest outlet across a concrete floor, so the cable route is planned before the bracket position, not after.

Stone-fronted and brick rowhouses. In the 45th Avenue houses and in Dutch Kills, party walls are brick under plaster and interior partitions may be wood framing. Plaster is drilled without hammer action to avoid cracking; the brick behind takes sleeve anchors. Interior work in a landmarked house is not normally what the Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates, but anything that reaches the facade — an outdoor speaker, a cable through a front wall — is a different conversation.

Housing-authority apartments. Walls are masonry or plaster over masonry and are handled like the rowhouse walls above, but permission comes from the development's management office. We work from what the tenant has been told is allowed and stop if the answer is no.

Glass, stone and tile. Window walls, back-painted glass splashbacks, stone-clad lobbies and tiled kitchens all appear in LIC apartments. We do not drill window glass or the window frames at all; tile and stone are drilled slowly with the right bit, at the tile surcharge, after a frank warning that glazed surfaces can still crack.

New-building households who add a streaming soundbar often want the TV wall on a wired network instead of fighting the building's Wi-Fi. Our Queens structured cabling team can run a data line to the TV location where the building permits, and network installation in Queens covers the router and access points behind it.

Rules that touch business soundbars in Long Island City

  • Sidewalk and roadway cafés. The city's Dining Out NYC rules for sidewalk cafés say there may be no musical instruments or amplified sound unless a Street Activity Permit authorises it, and cafés must also comply with the Noise Control Code. We set business soundbars for the indoor room and do not aim them at the street.
  • Apartments over the shop. Most Vernon Boulevard and Jackson Avenue storefronts have homes above or beside them. The Department of Environmental Protection's noise guide caps commercial music at 42 decibels inside a nearby residence, with tighter late-night limits and a separate test for bass, so subwoofers in restaurants are placed and set conservatively.
  • Bars that stick out into a walkway. In a clinic corridor or a waiting area, a long bar on its bracket can project further than the 4 inches the 2010 ADA Standards allow for wall objects mounted between 27 and 80 inches above the floor; we mount it above that band or out of the walking route.
  • Landlord and building paperwork. Commercial landlords in mixed-use towers want insurance on file and often restrict drilling hours; we supply the certificate at no charge and book around the building's schedule.
  • Power belongs to an electrician. A soundbar connection is low-voltage work, but a new or moved receptacle is electrical work for a licensed electrician; we plan around existing outlets and tell you plainly if one is needed.
  • Street-level spaces near the water. In Hunters Point storefronts and cellars, keep amplifiers, power strips and subwoofer power off the floor; we mount what we can on the wall.

Businesses that call about a screen's sound usually have a door-entry panel, cameras or a back-office network that needs attention as well. Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Long Island City: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island City residents and businesses raise before a soundbar goes up, grouped by what is behind them: concrete you can't see into, the systems and staff of a managed building, glass and shared households, lofts, and businesses. Booking details and quicker questions are in the FAQ further down.

Inside the concrete

What happens if the drill meets rebar inside the concrete wall of our tower apartment?

If we have done our job, it doesn't, because the wall is scanned before the first hole. Reinforced concrete walls and columns carry steel bars in a grid a short distance below the surface, and the reinforcing in a shear wall is part of what holds the building up. We run a scanner over the area where the bracket will go, mark the bars and any embedded conduit, and shift the bracket's holes to land between them. Most soundbar brackets have slotted or multiple holes precisely so there is room to do that, and the bar itself hides the result.

If a bit does touch steel despite the scan, the drill tells you at once: the dust changes, progress stops and the hammer action starts to ring. We stop. We do not switch to a rebar-cutting bit or try to drill through, because cutting reinforcing in a building we don't own is not our decision to make and almost never the building's policy either. The hole is abandoned, filled, and a new one is made a little to one side, which is why we keep a few inches of adjustment in every layout.

What this means for you is mostly time. On a concrete wall we allow for the scan, the occasional relocated hole and the cleanup of concrete dust, and the Queens concrete line on our price list covers that work. If the scan shows a wall so congested with steel or conduit that a safe pattern isn't possible, we tell you before drilling and switch to a steel-stud wall nearby or a TV-attached bracket.

Management told us the floors are post-tensioned — does that limit where cables or a ceiling bracket can go?

Yes, and it is worth taking seriously. In a post-tensioned slab, steel tendons inside the concrete are stretched tight after the pour; cutting or drilling into one can release that tension, damage the slab and injure people nearby. Concrete-scanning firms describe ground-penetrating radar as the only reliable way to confirm where those tendons run before anyone cores or drills. Not every LIC building uses post-tensioning, and we never assume yours does, but if management mentions it, the rule for your apartment is simple: no holes in the floor or the ceiling slab without the building's say-so and a proper scan.

For a soundbar, that rarely matters, because the bar goes on a wall, not the slab. Where it does matter is everything around the bar: anchoring a cable clip into the ceiling, hanging a projector or a ceiling TV mount, cutting a floor box for a sofa-side outlet, or fixing a rear speaker bracket overhead. We plan those around the slab: rear speakers on stands or on the walls, cable along walls rather than across the ceiling, and nothing that needs a floor core.

If you genuinely need something fixed into a post-tensioned slab — usually a ceiling-mounted screen — that becomes a building-approved job with its own scan by a specialist, arranged through management, before we come back to install. We would rather tell you that on the phone than discover it with a drill in hand.

The ceiling in our Court Square unit is bare concrete — is there a way to get surround or height sound without running wires overhead?

There is, and in most LIC apartments it is the better answer anyway. A bare concrete ceiling means no hollow space above for in-ceiling speakers or for hiding wire, and painted conduit across a concrete ceiling is a lot of visible metal for a pair of speakers. The alternatives are bars that create height effects themselves and wireless rear speakers. Dolby Atmos soundbars with upward-firing drivers bounce sound off the ceiling, and a flat, hard concrete ceiling at ordinary height actually reflects that sound well; the effect weakens in very tall loft ceilings, which we explain before you buy.

For surround, most current bars pair with a set of wireless rear speakers. “Wireless” means no speaker cable from the bar, not no cables at all: each rear still needs power. In an apartment where the sofa floats in the middle of an open-plan room, we look for outlets on the walls behind and beside it, use stands or small shelves rather than ceiling brackets, and run the short power leads along the floor edge or the furniture.

If you want proper overhead speakers in a concrete-ceilinged room, the honest route is a dropped soffit or a ceiling built down by a contractor, which some owners add during a renovation. That is a building alteration with its own approvals; we can wire into it once it exists, but it is not something a soundbar visit can create.

A sidewall sprinkler head sits high on the wall we picked for the TV — does the TV and soundbar layout have to work around it?

Yes. Many apartments in newer towers are protected by sprinklers that come out of the wall near the ceiling rather than down from it, and they are designed to throw water across the room in a particular pattern. A large TV pushed high on the wall, a tall bracket arm or a shelf above the screen can sit in the path of that spray. Sprinklers are life-safety equipment owned and maintained by the building; we never touch the head or its piping, never paint or cover it, and keep the screen, the bar and anything mounted with them clear of the head and below its spray.

In practice that usually means a slightly lower TV than people first imagine, which suits a soundbar anyway: the bar should sit close under the screen at roughly seated ear height, not up near the ceiling. Where the room is tight, we move the TV along the wall rather than up it. If you are unsure whether something on your wall is a sprinkler, a heat detector or a smoke detector, send a photo before the visit and we will tell you; if your building has specific clearances in its house rules, those win.

Drilling near any of those devices also raises dust, and in a building with a central fire alarm that can be a problem of its own. That is covered in the FAQ below, along with what we do to avoid setting it off.

Building rules, systems and staff

Our condo's alteration application wants a written scope of work and the bracket's spec sheet — what do you send?

A short, plain document that a managing agent can approve without phoning anyone. It names the apartment, says what is being installed and on which wall, what the wall is made of and how it will be fixed (for example, four wedge anchors into concrete after a scan, or steel-stud fasteners into an interior partition), confirms that no electrical, plumbing or sprinkler work is involved, and states the expected hours and duration of drilling. Attached are the manufacturer's product sheet for the bracket and the soundbar, our NYS low-voltage license number, and the certificate of insurance naming whoever the building lists.

Some LIC condos treat a soundbar as minor work that only needs the certificate and a service-elevator booking; others run every hole through the alteration process. We match the paperwork to what your building asks for rather than sending the same packet everywhere. If the application asks for something we can't properly provide — an architect's drawing, or a structural engineer's letter for a heavy item on a slab — we tell you so and explain what it would involve.

Start this early. Management offices often take a few business days to respond, and a visit booked before approval may hit the $150 return-trip charge if the lobby turns us away. Send us the managing agent's contact when you book and we will deal with them directly.

We just closed on a new condo — could drilling for the soundbar affect the sponsor's warranty on the apartment?

It is a fair worry in a neighbourhood full of first-generation condos, and the answer lives in your own paperwork rather than in a rule we can quote. New-development sponsors usually set out what they will repair, and for how long, in the offering plan, the purchase agreement or a separate warranty letter, and those documents commonly exclude damage caused by an owner's own alterations. Four anchor holes for a soundbar bracket are about as minor as alterations get, but the logic still applies: if something we drilled caused a problem, the sponsor could reasonably say it isn't theirs to fix.

So we keep the work well away from anything a warranty is likely to cover. We scan before drilling so we don't hit pipes, conduit or reinforcing; we don't open walls that carry plumbing; we never touch the building's electrical, sprinkler or ventilation systems; and on a steel-stud partition we fix into the framing rather than cutting it. If you are still inside the period when the sponsor is fixing punch-list items, it is sensible to let those repairs on the TV wall finish first, so nobody argues later about who made which hole.

If you want certainty, read the warranty section of your offering plan or ask the sponsor's customer-care contact in writing whether mounting a TV or soundbar affects coverage. Nothing on this page is legal advice. What we can give you is a written record of exactly what was installed, where and how, which is the document you would want if the question ever came up.

The building's in-house handyman offered to put the soundbar up — what would we get from a licensed installer instead?

Plenty of building staff are skilled and know the walls in their building better than any outsider, and for a light bar on a console there may be no difference at all. The differences show on harder jobs. We scan concrete before drilling, carry anchors rated for concrete and steel studs rather than the plastic plugs and wood screws packed in most bracket boxes, and set up the electronics as well as the hardware: eARC, HDMI-CEC so one remote works, lip-sync, the bar's app and its firmware, and the night and dialogue settings that matter in a building with near neighbours.

There is also the question of who stands behind the work. Building staff often do small jobs for residents as a favour or on their own time, which can leave nobody clearly responsible if a bracket fails or a wall is damaged. We carry liability insurance, provide a certificate the building can file, hold a New York State low-voltage license, and the terms of our work are in a written service agreement.

If you are happy with the handyman doing the mounting, we can still do the setup-only part — connecting and configuring the bar, sub and remotes — billed by time at the Queens hourly rate. Many residents find that the hardware is the easy half.

Our new condo came with a recessed box and a pipe in the wall where the TV is meant to go — can the soundbar use them?

Very likely, and it is one of the nicest surprises in a new LIC apartment. Some developers rough in a recessed media box at the TV location, with power and a conduit or flexible pipe running down inside the wall to an outlet or a box near the floor where a cabinet would sit. If yours is there, the soundbar's HDMI and the TV's connections can travel through that pipe out of sight, without opening the wall or using a separate in-wall kit.

We check three things on site. First, whether the pipe is continuous and clear: builders sometimes leave a pull string, sometimes nothing, and occasionally the pipe was cut or crushed during construction. Second, whether it is big enough for the connectors you need; an HDMI plug is larger than the cable, and a pipe sized for a single coax cable may only take a slim HDMI or nothing at all. Third, whether the recessed box sits at a sensible height for the TV and leaves room beneath the screen for the bar.

If the pipe works, the job is cheaper and neater than an in-wall kit. If it is blocked, we tell you, and use a raceway or the in-wall kit instead. Either way we do not run power cords through a low-voltage pipe; the recessed box's own outlet powers the TV, and the bar plugs in wherever the building's electrician put the lower receptacle.

Glass, bass and shared households

Our sliding balcony door and the glass panel beside it buzz whenever the subwoofer plays — what stops that?

This is a common complaint in waterfront and tower apartments, and it is almost always fixable without giving up the subwoofer. A sliding door is a large panel held in a track with a little play in it, and window walls have their own gaskets and frames; a subwoofer playing a note close to the frequency at which that panel likes to vibrate makes it rattle in sympathy. The fix starts with the subwoofer's position, because moving it even a couple of feet changes which frequencies the room reinforces. Corners and the floor directly beside the glass are usually the worst places.

After position, level and crossover. Many people run the subwoofer far louder than the bar, which both rattles the glass and pushes bass into the apartments around you. We set the sub to blend with the bar rather than dominate it, and use the bar's room-correction or bass adjustment where it has one. An isolation pad or riser under the sub reduces what goes into the floor, which in a concrete building is the path most likely to reach the neighbours. Finally, check the door itself: a loose latch or a missing bumper in the track rattles at almost any volume, and building maintenance can adjust it.

If one particular note still sets the glass off, many bars and their apps allow a lower subwoofer level or a night or late-evening mode that reins in the deepest bass. We play a few test scenes with you and stop at the level where the glass is quiet.

Three of us share a two-bedroom — whose phone and account should the soundbar be set up under?

Whoever is most likely to stay in the apartment longest, and ideally the person who bought the bar. Most smart soundbars are tied to one account in the manufacturer's app, which controls settings, updates and, for some brands, who can add the bar to a speaker group or a voice assistant. If that account belongs to a roommate who moves out, the bar may need a factory reset and fresh setup before anyone else can manage it, and any custom settings go with them.

During the visit we set the bar up under the account you choose, then add the others as household members or guests where the app supports it, so everyone can stream to it and change the volume without owning it. We write down the settings that matter — speech mode, night mode, subwoofer level, the TV's audio output setting — on a card that stays with the bar, so a reset doesn't mean starting from nothing. Basic TV sound needs no app at all: the TV remote controls the bar over HDMI-CEC for everybody.

It is also worth agreeing who owns the hardware. If the bar and bracket belong to one roommate, they will want to take them when they leave; a bracket screwed into concrete comes down in minutes, but the anchor holes stay behind, so it is worth knowing how your lease treats them before the drill comes out.

Lofts and older buildings

Our loft has exposed metal conduit everywhere — can the soundbar's cables run the same way instead of a plastic raceway?

Yes, and in a converted factory it usually looks far better than a white plastic channel. Painted or bare steel conduit, fixed to concrete with proper straps and set out in straight runs with square bends, matches the industrial pipework already in many LIC lofts. We use it for the low-voltage side of a soundbar install: HDMI from the bar or the TV down to the media cabinet, speaker or subwoofer leads, and network cable, each in a pipe sized for the connectors that have to pass through it.

What we do not do is carry mains power in that pipe. Extending or adding outlets is electrical work for a licensed electrician, and power and low-voltage cables are kept in separate pathways. In practice the electrician and we often run parallel pipes along the same route, which keeps the look consistent. In a condominium loft, exposed conduit on a shared wall or ceiling may also need the board's approval, the same as any other alteration.

Plan the route before choosing the TV position. A pipe that has to cross a doorway, a window or a column face gets complicated quickly, and the cheapest, cleanest loft installs are the ones where the TV goes on the wall nearest to where the cables need to end up.

Businesses

Our Vernon Boulevard restaurant wants the game to be heard at the sidewalk tables — is that allowed?

Not through speakers aimed outside. The city's Dining Out NYC rules for sidewalk cafés state that there may be no musical instruments or amplified sound unless authorised by a Street Activity Permit, and cafés must also comply with the Noise Control Code's rules on unreasonable noise. A soundbar pointed through an open window or door at the café tables is amplified sound outdoors in all but name, and with apartments above most Vernon Boulevard storefronts it is also the quickest way to a complaint.

What we can do is make the indoor room work well. A bar under the main screen, set for clear commentary at a moderate level, carries to the bar seats and the front tables; the outdoor screen, if you have one, runs captions. We keep the bar's level in check with the doors open, and leave the subwoofer off or very low in rooms with homes overhead, since bass is what travels.

If you are planning a special event with outdoor sound, the permit question belongs to the city's street-activity process, not to us. We are glad to set up indoor sound for it and take it out again afterwards.

Our Court Square office's glass-walled meeting room leaks sound into the open office — will adding a soundbar make that worse?

It can, if the bar is set up for a living room. Glass-fronted meeting rooms reflect sound around the room and pass more of it through than a solid wall would, so a soundbar running at living-room volume with its virtual-surround mode on makes calls clearer for the people inside and louder for everyone outside. Set up for speech instead, a bar usually helps: people in the room hear the far end clearly at a lower level, so they stop raising their own voices.

We mount the bar close under the display, aimed at the table, with surround and bass-boost modes off and dialogue mode on, and we cap the maximum volume where the bar or display allows it. For video calls the bar has to be the system's speaker output and the room microphone has to be a separate device that cancels the bar's sound; we check that the conferencing setup does that before leaving, because echo complaints are usually a settings problem, not a hardware one.

If the room needs real privacy rather than just a better speaker, that means acoustic treatment or a sound-masking system, which is a fit-out decision for the landlord and your facilities team. We will say so if a bar alone won't meet the need.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Long Island City

First, how this section was built. Google's AI Overview could not be displayed in the research environment for this page, so nothing below is quoted as something it said. Each heading is a claim published by a cost guide, a manufacturer or a forum — the sort of sentence answer engines summarise and repeat — followed by what it misses in a Long Island City apartment.

“The estimated national average cost to install a soundbar starts at $58.43 to $231”

That range comes from Homewyse's soundbar installation estimate, and as a national figure for a bar on a wood-stud wall it is reasonable. Summaries tend to present it as what a soundbar costs to install, full stop.

The figure doesn't know about the concrete. In an LIC tower the TV wall is often a reinforced shear wall that needs a scan, a hammer drill and proper anchors, and the job usually starts with paperwork for a managing agent. Our Queens list prices the wall mount at $135.45 and adds $78.75 for concrete or block, so a bar on a concrete wall with our Sanus bracket comes to $303.44 — higher than the bottom of the national range, and fixed in writing before anyone drills.

The useful takeaway is to price the wall, not the soundbar. Ask any installer what their figure assumes about the wall, and what changes if it turns out to be concrete.

“Mount the soundbar about 42.5 inches off the ground, where your ear falls when seated”

Yamaha's mounting guidance gives roughly that height as a target for seated ear level, and the principle — bar close to ear height, aimed at the listener — is sound.

LIC living rooms complicate the number. Many furnish with low modern sofas, so seated ear height is lower than the guide assumes; a sidewall sprinkler or a window header can limit how high the TV goes; and on a concrete wall the bar's height also has to dodge reinforcing steel found by the scan. A fixed number can put the bar in the wrong place or put a hole in the wrong spot.

We measure from your actual seat, check the TV's lower edge and remote sensor, then fit the height to the scan. The result is usually within a few inches of the guide, but it is your room's number, not a national one.

“If you use an adjustable TV mount, attach the soundbar to the TV mount”

Dolby's placement article suggests a bracket connected to the TV mount when the screen moves, so the bar follows the picture. That is good advice, and in LIC it often doubles as the no-new-holes option a lease or condo board prefers.

The catch is weight and reach. A full-motion arm carrying a large TV is already working near its limits, and the anchors behind it carry everything; in a steel-stud partition that matters far more than it does in a wood-framed house. A long Atmos bar also changes how far the arm can swing before it hits a window frame or a column.

We check the arm's rated load against the TV plus bar and bracket, confirm the arm is fixed into concrete or properly into steel studs, and test the full swing before handing it over.

“You need eARC to get Dolby Atmos”

It is repeated constantly. AWOL Vision's explainer sets it straight, as does a Sonos owner on Reddit who points out that eARC is not required for Atmos. Plain ARC can carry Atmos inside Dolby Digital Plus from streaming apps; eARC is needed for lossless formats such as Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio from a disc player or console.

This matters in LIC because many residents rent and inherit whatever TV the last owner or the furnished-apartment company left, or are buying a mid-range TV for a small room. Spending money on a new TV purely for eARC may be unnecessary if you only stream.

We look at your TV's ports and how you watch, set the TV to pass Atmos where it can, and tell you plainly whether eARC would change anything for you.

“Universal soundbar mounts hold most soundbars up to 20 lb”

That is how Sanus describes its universal soundbar mount, and it is an accurate rating for the bracket. Quick answers stretch it into “any universal bracket will hold your bar”.

A bracket rating says nothing about what holds the bracket. The weak point in a tower apartment is almost never the steel arm; it is a wood screw in a steel stud, or a plastic plug in a concrete hole drilled too large. Those failures are slow — a bar that starts to tilt months later — and the bar usually hits the console on the way down.

So we treat the bracket rating as half the answer, choose the fixing for the wall the scan finds, and leave the plastic plugs in the box.

“Bass travels easily, so either don't use a subwoofer or get one that can be adjusted”

That advice comes from an r/AskNYC thread about neighbour-friendly soundbars, and it is a fair rule of thumb. It gets shortened to “skip the sub in an apartment”.

In a concrete LIC tower, the main route for bass to a neighbour is through the structure — the subwoofer sitting on a slab, or a bracket on a party wall — more than through the air. A modest sub on an isolation pad, away from shared walls and corners and set to blend with the bar, is often quieter next door than a bar with its bass turned up to compensate for no sub at all.

We set the level with you and, if you can arrange it, check with a willing neighbour before we leave. That is worth more than any rule of thumb.

“Big-box stores list soundbar setup and soundbar mounting as separate menu items”

They do; national retailers publish flat-price menu lines for setup, networked setup and mounting. That makes it look as if every soundbar job is a menu choice.

Menus don't list the parts of an LIC install that take the time: the managing agent's paperwork, the service-elevator booking, the scan of a concrete wall, the building-supplied Wi-Fi that needs a device registered, or the sprinkler head that decides the TV height. Those end up as surprises on the day, or as a technician who can't proceed.

Ask before you book what happens if management wants a certificate, or the wall is concrete. If the answer comes back with a line from a price list and a method, the installer has worked in buildings like yours.

Tower Checklist

Before the Installer Comes to Your Long Island City Building

Most failed install days in managed buildings come down to something that could have been arranged by email the week before. These are the things worth sorting before the date.

  • Who approves work in your apartment. Renters: the management office and the lease rider. Owners: the managing agent and, for anything more than minor work, the board's alteration process. Send us the contact and we take it from there.
  • The certificate of insurance. Buildings name specific parties — the condominium, the managing agent, sometimes the developer. We issue it free; it simply has to be on file before we arrive.
  • The service elevator and contractor hours. Many buildings allow contractor work only on weekdays within set hours and need the elevator booked. Tell us the window and we pick an arrival slot inside it.
  • Drilling rules. Some buildings ask contractors to avoid shared walls, require notice before hammer drilling, or forbid any holes in the ceiling or floor slab. We plan the wall around them.
  • Devices near the ceiling. Note sprinkler heads, smoke and heat detectors on the TV wall. Photograph them for us.
  • The network. Find out whether the building supplies your internet and how residents register a device without a browser. Have the Wi-Fi name and password ready if it is your own router.
  • The bar, its bracket and the remotes, in the apartment. Deliveries held at the front desk or in a parcel locker should be collected before the slot.
  • A clear path and a clear wall. A few feet in front of the TV wall, and the furniture under it moved if you can.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Long Island City Soundbar Walls

How our crews read a tower or loft wall — working practice, not a story about any particular building

Start with your knuckles. A steel-stud partition sounds hollow between the studs and gives a little; a concrete wall sounds dead and feels cold. Then look at where the wall is: walls around the elevator core, beside the window wall and at the ends of the apartment are the likeliest to be concrete. A magnet slid along a hollow wall finds the screws in the steel studs faster than most stud finders.

On concrete, scan before you mark, mark before you drill, and keep the vacuum nozzle at the bit. Concrete dust is fine enough to drift into a smoke detector across the room and heavy enough to settle on everything else. If the bit stops and rings, you are on steel: stop, move, and fill the old hole.

In lofts, walk the cable route before agreeing the TV position. In rowhouses, assume the plaster is older than it looks and drill without hammer action until you reach brick. Everywhere, look up at the ceiling line for sprinkler heads and detectors before choosing a height, and look down for the outlet before choosing a wall.

FAQ

Long Island City Soundbar Installation FAQ

How long will the hammer drill be running, and how much noise and dust should the neighbours expect?
+

On a concrete wall, the drilling itself usually takes a few minutes per hole and only four to six holes are needed for a soundbar bracket, so the loud part is short. We drill inside the building's permitted hours, keep a vacuum nozzle at the bit to catch the dust, and tell management in advance if the house rules require notice of hammer drilling.

Could concrete dust from drilling set off our apartment's smoke detector and the building alarm?
+

It can if the detector is close to the work, which is why we take it seriously. We capture dust at the bit with a vacuum, keep holes few and clean, and if a detector is within a few feet of the wall we ask management how they want it handled; covering or isolating life-safety devices is the building's decision, never ours.

How can I tell whether our TV wall is concrete or drywall before booking?
+

Knock on it: a hollow, slightly springy sound means drywall on studs, while a dead, solid sound and a cold surface usually mean concrete. Walls around the elevator core, beside the window wall and at the ends of the apartment are the likeliest to be concrete. A photo with the outlet in view lets us confirm before the visit.

Can I just stick the soundbar to the concrete wall with adhesive strips instead of drilling?
+

We don't recommend it for anything but the lightest bars. Adhesive strips are rated for clean, smooth, dry surfaces and lose grip on painted or slightly dusty concrete over time, and a bar is held at the edge, which puts a peeling force on the strip. A TV-attached bracket or a floor stand gives you a no-hole option that won't let go.

Is a floor-standing TV stand with a soundbar shelf a good option for a window-wall apartment?
+

Often it is the best one. A sturdy floor stand keeps the screen and bar together, leaves every wall undrilled and can stand in front of a column or a short stretch of wall between windows. We assemble it, fix the bar to its shelf or bracket, run the cables down its spine and connect everything at the console price.

Do you fit soundbars in hotel rooms or extended-stay suites around Queens Plaza?
+

Yes, for owners and managers. Guest-room bars need to be fixed so they can't be lifted off, locked to a sensible maximum volume, set to follow the TV's power and volume over HDMI-CEC, and stripped of Bluetooth pairing and voice features guests could misuse. We work room by room around occupancy and leave a written settings sheet for the engineering team.

We live in a furnished apartment and the TV belongs to the landlord — can a soundbar still be added?
+

Usually, without changing anything permanent. The bar sits on the media unit or clips to the existing TV mount, connects to the TV's HDMI ARC or optical socket, and comes away again when you leave. If the landlord's TV has been locked into a hotel or commercial mode that hides its sound settings, we need the landlord to unlock it first.

Our living room has a dropped bulkhead along the ceiling — can the soundbar cables run inside it?
+

Sometimes, but not without the building's agreement. Bulkheads in tower apartments usually hide ductwork, sprinkler piping or the heating and cooling lines, so opening one is an alteration rather than a cable job. Where management allows access, we can route low-voltage cable inside; otherwise a painted raceway along the bulkhead's face does the job without opening anything.

Will drilling crack the smooth skim-coat finish on our concrete wall?
+

Rarely, if the hole is started carefully. We start each hole without hammer action until the bit is through the skim coat, then switch to hammer mode in the concrete, and keep the bit sharp. A tiny chip at the edge of a hole is hidden by the bracket; if your finish is a decorative plaster, tell us beforehand.

Do you set up TVs and soundbars in model apartments and leasing lounges for new buildings?
+

Yes. Leasing teams and stagers book us to mount screens and bars, hide every cable and leave a single, simple remote, so a tour runs without anyone hunting for inputs. We work to the building's schedule and paperwork and can return to take everything down when the model unit is sold or let.
