The Neighborhood

Why Longwood Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Longwood packs more kinds of buildings into a few blocks than almost any part of the South Bronx, and that is what shapes camera work here. CityNeighborhoods draws the neighborhood between East 163rd Street on the north, Prospect Avenue on the west, East 149th Street on the south and the Bruckner Expressway on the southeast; another history of the area puts the northern edge at East 167th Street. Southern Boulevard is the main road through it, the ZIP codes are 10455 and 10459, and the Longwood Avenue station on the 6 train opened on January 7, 1919. The land was once part of the old township of Morrisania and stayed mostly rural until plans for an IRT link to Manhattan were announced in the late 1800s. The developer George B. Johnson set up in the old White mansion and was among the first to see what was coming. Between 1897 and 1900 the architect Warren C. Dickerson designed rows of two- and three-story houses in Renaissance and Romanesque Revival styles, and after the subway opened in 1904 large five-story buildings filled in around them. By 1925 the neighborhood was 71 percent Jewish; by 1960 it was 47 percent Puerto Rican. In the 1970s, arson and abandonment hit hard. Banana Kelly says 70 percent of the housing stock in the Hunts Point-Longwood district was lost; CityNeighborhoods puts it at nearly 60 percent by 1977. Residents formed the Banana Kelly Community Improvement Association in 1977 under the motto "Don't Move, Improve," two homeowners began the push that became the historic district, and the city began rehabilitating abandoned tenements as low-income housing. The result is a single block that can hold a 1899 row house, a five-story apartment house from the IRT years, a tenement rebuilt by a nonprofit, and a new affordable building on what was a parking lot, each with different walls, owners and rules for cameras.

The local reporting shows what people need cameras to do here. CBS New York reported the armed robbery of a 77-year-old man at the Erma Cava Houses, senior housing on Southern Boulevard near Barretto Street, and noted that the suspect walked by the security desk just before 11 a.m. on a Monday, no questions asked. News 12 reported that a 14-year-old boy was robbed of his sneakers at gunpoint in front of 1021 Longfellow Avenue at noon on May 24, 2026, and in June 2026 that neighbors at 1006 Longfellow Avenue feared a suspected killer had returned to their building, with video evidence showing the suspect entering the Whitlock Avenue station about six minutes after the discovery. In September 2026, PIX11 reported a string of violent robberies centered around Starlight Park, Hunts Point and Longwood, in the 41st, 42nd and 43rd Precincts, and police released videos of the suspects. An older ABC report described surveillance cameras catching a man breaking into the Batista Barber Shop on Longwood Avenue. Just past the neighborhood's industrial edge, New York State Police reported in March 2026 that about 60,000 pounds of shelled walnuts were stolen from two 52-foot trailers in the Hunts Point area. We are not ranking any block, and none of this describes every building. What it does show is the range of moments a Longwood camera has to record clearly: a lobby in broad daylight with a desk nearby, a sidewalk at midday, a person heading for the train, a shop after closing and a trailer parked overnight.

Three sets of rules decide where those cameras can go. The first is Landmarks. The Longwood Historic District covers about three square blocks roughly bounded by Beck Street and Longwood, Leggett and Prospect Avenues, with 61 contributing buildings on Dawson, Kelly, Beck and Macy Streets, and an extension added in 1983 for houses on Macy Place. LPC's rules list security cameras alongside intercoms, light fixtures and security grilles as objects that need approval when attached to facades or areaway features, and its staff criteria for equipment on a primary facade include that it not damage or remove significant architectural features and not be visible. Being old or famous is not the same as being designated: the building that houses Casa Amadeo at Longwood and Prospect Avenues is on the National Register of Historic Places, yet one source notes that the store and its building are not protected as city landmarks. The second is permission. Most Longwood residents rent, and tenants generally cannot put cameras in hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces without the landlord's consent; in the many buildings owned by nonprofits or co-ops, the decision sits with a management office or a board. The third is the building itself. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, a two-way intercom in those with eight or more apartments, and the same in older buildings if a majority of apartments ask. Cameras sit on top of those requirements, never in place of them. And in every building we work in, nothing we mount touches a self-closing door or its closer, every opening through a rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop, no camera goes inside an apartment, and hallway views stop short of what a unit shows when its door opens.

Longwood facts that shape a camera plan

  • A desk is not a camera: at the Erma Cava Houses, a suspect walked past the security desk unchallenged, so desk-side views and alerts need planning, not just a guard's chair.
  • Daylight crime: the Longfellow Avenue sneaker robbery happened at 12 p.m., which means daytime pictures, with their glare and hard shadows, matter as much as night ones.
  • Video that travels: footage tied a suspect at 1006 Longfellow Avenue to the Whitlock Avenue station minutes later, which only works when clocks are right and clips export cleanly.
  • Facade rules: LPC lists security cameras among facade attachments it reviews, inside a district of 61 contributing buildings plus the Macy Place extension.
  • Institutional owners: Banana Kelly says it has redeveloped more than 2,000 affordable units in Hunts Point-Longwood and directly managed more than 1,000, so many camera decisions run through an office, not a single landlord.
  • Busy commercial spines: the city's needs assessment lists Westchester Avenue, East 163rd Street, Longwood Avenue, Southern Boulevard, Hunts Point Avenue, East Bay Avenue and Tiffany Street as the area's commercial corridors.

What Longwood owners often ask about next. A building that starts with a lobby camera usually comes back about the front door itself: whether it latches, whether the intercom works, whether residents could use a fob instead of a key. The camera footage is often what shows the door failing. We leave a spare cable and a labeled port at the recorder for that next step, so the cellar ceiling only has to be opened once.

The Clip Test: Will a Longwood Camera Give Someone Something They Can Act On?

Every Longwood story in the reporting turns on the same question: did a camera record something a manager, a family or a detective could use? A wide picture of a lobby that shows a shape walking past a desk does not answer it. Neither does a camera aimed at a glowing doorway at noon, or a recorder whose clock is twenty minutes off when police try to match its clip with the one from the next building. So before we count cameras, we run each spot through a simple test: what could happen here, what would a useful clip need to show, and how does the camera have to be set to show it. The garden gate: a row house camera starts at the fence

The Dickerson row houses are set back from the street behind fenced-in gardens, which puts the gate, not the door, at the edge of the property. A camera mounted on the house and pointed straight out at the street sees the tops of heads passing beyond the fence and very little that identifies anyone. A camera placed near the door, looking back down the walk toward the gate, records each person who opens the gate and walks up, facing the lens the whole way. In the historic district, the question is where that camera can sit. LPC's staff criteria for a primary facade ask that equipment not damage significant architectural features and not be visible, so we look first at positions that keep the camera small, tucked under an existing overhang or inside the doorway recess, and at options that do not attach to the facade at all, such as a camera behind the glass of the door or a parlor window. Each has trade-offs, which the questions further down explain. The gate itself, the fence and the front garden are not places we fix hardware without checking with the owner and, inside the district, with Landmarks.

The desk: a guard and a camera do different jobs

A staffed desk is a strong deterrent and a weak record. The person at the desk can be on the phone, signing for a delivery or helping a resident, and the CBS report from the Erma Cava Houses describes a suspect walking past the desk in the middle of the morning with no questions asked. In a senior building or any building with a desk, we use two views. One looks across the desk line from the side or behind, so everyone who passes the desk is recorded walking through it. The other covers the elevator lobby or the stair door, so the recording shows where that person went next. The desk monitor shows both, and where the recorder supports it, a short alert can sound when someone crosses the line toward the elevators while the desk chair is empty or after a set hour. None of that replaces a staff member asking who someone is visiting. It means that when the question goes unasked, the building still knows who came in.

The noon problem: daylight is not automatically a good picture

People expect the night picture to be the hard one. In Longwood, the Longfellow Avenue robbery happened at noon, and midday brings its own trouble: a bright sidewalk behind a person standing in a dim doorway, sun striking the lens at certain hours, deep shadow under trees or a building's own cornice. A camera set only for night conditions can give a silhouette against glare by day. We check each outdoor and vestibule view at the time of day the sun hits it hardest, choose cameras that handle bright and dark areas in the same frame, and aim slightly down rather than level with the street, so the sky is out of the picture. We also set outdoor cameras to record continuously, not only on motion, because a busy daytime sidewalk can trigger and stop a motion clip so often that the moment that matters falls between recordings.

The route out: clips that can be matched with the next building's

The News 12 report from 1006 Longfellow Avenue describes video showing a suspect entering the Whitlock Avenue station about six minutes after the discovery. That kind of timeline only comes together when clips from different buildings and stations line up. A recorder whose clock has drifted, or which was never set to the right time zone, makes its clip harder to use next to anyone else's. We set every recorder to keep its clock in sync automatically, name each camera by its street and door, such as "Kelly St gate" or "Longfellow Ave vestibule," and show the owner or manager how to export a clip with its time stamp intact. A camera that looks down the block from the building's own wall, toward the corner or the avenue, shows which way someone went. We do not mount anything on lampposts, street trees or subway structures, and we do not aim cameras into the station.

Dickerson row houses: small cameras, careful positions

The houses Warren C. Dickerson designed between 1897 and 1900 are the heart of the Longwood Historic District, and they are where a camera plan has the least room for error. LPC's rules name security cameras among the objects that need approval when attached to a facade or areaway feature. Its criteria for staff approval on a primary facade include that the installation not damage or remove significant architectural features and not be visible; on a secondary facade, that it be no more than minimally visible. In practice, that steers us toward a few approaches. Inside the house, a camera behind the glass of the front door or a parlor window, aimed down the walk, attaches to nothing outside. At the back of the lot, a camera on the rear wall covers the yard and back door with far less concern about visibility from the street. Where the owner wants an exterior camera at the front, we plan the smallest housing in the least visible spot, document the position with photos, and the owner applies to LPC before any work is done on that position. Cable stays inside, through the cellar or the existing service entry, so no new wire runs across the face of the house. These houses have stood since the 1890s; nothing we do should change how they look.

Five-story apartment houses: cellar, stair hall, rear yard

The big buildings that went up after the 1904 subway opening tend to share a layout: a street door into a vestibule, an inner door into a lobby with mailboxes, a stair running up the middle, a cellar holding meters, the boiler and the internet provider's box, and a rear yard that only a few back windows overlook. The cellar is the hub. The recorder goes there in a locked, dry spot away from the boiler's heat and from any cellar door that opens to the street. Vestibule and lobby cameras are the shortest runs. Landing cameras go up a pipe chase or a stacked closet where one exists, and only where there is truly no hidden path do we use slim raceway painted to match. Yard cameras are fed through the rear cellar wall with one drilled opening, sealed afterward. Every floor the cable passes through is sealed with rated firestop. Where an old intercom still runs on its original wiring, we trace those wires first and keep clear of them, so tenants never lose their buzzers during the job.

Rehabilitated and nonprofit buildings: trace first, then plan with the office

Many Longwood apartment buildings were rebuilt after the 1970s. The Kelly Street rehab, for example, covered five buildings at 916, 920, 924, 928 and 935 Kelly Street, which had been in HPD's Alternative Enforcement Program before the work. A rehab can leave good paths for new cable, such as empty sleeves or a chase built into new walls, and it can also leave abandoned runs from intercoms, cable TV and earlier cameras that no longer reach anything. We trace before we cut. These buildings usually have an institutional owner, so the plan goes to a management office or board, not just a super. We write it so it can be read in a meeting: each camera, what it shows, what it leaves out, who can view live and who can export clips, and how long footage is kept. Where one organization runs several buildings, each building keeps its own recorder, and the office gets its own login with the limits it chooses.

Senior housing and new affordable buildings: desks, elevators and block walls

Senior buildings such as the Erma Cava Houses, and the new affordable buildings the city's needs assessment describes rising on former parking and vacant lots, tend to share concrete or block construction, an elevator, a lobby with a desk or a door attendant, and often a community room or laundry. Cable for added cameras goes up the building's riser or another approved chase, never into the elevator hoistway, and where floors are far from the recorder, small PoE switches along the way keep each run short. Block walls between a lobby and an equipment room get one planned opening, sealed with firestop afterward. In senior buildings we keep install days calm and predictable: notice in the lobby ahead of time, technicians in clearly marked clothing, and a staff member who knows where we will be working each hour, because older residents are rightly wary of strangers in the halls.

Chain-store blocks and the independent shop next door

On the blocks of Southern Boulevard where national retailers cluster, many of the big stores arrive with their own camera standards. The independent shop between them usually does not, and it is often the one that needs the clearest door camera, because a crowded sidewalk sends a steady stream of people past its window. We start inside the door, framed for faces at head height as customers walk in, then cover the counter from above and slightly in front, showing hands, the drawer and the customer, then the stock room or rear door where deliveries come in. Display walls and cases get their own cameras where merchandise is small and valuable. On a block this busy, we set the door camera to record continuously during open hours and to send alerts only after closing, so the owner's phone is not buzzing all afternoon.

Barbershops, salons and small shops on the side corridors

The ABC report about the Batista Barber Shop on Longwood Avenue is a reminder of how many small shops here keep tools, chairs, product and a cash drawer behind one door. After closing, a camera has two jobs: show how someone got in, and show who it was. During open hours, the counter camera covers the register without recording clients in the chairs. Shops with a roll-down gate get a camera that still sees the inside of the door when the gate is down, and a separate view from the shop's own façade where the landlord agrees. Small offices, takeout counters and service businesses on East 163rd Street and Prospect Avenue follow the same pattern: every opening, the money, and the route out.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Longwood

  • Private places stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas, locker rooms and treatment rooms are never recorded, and we test every view so a mirror does not show one.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Longwood: Questions Answered

Row houses in and around the historic district

Is there any way to watch the front of a historic-district row house without attaching anything to the facade?

Yes, with limits. A camera inside the house, behind the glass of the front door or a parlor window and aimed down the walk toward the garden gate, attaches to nothing outside, and guidance for landmarked storefronts notes that fixtures which do not physically attach to the building do not need an LPC permit. The trade-offs are real. Glass reflects at night when the room behind it is lit, so the camera's own infrared lights must be off and the lens pressed close to the glass with a hood around it, which means relying on a porch or street light for the night picture. Textured or old glass can distort the image. And a camera inside sees only what that window sees. For many row houses, an inside front camera plus an exterior camera on the rear wall, where visibility from the street is far less of a concern, covers the property without any new hardware on the front. Owners who want an exterior front camera apply to LPC for that position first.

LPC put up new historic district markers in Longwood in June 2026. Did the rules for cameras on these blocks change?

Not that we found. New York YIMBY reported that LPC unveiled four new markers commemorating the Longwood Historic District and its extension, and markers like these identify a district rather than changing its rules. What decides a camera job is LPC's rules, which list security cameras among the objects attached to facades and areaway features that need approval, and its staff criteria for how visible and how damaging an installation may be. If the markers have made more owners aware that their house sits in the district, that is useful: it is better to check before drilling than after. If you are unsure whether your address is covered, look it up on LPC's map or ask LPC directly before ordering exterior equipment.

Our building sits across from Playground 52 on Kelly Street. How do we keep our front camera on our own door rather than on the children?

Frame tight and angle down. Playground 52 is a 1.8-acre playground at 681 Kelly Street, and a wide camera on a building facing it will take in the fence, the benches and the kids if it is aimed level with the street. We mount the front camera so it looks down at the building's own door, steps and the sidewalk directly in front, and stop the frame short of the far curb. Where the camera supports privacy masking, we black out any part of the picture that still reaches across the street. The goal is a clear face at your own entrance, which is what helps you; footage of a playground helps nobody and worries parents. Residents are also more comfortable with a camera they can see is pointed at the building, not at them.

Apartment buildings, senior housing and nonprofit owners

At the Erma Cava Houses a robber walked past the security desk unchallenged. Can cameras warn desk staff when someone heads for the elevators without stopping?

Many recorders can, within limits. We draw a virtual line across the path from the entrance to the elevators, on a camera that looks along the desk, and set the recorder to flag anyone who crosses it during the hours the desk is empty or after a set time of night. The alert can show on the desk monitor, sound a tone, or reach the on-call phone of the manager or a staff member. During staffed hours, an alert for every resident who walks by would be useless noise, so most buildings use the line only when the desk is unstaffed and rely on a second camera at the elevator call button for the record. The camera does not decide who belongs. It gives staff a moment to look up, and it gives the building a clear face if nobody did.

Our building is run by a nonprofit that manages many others. As tenants, how do we put a camera request in front of the people who decide?

Write it down and be specific. A short letter to the management office, signed by as many households as are willing, works better than a call to the super. Say which spot you are worried about, such as the vestibule, the cellar door or the rear yard, what has happened there and roughly when, and what you are asking for. Include any police report numbers. Ask for a written answer. Organizations that manage hundreds or thousands of units, as Banana Kelly says it has, usually have a capital or repair budget process, and a clear written request is what enters it. You can also ask whether the building already has cameras that are not working, which is often the quickest fix. We work for whoever signs off, and when the office asks, we provide a plan and a written quote it can take to its own decision makers.

Our limited-equity co-op has shareholders who offer to watch the lobby monitor in shifts. Is that a good idea?

It can be, with rules. Volunteers watching a live monitor in the lobby or the office can spot a stranger at the door or a door propped open, and in a cooperative like Fox-Leggett, shared responsibility is part of the point. The risks are about privacy and fairness: volunteers see their neighbors' comings and goings, and a live view can turn into gossip. If the board goes ahead, give volunteers a live-only login with no playback or export, keep stairs and halls off the volunteer monitor if they reach apartment doors, write down what volunteers should do if they see trouble, and keep exports with one or two named officers. We set those account limits before we leave, so the board does not have to trust that nobody will scroll back.

A resident idea for Hunts Point and Longwood in the city's participatory budget asked for resident-only door codes. Can residents propose cameras the same way?

Residents can submit ideas through the city's participatory budgeting site, and one proposal tagged Hunts Point and Longwood asked that every building have a code or key only residents could use. We do not know which ideas the city funds, or whether private buildings are eligible, so we cannot promise that route will pay for anything. What helps any proposal is detail: which places, what problem and how many people it affects. For a private building, cameras are usually a decision for the owner, board or management office, and a resident proposal can be a way to show that a block shares a concern. If a public project goes ahead near you, it will be handled by the agencies involved, not by private installers.

The intercom rules put the speaker four to five feet off the floor. Is that why the panel camera at our door shows foreheads and chins instead of faces?

Often, yes. City rules for intercoms say the push buttons may be no more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must sit between four and five feet, and many video panels place their small camera in the same housing. That height suits a person standing still at the panel, but someone tall, someone wearing a hood or someone who walks past without stopping is seen at a steep angle or not at all. The panel camera is built for answering the door, not for recording everyone who enters. A separate camera inside the inner door, at head height and facing people as they come through, is what reliably records faces. We can put both on the same recorder, so the panel view and the doorway view sit side by side on one timeline.

The Longfellow Avenue robbery happened at noon. Should a building's outdoor cameras record all day, not only when they sense motion?

For the front of a building in Longwood, we recommend continuous recording around the clock. On a busy sidewalk, motion recording starts and stops constantly, and the gap between clips can fall exactly when something happens. Continuous recording uses more storage, so we size the recorder for the number of days you want kept, and we mark motion events on the timeline so you can still find a moment quickly. Daytime also needs its own setup: exposure that handles bright pavement and a dark doorway in the same picture, and an angle that keeps the sky out of frame. Back doors and cellars, where almost nobody should be, can record on motion with alerts, which saves storage without risking gaps.

Southern Boulevard, Westchester Avenue and Longwood Avenue businesses

Surveillance cameras caught the man breaking into a barbershop on Longwood Avenue. What should a barbershop or salon cover for the hours after closing?

Every way in and the things worth taking. Start with a camera inside the front door, framed so anyone coming through the door or under a gate faces it. Add one at any rear door or reachable window, because those are often the way in. Then one overview of the room that shows the stations, the product shelf and the register. Overnight, set those cameras to alert the owner's phone on motion, and record continuously so nothing is missed. During open hours, the counter camera covers the register and the door without filming clients in the chairs. Put the recorder somewhere a burglar would not find in a hurry, such as above the ceiling or in a locked box, and copy key clips off site, so the evidence survives even if the recorder is taken.

In a sneaker or apparel store on Southern Boulevard, which views matter most?

The door first, because everyone walks through it facing the same way under the same light, and a clear face there is worth more than any other view. Second, the display walls and tables, from cameras mounted high at the ends of each aisle, looking along the merchandise. Third, the stock room door and the back door, where deliveries arrive and where losses often happen out of sight of the floor. Fourth, the register, from above and slightly in front. In busy stores, staff need to see the floor live, so a monitor at the counter showing the aisles helps them spot trouble as it happens. We keep cameras out of fitting rooms entirely and frame any view of their entrance so it never looks inside.

We are getting a new storefront facade and sign. Is that the moment to add exterior cameras?

It is the best moment. Bronx Times reported that WHEDco ran a commercial corridor touch-up initiative that gave small businesses a facelift near the Freeman Street station, and any facade or sign job opens up the very spots a camera needs: the sign band, the soffit above the door, the gate housing. If cable is pulled into those spaces before they are closed, the exterior camera can be mounted cleanly with no exposed wire, and the camera positions can be agreed with the landlord at the same time as the sign. Tell us the sign contractor's schedule, and we will plan the cable a few days ahead of their install. If the building is inside the historic district, the facade work and any exterior camera should go to LPC together.

LPC's rules name security cameras specifically, alongside intercoms and light fixtures, among the objects that need approval when attached to a facade or areaway feature.
