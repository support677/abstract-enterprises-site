Why the Hudson Valley Is Different

Hudson Valley Condo Cameras Are Planned Around Hills, Trees and Weather

North of the city line, a condominium is rarely one building. It is more often a cluster of townhouses climbing a slope, a garden complex tucked behind a commercial strip on Route 9 or Route 59, or a gated community whose clubhouse sits a quarter mile up a private drive from the town road. The common elements a board is responsible for are spread across lawns, woods and parking areas, and the ground between them is seldom flat. A condominium security camera plan that works in a Bronx lobby falls apart on a property where the pool is forty feet below the clubhouse and the entrance sign is hidden behind a stand of oaks.

The valley also packs more variety into one region than anywhere else we work. Westchester's condo market is the largest of the six counties by a wide margin: OneKey MLS counted 1,250 condo sales there in the twelve months through October 2026, at a median of $560,000 (OneKey MLS, Oct. 2026). Ulster recorded 24 condo sales in the twelve months through September (OneKey MLS, Q3 2026). Between those extremes sit Rockland's river villages, Orange County's fast-growing towns along Route 17, Putnam's lake and reservoir country and the Route 9 corridor in Dutchess. A board in Mamaroneck and a board in Saugerties are both "Hudson Valley condos," but they share very little except the river.

Weather is the third difference, and it shapes the hardware more than anything else. Outages here are not rare events. A nor'easter in late September 2026 left more than 19,000 Central Hudson customers without power, with the worst damage in Woodstock, Shawangunk, Plattekill and Hurley (Central Hudson, Sept. 26, 2026), and storms in early July had already affected more than 47,000 Orange & Rockland customers. Lightning is a regular summer visitor on ridges and open lots. A condo camera system here needs battery backup, surge protection on every long run and a recorder kept somewhere dry and heated, or it fails on exactly the night the board needs it.

The fourth difference is law. Hudson Valley condos answer to the same state Condominium Act as city condos, but none of New York City's local laws travel north with the commuters. Boards here work from their declaration and bylaws, state statutes and their own town or village. We cover what that means for cameras, with sources, further down. For the whole security picture, including gates, call boxes, fobs and intercoms, see our condo security systems page for the Hudson Valley; this page is about cameras only.

Hudson Valley numbers that change a condo camera plan

  • Rockland condos sold at a twelve-month median of $475,000 through October 2026, across 543 sales, while Orange County's 359 condo sales had a median of $320,000 (OneKey MLS Rockland; OneKey MLS Orange). Budgets, and expectations, differ accordingly.
  • Putnam County recorded 75,093 lightning pulses and 35 thunder days in 2020, according to Earth Networks' state lightning report (Earth Networks, 2021). Long copper runs to poles and outbuildings are where strikes do their damage.
  • Roughly three-quarters of Putnam lies in the New York City watershed and more than nine-tenths sits on Hudson Highlands bedrock, per the county's hazard mitigation profile (Putnam County). Trenching for camera conduit regularly meets rock.
  • Dutchess County's July 4, 2026 storm knocked out more than 33,000 Central Hudson and 7,400 NYSEG customers, with the worst damage around Poughkeepsie, East Fishkill, LaGrange and Pawling (Dutchess County).
  • Police in Rye and the Town of Wappinger warned about thefts from cars in May 2026, with Rye police pointing to "cars left unsecured with keys or key fobs inside" (News 12, May 2026). Parking areas are where condo cameras earn their keep.
  • The Multiple Residence Law, not the city's Multiple Dwelling Law, covers cities under 325,000 people and every town and village (NY MRL §3), which takes in every condo in the six counties.

Coverage

Where Cameras Belong on a Hudson Valley Condo Property

We plan coverage from the outside in. The first cameras go where every car and every person must pass, then to the shared buildings, then to the places where problems have actually happened. Common elements under New York's Condominium Act include entrances and exits, "recreational or community facilities, parking areas and storage spaces" and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" (RPL §339-e), which is a fair map of where an association's cameras may go.

The entrance drive

Most Hudson Valley townhouse communities have one or two ways in, often a private drive that leaves a county or state road and winds uphill past a stone sign. An overview camera at the point where cars slow down, set to see the vehicle from the side rather than head-on into headlights, records every arrival. Where the board wants plates, a second camera with a narrow lens is aimed low at the spot where cars are slowest, usually a speed bump or a stop line. If the community has a gate, the cameras mount separately from the gate operator and call box.

The clubhouse

The clubhouse is usually the community's most valuable building and the natural home for the recorder. Cameras cover each exterior door, the lobby or entry hall, the management office door and any room with shared equipment, such as a fitness room or a billiards room. Rooms used for meetings and parties get cameras only at their doors, if at all, and restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms never.

The pool and courts

State law already requires that pool enclosure gates be self-closing and self-latching and kept locked when the pool is not in use or supervised (Executive Law §378). A camera on the gate shows whether the gate was left propped and who came through after hours. A second camera covers the deck from a corner, aimed away from the changing building. Tennis and pickleball courts usually share one camera on the entry gate.

Parking areas and garage rows

Car break-ins and hit-and-run damage are the most common reasons a board calls us after the fact. Cameras go at the entrance to each parking area and along rows of detached garages, mounted high enough to clear the snow piles plows push against the curbs every winter. In a townhouse community, cameras watch the shared lanes and guest spaces, not an owner's own driveway or garage door.

Lobbies, garages and elevators in condo buildings

Downtown condo buildings in White Plains, New Rochelle, Yonkers, Tarrytown and Nyack, including the region's few high-rise condo towers, need the coverage of a city building: both sides of the front door, the mail and package room, each elevator cab, the garage ramp and gate, and the stairwell doors at garage level. Elevator cab cameras use a wireless link or the traveling cable the elevator contractor approves; anything inside the elevator controller belongs to the elevator company.

Maintenance buildings, mail and trash

Maintenance garages, salt sheds and equipment buildings hold the association's tractors, snowblowers and tools. A camera on each door is cheap insurance. Mail kiosks and trash enclosures each get one camera aimed so the face of whoever is using them is visible.

Where a condo camera must never point

  • Into a home. Windows, sliding doors, patios, decks and private yards of individual units are out of frame or masked.
  • Restrooms, locker rooms and changing rooms. New York prohibits viewing devices in these spaces (GBL §395-b), and recording in them can be a felony (Penal Law §250.45).
  • Staff break rooms and changing areas used by maintenance or clubhouse staff.
  • Neighboring properties. Cameras along a property line are aimed at the association's land, and any part of a neighbor's yard in view is masked.
Cameras & Cable

Condo Security Camera Systems Built for Slopes, Rock and Distance

In condo video surveillance, the cameras themselves are the easy part of a Hudson Valley project. The hard part is getting a clean, powered signal from a pool fence or an entrance sign back to a recorder that may be hundreds of feet away, uphill, through trees, across ground that hides rock a few inches down.

Three ways to move the video

A network camera runs on one cable that carries both power and video, up to about 100 meters (328 feet) from the switch that feeds it. Inside that distance, we pull cable through attics, crawlspaces and conduit. Past it, there are three answers. Fiber between buildings carries video any distance and does not conduct lightning, which matters here more than almost anywhere. A point-to-point wireless bridge links two buildings with a clear line of sight and no digging. And an outdoor switch in a weatherproof box, with its own power, extends the reach from a second building. Each run on our site plan says which method it uses and what it costs.

Hills and sight lines

Hillside communities give cameras good views and wireless links bad ones. A camera on a clubhouse that sits above the parking areas can see a long way, but a wireless bridge between two buildings on either side of a rise may have no line of sight at all. We check each link with the actual mounting heights during the walk, and account for summer leaves, which can block a link that worked fine in March.

Rock, roots and frozen ground

When a run has to go underground, New York's dig-safe rule requires notice "at least two but not more than ten working days" before excavation (16 NYCRR Part 753), so markouts come first. Trenches across Hudson Valley lawns meet ledge rock, boulders and tree roots more often than they do on Long Island's sandy soil. Where rock is likely, we price an alternate route, such as along a building, under a walkway that is being replaced anyway, or over a wireless link, before the board approves anything. Trenching stops when the ground freezes and resumes in spring, which is a scheduling point for boards approving work in October.

Cameras that suit the valley

Turret cameras cover building doors and lobbies. Bullet cameras with long lenses suit entrance drives and parking areas. Full-color low-light cameras matter on properties lit only by a few path lights, where infrared turns everything gray. Dual-lens and panoramic cameras cover a whole parking area from one mount. Pan-tilt cameras have a place on a large site, but only as an extra; a camera that is looking left when something happens on the right records nothing useful.

The recorder and its closet

The recorder goes in a heated, dry, locked space, usually the clubhouse office or a utility room, never a pool pump room, an unheated garage or a shed. It sits on a battery backup sized to ride out short outages and shut down cleanly in long ones. Drives are sized for the retention the board chooses, usually 30 days or more at the resolution and frame rate the cameras need.

Seeing the cameras from somewhere else

Managing agents in the valley often handle communities in three counties from one office, and board members may be in Florida for the winter. Remote viewing through the manufacturer's encrypted app lets authorized people check the cameras and pull clips without driving over. Where the clubhouse has no wired internet, a cellular or fixed-wireless connection can carry remote viewing, with the data cost shown in the proposal.

Privacy & State Law

Hudson Valley Condo Camera Privacy: Your Bylaws and New York State Law

Nothing on this page is legal advice, and the association's attorney should review any camera plan that raises a question. What we can do is point boards to the rules that actually apply north of the city, with sources, so the conversation with counsel starts in the right place.

The declaration and bylaws come first

A condo board's authority to put cameras on common elements comes from the condominium's own documents, read with the state's Condominium Act. The Act defines the common elements and requires bylaws that cover, among other things, reserves "for major and minor maintenance, repairs, additions, improvements, replacements" (RPL §339-v). Most declarations let the board install equipment in common areas; some require an owner vote for improvements above a dollar threshold. That threshold, not a camera law, is what usually decides whether owners must vote.

Homeowners associations that are not condominiums

Many Hudson Valley townhouse communities are organized as homeowners associations, where each owner holds title to the lot and the association owns the roads, open space and clubhouse. The Attorney General's offering plan questionnaire for HOAs is issued under 13 NYCRR Part 22 (NY Attorney General). For cameras, the difference matters at the property line: an HOA can usually mount on what it owns, while a camera on an owner's townhouse wall needs that owner's agreement or an easement in the documents.

Video, yes; audio, almost never

New York's eavesdropping law makes it a crime to mechanically overhear a conversation without the consent of at least one party (Penal Law §250.00; §250.05). A microphone in a clubhouse lobby records conversations among people who never consented. We ship every condo system with audio turned off and leave it off unless the board's counsel approves a specific use in writing.

Spaces that are off limits

Bathrooms, locker rooms, changing rooms and similar rooms are covered both by the General Business Law ban on viewing devices and by the unlawful surveillance provisions of the Penal Law, which list "a bedroom, changing room, fitting room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom, shower" (Penal Law §250.45, cited above). Pool changing buildings and clubhouse locker rooms are the places a Hudson Valley condo is most likely to have one; they get no cameras inside, and the outside cameras are aimed so their doorways show who entered, not what is inside.

Owners' own cameras

Video doorbells and owner cameras are common in townhouse communities. Whether an owner can mount one on a wall the association maintains is a question for the declaration and the house rules. Practical boards adopt a short rule: owner cameras are allowed at the owner's own door, aimed at the owner's own entry, with no view into a neighbor's windows or yard. We can install owner cameras under the same rule, as a separate job billed to the owner.

Who may legally install the system

The installer should hold New York's state license for security and fire alarm installers, administered by the Department of State (GBL §69-m); ours is NYS license #12000287431. Power is a separate trade. Westchester requires plumbers and electricians to hold a county trade license (Westchester County Consumer Protection), and Rockland's code defines electrical contracting to include wiring for light, heat or power (Rockland County Code ch. 250). We do not do electrical work. When a camera pole or a new outlet needs line power, the association's licensed electrician provides it, and we coordinate the location and timing.

City laws that stay in the city

Board members who work in Manhattan sometimes bring city rules home with them. The Multiple Dwelling Law covers cities of 325,000 or more, so its entrance and intercom provisions do not reach Westchester's cities or any Hudson Valley town. The city's Local Law 63 of 2021 on smart access systems is a New York City law (intro.nyc). State laws such as the Penal Law, the General Business Law and the Executive Law's pool gate rule apply everywhere in the six counties.

Footage Policy

Footage, Police Requests and Who Holds the Passwords

A camera system is only as useful as the board's rules for its recordings. A clear written policy, adopted before the cameras go live, prevents most of the arguments we hear about later.

How long to keep video

FirstService Residential, one of the largest community management firms in the region, says condo and co-op footage is "generally stored for 30 to 90 days depending on the building size" (FirstService Residential, Sept. 23, 2024). For a Hudson Valley community where incidents in a parking area may not be noticed until an owner returns from a trip, 30 days is the practical minimum. We size the drives to the retention the board sets, and the recorder overwrites the oldest video automatically.

Who can see what

The same article recommends keeping "a log of requests and a list of anyone who accessed footage." We set up named accounts by role: the managing agent sees everything and can export; the superintendent sees live views; a board officer sees what the policy allows; the pool contractor, if any, sees only the pool. Owners asking for a clip go through the managing agent, and the log records each request.

When police ask

Local departments in the valley increasingly want to know where private cameras are. Rockland County Sheriff Lou Falco's Project Community Eyes, reported by News 12, aims to tell detectives "precisely where to knock after a crime or terrorist incident occurs," and Clarkstown police have asked residents and businesses to register their systems (News 12). Registration of that kind is a list of locations; it does not give police a live view. The board's policy should say who handles police requests, how clips are exported and logged, and when the association's attorney is called.

Signs

Signs at the entrance and at clubhouse doors telling owners and visitors that the common areas are recorded are standard practice and help deterrence. We supply and mount them as part of the job, worded to match the board's policy.

Board Approval

Taking a Camera Plan Through a Hudson Valley Board or HOA

Condo association security cameras are a board decision, and most Hudson Valley boards meet monthly, many are volunteers with day jobs in the city, and some govern communities where a third of the owners spend winter somewhere warmer. A camera proposal has to be easy to approve in one meeting and easy to explain to owners afterwards.

What goes in the board packet

  • A marked-up aerial showing each camera, what it sees and how its cable or link gets back to the recorder.
  • Phases with separate prices, so the board can approve the entrance and clubhouse now and the parking areas next budget year.
  • Running costs, including drive replacement, any internet line for remote viewing and any brand subscription.
  • A privacy page listing what is masked, that audio is off and who will have access.
  • The license number and insurance carrier, with the certificate issued on award naming the association and the managing agent.

Paying for it

Boards fund cameras from the operating budget, from reserves or through a special assessment, as their documents allow. A camera system is a reasonable reserve item because its parts wear out on a schedule: drives in a few years, cameras in seven to ten. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Developers still in control

In newer communities where the sponsor still controls the board, cameras installed now will belong to the owners after turnover. The HOA offering plan questionnaire asks, for example, whether certified financial statements will be provided to members while the sponsor is in control (13 NYCRR Part 22 questionnaire, cited above). Owners' representatives should ask the same kind of question about the camera system: who holds the passwords, and will they transfer at turnover?

Telling owners

The managing agent sends owners a short notice before work starts: which areas will be recorded, that nothing points into homes, who sees the footage, and the work dates. Owners who are away for the season get it by email. Communities that send the aerial with the notice get far fewer questions than those where cameras simply appear.

Weather & Power

Lightning, Nor'easters and Long Outages: Hardening a Condo Camera System

The single most common reason we replace a Hudson Valley condo camera system early is not theft or vandalism. It is weather: a strike that traveled down a long copper run, a recorder that corrupted its drive when the power dropped, or a camera that froze shut behind ice. Each has a known fix, and each fix belongs in the original design.

Outages measured in days

The valley's utilities report outages in the tens of thousands several times a year. After storms in early July 2026, Orange & Rockland said more than 47,000 of its customers had been affected (Orange & Rockland, July 6, 2026). In August 2020, Tropical Storm Isaias left 34,150 of NYSEG's 38,693 Putnam customers without power (Daily Voice, Aug. 2020). A battery backup on the recorder and the switch that powers the cameras keeps the system recording through short outages and shuts it down cleanly in long ones, so the drive survives. Communities with a clubhouse generator can put the camera equipment on it; we plan the outlet location with the association's electrician.

Lightning on ridges and open lots

A copper cable running a few hundred feet to a pole camera or a pool house is an antenna in a thunderstorm. We put surge protection at both ends of every outdoor run, bond it to a proper ground and use fiber for links between buildings wherever the distance or the exposure justifies it, since glass does not carry a strike from one building into the next. A protected system still loses a camera to a direct hit now and then; an unprotected one can lose the recorder, the switch and every camera on it.

Flooding near the river and the Sound

Riverfront and Sound Shore buildings keep recorders, switches and battery backups off garage floors and out of basements that take water. Cameras in garages that flood are mounted high, with connections in sealed boxes. After a flood, a camera that looks fine can have water in its connector, so a post-storm check of each low camera is part of our maintenance visits.

Snow, ice and cold

Plows pile snow along curbs and at the ends of parking rows, so cameras along lots go high enough to see over the banks and out of reach of the plow blade. Eave cameras are placed away from drip lines where icicles form. Cameras rated for low temperatures, with heaters where the spec calls for them, keep working through upper-valley January nights.

Trees, leaves and wildlife

Most walks happen when the trees are bare or leafing out, and what a camera sees in April is not what it sees in July. We ask the manager where the canopy fills in and aim accordingly. Motion analytics trained to recognize people and vehicles keep deer, branches and blowing snow from filling the alert log.

Existing Systems

Repairing, Upgrading or Replacing an Older Condo Camera System

Many Hudson Valley communities already have cameras, put in by the developer or by a board ten or fifteen years ago. Before recommending anything new, we find out what can be kept.

When a repair is the answer

A dead power supply, a failed drive or a camera knocked out of aim is a repair, not a project. If that is all the community needs, our Hudson Valley security camera repair service is the place to start.

When the cable can stay

Coax runs from an older analog system can often carry modern high-definition analog cameras to a new recorder, which saves the most expensive part of any upgrade: pulling new cable through finished buildings or across lawns. We test each run before we promise anything.

When the whole system goes

Systems with no working administrator password, recorders the manufacturer no longer supports, or cameras whose picture cannot identify a face at the door usually get replaced. Where possible we phase it, entrance and clubhouse first, so the community is never without recording.

Getting control back

The most common takeover problem is a system registered to a former vendor or a former board member's personal email. Resetting it can mean factory-resetting each device. We document the process and register everything to the association.

Keeping it working

A visit each spring and fall covers lens cleaning, aim checks after winter, drive health, firmware and a test export. Between visits, the recorder emails the managing agent when a camera drops offline or a drive reports errors, so nobody learns of a dead camera from an incident.

Network Security

Keeping the Community's Camera Network Private

A condo camera system shares the clubhouse with guest Wi-Fi, the office computers, smart TVs in the community room and sometimes a pool contractor's tablet. Each one is a way in unless the design closes it.

  • A separate network for the cameras, apart from guest Wi-Fi and office machines.
  • Nothing on the recorder exposed to the internet. Phones and laptops connect through the camera maker's encrypted cloud relay, so the clubhouse router never forwards traffic to it.
  • Factory passwords replaced on every device that has one, from the recorder down to each radio on a building link; the board keeps the only copy of the list.
  • Logins named for the job (manager, superintendent, treasurer) instead of anyone's personal email, so access ends the day a person leaves the role.
  • Encrypted wireless bridges with hidden network names.
  • Firmware current at handover and checked on every maintenance visit.

Some management firms in the valley run their own IT standards across every property they handle. If yours does, its rules govern the camera network too, and we settle the details with its technician well before our crew arrives.

Real Questions

Questions Hudson Valley Condo Boards Ask Us About Cameras

Our community climbs a steep hillside. Can one camera at the top see the lower parking areas?

It can see them, but usually not well enough to identify anyone. A camera looking down a long slope sees roofs and the tops of heads, and details shrink with distance. We use the high vantage point for an overview camera and put identifying cameras at the bottom, near where people park and walk, linked back up the hill by cable along a building or by a short wireless hop.

Cable internet doesn't reach our clubhouse. Can we still check the cameras from home?

Yes. The cameras record to the recorder in the clubhouse whether or not there is internet; remote viewing is the only part that needs a connection. A cellular router or a fixed-wireless service can provide it, and because live video uses data, we set remote viewing to a lower-resolution stream and pull full-quality clips only when needed. The monthly data cost is listed in the proposal.

Our water treatment building sits off by itself at the edge of the woods. Is a camera there worth it?

Usually yes. A community that runs its own well or pump building has equipment that is expensive to replace and essential to every owner, sitting in the place least likely to be noticed. One camera on the door, fed by fiber or a wireless link with surge protection at both ends, shows who opened it and when. Check whether the community's water operator or the health department has its own requirements for that building before work starts.

Plowed snow piles up along our parking rows every winter. How high should the lot cameras be?

High enough to look over a five-foot bank and stay out of reach of the plow and of anyone standing on the pile, which usually means 12 to 16 feet on a building or pole. Higher than that, faces become hard to identify, so we pick a lens that frames the parking row tightly from that height and aim it to see under the brim of a hat.

River fog hides our waterfront views on summer mornings. Is there a camera that sees through it?

Not in any meaningful way. Fog scatters both visible and infrared light, and infrared can make it worse by lighting up the droplets in front of the lens. What helps is moving identifying cameras closer to the doors and paths people use, so fog between the camera and the subject is a few feet rather than a few hundred, and keeping the long river view for overview only.

Residents in our 55-plus community want cameras that notice when a neighbor falls. Can the system do that?

A condo camera system is not a medical alert or wellness service, and we do not sell it as one. Cameras in common areas record what happened, but nobody is watching live, and fall-detection features in consumer cameras are not designed for this. Owners who want that protection should look at personal emergency response devices; the association's cameras can still help the board understand what happened on a path or in the clubhouse after the fact.

Some owners rent their units out on weekends. Can the cameras show the board how often guests come and go?

The cameras will record guests in common areas the same way they record everyone else, but turning them into a rental-tracking tool is a different matter. Whether the board may use footage to enforce rental rules depends on the declaration, the house rules and the footage policy, and counsel should weigh in first. We set the cameras up for security; how the board uses recordings is a governance decision.

We just put EV chargers in the guest lot. Should a camera cover them?

Yes. Chargers attract cable theft and damage from cars backing in, and disputes over who has been parked there all day. One camera at the end of the row, framing the chargers and the plates of cars using them, answers all three. Its cable can often share the trench or conduit that was dug for the chargers, which is why it pays to call before the lot is repaved.

Our condo is in a converted 19th-century mill with walls two feet thick. How does camera cable get through?

Slowly and deliberately. Wireless signals do not pass through that kind of masonry, so each floor or wing usually needs its own cable path. We follow existing chases, old freight shafts, sprinkler and electrical routes and the spaces above dropped ceilings, and core through a wall only where the board approves it. If the building is in a historic district, exterior cameras are placed where they are least visible from the street.

Our garden buildings share one attic with firewalls between units. Can camera cable cross them?

It can, but every hole through a fire separation has to be sealed again with a listed firestop product so the wall keeps its rating. We drill the smallest hole that works, sleeve it, seal both sides and note each penetration on the handover plan. Where a route would mean crossing many firewalls, running cable outside along the eaves in a protected channel is often cleaner.

Will summer leaves block a wireless link between two of our buildings?

They can. Wet leaves absorb the signal that point-to-point bridges use, so a link that tests perfectly in March can drop in July. We test with the antennas at their final height, look for paths above or around the tree line, use higher-gain equipment for wooded paths and recommend fiber where no clear path exists.

Can trenching for camera cable still happen after the ground freezes?

Generally not well. Frozen ground breaks equipment and the lawn cannot be restored until spring, so we finish trenched runs before the freeze and schedule the rest for when the ground thaws. Cameras on buildings, cable inside buildings and wireless links can go in all winter, so a late-fall approval usually means a winter install with the buried runs added in spring.

Reality Check

What Online Answers Get Wrong About Hudson Valley Condo Cameras

Board members usually research before they call. Search results, chatbots and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are mostly written for single-family houses or for city buildings. Here is how common claims hold up on a Hudson Valley condo property.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer for a spread-out property"

Battery and Wi-Fi cameras are easy to put up, which is why they show up in so many answers. On a condo property they run into the same three problems again and again: Wi-Fi from the clubhouse does not reach the far parking area, batteries die faster in a Hudson Valley January, and each camera stores clips in its own cloud account rather than in one recorder the association controls.

They also record clips, not continuous video. When the managing agent needs to see who walked past a car at 2:14 a.m., a clip-based camera may have recorded nothing at all.

Wired network cameras, with fiber or proper wireless bridges between buildings, cost more on day one and far less over the life of the system.

"Camera rules are the same everywhere in New York"

State laws, such as the Penal Law sections on eavesdropping and unlawful surveillance, do apply statewide. But many answers mix in New York City rules, such as the Multiple Dwelling Law or the city's local laws on building entry systems, as if they applied in Westchester or Dutchess. They do not.

The Multiple Residence Law covers cities under 325,000 people and every town and village, and local codes differ from town to town on things like alarm permits and electrical inspections.

For cameras, the condo's own declaration and bylaws usually matter more than any statute. Counsel should read them before the board votes.

"A cost guide tells you what a condo camera system costs"

National guides quote per-camera ranges built from house installs, where a camera goes on a wall twenty feet from a router. A condo camera at a pool fence 400 feet from the clubhouse, through rock, is a different job, and no national range accounts for it.

Guides also leave out what Hudson Valley jobs routinely need: surge protection, battery backup, trenching, restoration and the drive time to Dutchess or Ulster.

Our county price pages give real starting prices for the base package, and the property walk prices the rest in writing before anything is ordered.

"Battery backup is optional"

Many installation guides treat a battery backup as an add-on. In a region where a single storm can affect tens of thousands of utility customers, it is the difference between a recorder that rides through a flicker and one that corrupts its drive.

A battery will not carry cameras through a two-day outage, and nobody should promise that. What it does is keep recording through short drops and shut the recorder down cleanly in long ones.

For longer coverage, the camera equipment can go on a clubhouse generator, with the circuit provided by the association's electrician.

"Any electrician can install the cameras"

Electricians are licensed for power wiring; camera systems fall under New York's separate license for security and fire alarm installers, administered by the Department of State. The two trades overlap at the outlet, and a good project uses both.

An electrician will run power to a pole. Aiming cameras to identify faces, setting retention, separating the network and configuring role-based access is a different skill set.

Ask any bidder for the state license number. Ours is printed on every page of the proposal.

"Point a 4K camera at the entrance and it will read every plate"

Resolution helps, but plate reading depends mostly on angle, speed and light. A wide camera watching a whole entrance drive in 4K will still blur a plate on a car moving 25 miles an hour at night with its headlights on.

Dedicated plate cameras use a narrow lens, a fast shutter and infrared tuned for reflective plates, aimed where cars are slowest, such as a speed bump or a stop line.

On a Hudson Valley entrance with a curve and a hill, placement matters as much as the camera; we test with real cars before we finish.

"Cameras stop package theft and break-ins by themselves"

Visible cameras do deter some people, and recordings help police. But a camera does not lock a car or bring a package inside. When thefts from cars rose this spring, police in Rye and the Town of Wappinger answered by telling drivers to lock up.

Recordings are most useful when the community pairs them with simple habits and with the board's footage policy, so a clip can be found and exported the morning after.

The strongest result comes from cameras placed where they identify people, not just where they look impressive.

No. Cameras and recorders connect to the association's own outlets and network equipment, and owners' units and services are not touched. If a clubhouse circuit has to be shut off for an electrician's work, the managing agent gets advance notice so it can be scheduled around community events.

Can a unit owner hire you to put cameras inside their own condo?
+

Yes, as a separate job billed to the owner and priced from the same county camera page. Cameras inside a unit are the owner's own business, though audio is still off by default, and anyone who works in the home, such as a cleaner or caregiver, should be told. Cameras on common-element walls still need the board's permission.

Can the camera system double as smoke or fire detection for the clubhouse?
+

No. Cameras are not life-safety devices and do not replace smoke detectors or a fire alarm system. Fire alarm systems are designed and installed to their own code requirements as a separate system. Some cameras can flag smoke-like images, but nobody should rely on that for safety.

Does a downtown condo tower need a different camera system than a townhouse HOA?
+

The hardware family is the same, but the plan is not. A tower concentrates cameras on a few critical points such as the lobby, package room, elevators and garage, all inside one building. A townhouse HOA spreads cameras across acres, so most of its budget goes to distance, outdoor mounting and weather protection.

Our community has a fenced lot for owners' boats and RVs. What camera setup covers it?
+

Usually one camera on the gate to record every vehicle and person entering, and one or two wide cameras covering the rows. Storage lots tend to sit at the edge of the property with no power, so the cameras are fed by fiber or a wireless link from the nearest building, with surge protection on any copper.

What should the notice to owners say before cameras go up?
+

Keep it short: which common areas will be recorded, that cameras do not point into homes, patios or yards, that audio is off, who can view and export footage, how long recordings are kept and the installation dates. Including the marked aerial answers most questions before they are asked. The board's counsel should review the final wording.

Can a camera be mounted on a tree instead of a pole?
+

We advise against it. Trees sway in the wind, grow around the mount and move the camera out of aim, and the cable on a trunk is easy to damage. A short pole, a building corner or an existing light standard with the right bracket gives a steady picture for years.
