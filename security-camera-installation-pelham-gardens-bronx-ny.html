The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Gardens Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Pelham Gardens grew up as a place of houses with driveways, and that single fact decides more about a camera plan here than any brand or resolution. The land traces back to Thomas Pell, who bought it from the Siwanoy in 1654, and it stayed in Westchester County until the city took it in 1895. CityNeighborhoods.nyc calls the building of Pelham Parkway, between 1897 and 1908, the area's turning point, and the Esplanade on the western side was laid out in 1912 over the covered trench of the New York, Westchester and Boston Railway, the line that now carries the 5 train. Wikipedia dates the main building to the 1920s with a boom in the 1950s, while CityNeighborhoods puts most houses between the 1930s and 1950s; Point2 Homes gives a median construction year of 1952 and Homes.com says 1948. Either way, most houses are roughly seventy to a hundred years old. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as dominated by single-family homes with some small apartment buildings and two- and three-family homes, and notes that many private houses have basement studio apartments while still classified as one-family. CityNeighborhoods lists brick and stucco houses in Tudor Revival, Cape Cod and Colonial styles, with pitched roofs, bay windows and private driveways. The boundaries differ by source: Wikipedia uses East Gun Hill Road to the north and east, Pelham Parkway to the south and the Dyre Avenue line ending at the Esplanade to the west, while others draw the western edge at Laconia Avenue or add Stillwell Avenue to the southeast. It is ZIP 10469, Community Board 11 and the 49th Precinct, whose station house is at 2121 Eastchester Road. We go by your address, not by a line on a map.

Look at a typical listing and the camera job almost draws itself. A Redfin listing for a brick two-family on Waring Avenue describes an owner's unit upstairs, a rental below, a front patio, a private fenced backyard, and a garage with a driveway for two cars. That is five separate places to watch on one lot: the patio gate at the sidewalk, the front door, the driveway where it meets the street, the garage door at the back, and the yard behind the fence. Add a side entrance for a downstairs household and there are six. None of them is a lobby, and none of them is watched by a doorman. The data on who lives in these houses disagrees, too: Nextdoor shows about 73 percent homeowners for the Pelham Gardens neighborhood, while RentCafe's census-based figures say about half the residents rent, many in small buildings and in apartments inside houses. Both can be true at once on the same block, and that matters, because an owner can decide where a camera goes on the outside of a house and a tenant generally cannot. On many lots there is one owner, one or two tenant households and a set of cameras that each of them has some interest in. Sorting out who sees what is part of the job, not an afterthought.

The crime numbers are mixed, and we would rather show them than wave at them. Wikipedia lists the 49th Precinct's 2022 figures as 133 burglaries, 611 grand larcenies, 371 grand larcenies of vehicles and 273 robberies. Bronx Times reported that burglary in the precinct was up 77 percent in July 2023 compared with the same stretch of 2022, and that early in 2024 burglaries were up 87.5 percent. In a 28-day period in the fall of 2025 the paper counted 35 robberies in the precinct against 16 a year earlier. A Best in the Bronx roundup put reported major crimes at 2,266 in 2025, up from 2,121 in 2024, and at the February 2026 meeting of the Pelham Parkway Neighborhood Association, reported by Bronx Post, the precinct captain said five of the seven major categories rose in 2025, while residents raised mail and package theft. The 49th Precinct also covers Allerton, Morris Park, Van Nest, Pelham Parkway and Eastchester Gardens, so none of this ranks individual blocks, and Pelham Gardens has long been described as one of the steadier parts of the borough. What the figures do show is that thefts from cars, driveways and storefronts are a regular part of the precinct's work.

Pelham Gardens facts that shape where a camera goes

  • Houses, not lobbies: Wikipedia describes a neighborhood dominated by single-family homes, with two- and three-family houses and small apartment buildings, and many one-family houses holding a basement studio.
  • Driveways and garages are the norm: CityNeighborhoods lists private driveways among the area's defining features, and listings regularly describe a garage at the end of one.
  • Old walls: median construction years of 1948 to 1952 mean brick, plaster on lath and basements that have been rewired more than once.
  • Two unstaffed stations: Wikipedia notes that Gun Hill Road and Pelham Parkway on the Dyre Avenue line are among only five subway stations without a booth staffed around the clock.
  • Precinct-wide pressure on cars: 371 grand larcenies of vehicles in 2022, and 19 of the 252 thefts in the 2026 Bronx car-parts case, were in the 49th Precinct.
  • New small buildings: NY YIMBY reported permits for a four-story, 22-apartment building at 1456 East Gun Hill Road and an affordable lottery at 1429 East Gun Hill Road, alongside three-story houses filed on Young Avenue and Dewitt Place.

What Pelham Gardens owners tend to add next. Once the driveway and gate cameras are in, owners often ask whether the front door picture can ring an intercom handset upstairs and down, or whether the side gate could sense when it is left open. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a second system is likely, a spare cable to the front door, so the basement ceiling only has to be opened once.

The Clip Chain

Lining Up the Clips: Why a Pelham Gardens Camera Has to Agree With the One Next Door

Most private cameras in Pelham Gardens will never catch a crime in the act from start to finish, and they do not need to. Think about how the cases in the 49th Precinct were actually put together. Officers reviewing footage from several stores linked six burglaries to one person. Prosecutors in the 2026 car-parts case pointed to surveillance video from many separate places. The Thwaites Place garage thief was seen lifting a door, and police then looked at three other garages. In each case, the value came from setting one recording beside another: the same jacket at two doors, the same car at two corners, a time on one clip that matches the time on the next. A camera on a Pelham Gardens house earns its keep when its picture can join that chain. That depends less on how many megapixels it has than on four plain things that are easy to get wrong: the clock, the names, the direction of travel and how long the footage is kept. One clock for the whole block

Names a stranger can read

The person who opens your recorder after an incident may not be you. It might be a relative who lives upstairs, a tenant, or an officer from the 49th Precinct looking at the screen in your kitchen. Camera numbers mean nothing to any of them. We name each view by what it covers and which way it looks: "Front gate, looking to sidewalk," "Driveway mouth, looking to street," "Garage door from house," "Side path to yard," "Basement door, side of house." On a corner lot we add the street name. The same names go on the cable labels at the recorder and on the written map we leave, so if a camera is replaced years later the new one goes onto the right port with the right name.

Coming and going in the same frame

A camera that looks straight down at a door shows that someone opened it. A camera that looks along the driveway from the side of the house toward the street shows where they came from and where they went, which is the piece that lets the next camera along the block be checked. On most Pelham Gardens lots we place at least one camera so that the gate, the first stretch of the driveway and a slice of the sidewalk on both sides sit in one frame, low enough to record a face at walking pace. That view, more than any other, is the one neighbors and investigators ask about, because it connects your property to the street.

Days kept and clips locked

News of a car break-in or a garage entry often travels slowly: one neighbor mentions it, another checks their own car, and by the time anyone asks for video several days have passed. A drive that holds only three or four days of continuous recording will have written over the night in question. We size storage for the cameras you have and the days you want kept, set the driveway and gate cameras to record continuously rather than only on motion, and show you how to lock a clip so the recorder skips it when it overwrites. That locked clip, exported with the recorder's own player, is the version to hand over.

The Walk Home

From the Platform to the Porch: What House Cameras Can Do Along the Walk From the 5 Train

Pelham Gardens' two stations shape how people move through it. The Gun Hill Road station sits at East Gun Hill Road and Seymour Avenue, and the Pelham Parkway station has its entrance in the Esplanade median just north of the parkway. Wikipedia notes that these are among only five stations in the whole subway system without a booth staffed around the clock. Late at night, residents walk from those platforms down the Esplanade, along Waring, Mace and Astor Avenues, or across to Eastchester Road, past rows of houses with dark porches. The NYPD released surveillance video from the Gun Hill Road station after the platform assault, and News 12 reported the August 2026 robbery of a teenager near the Esplanade and Waring Avenue. Station cameras record the station. Everything between the turnstile and a front door is covered, if it is covered at all, by private cameras on private houses.

A house camera cannot watch the whole walk and should not try. What it can do is record its own short stretch well. That means a camera low enough at the front of the house to see a face, not the top of a head, as someone passes the gate; a shutter speed fast enough that a person walking briskly does not blur, which matters more at night than resolution does; and enough light at the gate and steps that the camera stays in color rather than dropping to grainy black and white. Many owners already have a motion light over the driveway, and we aim the camera to make use of it rather than stare into it. Framed this way, each house records a few yards of sidewalk clearly, and a block of such houses records the walk in pieces that can be lined up by time.

Brick two-families: one basement, two households, five outdoor spots

The brick two-family is the job we expect most in Pelham Gardens, and the basement is what makes it manageable. Most have an open or partly open basement ceiling running front to back, so cable for the front gate, the driveway and the rear yard can travel along the joists and leave the house through a few small, sealed openings in the brick rather than across the outside walls. We drill into mortar joints where we can, because mortar is easier to patch than brick, and every opening is sloped slightly outward and sealed so water runs away from it. The recorder usually goes in the owner's part of the house, in a closet or a dry corner of the basement well above any spot that has taken water. Because two households share the lot, we set up separate accounts before we leave: the owner sees everything, the tenant sees the front door and the shared gate, and neither sees inside the other's space. Where the downstairs household has its own side entrance, that door gets its own camera and goes on the tenant's account as well as the owner's.

Tudors and Colonials: deep eaves, stucco and an attic path

The Tudor Revival and Colonial one-families CityNeighborhoods describes tend to have steep roofs and generous eaves, which are a gift for cameras: a camera tucked under an eave is out of the rain, hard to reach from the ground and in shade for much of the day. The cable path usually runs through the attic to the eave and down inside the soffit, so nothing shows on the front of the house. Where the wall is stucco over masonry, we use a mounting block and anchors suited to it so the stucco does not crack around the screws, and we seal each anchor. The catch with a high eave is that the camera looks down steeply, so faces at the front door can come out as the tops of heads. On these houses we often pair a high eave camera on the driveway with a lower camera at the front door or under the porch roof, each doing the job it is best placed for.

Cape Cods and bungalows: low eaves and knee walls

Homes.com describes rows of bungalows on some Pelham Gardens blocks, and the Cape Cods sit alongside them. These houses put cameras closer to the ground, which is good for faces but means a camera can be reached by hand, so we choose hard-cased models and mount them on the eave corner rather than beside the door. Upstairs rooms often sit behind short knee walls, and the low triangle of space behind them can carry cable from one end of the house to the other without opening finished walls. Where there is neither a usable knee-wall space nor an open basement, a short run in painted outdoor conduit along a corner is the tidier choice.

New small buildings: plan cameras while the drawings are still drawings

The four-story, 22-apartment building filed at 1456 East Gun Hill Road, the 16-apartment building at 1429 East Gun Hill Road and the three-story houses filed on Young Avenue and Dewitt Place will all have block or concrete walls, drywall corridors and, in the larger ones, a riser closet. Camera cable in buildings like these should go in during construction, above the corridor ceilings and through one planned core in each block wall, not afterwards through a dozen small holes. The views a new building needs are the street door from inside and out, the mailboxes, the elevator or stair landing, any side door used for trash and the parking area if there is one. We work from the drawings with the developer or the general contractor, mark each camera and cable path, and leave a pull string and a labeled port for anything the owner decides to add after the tenants move in.

Medical practices on Eastchester Road: the front door, not the exam room

The practices that line Eastchester Road have a narrower camera job than a store, and a more sensitive one. The views that matter are the street door from inside, the waiting-room entrance, the reception counter from behind the staff and any rear or side door used for deliveries and staff. Exam rooms, treatment rooms, restrooms and changing areas are never on camera, and we check each view for mirrors and glass partitions that might reveal one. The reception camera is angled to show who is at the counter and what is handed across it, not the screens, the sign-in sheet or the paperwork on the desk; where a screen cannot be kept out of frame, we black out that part of the picture in the camera itself. Many of these practices occupy converted houses or the ground floor of a building with apartments above, so we keep the practice's cameras on the practice's doors and away from the residential entrance next to them.

Fuel and auto businesses: canopies, kiosks and glass doors

Norwood News described someone getting into the gas station at 1280 Allerton Avenue by breaking the glass of the front door. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Pelham Gardens: Questions Answered

Driveways, garages and cars

Most houses on our block have a long side driveway that ends at a garage behind the house. Is the camera better at the street end of the driveway or at the garage end?

They do different jobs, and if you can only have one, put it near the street end. A camera mounted on the front corner of the house, looking along the driveway toward the sidewalk, records each car and person entering, their direction and a usable face at walking distance. A camera at the garage shows the garage door and whoever is working on it, but by then the person has already walked the length of the driveway, often in shadow, and may be facing away. On a long Pelham Gardens driveway we usually recommend both: the street-end camera recording continuously at full quality, and a garage camera that records continuously and sends an alert only overnight. If the garage is detached and far from the house, the garage camera can wait for a second phase, but we would leave a cable or conduit for it during the first visit.

The Thwaites Place garage thief got into 23 cars in one visit. In a shared co-op garage, should the cameras watch the rows of cars or the ways in and out?

The ways in and out come first. In the ABC7 report, the most telling footage showed the thief lifting the garage door; that is the moment every incident in a shared garage passes through. Put a camera on the inside of each vehicle door and each pedestrian door, close enough to record a face as someone comes through, and a second on the ramp or driveway outside, aimed along it, to show how they arrived. Row cameras come after that and work best as overviews from the ends of each aisle, mounted high and hard-cased. A camera per car is rarely sensible and never complete. Record all of them continuously, sync the clock, and set the door cameras to alert the board's chosen contact if the door opens in the small hours.

Is a detached garage a sensible place to keep the recorder, given the summer heat and winter cold in an unheated building?

Usually not. A recorder's hard drive is the part most sensitive to heat, and a closed garage in a Bronx August can run far hotter than the house, while damp and cold in winter are hard on the electronics. A garage is also one of the first places a burglar looks, and a recorder there can be carried off along with the evidence of the break-in. We put the recorder inside the house, in a closet, a dry basement corner well above any spot that has taken water, or a cabinet in the owner's apartment, on battery backup. The garage cameras send their picture back over the cable we run from the house, so nothing of value needs to sit in the garage itself.

The street, the station and the front yard

Our house is on the walk between the Gun Hill Road station and Eastchester Road, and neither station on our line has a booth staffed all night. Is a camera on our house any use to neighbors walking home late?

It can be, within limits. Your camera can record your gate and a few yards of sidewalk each way clearly, with faces large enough to recognize, and with a clock that matches the next house's. On a walking route, a run of houses doing the same thing creates a record of the walk in short, matching pieces. To make yours useful, mount the front camera low enough to see faces, keep it in color with the porch or driveway light, record continuously rather than only on motion, and keep enough days that a neighbor asking later still finds the footage. Keep the frame on your own frontage and the curb, not into the houses opposite. A camera does not protect anyone on the walk at the moment it happens, but it gives the block a clear account afterwards.

Someone carried off a neighbor's beach chairs on Astor Avenue at noon on a weekday. Can a front camera tell us when something leaves our patio in daylight without pinging us for every passerby?

Yes, by narrowing what the camera alerts on rather than what it records. The camera keeps recording the whole patio, gate and sidewalk all day. Separately, we draw a detection zone tightly around the patio itself, inside the fence line, and switch on person detection only within that zone, so people walking past on the sidewalk do not trigger anything. Many cameras can also alert when a person crosses a line, such as the gate, in one direction. Daytime alerts can be limited to the hours nobody is usually home. The alert arrives with a short clip so you can see in a few seconds whether it is the mail carrier or someone lifting a chair. Recording continues whether or not an alert goes out.

Houses, wiring and household routines

Our Cape Cod's upstairs rooms sit behind short knee walls under the roof. Can camera cable travel through that space to reach the eaves?

Often, yes, and it is one of the neater routes in these houses. The triangle of space behind a knee wall usually runs most of the length of the house and opens onto the eave at each end. We get in through a small access panel or a closet that backs onto it, run the cable along the floor joists behind the wall, and come out through the soffit or a small sealed opening right at the camera. Insulation is moved and put back, not crushed. Where the knee-wall space is closed off or floored over, the basement and a short outdoor conduit up a corner of the house may be the better path, and the walk-through decides which.

Our 1940s brick two-family still has old alarm and phone wire from earlier owners. Does all of that have to come out before new cameras go in?

Not necessarily. Old wire that runs nowhere does no harm sitting in a wall, and pulling it out of plaster can do more damage than leaving it. What matters is knowing which wires are live and which are not. We trace each one with a tone generator, mark the dead runs, and cut back and cap any loose ends at the basement so nobody later mistakes them for camera cable. Occasionally an old run can be used as a pull line to draw new cable through a wall, which saves opening it. We remove old cameras, boxes and recorders that no longer work, patch their mounting holes, and leave the doorbell or any working intercom wiring untouched.

Several of us on the block work night shifts at the nearby hospitals and sleep during the day. Can the cameras keep recording while the alerts stay quiet during our sleeping hours?

Yes. Recording and alerting are separate settings. The cameras record continuously around the clock, and the alerts run on a schedule you set, so the phone stays silent from, say, nine in the morning to four in the afternoon while you sleep, and wakes up for the hours the house is empty or the night hours you are at work. If another household member is home during the day, their phone can take the daytime alerts instead. One exception is worth keeping: an alert for the side or basement door, if that door should never open while you are asleep, can still come through as a single quiet notification rather than a ring.

Practices, shops and stations

Our front desk on Eastchester Road has monitors and a sign-in sheet within sight of where a camera would go. How do we keep patient information out of the recording?

By aiming and masking, decided before the camera is mounted. We place the reception camera so it looks across the counter at the person standing there and at what is handed over, with the screens and the sign-in clipboard below or behind the frame. Where the layout makes that impossible, most professional cameras let us black out a fixed rectangle in the picture, so that area is never recorded. Audio stays off. The recorder sits in a locked room, and the practice keeps a written list of who may view or export clips. We also check the waiting-room view so it covers the entrance rather than recording patients sitting for long periods.

Officers in the 49th Precinct linked six store burglaries to one person by reviewing camera footage from several shops. What does our shop's footage need so it can be matched with the stores near us?

Four things. A synced clock, so your times agree with the other stores' and with police reports. A door camera at head height that records a recognizable face and clothing, since clothing and gait are often what link one store to the next. A view of the sidewalk outside the door that shows which way the person came and went. And enough days of storage that a request arriving a week later still finds the footage, which in practice means continuous recording on the entrance and at least a couple of weeks on the drive. Add plain camera names and a practiced export, and your store's recording becomes one usable piece of a pattern rather than a file nobody can line up.

We run a gas station with pumps under a canopy and a small kiosk. Which views matter most after dark?

Start with the kiosk door, recorded from inside the glass so the camera survives a broken door, and the counter. Then the pump islands, from cameras under the canopy angled so the canopy lights fall behind them rather than into the lens, which keeps faces and vehicles readable. Next, the entrance and exit lanes, each with a camera aimed along the lane at about bumper height so every vehicle arriving is recorded front-on. Finally, the back of the building and any air or vacuum stations. Record everything continuously, sync the clock, and keep the recorder in a locked, ventilated spot away from the counter.

Licenses, Privacy and Permission

The Rules Behind a Pelham Gardens Camera Job, Without the Guesswork

Most Pelham Gardens camera jobs need no city paperwork at all, which makes it easy to forget the rules that do apply. They come from four places: the state's installer licensing law, New York's privacy laws, the ownership of the house or building, and the city's rules about electrical work and fire-rated walls. Here is how each one plays out on the houses, buildings and shops in this neighborhood.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D, administered by the Department of State, requires a license for anyone in the business of installing or servicing security systems. A legal article from Kirschenbaum & Kirschenbaum explains that a camera system counts when it is used, full-time or part-time, to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft or movement, which describes nearly every house and shop camera in Pelham Gardens. The Department of State's application also makes clear that home or business owners installing a system on their own property are not "in the business" of installing, so a homeowner putting up their own cameras is not breaking the law. The question is the handyman or contractor who does it for pay. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, and we will send a copy to an owner, a board or a landlord who asks. One installer's website claims New York City requires a licensed electrician for any wired camera installation. We could not confirm that, and it does not match the state's framework for low-voltage work. What is true is that new line-voltage power, such as a new outlet at a garage or a new circuit in a basement, is electrician's work, and when a job needs it we say so on the quote.

Where a camera may look. A New York news explainer on neighbor cameras sums up the law plainly: New York does not prohibit filming people in places that can be seen from public, including lawns, driveways and front porches. Civil Rights Law §52-a, often called the backyard camera law, gives a resident a civil claim against a neighbor who records them where they reasonably expect privacy, and Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places such as bathrooms or bedrooms without consent. On these close-set lots, the practical rule is simple: we frame every outdoor camera on your gate, your steps, your driveway, your yard and your curb, and if a neighbor's window, yard or door falls inside the frame, we change the angle or black out that part of the picture. We set cameras to record video, not sound.

Who decides. The owner of a house decides what goes on its outside walls and in its shared spaces. In a two-family where the owner lives upstairs, that is straightforward; in a house owned by family members who live elsewhere, we ask for written approval from whoever holds title or manages it. A tenant can put an indoor camera inside their own apartment, aimed at their own door or rooms. In a semi-detached house, the party wall and any shared walk involve both owners, and we will not mount on a shared surface without both agreeing in writing. In a co-op on the Pelham Parkway edge, building-wide camera changes usually go through the board, as one New York building-security guide notes. A new building's cameras belong to its developer until the building is handed over, and we put the handover of logins in writing.

Is anyone required to have cameras? No general city law requires cameras in houses or apartment buildings, as one New York compliance guide notes. Owners add them because they want a record. That also means there is no city standard for what a good system is, which is why we write down what each camera covers and how long it keeps footage.

Landmarks. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district or individual landmark inside Pelham Gardens; the nearest landmarked site the research turned up is the Bartow-Pell Mansion in Pelham Bay Park, well outside the neighborhood. We still check the address on the Commission's map before drilling, because it takes a minute and saves a problem.

Walls and openings. Every hole we make through brick is sealed against water, and every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in a small building or a new building is sealed with rated firestop. In a two-family, the basement ceiling is often the separation between the downstairs household and the stair, and we treat any opening through it the same way.
