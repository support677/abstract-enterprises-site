The Neighborhood

Why Soundview Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Soundview was farmland, then a resort shore, then one of the largest concentrations of public housing in the city, and each of those layers left a different kind of building for a camera to sit on. The land east of the Bronx River was part of Westchester County until New York City annexed it in 1895. Clason Point, at the southern tip, drew crowds to beaches and to an amusement park that ran from 1910 to 1935. In 1941 Clason Point Gardens became the first public housing development the New York City Housing Authority built in the Bronx, and in 1947 a temporary project of 947 apartments in 473 Quonset huts went up nearby. The area stayed largely undeveloped until the 1950s, when the Bronx River Parkway and the Bruckner Expressway made it easy to reach, and then it filled quickly: Bronx River Houses in 1951, Soundview Houses in the mid-1950s, the Bronxdale Houses, now the Sotomayor Houses, in 1955, and the James Monroe Houses in 1961. Mitchell-Lama towers followed in the 1960s and 1970s along Lafayette, Morrison and Boynton avenues and at Stevenson Commons. Today the area people generally call Soundview runs from the Cross Bronx Expressway in the north to Lacombe Avenue in the south, and from the Bronx River in the west to White Plains Road in the east, with the Bruckner Expressway cutting it in two. Community Board 9 treats Bronx River, Clason Point, Harding Park and Soundview/Bruckner as communities of their own, and this page concentrates on the blocks between the Cross Bronx and Lacombe Avenue.

The housing numbers explain why so much of the camera work here is private, even in a neighborhood known for its public developments. Point2 Homes puts the median construction year of Soundview's homes at 1956, with about 36.6 percent built before the 1940s and another 9 percent by 1949. Of roughly 15,405 homes it counts, about 6 percent are detached houses, 8.5 percent attached houses and 12.7 percent two-unit buildings. City Neighborhoods describes one- and two-family homes filling the streets north of Lacombe Avenue and modest apartment buildings rising along Watson and Soundview avenues. Nine NYCHA developments sit inside the neighborhood, among them the 28 buildings and nearly 1,500 apartments of the Sotomayor Houses, the thirteen seven-story buildings of Soundview Houses and the twelve buildings of 8, 14 and 15 stories at the James Monroe Houses. Those developments have their own cameras, run by their own operators. Every brick walk-up, every two-family house, every co-op and every store in between depends on its owner to decide what is covered and how well.

The reporting from the last few years shows where private footage has counted. News 12 described how a 17-year-old was fatally shot around 3 a.m. on September 21, 2024, and how surveillance cameras at a deli at Evergreen and Westchester avenues recorded it; police later identified a man they wanted in the case. News 12 also reported that, for a December 8 shooting on Watson Avenue, police released video showing both suspects walking into a residential building. CBS New York reported that in June 2026 two men were killed just after 10 p.m. outside a home on Westchester Avenue, two men who often played dominoes with neighbors at the Morrison Avenue Street Plaza, and that the 43rd Precinct had by then recorded five homicides in 2026, about 5 percent of the citywide total. The Bronx Times reported that a masked gunman fatally shot a man outside a smoke shop on Westchester Avenue on August 7. And in 2020 the Bronx Times described a phone store on Westchester Avenue whose glass window was smashed at about 2:40 a.m. and whose front gate was broken before cash and merchandise were taken. These are painful stories, and we include them only because they show plainly what this page is about: the camera that answers the question afterward is very often one a shopkeeper or a building owner chose to install.

The longer picture is calmer than those headlines suggest, and it is worth saying so. Wikipedia's summary of the NYPD's figures for the 43rd Precinct, which patrols Soundview along with Police Service Area 8 for the housing developments, shows major felonies falling from about 9,500 in 1990 to 3,143 in 2025, and burglaries down by more than 85 percent over that period. So a Soundview camera plan is rarely about blanketing a building. It is about a handful of places that keep coming up: the street door and vestibule of a walk-up, the courtyard or side path that leads to it, the cellar door nobody watches, the counter and back door of a store that handles cash late at night, the sidewalk in front of it, and the front door and curb of a house. No city law requires cameras in houses or apartment buildings, so each of these is the owner's choice. Our job is to make that choice produce footage someone can use.

Soundview facts that decide where a camera goes

  • A mid-century building stock: a median construction year of 1956 means masonry walls, cellar ceilings with decades of old wire, plaster halls and stairwells built long before anyone planned for cameras.
  • Private buildings between public campuses: nine NYCHA developments sit inside the neighborhood, and their cameras end at their property lines, so neighboring walk-ups, houses and stores need their own coverage.
  • A long camera history: NYCHA tested closed-circuit TV in three Bronxdale Houses buildings in 1976, and the NYPD finished installing 135 cameras at Bronx River Houses in 1999.
  • A transit and shopping spine: the elevated 6 runs above Westchester Avenue with stops at Elder Avenue, Morrison Avenue–Soundview and St. Lawrence Avenue, and the avenue mixes a few national chains with many family-owned shops.
  • Towers with boards and housing companies: Lafayette-Boynton's four buildings hold 972 apartments, Stevenson Commons was built with nine Mitchell-Lama buildings and 948 apartments, and co-op towers such as the 19-story building at 825 Morrison Avenue each run decisions through a board.
  • New buildings still arriving: construction has started on a 22-story building at 1920 Turnbull Avenue with 228 affordable apartments, and new buildings have opened in recent years on former parking and open land at Stevenson Commons, Lafayette-Boynton and the Sotomayor campus.

What Soundview owners tend to add next. Once a walk-up's street-door camera is working well, owners often ask whether the same view can show on a video intercom panel, or whether the door can be released for a delivery from the phone app that shows it. Store owners who start with a counter camera usually come back for the sidewalk view. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where an entry upgrade is likely, a spare cable to the door, so the cellar ceiling only has to be opened once.

Fifty Years of Footage

From a Lobby Channel at Bronxdale to a Deli Camera on Westchester Avenue: What Soundview Has Learned About Cameras

Few neighborhoods in New York have as long a record with cameras as Soundview does. In 1976 the Housing Authority started an experimental closed-circuit TV program in three buildings of the Bronxdale Houses, now the Sotomayor Houses, to deter rising crime. Cameras went into the lobbies and the elevators, and the pictures were sent on a split screen to a channel on residents' own television sets, so tenants could watch their common areas and report anything suspicious to the Housing Authority police. An earlier security plan for the same development had proposed surveillance of the grounds, intercoms and other changes meant to give residents more oversight of shared space. At Bronx River Houses, residents organizing in the late 1970s pushed for visitor sign-ins and better building security, and by the early 1990s the development had cameras in lobbies and elevators. In 1999 the NYPD finished installing 135 closed-circuit cameras there. That history is worth knowing because the questions it raised are the same ones a Soundview owner faces today: who watches, who gets told, how far the coverage reaches, and whether the picture is good enough to help.

The recent cases add a second lesson. The footage that police described in News 12's and Fox 5's reporting did not come from public cameras. It came from a deli at a busy corner, from a residential building's entrance, from inside a bodega. Each of those owners had decided, at some point, to put up cameras for their own reasons, and each camera ended up recording something far more serious than the shoplifting or the package dispute it was probably bought for. We cannot promise that any camera will matter in that way, and we would never sell one on that basis. But it does tell a Soundview owner what to ask for: a camera that records continuously, with a clock that is correct, mounted where it sees a face, on a recorder that keeps footage long enough for someone to ask.

Who watches: the Bronxdale question, fifty years on

The 1976 idea of putting the lobby on every tenant's TV was bold, and it shows a real tension. A camera nobody looks at is a recording for later. A camera everybody can look at becomes a way to watch neighbors come and go. For a private Soundview walk-up today, we usually recommend a middle path: the owner and the super hold full access, any managing agent gets the views they need, and tenants, if the owner agrees, see only the street-door view, live, without the ability to scroll back through other people's comings and goings. In a co-op the board writes that rule down. The aim is that someone who can act sees the picture quickly, and nobody gets a tool for keeping tabs on the building.

How far the coverage reaches: the edge of the campus

The 135 cameras at Bronx River Houses, and the camera systems at the other developments, watch the developments. The private walk-up on the next block, the row of two-family houses across the avenue and the corner store facing the campus get nothing from them. Owners sometimes assume otherwise because they can see police or housing cameras from their windows. When we plan a building that faces a campus, we cover its own door, its own side path and its own stretch of sidewalk, and we keep the view off the development's grounds, which belong to someone else.

Whether the picture helps: the deli's lesson

A store camera on Westchester Avenue sits under the steel of the elevated line, beside bright signs and among headlights, and the hour it matters most may be 3 a.m. That is a hard place to get a clean picture. The details that decide whether a night clip shows a face are not exotic: the camera sits just inside the glass so it cannot be knocked out of aim, it looks along the sidewalk rather than straight at the headlights, its exposure is set for the street lights rather than for the bright sign beside it, it records continuously rather than only when it senses motion, and its clock is synced so the time on the clip is the real time. We set those details deliberately on every storefront we do.

What a building's entrance camera owes the people inside

The Watson Avenue video showed people walking into a residential building. Somewhere in that building were tenants who never saw who came in. A street-door camera in a Soundview walk-up should be mounted so it records each person's face on the last few steps before the door, with a second view inside the vestibule that records the inner door. Those two views together show who came in, whether they were let in or followed someone, and when. They should be on a recorder in a locked room, not in the vestibule itself, and the owner should be able to save a clip in minutes when a tenant or an officer asks.

Who Owns the Wall

Tenant, Owner, Board, Housing Company or Store Landlord: Who Says Yes to a Soundview Camera

Before anyone talks about cameras, a Soundview job has to answer a plainer question: whose wall is it? The neighborhood mixes ownership more than most. Within a few blocks you can find a NYCHA tenant, a renter in a private walk-up, a shareholder in a co-op tower, a resident of a Mitchell-Lama building whose contracts pass through HPD, a condo owner, a family that owns a two-family house and rents the upstairs, and a store tenant whose facade belongs to the landlord upstairs. Each of them can say yes to some cameras and not others. Getting this right first saves money, because a camera mounted without permission may have to come down, and it saves arguments, because the person who approved a camera is usually the person who decides who sees it.

NYCHA households: start inside

The developments that dominate Soundview's map are home to thousands of families, and many of them call us wanting a camera at their door after a package theft or a frightening encounter. The honest answer starts with the fact that the door, the hallway and the lobby belong to the Housing Authority. Tenants generally cannot install cameras in common hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces without the landlord's permission. What a household fully controls is the inside of the apartment, and a small indoor camera facing the inside of the front door, recording who comes in and when, is often the most useful thing a tenant can do. If management allows a particular door camera, we can help set it up so it does not need drilling and keeps its recordings on a memory card. We never mount anything on NYCHA property without the Authority's approval.

Private walk-ups: the owner decides, and owes tenants clear limits

Owners of the brick walk-ups along Watson and Soundview avenues can put cameras in their lobbies, hallways, stairwells, cellars and parking areas. They cannot point them into apartments, and New York's Penal Law makes it a crime to record people in places like bathrooms and bedrooms without consent. Recording sound brings in the state's eavesdropping law, so we set building cameras to record video only. Good practice goes further than the law: tell tenants where the cameras are, frame hallway views so an open apartment door does not show the room behind it, and decide in writing who may review footage. A building that does this rarely has a camera dispute.

Co-op towers: the hallway belongs to the board

In a co-op, the hallway outside a shareholder's door is a common area the board controls, so a shareholder cannot install hardware there alone. Soundview's co-op towers, including the former Mitchell-Lama buildings along Morrison and Lafayette avenues, run exterior and common-area cameras through the board and usually through the managing agent. We write a proposal that names each camera, shows what it sees, lists the cable route and says who will have access, because that is what boards need to vote.

Mitchell-Lama buildings: contracts that HPD reviews

For housing companies supervised by HPD, the city's Mitchell-Lama rules require contracts to be awarded at the lowest cost compatible with quality. Above a dollar threshold set in the rules, the company needs at least three competitive bids and HPD's written approval, and HPD can ask to see smaller contracts too. Required contract language includes a clause that no work begins until HPD approves the agreement and that HPD may inspect before final payment. That is not a reason to avoid cameras. It is a reason to ask every bidder to price the same list of cameras and runs, so the comparison is real. We read the rules as an installer, not as lawyers; the housing company's attorney and managing agent are the people to confirm what applies.

Houses with tenants: whose camera, whose view

Many Soundview two-family houses have an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other. The owner controls the outside of the house and the shared entrance. The tenant's apartment is the tenant's. We set up separate views when both households want to see the front door, keep the yard or driveway views with the owner, and make sure no camera looks into either apartment's windows. A written note in the lease about where the cameras are and who sees them heads off most disagreements.

Six-story walk-ups: the door, the stair and the cellar

Soundview's mid-century walk-ups follow a familiar pattern: a street door at the top of a few steps, a small vestibule with mailboxes, a single stair that turns at each landing, and a cellar reached by an inside stair and an outside door at the side or rear. Some open from a courtyard rather than straight onto the sidewalk. The camera plan starts outside the street door, with a camera mounted at about head height on the approach so it records faces on the last few steps, rather than high on the facade looking down at hats. Inside the vestibule, a second camera records the inner door. The cellar door, which no tenant sees from a window, gets its own camera, and in buildings with a courtyard we add one that follows the path from the sidewalk. Stair landings come next, framed so each run of stairs is visible without catching any apartment door straight on. Cable for the outside and vestibule cameras travels across the cellar ceiling and comes up through one or two sealed openings; cable for the landings rides a pipe chase, a stair stack or a closet stack rather than the face of a hallway wall. The recorder sits in a locked cellar room or the super's office, away from the boiler and above any spot that has taken water.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Soundview: Questions Answered

Stores and counters on Westchester Avenue

If someone comes around behind our bodega's counter, which camera shows it best: one over the clerk's shoulder, or one facing the counter from the door?

You need both, because each answers a different question. A camera mounted above and slightly behind the clerk's position looks out across the counter and records the face of everyone who stands there, which is the view that identifies a person. A camera on the customer side, aimed at the register and the space behind the counter, records what happens back there: who comes around, what they reach for and which way they go. In the August 2026 case Fox 5 described, surveillance video showed the gunman entering, firing and then going behind the counter to take cash, which is exactly the sequence a single front-facing camera would only half capture. Add a head-height camera at the door and the recording shows a person arriving, approaching the counter, crossing behind it and leaving, each from a clear angle.

Our deli's coolers and freezers cycle on and off all day. Can that upset the camera recorder?

It can. Large compressors starting up draw a surge that can make lights flicker and, on a shared circuit, cause a brief dip in voltage. A recorder and a switch powering several cameras do not like those dips, and some power supplies restart when they happen, which leaves gaps in the recording and can corrupt a drive over time. The fix is to keep the recorder, the switch and the modem on a battery backup unit that steadies the power rather than only switching over in a full outage, and, where an electrician agrees, to keep them off the circuit that feeds the coolers. We size the battery unit to the actual load and check the recorder's log for unexpected restarts before we leave.

Our shop's counters and display cases are all glass. How do you keep the counter camera from recording nothing but reflections?

By choosing the angle before choosing the camera. A camera looking straight down onto a glass case at the same angle as the ceiling lights sees the lights reflected back, and at night the case can look like a white sheet. Mounting the camera so it looks across the counter at a shallower angle, from a spot where no bright fixture sits directly behind it in the reflection, usually solves most of it. Moving the camera a foot or two often matters more than any setting. Where the reflections persist, a camera with a lens chosen for that distance and exposure set for the faces, not for the brightest spot, keeps the customer readable. We test with the store's real lighting on, at the hours you are open.

Walk-ups and mid-century buildings

A ceiling came down in a building on St. Lawrence Avenue. Before you mount a camera on our old hallway ceiling, how do you know it will hold?

We look before we drill. Citizen logged a person hurt after a ceiling collapsed at an address on St. Lawrence Avenue, and older Soundview buildings can hide sagging plaster, water damage or loose lath behind fresh paint. On the walk-through we check each ceiling spot for cracks, stains, bulges and hollow sounds, and we ask the super about any leaks above. A camera itself is light, but the mount has to grip something sound. Where the ceiling is doubtful, we move the camera to a wall position that sees the same space, fix it into masonry or a solid stud, or mount it on a small plate spanning to solid framing. If we find damage that looks like a hazard, we tell the owner in writing, because that is a building repair, not a camera job.

Tenants charge e-bike batteries in our cellar. Can a camera there tell us when someone plugs one in overnight?

A camera can show who brings a battery into the cellar and when, and it can send an alert when a person enters that room at night, which gives an owner a factual basis for enforcing whatever charging rule the building sets. It cannot tell you that a battery is overheating, and it is not a fire detector. The FDNY confirmed lithium-ion batteries were found after a 2025 fire on Fteley Avenue, which is a reminder that the right protection for charging areas is proper smoke and heat detection, installed to code, and a clear building policy. We mount the cellar camera high in a corner so it sees the door and the outlets, record continuously, and keep the recorder somewhere other than that room so the footage survives whatever happens there.

A tenant asked to see the hallway footage after her door was damaged. Does the owner have to show it?

As a general matter, a landlord is not obligated to share surveillance footage with a tenant, though many owners choose to when it helps settle a problem. What we recommend is a written rule, decided in advance, that says who may review footage, how a tenant asks, and when a clip will be saved and handed to police instead. If the damage might be a crime, the cleaner path is for the tenant to report it and for the owner to save the clip and give it to the officers, so a recording of other tenants' comings and goings is not passed around the building. Either way, lock the clip right away so the recorder does not write over it while the decision is made.

Can our building put a camera in the shared laundry room in the cellar?

Yes, a laundry room is a common area, and owners can install cameras in common areas. The camera should be mounted high in a corner opposite the door, so it records each person's face as they enter and an overview of the machines, without pointing at a folding area where someone might be changing or at any adjoining room. Record video only. Tell tenants the camera is there. Laundry rooms are usually damp and warm, so we use a sealed dome housing and keep cable runs away from the dryer vents. Many owners find that a laundry room camera ends arguments over damaged machines and missing clothes quickly.

Co-op and Mitchell-Lama towers

Our Mitchell-Lama housing company has to collect competing bids for a camera project. How do we make sure the bids can actually be compared?

Write the scope before you ask for prices. HPD's rules for Mitchell-Lama contracts call for the lowest cost compatible with quality, competitive bids above a threshold, and HPD's written approval before work starts, and none of that works if one bidder prices eight cameras, another twelve, and a third leaves out the cable to the parking lot. A scope that names each camera location, what each must show, the cable route, the recorder's storage in days, battery backup, the number of user accounts and the handover items lets every bidder price the same job. We will walk the property and help a board draft that list, and we price our own bid against it line by line. The housing company's attorney and managing agent should confirm how the rules apply to your contract.

Our tower has old analog cameras in the garage and newer network cameras in the lobby, on two separate recorders. Can one recorder run both?

Often, yes. Hybrid recorders accept both the coaxial cable from older analog cameras and network cameras over Ethernet, so a building can bring both onto one screen and one set of accounts while it decides when to replace the garage cameras. Whether it makes sense depends on what the old cameras can still show; analog cameras of a certain age may record so little detail that keeping them costs more in usefulness than replacing them. We test each old camera's picture, trace its coax back to see if the cable can carry a modern signal, and give the board a written choice: keep and connect, replace on the same cable with a newer camera type that uses coax, or rewire.

Licenses, Leases and the Law

The Rules Behind a Soundview Camera Job, Laid Out Plainly

Soundview has fewer layers of permission than some Bronx neighborhoods. We found no city historic district or individual landmark among its residential blocks, so the Landmarks Preservation Commission is unlikely to be involved in an ordinary camera job here; if your building has a special designation, we check before planning anything visible. What does apply comes from five places: the state's installer licensing law, the lease or co-op documents, HPD's Mitchell-Lama rules for those buildings, the state's privacy and eavesdropping laws, and ordinary building code requirements for the holes we make.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D says no one may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems, or advertise that they can, without a license. The state's own application materials describe some passive devices differently, and installers argue about where cameras fall, but we do not ask customers to sort that out. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and we send a copy to any owner, board or managing agent who asks. Owners mounting a camera on their own property are not in the installing business. New line-voltage power, such as a new outdoor outlet or a circuit for a lot pole, is electrician's work, and the quote says so when a job needs it.

Tenants and common areas. Landlords can install cameras in common areas such as lobbies, hallways, stairwells and parking areas. Tenants generally cannot put cameras in those shared spaces without the landlord's permission. In a co-op, the board controls the hallways and the building's exterior. In public housing, the Housing Authority controls everything outside the apartment. Our practice is to get written permission from whoever controls the wall before anything is mounted, and to keep a copy with the job file.

Where a camera may look. New York does not prohibit recording what anyone can see from public, such as a sidewalk, a stoop or a storefront. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives residents a claim against a neighbor's camera aimed into places where they reasonably expect privacy, such as a backyard, and Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places like bathrooms and bedrooms without consent. On Soundview's tight blocks, where a two-family house's side camera can be a few feet from the neighbor's window, we frame every view on your own door, path, yard and curb, and black out anything else in the camera itself.

Sound. Recording conversations brings in the state's eavesdropping law, which turns on whether one party to the conversation consents. Building and business cameras record video only on our installs, which avoids the question entirely.

Mitchell-Lama contracts. Housing companies supervised by HPD follow the city's rules for awarding contracts, which call for the lowest cost compatible with quality, competitive bids and HPD approval above set thresholds, and HPD inspection before final payment. We supply scopes and pricing in a form that fits that process.

Is anyone required to have cameras? No general city law requires cameras in houses or apartment buildings. The state's Multiple Dwelling Law requires buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a working two-way intercom and self-closing, self-locking doors, which is a separate requirement. A camera does not take the place of a required intercom, and an intercom violation is fixed by fixing the intercom.

Holes and firestop. Any opening we make through a fire-rated wall or floor, such as between a cellar and a stair hall, is sealed with rated firestop before we leave, and outdoor penetrations are sealed against water.
