The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Security Cameras

A security camera on Long Island has a longer job than the same camera in the city. In Brooklyn or Queens most of what needs watching is a front door, a hallway and a few feet of sidewalk. In Nassau and Suffolk the property itself is the problem: a detached house on its own lot, a driveway that runs sixty or a hundred feet back to the garage, a backyard with a pool and a shed, a side gate nobody remembers to lock. A plaza tenant on Sunrise Highway or Jericho Turnpike has a parking field out front, a service lane behind and a dumpster corner at the end of the building. The equipment you buy has to reach all of that, and it has to keep recording when the weather turns.

That is why the questions our Long Island callers ask are less about which brand is best and more about distance, power and weather. Can a camera on the garage see the end of the driveway? Will a Wi-Fi camera work out by the pool, two walls and a patio away from the router? What happens to recording when PSEG Long Island loses a feeder in a nor’easter? Will the housing survive salt air on a canal in Massapequa or a bay house in Hampton Bays? Those answers decide what you should buy before brand ever matters, and they are what this page is built around.

Package theft is the reason a lot of people start shopping in November. Nassau County Police reported a run of FedEx package thefts in October 2023 across Massapequa Park, Massapequa and Wantagh: in each case, police said, the suspect walked up to the front door, took the box and left on foot, according to FOX 5 New York. CBS New York reported that in Massapequa Park mobile devices were stolen six times in 11 days, each right after a delivery, and a Wantagh homeowner’s own video showed a box of new phones taken about four minutes after it landed on the step. Video is what turned those thefts into a case: police released camera images of the people involved.

The wider numbers are moving the right way. New York State Division of Criminal Justice Services figures for Nassau County, reported by the Long Island Press, show 1,026 burglaries in 2024 against 1,241 in 2023, grand larcenies down from 13,698 to 12,384, and motor vehicle thefts down from 889 to 551. Fewer incidents is good news, and it also means each incident gets a detective’s attention. The ones that close are the ones where somebody can hand over a clear clip with a face, a vehicle and a time stamp.

“Cameras are getting added all the time.”

— Stuart Cameron, then Suffolk County Police Chief of Department, on the county’s camera registration program (Officer.com, August 2019)

Long Island facts that change what you should buy

  • Nassau County, 2024: 1,026 burglaries (down 17.3% from 2023), 12,384 grand larcenies and 551 motor vehicle thefts, per NYS DCJS data reported by the Long Island Press. Driveway and garage cameras that can read a vehicle matter more than a doorbell alone.
  • Porch thefts follow deliveries: six device thefts in 11 days in Massapequa Park in fall 2023, each right after a FedEx drop (CBS New York, November 2023).
  • Suffolk camera registration: Suffolk County Police run a voluntary registration program where residents and businesses record where their cameras are, and it was expanded to include home cameras such as doorbells (Officer.com).
  • Storm outages: the LIPA task force report on Tropical Storm Isaias (August 2020) described more than 646,000 outages on Long Island (Newsday via NYSenate.gov). A recorder with no battery backup records nothing while the power is out.
  • Empty houses: almost half of the housing units in the Town of Southampton are occupied seasonally, and in Bridgehampton and Water Mill more than 60% are seasonal (Town of Southampton Housing Plan, October 2022).

Put together, a Long Island camera purchase comes down to four things: cameras that cover the approaches to the lot rather than just the front step, a recorder on the property so footage survives an internet outage, power that rides through a storm, and housings and mounts rated for the outdoors. If you already know you want the whole job done for you, our security camera installation page for Long Island walks through the install side. Field Notes

Field Notes: A Turret Dome Under a Storefront Fascia

The hero photo and the close-up below come from a storefront job in our library, and they show a buying decision that matters on Long Island more than almost anywhere: where an outdoor camera can live so the weather never reaches the lens. The tenant had a deep, boxy sign fascia over the entrance, painted bright yellow, with a mural running down the pier beside it. We mounted a white turret dome on the underside of that fascia at the outside corner, with a second camera housing tucked just below it facing the other way. The overhang works like a built-in hood: rain, sleet and the low winter sun stay off the dome, and the camera looks along the storefront and out over the lot.

Two details on that job are the reason we sell the hardware we sell. First, the turret body. A turret has no plastic bubble over the lens, so there is nothing to fog in a cold snap, nothing to catch infrared bounce at night, and nothing to scratch. That makes turrets our default outdoor choice under an awning or soffit. Second, the wiring path. The cable comes down inside the fascia rather than across the face of it, so it is protected from weather and from anyone who wants to pull it. Outdoors on the Island, a camera rarely fails at the camera; it fails at an exposed connector that took on water.

The photo was taken on a clear day with bare trees behind the building. That is the season when Long Island outdoor cameras get tested: shorter days mean more hours on night vision, wet snow sticks to anything flat, and wind drives rain sideways under shallow eaves. If you are buying outdoor cameras this month, look for a published weather rating, a body style that suits your mounting spot, and night vision range that actually reaches the far edge of what you want to see. We list all three for every house-brand package further down.

Indoors, the library photos further down this page show the other half of most commercial jobs: small black cameras on a tall mezzanine wall, at the edge of a ceiling grid, and high on a plain wall above an alarm motion sensor. The common thread is height and angle. A camera mounted high and aimed across a room sees faces and hands; a camera aimed straight down sees the tops of heads. Small indoor bodies also stay out of the way of signage, TVs, speakers and lights, which matters in a showroom or a service lobby where the room has to look finished. When you buy indoor cameras from us for your own install, we suggest the mounting height and angle for each one based on photos of the room.

Outdoor cameras for winter on the Island

Winter security cameras are not a separate product; they are outdoor cameras with a weather rating and an operating temperature range that covers a Long Island January. The weather rating is the IP code. IP66 means the housing is dust-tight and protected against powerful water jets; IP67 adds protection against temporary immersion. Of the house-brand packages we list below, the 5MP F-series and E-series and the D4/D5 deterrent packages carry IP67 on their spec sheets, the 8MP C- and D-series carry IP66, and the 2MP A-series sheets list no IP rating at all, so we steer A-series buyers to sheltered spots under an eave or a porch roof. For name brands we read the manufacturer’s page: Reolink’s RLC-810A, for example, lists IP67, 4K (3840×2160) resolution, infrared night vision up to 100 feet and an operating range of 14°F to 131°F on its official spec page.

Two buying tips for weatherproof security cameras in winter. Pick a body style that sheds snow: turrets and domes have rounded tops that wet snow slides off, while a bullet’s flat sunshield can hold a ridge of it over the lens. And look at the mounting accessories as carefully as the camera. A junction box behind the camera keeps the cable splice out of the weather, and on a coastal property it is the piece that decides whether the camera is still working in March.

Indoor cameras for homes, garages and shops

Indoor security cameras go where weather isn’t a factor but placement is. In a Long Island house that usually means the attached garage door to the kitchen, the basement entry, the main hallway or the stairs. In a shop it means the register, the stockroom and the inside of the front door at face height. The photos on this page show the pattern we follow on commercial ceilings: small cameras high on the wall or at the ceiling grid, angled down across a room rather than straight down, and kept clear of light fixtures so the image doesn’t wash out.

Recorders & Storage

NVR and DVR Recorders, Hard Drives and How Long Footage Lasts

The recorder is the part of a security camera system that people buy last and should think about first, because it decides how many cameras you can add, what resolution they can record at, and how many days of footage you keep. We sell network video recorders (NVRs) for IP cameras and digital video recorders (DVRs) for HD-analog cameras, from 4-channel units up to 64-channel recorders for plazas and multi-building sites.

RecorderTypical Long Island useNotes
4 channel NVR / DVRSmall house, a single storefront, a vacation cottageFills up fast; buy 8 if you might add a garage or backyard camera later
8 channel NVRMost detached homes, small offices, delis and restaurantsRoom to add driveway, yard and garage cameras later

A PoE NVR has a built-in switch, so cameras plug straight into the back and get power and data on one cable. A 4K NVR can decode and record 4K cameras; buying one now keeps the door open even if you start with 2K cameras. If you’re working through how to choose an NVR, our camera calculator helps with camera count and storage before you call.

Surveillance hard drives: 4TB, 8TB and up

Security recorders write video around the clock, so they need surveillance-rated hard drives built for continuous writing rather than a desktop drive. We stock 4TB and 8TB surveillance hard drives for most homes and small businesses and plan 16TB and larger storage for big systems or long retention, spread over multiple drive bays on larger NVRs. A drive is also the most common replacement part we sell, because it is the component that wears out first.

How much storage do you need? A worked estimate

Here is the method we use, clearly labeled as an estimate: storage equals bitrate × hours × days × cameras. Bitrate depends on resolution, frame rate, compression (H.264 or H.265) and how busy the scene is, so treat every number below as an example, not a spec.

  • Step 1 — bitrate per camera. Example: a 4K camera set to 4 Mbps (megabits per second).
  • Step 2 — per hour. 4 Mbps × 3,600 seconds = 14,400 megabits = 1,800 megabytes, about 1.8 GB per hour.
  • Step 3 — per day. 1.8 GB × 24 hours = about 43 GB per camera per day of continuous recording.
  • Step 4 — per system. Four cameras × 43 GB = about 173 GB a day; 14 days ≈ 2.4 TB; 30 days ≈ 5.2 TB.
  • Step 5 — motion recording. If the cameras only record on motion and the scene is quiet half the day, the same drive holds roughly twice as many days.

So security cameras with two weeks of recording on four 4K cameras fit comfortably on a 4TB drive in this example, and security cameras with one month of recording want an 8TB drive. A 2K system at a lower bitrate stretches the same drive much further. The recorder overwrites the oldest footage automatically once the drive is full, which is how security cameras record “forever” without you doing anything — the retention window is simply the drive size divided by the daily total.

Local storage, cloud storage and what “no monthly fee” means

Every system we sell records locally to the NVR or DVR on your property, which is why there is no monthly fee. You view live and recorded video on your phone through the recorder’s app. Some name-brand cameras also offer optional cloud plans; where a product actually offers cloud storage we will tell you what the plan covers, but you never need one to record. Hybrid setups — local recording plus cloud backup of motion clips — are possible on products that support them and are worth considering on a vacation home where a thief could take the recorder.

Distance is the accessory question we answer most on the Island. A standard Ethernet run for a PoE camera is limited to 100 meters (328 feet) from the switch. A camera at the end of a long Suffolk driveway, at the back of a deep Nassau lot or on a dock can exceed that, and the fix is usually a small PoE switch, a PoE extender, or a point-to-point wireless bridge between buildings rather than a longer cable. We’ll tell you which applies when you send the distance.

We don’t publish step-by-step wiring instructions on this page, on purpose. Cable that is crimped wrong, a splice left outside a junction box, or a camera on the wrong power supply is the most common reason a self-installed system fails within a year. If you’re buying parts for your own project, tell us what you’re connecting and we’ll make sure the parts match; if you’d rather not do it, our crew installs it.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Packages for Long Island Homes

Long Island housing is mostly detached single-family homes — capes, ranches, split-levels, colonials and high ranches on their own lots — with condos and co-ops near the train lines and in the village centers, and seasonal houses concentrated on the East End and the barrier beaches. Here is how the equipment changes with the property.

Cape or ranch on a quarter-acre lot

Four cameras: front door and walk, driveway, backyard, and one side yard. A 4- or 8-channel recorder; attic access usually makes cable runs simple.

Colonial or split-level with a detached garage

Six to eight cameras. The garage camera often needs its own small PoE switch or a bridge, because the run back to the house is long.

Long driveway, flag lot or estate

Long-range 4K cameras for the driveway entrance, plus a camera at the house end so you get both the plate and the face.

Pool, deck and backyard

A wide-angle turret over the yard and a camera on the shed or pool house; full-color night vision helps where there’s patio lighting.

Canal and bay houses

IP66/IP67 housings, junction boxes, and stainless or coated mounts away from direct spray; a camera on the dock if you keep a boat.

Condo, co-op or HOA

Your own door and parking space on your own unit’s equipment; common areas only with the board’s approval and on its system.

How many cameras does a Long Island house need?

For a detached house on a typical suburban lot, four is the floor and six to eight is common. Count the ways a person or a car can reach the house — front walk, driveway, side gates, back fence, garage — and make sure each one passes a camera close enough to read a face or a plate. A corner lot adds a second street. A long driveway adds a camera at the road end. A detached garage or pool house adds one each. Indoor cameras are a separate decision; most homeowners add one at the door from the garage and one at the main stairs.

Vacation and seasonal homes on the East End and the South Shore

A summer house in the Hamptons, on the North Fork or on Fire Island that sits empty for months needs a different shopping list than a year-round home. The equipment has to keep recording through winter storms with nobody there to reset it, and somebody has to be able to look in from far away. We put the recorder somewhere less obvious than a living-room shelf, put it and the modem on battery backup, and set retention long enough that a break-in discovered in April is still on the drive. Where a product supports it, a cloud backup of motion clips covers the case where a thief takes the recorder. Cellular routers handle houses where the internet is shut off for the season.

Package theft prevention cameras

Security cameras for holiday package theft need two angles, not one. A doorbell camera or a small turret at the door catches the face at close range; a second camera covering the walk and the curb catches the car and the direction it leaves in. That second view is what a detective wants. Mount the door camera at about head height rather than high under the eave, so it sees faces instead of hat brims.

Smart Features

AI Detection, License Plates, NDAA and Other Spec-Sheet Words

Camera boxes are covered in acronyms. Here is what they mean and how we handle them, because a feature only counts if the product’s spec sheet says it has it.

  • AI human and vehicle detection: the camera or recorder tells a person or a car apart from a swaying branch, so alerts and searches are useful. Our D4 and D5 deterrent packages list smart human and vehicle detection; Reolink’s RLC-810A lists person, vehicle and animal detection.
  • Smart search: searching recorded video for “people” or “vehicles” in a time range instead of scrubbing hours of footage. It depends on the recorder and camera pairing.
  • License plate recognition (LPR / ANPR) and license plate capture: LPR reads plates into text; plate capture simply means a camera set up to get a readable plate image. True LPR needs a camera built and placed for it; we only call a product LPR when its sheet does.
  • Facial recognition and people counting: analytics on specific commercial products, quoted only where the model’s spec sheet lists them. Face matching raises privacy questions for customers and staff, so we point Long Island business owners to their own counsel before they switch it on.
  • NDAA compliant, ONVIF, RTSP, H.265: procurement and compatibility terms. NDAA matters for some government-funded buyers; ONVIF and RTSP describe how cameras talk to recorders and software; H.265 is a compression standard that shrinks storage. We confirm each one against the specific model’s sheet before we quote it.
  • Encrypted and cybersecure cameras: what protects a system day to day is a unique admin password, current firmware, and keeping cameras off the open internet. We set those on every system we install.

Active deterrence security cameras — with a siren, strobe light and two-way talk — are popular on Long Island driveways and plaza back doors, because they turn a recording into an interruption. Two-way audio security cameras let you speak through the camera from your phone. If audio recording is part of your plan, read the next section first.

Security Camera Laws

Security Camera Laws and Privacy on Long Island

This is general information with sources, not legal advice. New York State law applies across Nassau and Suffolk; villages and HOAs can add their own rules about what goes on a building.

  • Audio recording: New York’s eavesdropping law makes it a crime to mechanically overhear a conversation without the consent of at least one party (NY Penal Law §250.05, with definitions in §250.00). Many of the cameras we sell have built-in microphones; on a business we usually recommend turning audio off in areas where customers or staff talk and the owner is not a party to the conversation.
  • Video on your own property: pointing cameras at your own entrances, driveway and yard is normal. Aim away from a neighbor’s windows and keep cameras out of bathrooms, bedrooms of other occupants and changing areas.
  • Police registration is voluntary: Suffolk County Police’s camera registration program and Nassau’s SPiN partnership are opt-in; you decide when and how footage is shared.
  • HOAs, condos and co-ops: check the bylaws before mounting anything on a common wall, and expect the board to control cameras in shared spaces.

When customers ask “are security cameras legal on Long Island?” the honest short answer is yes for video of your own property, with care needed around audio and around anyone else’s private space. If you run a business with employees, ask your attorney or HR advisor about notice before you install.

Buying Guide

How to Choose a Security Camera System for a Long Island Property

Shoppers who call us after buying the wrong thing usually skipped one of these decisions. Work through them in order and the shopping list writes itself. None of this is wiring instruction; it is the thinking that happens before anything gets mounted.

1. Walk the property and list what you want to see

Start outside, not on a website. Walk from the street to every door and write down each place a person or a car can come in: the front walk, the driveway apron, the side gate by the trash cans, the back fence along the neighbor’s yard, the path to the dock, the detached garage door. Then decide what you need from each one — to identify a face, read a plate, or just see that something moved. That list is your camera count, and it is usually longer than people expect on a Long Island lot.

2. Measure the distances

For each spot, estimate how far the camera will be from what it needs to see, and how far the camera will be from where the recorder sits. The first number decides resolution and lens: identifying a face at the end of a long driveway takes more pixels than seeing a face at the front door. The second number decides how the camera connects: under about 300 feet of cable, a PoE camera runs straight back to the recorder; beyond that, you add a switch or a bridge. Distances are the information we ask for first when you call.

3. Pick the connection, then the recorder

Once you know the distances, choose PoE, Wi-Fi or HD-analog for each camera, and the recorder follows: an NVR for IP cameras, a DVR for coax. Then choose channels with room to spare. A house that needs five cameras today should buy an 8-channel recorder; a plaza unit planning ten cameras should buy 16. Adding a camera to a recorder with a free channel is simple; adding one to a full recorder means buying a new recorder.

4. Choose resolution by distance, not by the box

Use 4K/8MP where the subject is far away or where you will want to zoom into recordings later — driveways, parking fields, docks, loading areas. Use 5MP or 2K where the subject is close — doors, hallways, registers. Mixing resolutions on one recorder is normal and saves storage. Our quotes show a 2K-class and a 4K version of the same layout so you can see the difference in parts.

5. Decide how many days to keep

Two weeks is enough for most homes, because you usually know about a problem within days. Businesses, landlords and seasonal homes often want a month or more, because an incident may not be noticed for weeks. Days of retention decide the hard drive size, using the estimate method in the storage section above.

6. Plan power and weather protection

Every outdoor camera needs a weather-rated body, a junction box or sealed connection, and a mounting spot that shelters it where possible. The recorder, switch and modem need a battery backup. On the coast, add corrosion-resistant mounts. These are inexpensive next to the cameras and are the difference between a system that lasts and one that fails the first winter.

7. Plan viewing and security

Viewing security cameras on a phone is done through the recorder’s app, which connects to the NVR or DVR at the property. Ask before you buy whether the app is supported for your phone and whether more than one family member or manager can have their own login. To keep a system from being hacked, change every default password to a unique one, keep firmware current, give each user their own account, and avoid opening ports on your router to the cameras. When we install a system, we set those up and show you how to keep them that way.

If you would rather hand all seven decisions to someone else, that is what our installed packages are for. If you would rather make them yourself and buy the parts, call or text the list and we will check it before anything ships to your door.

Are wired security cameras better than wireless for a big Long Island yard?

For a big yard, yes. A wireless camera still needs power, and its video has to cross the house, the deck and the yard to the router; every wall and every foot of distance cuts the signal. The cameras that drop out first are always the ones farthest away — the garage, the shed, the pool house — which are usually the ones you bought them for.

Wireless still has a place: a single indoor camera, a rental where you can’t run cable, or a temporary view while you renovate. For anything you’d want footage from after a break-in, wired is the safer purchase.

Do security cameras need WiFi to record?

Wired cameras on an NVR or DVR don’t. They record to the hard drive in the recorder whether or not the internet is up. Wi-Fi or internet is only needed to view the cameras remotely and to get phone alerts. Many consumer Wi-Fi cameras are different: they depend on the network to reach their cloud service, and some stop recording when it drops.

This matters on the Island because internet service goes down in the same storms that bring trouble, and seasonal homes often have service suspended for the winter. A local recorder keeps footage either way.

What keeps a camera system recording through a Long Island storm outage?

A battery backup (UPS) on the recorder, the PoE switch and the modem. PoE cameras draw their power from the switch, so as long as the switch has power, the outdoor cameras keep recording. Without a UPS, the whole system goes dark the moment the power drops.

We size the UPS to the camera count; it carries the system through short outages and lets the recorder shut down cleanly in a long one so the hard drive isn’t damaged. For a property that loses power often, a whole-home generator is the next step, and that’s an electrician’s job.

Which outdoor cameras hold up to salt air on the South Shore and the North Fork?

Start with a published weather rating — IP66 or IP67 — and then look past the camera to the mount and the connections, because those corrode first. A junction box behind every outdoor camera keeps the cable splice dry, and mounting under an eave, soffit or fascia keeps spray and driving rain off the housing.

On waterfront houses in places like Freeport, Lindenhurst or Greenport we also avoid pointing cameras straight at the water, where reflections at night confuse motion detection, and we check every exterior entry for a drip loop and a sealed penetration.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Buying Security Cameras on Long Island

AI search answers about buying security cameras are written for an average house in an average place. Long Island has bigger lots, salt air, storm outages and a lot of empty winter homes. Here are seven claims we see repeated, and what we see on real properties.

“A video doorbell is all most homes need”

A doorbell camera watches one door from one height. On a Long Island lot it misses the driveway, the side gates, the backyard and the detached garage — the places where cars are broken into, bikes and tools disappear, and someone tries a back door.

The doorbell is still useful. It gets the face of whoever comes to the front step, and it lets you answer the door from your phone. It just isn’t a system.

The fix is a small recorder-based system with the doorbell as one view among four to eight. Many recorders can show a compatible doorbell alongside the other cameras.

“Wireless cameras are as reliable as wired ones”

On a small apartment, they can be. On a house with a pool and a shed out back, the signal has to cross exterior walls, a deck and open yard, and it competes with every neighbor’s network. The cameras at the edge of the property drop first.

Wireless cameras also still need power. Battery cameras need charging, and the camera you forget to charge is the one that misses the event.

Wired PoE cameras solve both problems with one cable, and they keep recording when the Wi-Fi doesn’t.

“Cloud storage is always safer than a recorder”

Cloud storage protects against one thing: someone stealing the recorder. It does nothing when the internet is down, and on the Island the internet often goes down in the same storm that takes the power.

Local recording is continuous, has no monthly fee and doesn’t depend on your upload speed. The risk of theft is handled by putting the recorder somewhere less obvious and, on products that support it, backing up motion clips to the cloud.

For a seasonal home, that hybrid approach is often the right answer. For a year-round house, a well-placed recorder on battery backup is usually enough.

“Higher resolution is always better”

Resolution helps when the subject is far away. On a front step three feet from the lens, 2K already shows a face clearly, and 4K just fills the hard drive faster.

Where 4K earns its keep is distance: the end of the driveway, a parking field, a dock. Placement matters as much as pixels; a 4K camera aimed into the sun or too high on a wall records a sharp image of nothing useful.

That’s why our quotes come as a 2K option and a 4K option, with the cameras that need the extra detail called out.

“Any outdoor camera is weatherproof”

“Outdoor” on a box is not a rating. The IP code on the spec sheet is the rating, and some budget cameras don’t list one at all. Our own A-series sheets don’t, which is why we put those cameras under cover.

Even a well-rated camera fails if its connection is left exposed. Water gets into an unprotected splice and the camera goes dark months later.

Near salt water, add junction boxes, sealed entries and corrosion-resistant mounts to the shopping list.

“You need a monthly subscription to watch your cameras on your phone”

Not with a recorder-based system. The NVR or DVR app shows live and recorded video on your phone with no subscription; the recorder is doing the storage.

Subscriptions come from cloud-first consumer cameras, where the plan pays for storing your clips on the company’s servers.

If a no-monthly-fee security camera system is the goal, buy a system that records locally and use the cloud only where you want it.

“A boxed kit from any store is the same as a local supplier’s system”

The cameras in a boxed kit may be fine. What the box can’t do is know your property: how long the driveway is, whether the garage is 40 or 140 feet away, whether the house is on a canal, how many days of footage you want to keep.

A local supplier matches the recorder, the drive, the switch and the mounts to the place, delivers the right parts, and answers the phone when the app needs resetting after a router change.

And if you’d rather not climb a ladder in January, the same supplier can install it.

How long do security cameras record before old footage is overwritten?
+

It depends on the hard drive size, the number of cameras, the resolution and whether they record continuously or on motion. A typical four-camera system with a correctly sized drive keeps about two weeks to a month; the recorder then overwrites the oldest footage automatically. We size the drive to the number of days you want when you order.

What security cameras work best for a vacation home that sits empty all winter?
+

Wired outdoor cameras with a published IP66 or IP67 rating, a recorder hidden away from the main rooms, battery backup on the recorder and modem, and remote viewing on your phone. Where the product supports it, add cloud backup of motion clips in case the recorder is taken, and a cellular router if the internet is shut off for the season.
