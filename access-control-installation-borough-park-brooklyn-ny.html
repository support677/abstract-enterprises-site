The Neighborhood

Why Borough Park Properties Need Access Control

Ask who decides what goes on a door in most Brooklyn neighborhoods and the answer is a homeowner, a landlord or a co-op board. In Borough Park the answer is very often a shul’s board, a yeshiva’s administrator or the director of a mossad. The Brooklyn Jewish history site counts more than 300 religious institutions in the neighborhood, and Wikipedia’s article lists Satmar schools on 53rd and 54th Streets, the Bobov headquarters on 48th Street and a long list of community organizations alongside them. That changes what access control is for. An institution does not need a lock so much as a door policy: who may come in, on which days, at which hours, and who can prove afterwards that the policy held.

Those institutions have been asked, more and more, to show their work. On March 12, 2024, the Boro Park JCC and the Community Security Initiative held a Security Summit for local shuls and mosdos, which Boro Park 24 described as the first in a series, to walk them through applying for New York State’s Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes grant of up to $200,000 and the federal Nonprofit Security Grant Program, which had offered $150,000 the year before. The summit offered a free CSI risk assessment, valued at $4,000, because an assessment is a requirement for the federal application. Both programs reimburse named equipment against a written plan. A door system that arrives as one lump sum on a contractor’s letterhead is hard to fit into that process; a door-by-door quote that names each reader, strike, contact and controller is easy. That is how we write every Borough Park quote, grant or no grant.

Yeshivas have their own route. The New York State Education Department’s Nonpublic School Safety Equipment grant, now past its tenth annual cycle since 2013, grew from a $15 million appropriation in its ninth year to $45 million in its tenth, allocated per pupil on enrollment. The department’s own question-and-answer document is plain that the grant is reimbursable, meaning the school pays first and claims afterwards, that internal door locksets and one-time security software licensing and installation are on the eligible list, and that security system maintenance fees are not. For a school, that last line matters more than any feature list: a system that only works while a yearly subscription is paid can leave the school carrying a cost the grant will never cover.

The houses are their own story. In 1992 the city gave Borough Park special zoning that allowed 65 percent lot coverage and smaller yards and setbacks, and by 1998 the New York Times was reporting that the neighborhood had the most private construction permits in Brooklyn since 1990. The Brooklyn Eagle’s 2016 report on a two-family house at 1311 44th Street being enlarged into a five-story, four-family building of about 5,633 square feet captures the pattern: houses grow as families do. When a house becomes four apartments for a parent and three married children, the single front-door key that served one family stops being a plan. Each household needs its own way in, the cellar and the boiler need a door the whole family can open, and nobody wants to rekey the house every time a grandchild loses a key.

The break-ins that reach local news are mostly about hours, not neighborhoods. Boro Park 24 reported that a man slipped into Bobov Shul at about 4:45 a.m. on a December morning in 2025 and took $285 from a locked pushka. In 2021 the same outlet described a run of yeshiva burglaries that began in July, including a mossad in the Eighteenth Avenue area and a Gerer yeshiva building, the latest on a motzei Shabbos. News 12 reported that two separate buildings on 61st Street were burglarized in the early hours of July 6, 2025. Boro Park Shomrim reported in March 2026 that a store was burglarized on Motzaei Shabbos by someone who smashed the front window and emptied the register, and that he was caught in under a day from camera footage. None of those reports says a reader would have stopped anything, and we do not claim it. What they share is a building that was empty, or nearly empty, at a known hour. A door system with a schedule, a contact on every outside door and an alert to the right person at that hour is what turns an empty building into one that tells somebody what is happening.

There is also the week itself. Wikipedia’s Thirteenth Avenue article notes that the stores and businesses on the avenue are shuttered on the Jewish Sabbath and holidays, chains included. Shuls fill and empty around davening. Schools follow their own calendars, with long breaks that do not match the public schools. A lock that only knows “locked” and “unlocked” forces somebody to be at the door at every change. A controller that holds a weekly schedule and a holiday calendar does the changing for you, and keeps a record of what each door did. Which settings suit a household or a shul on Shabbos and Yom Tov is a question for your rav, not for us; our job is to make sure the door does exactly what you decided, every week, without anyone touching it.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Borough Park’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware. Borough Park’s housing is mostly brick: attached and semi-attached one- to three-family houses, many of them enlarged since the 1992 zoning, walk-ups on the side streets, and mixed buildings with stores below on the avenues. Its institutions often occupy buildings that were built as something else. These are the six kinds of entrance we plan for most often.

The shtiebel in a former house

A small congregation using the ground floor of a house, sometimes with a family upstairs. Usually two doors: the old front door, now the shul’s, and a side or rear door to the stairs. The job is keeping the two groups of people apart on one controller, with the family’s door never opening on the shul’s schedule.

The purpose-built shul

A masonry building with heavy main doors on the street, a side entrance members use on weekdays, a basement hall and an office. Main doors often stay on their mechanical hardware; the readers go on the side door, the office and the hall, which are the doors used at hours when the building is otherwise empty.

The yeshiva building

Brick school buildings with several entrances on more than one street, steel doors in steel frames, panic bars already fitted on many exits, and a front office. The work is mostly strikes and electrified panic hardware rather than new locks, a buzz-in station at the visitor door, and a controller that can secure every outside door from the office.

The enlarged family house

A brick house that has grown upward or backward into three, four or five apartments, often with a new street door and stair hall added in the work. The new door frame is usually sound steel or solid wood; the wiring path depends on whether the enlargement left a chase from the cellar to the vestibule.

The side-street walk-up

A four- to six-story brick building with a vestibule, an outer and inner door, a buzzer panel and a cellar with the laundry and meters. The inner door gets the strike and the reader; the cellar door to the yard gets a contact or its own reader if tenants use it.

The avenue building

A store on Thirteenth, Sixteenth or Eighteenth Avenue with offices or apartments above, a roll-down gate over the storefront, a narrow street door beside it and a back door to the yard or the stockroom. Two separate groups, two schedules, one controller, and nothing that lets the store’s staff into the stair hall.

What the materials mean for the install

Brick is the common case here, and the kind one. Readers and stations are fixed into the mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so a later change leaves a joint to repoint rather than a cracked brick to replace. Where a pier beside the door is narrow, a slim reader goes on the door frame itself instead. Cable from the cellar comes up inside the wall where a chase exists, or through the floor of the vestibule and up inside the frame where it does not; on most two- and three-family houses the cellar sits right under the front door, which keeps that run short.

Enlargements change things. When a house has been extended forward or built upward, the original cellar may stop short of the new street door, and the new vestibule may sit on a slab. In that case the cable follows the new stair hall, or we use a reader that talks to the controller over a short run from the nearest point the cellar reaches. We look at the enlargement plans if you have them; they save time on the day.

Institutional buildings bring steel. A hollow-metal door in a steel frame takes a heavy-duty strike or an electrified panic device, both of which need the frame to be sound and square; a frame that has been bored for three generations of locks, or packed solid with grout, may need repair or a different device. School and shul doors also see far more openings a day than a house door, so we size closers and strikes for traffic, not for appearance, and we check that every door closes and latches by itself before any electronics go on. HPD’s rules already expect hallway and entrance doors in apartment buildings to swing closed and latch on their own; a door that does that is the foundation of every access system we install.

A Door Policy Before a Door System: Planning for a Shul

Every shul we have planned starts with the same conversation, and it is not about readers. It is about who comes in, when, and through which door. A shul of any size has several groups of people with different needs: members who daven every weekday, the gabbaim and officers, the rav, the office staff, the caterers and the people who rent the hall, the cleaning staff, and the guests who arrive for a simcha and will never come again. Each group uses a different door at different hours. Writing that down, door by door, is the most useful hour a board can spend before it asks anyone for a price.

From that list the hardware follows almost by itself. The door that members use on weekday mornings gets a reader and a group schedule that opens to members’ fobs from a set time before the first minyan. The office and any room holding money or records gets its own group, open only to the people who need it, and a record of every opening. The hall gets a group that can be switched on for an event and off again afterwards. The main doors, which on Shabbos and Yom Tov may be opened and left open by hand, often keep their mechanical hardware and simply get a contact, so the board knows when they were opened and when they were secured.

The question we ask last is who holds the keys to the system itself. In many shuls the person who set up the old keypad was a volunteer, and when he moved, the administrator password moved with him. We set up every institution’s system in the institution’s name, with at least two administrator accounts held by officers, and a sealed paper record of the master code kept wherever the board keeps its other important papers. That way the doors belong to the shul, not to whoever happened to install them or program them.

What a shul door plan usually contains

  • A list of doors with a photo of each, from outside and inside, and what each one is used for.
  • A list of groups (members, officers, office, hall, staff, contractors) and which doors each group may open.
  • A weekly schedule for each door, and a calendar of days when the schedule changes, which the shul supplies and we enter.
  • Exit and fire rules for each door: what opens it from inside, what releases it in a power cut or a fire alarm.
  • Alerts: which door being held open, forced, or opened at a set hour should send a message, and to whom.
  • An itemized price with each device and license on its own line, which a grant consultant can map to an application.

Locked From the Street, Always Open From Inside: School Entrances

A yeshiva’s doors have two jobs that pull against each other. During the school day the building should be closed to anyone the office has not seen, and at the same time every child and teacher must be able to get out of any exit at once, without a key, a code or any special knowledge. Getting both right is the whole of school access control, and it is why the hardware on a school door is usually different from the hardware on a shul or a house.

The State Education Department has been blunt about one option. Its Facilities Planning guidance to school districts concludes that electromagnetic locking devices are not appropriate for school buildings, says existing systems should be deactivated whenever the building is occupied, and recommends instead an electrically operated strike system with panic bars, which it believes satisfies both the egress code and controlled entry. That guidance is written for districts, and it does not say whether it binds a nonpublic school. We follow it at yeshivas anyway, because the reasoning is sound: a strike with a panic bar fails the safe way. Push the bar and the door opens, every time, whatever the electricity is doing. A magnetic lock depends on sensors, buttons and power relays all working to let people out.

The visitor door is where most of the design effort goes. We put a video station at the one entrance visitors are told to use, answered from the office, so the secretary sees and speaks to whoever is outside before releasing the door. Staff use cards or fobs on a schedule that covers their working hours. Parents and drivers use the visitor door like any other guest. Where the building allows, a second set of doors inside the entrance creates a small vestibule, so a visitor who has been let through the street door still waits at a locked inner door until the office is satisfied. The NPSE question-and-answer document lists adding a second set of doors at an existing entrance, without adding square footage, as an eligible expense; the program decides what it will reimburse at your school, but the design is a good one whether or not the grant covers it.

One Street Door, Several Households: Houses That Grew

The enlarged house is the most Borough Park job we do. The pattern is familiar from the Brooklyn Eagle’s account of 44th Street: a two-family house becomes a five-story, four-family building, and the people living in it are often one extended family. Parents on one floor, married children on the others, grandchildren running between them, a cellar with the boiler, the meters and the storage that everyone needs. On paper it may be a four-family building; in daily life it is one household with four front doors behind a single street door.

That street door is where a keypad or a fob reader earns its keep. Each household gets its own code, so the parents never need to know who in the second-floor family has been given one and nobody has to collect keys when a grandchild loses his. Each household can have its own administrator in the app, who adds and removes people for that family only. The parents, or whoever owns the building, keep an owner account that can see and change everything. The cellar door gets its own strike on the same controller, open to adults in every household, so the boiler and the meters can be reached without handing out a cellar key.

Two points need care. First, if any apartment is rented rather than lived in by family, New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law may apply to the system’s records, and HPD’s guidance on that law expects an owner of a multiple dwelling who uses keyless entry to give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, keep only the minimum data, and not make the smart system the only way into a tenant’s own home. Second, we never set up a house so that a lost phone, a flat battery or a forgotten code locks anyone out: the original key cylinder stays, and the controller has a battery that keeps the strike working through a power cut.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Borough Park

Borough Park’s businesses cluster on a few avenues, and almost all of them keep the same weekly rhythm: busy through the week, packed on Thursday and Friday morning, closed from before Shabbos until after it ends, and closed again for Yom Tov. Wikipedia’s Thirteenth Avenue article counted fourteen banks on the avenue between 45th and 55th Streets in 2009, next to Judaica, electronics, wig, book and food stores. These are the corridors we work on most.

Thirteenth Avenue

The main shopping street, about a mile long in Borough Park and one-way since 1989. Storefronts under roll-down gates with a narrow street door beside them for the floors above. The usual job is a back-door or stockroom reader for staff and a strike on the residential street door, on separate groups.

Sixteenth Avenue

A shopping corridor that also holds the 66th Precinct station house at 5822 16th Avenue. Stores with offices upstairs, where the upstairs tenants come and go on a different schedule from the store below and need their own credentials on the shared door.

Eighteenth Avenue

The third of the neighborhood’s shopping avenues in Wikipedia’s list, running toward Bensonhurst, and the area where Boro Park 24 placed one of the mossad burglaries in 2021. Back doors to yards and stockrooms are the doors that matter at night.

New Utrecht Avenue

The avenue where, in Wikipedia’s words, the West End line becomes an elevated structure, with the 50th Street and 55th Street stations overhead. Where a business door sits under the el, trains pass close above all day, so we fit heavy-duty strikes and closers and check their fixings at the follow-up visit.

Fort Hamilton Parkway

The neighborhood’s western edge by the Brooklyn Jewish history site’s boundary, served by Fort Hamilton Parkway stations on both the West End and Sea Beach lines. Buildings with a business on the ground floor and a separate door for the people upstairs are the common access job along it.

Tenth Avenue near Maimonides

Maimonides Medical Center at 4802 10th Avenue, founded in 1911 as the New Utrecht Dispensary, sits at the neighborhood’s northwest. For practices and offices near it, the work is suite doors with card readers, shift groups for staff, and a separate group for the room that holds records.

The store that closes for a day every week

A Thirteenth Avenue store is dark for roughly a full day each week and for longer stretches over Yom Tov. Access control helps that store in two specific ways. First, it removes the question of who has a key. Staff get codes that work only during store hours, so a former employee’s code simply stops working the day he leaves and nobody has a working credential while the store is closed except the owner. Second, it gives the owner a record. If the back door opens at an hour when nobody should be there, the controller logs it and can send a message to the owner’s phone.

It does not stop a smashed window, which is how the store in Boro Park Shomrim’s March 2026 report was entered. That job belongs to an alarm with glass-break sensors and to cameras; Shomrim tracked that suspect from footage. We say this plainly because owners deserve to know where each part of their security starts and stops. Many avenue stores end up with all three on one plan: a reader on the back door, an alarm on the glass, and cameras covering both.

Rules that apply to Borough Park business and institution doors

  • Exit always works. The NYC Building Code expects an electrically locked egress door to unlock when its power is lost and to release when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate. Where a sensor releases the door, a PUSH TO EXIT button between 40 and 48 inches high, within 5 feet of the door, must cut the lock’s power and keep it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, and the locking system must be listed to UL 294.
  • Magnetic locks need special release hardware. Under the code’s section on electromagnetically locked doors, a maglock on an egress door must be released by the door’s own hardware, operated with one hand, immediately, and must release on power loss. That is one reason we prefer strikes with panic bars on busy doors such as schools and shuls.
  • Apartment buildings above stores. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, class A buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and a remote release at the main door. A reader adds to those requirements; it never replaces them.
  • Roof doors. The same section requires roof doors to be self-closing but not self-locking, openable from inside without a key. We never put a reader that locks a roof door against people inside.
  • Tenants’ data. Where a building has apartments, HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law page expects owners using smart access to collect only minimum data, publish a retention policy, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use it to harass or evict.
  • Schools. NYSED’s guidance to districts recommends electric strikes with panic bars over magnetic locks in school buildings; we follow it at yeshivas.

This is a practical summary of the rules we design to, not legal advice. Where a building has its own engineer, architect or fire-alarm contractor, we coordinate with them on any door tied to the fire alarm.

Access Control in Borough Park: Questions Answered

Shuls, mosdos and security grants

Is a card reader on a shul’s door the kind of equipment the federal Nonprofit Security Grant will pay for?

It can be. The program works from an Authorized Equipment List, and the version published with the 2024 cycle includes a line for physical access control systems, item 14SW-01-PACS, described as locking devices and entry systems for control of physical access to facilities. Neighboring lines cover personnel identification systems, impact-resistant doors and gates, intercom systems and software that supports access controls. Readers, strikes, controllers and their software generally sit under those headings.

Whether your shul’s particular project is approved is decided by the program and the state agency that administers it, against your application and risk assessment, not by us. What we provide is a quote that makes the match easy: each device on its own line, with its make, model and quantity, and the labor shown separately, so whoever prepares the application can point each line at the right category.

Our yeshiva’s state safety-equipment money is paid back after we spend it, and the rules say maintenance fees are not covered. Should that change which system we buy?

Yes, it should. The NPSE question-and-answer document says the grant is reimbursable and lists one-time security software licensing and installation as eligible while security system maintenance fees are not. A system whose readers stop working when a monthly or yearly cloud subscription lapses leaves the school with a recurring cost the grant will not reimburse, year after year.

For most yeshivas we recommend a controller that runs the doors locally, on site, with a one-time software license, and treats any cloud service as an optional extra rather than a requirement. The doors keep working if the internet drops or the subscription is not renewed. If the school wants remote management, we price it as a separate line so the board can see exactly what is recurring and what is not.

A man slipped into Bobov Shul at about 4:45 one December morning and took money from a pushka. What can a door system actually do about that hour?

Boro Park 24’s report does not say how he got in, so we will not pretend to know what would have stopped him. What a door system does in that hour is three things. It keeps doors that should be locked actually locked, because a strike relocks the moment the door closes and a schedule does not unlock anything until the time the shul has chosen. It records every opening, by credential, so the board knows whose fob opened a door and when. And with a contact on each door, it can send a message the moment a door opens outside its schedule or is forced.

Our risk assessment flagged the side door as the weak point. What does fixing a side door actually involve?

Usually four pieces of work, in this order. First the door itself: hinges, closer and frame adjusted or repaired so it closes and latches every time, because no electronics can secure a door that drifts open. Second, the lock: an electric strike or electrified panic bar so the door is locked from outside and always opens from inside. Third, the reader and the schedule, so members use fobs rather than keys or a shared code. Fourth, a door contact that reports the door held open or forced.

On a typical shul side door that is one controlled door on our Brooklyn list, $1,500 to $2,500, more if the frame needs repair or the door needs panic hardware. If the assessment also flagged the lack of a view of the door, a video station there instead of a plain reader adds sight and speech to the same work.

Candle-lighting moves every week and the Yom Tov dates change every year. How do the door schedules keep up?

A controller does not know the Jewish calendar unless someone tells it. Most systems we install hold weekly schedules plus a separate list of holiday dates, and each holiday date can follow its own schedule. Before each year we enter the dates and times the shul or school gives us, and many institutions simply choose times with enough margin that the schedule does not need weekly changes. Others prefer to update the Friday times season by season, which takes a few minutes in the app.

What each door should do on Shabbos and Yom Tov is the shul’s decision, made with its rav; we do not rule on those questions. Our part is to enter exactly what you decide, test it with you before the first week it applies, and show the officers how to check and change it.

Yeshivas and schools

Can our yeshiva put magnetic locks on its entrance doors?

We advise against it. The State Education Department’s Facilities Planning guidance to school districts says electromagnetic locking devices are not appropriate for school buildings, that existing systems should be off whenever the building is occupied, and that it will not approve such systems; it recommends electric strikes with panic bars instead. That guidance is addressed to districts, but its reasoning applies to any school: a magnetic lock relies on sensors and buttons to let people out, while a panic bar on a strike-locked door opens mechanically every time.

If your building already has magnetic locks, we can usually convert those doors to strikes or electrified panic hardware without replacing the doors, and keep the existing readers and cable where they are sound.

Hundreds of children leave through one door at dismissal, and the rest of the day it must stay closed to strangers. How is one door set up for both?

By separating entry from exit. With panic hardware on a strike-locked door, the door always opens from inside, so at dismissal the children push the bar and walk out, all at once, with nothing to unlock. From outside, the same door stays locked unless the office releases it or a staff card opens it. Nobody needs to prop the door, and nobody outside can walk in against the flow of children unless the door is held.

Because a held door is the real risk at dismissal, we add a door contact and a held-open alert to the office, and for the busiest doors a closer strong enough to pull the door shut once the last group is out. If the school wants the door unlocked for a short window for parents, that can be scheduled, but most schools find the buzz-in station is enough.

Morning buses unload at our side entrance on the side street, while the visitor door faces the avenue. Can the side door open only while the buses arrive?

Yes. Each door on the controller has its own schedule, so the side door can be set to accept staff cards or stand unlocked only during the arrival window the school chooses, and lock again automatically at the end of it, while the visitor door stays on buzz-in all day. A staff member at the side door during that window keeps it supervised.

Yeshiva buildings stand empty from Friday afternoon to Sunday, and Boro Park 24 reported a run of yeshiva break-ins, one of them on a motzei Shabbos. What should the doors be doing while nobody is there?

They should be locked to everyone except a small administrator group, and every outside door should report any opening. The 2021 reports do not say how the buildings were entered, so we cannot say a door system would have prevented them; detectives were taking fingerprints off door handles at one school, which tells you the doors were part of the story. What a system does is make sure the building knows. A contact on each outside door, an alert to two or three named people when any door opens while the building is set to empty, and a record of which credential, if any, was used.

We pair this with cameras at the same doors wherever the school wants them, because an alert is far more useful when the person receiving it can see the doorway. Whether anyone reacts at that hour is a decision for the administration; we make sure the message reaches them.

Family houses

Our house was enlarged into apartments for our married children. Can each family manage its own codes without the whole house becoming a building-style system?

Yes, and it does not need a lobby panel. A single keypad or reader on the street door, one controller in the cellar, and a separate user group for each household is enough. Each family gets an administrator login that can add or remove only its own people; the parents keep an owner login that sees everything. The cellar door can be on the same controller, open to adults in every household.

The price is the single controlled door on our Brooklyn list, $1,500 to $2,500, plus a second door at the cellar if you want one. If one of the apartments is rented to someone outside the family, ask us about the city’s tenant data rules before we set up the logs, because they may apply to that apartment.

Two buildings on 61st Street were burglarized before dawn on the same July morning. On a three-family house, which door matters most at that hour?

The News 12 report does not say how either building was entered, so we use it only as a reminder of the hour. On most Borough Park houses we look first at the door the family uses least and checks least: a cellar door to the yard, a side door to an alley, or a rear door off the kitchen. Those are often on a single old lock with keys nobody has counted in years, and they are out of sight of the street. A strike and a contact on that door, with an alert if it opens between set hours, usually does more for a house than a fancier reader on the front door.

Windows are a different matter. Local reports over the years, including a 2014 run of house burglaries entered through rear windows, are a reason to pair a door system with an alarm that covers ground-floor and rear windows; a reader does nothing for a window.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Borough Park

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Borough Park door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

“Access control is a business product; homes just need a smart lock.”

That is true of a one-family house with one door and one family. It is not true of the house Borough Park builds most often now, the enlarged house with three or four households behind one street door. That house has the problems of a small apartment building: several groups of users, a shared cellar, keys copied over many years, and people moving in and out as families grow.

A battery smart lock bought online usually manages one door from one app, with one or two administrators. It has no idea that the second floor is a different household from the third, and it cannot open a cellar strike on the same code. When the batteries go, it goes with them, often in the coldest week of the year.

For these houses a small wired system, one controller in the cellar and a reader or keypad on the street door, gives each household its own codes and its own administrator, keeps working on battery backup, and costs about the same as one controlled door on our Brooklyn list.

“Magnetic locks are the standard for secure entrances.”

Magnetic locks are common because they are simple to mount on almost any door. They are also the device that depends most heavily on sensors, buttons and power relays to let people out, which is why the code surrounds them with extra rules about release hardware and why the State Education Department’s guidance calls them not appropriate for school buildings.

In Borough Park, where many of the busiest doors are on schools and shuls, the better default is an electric strike or electrified panic hardware. Those keep the door’s mechanical latch and panic bar, so getting out never depends on electricity. They cost a little more in labor on a steel frame and a great deal less in risk.

We still install magnetic locks where they are the right tool, for example on a glass door with no frame for a strike, and when we do, we fit the release hardware the code requires and test it with the power off.

“Cloud-based access control is always the better choice.”

Cloud management is convenient: an administrator can add a user from a phone anywhere. But a system that needs the cloud to unlock its doors, or needs a subscription to keep working, carries a cost and a dependency that a shul or yeshiva should choose knowingly, not by default.

For schools there is a second reason. The state’s NPSE program lists one-time software licensing and installation as eligible and maintenance fees as not eligible, so a recurring subscription is a cost the school pays every year out of its own budget. A board that does not see that line in the quote finds out when the first renewal arrives.

Our default is a controller that runs locally and keeps every door working through an internet outage, with cloud management priced as a separate, optional line. Some institutions take it; many do not.

“A fob system pays for itself by replacing keys.”

Fobs replace keys only if someone manages them. A fob system with no one in charge drifts back to the same problem it was installed to solve: credentials held by people who left, shared codes written on the wall, and a door propped open because someone lost a fob and nobody issued a new one.

In an institution the real benefit is not the fob; it is the ability to take access back. When a teacher leaves, a caterer finishes, or a hall rental ends, one click ends that access. That only happens if the system belongs to the institution, has more than one administrator, and someone checks the user list from time to time.

We set up every Borough Park institution with named administrators and a short written procedure for issuing and removing credentials, because the procedure matters as much as the hardware.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides often quote the price of a lock or a reader off the shelf. An installed door in Brooklyn includes a strike or panic device, a reader, cable from a controller, the controller itself with a battery, a door contact, programming and testing. On our Brooklyn list a single controlled door with ten fobs runs $1,500 to $2,500, and a multi-door system runs $4,000 to $15,000.

Some Borough Park doors sit at the top of those ranges for local reasons: steel school frames that need repair, panic hardware on busy doors, enlargements where the cellar does not reach the new street door. Others, such as a house street door with an open cellar right below it, sit near the bottom.

The figure that matters is the written price for your doors, after a visit. It is itemized, so a board or a grant file can see what each line buys.

“Facial recognition is the modern way to control a door.”

Some answers present face readers as the next step after fobs. In New York City apartment buildings, biometric entry is on the list of smart access methods covered by the Tenant Data Privacy Law, which limits what owners may collect and how long they may keep it, and Brooklyn tenants have publicly fought a landlord’s plan to install facial recognition in a rent-stabilized complex.

For shuls and schools, a face reader also raises questions about recording children and congregants that most boards would rather not take on. Fobs, cards and codes do the job without storing anyone’s face.

We install biometric readers only where a client has a clear reason and has thought through the records, and we usually suggest a card or a code first.

“More cameras mean you don’t need access control.”

Borough Park has a great many cameras, and local reporting shows how useful their footage is: Shomrim tracked the Motzaei Shabbos store burglar from camera footage in March 2026, and in 2020 a building superintendent watching his camera feed helped catch a package thief who had been coming through an open window at a building at Caton and McDonald Avenues for about a year and a half.

Cameras show what happened. They do not decide who may open a door, they do not relock a door left on the latch, and they do not end a former employee’s access. A door system does those things, and its record of which credential opened which door at what time complements the footage rather than duplicating it.

The two work best together, and many of our Borough Park jobs install both on the same visit, with the camera aimed at the door the reader controls.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Write Down the Week Before Choosing a Reader

This describes how we approach an institution, not a particular customer. When a shul or a school calls us, the first thing we ask for is not a floor plan but a week. Who opens the building on a Sunday morning? Who has a key to the office, and who had one last year? When does the first person arrive on a weekday, and when does the last one leave? Which days does everything change? The answers are almost always spread across three or four people, and nobody has ever written them down in one place.

Once the week is on one page, the doors sort themselves. One door turns out to carry most of the daily traffic and deserves the reader. Another is used once a week and needs only a contact. A third has five keys in circulation and nobody knows where two of them are; that door goes on the system first. Then we look at the hardware, beginning with whether each door closes and latches by itself, because a schedule that unlocks and locks a door that does not close protects nothing. The reader is the last thing we choose, and by then it is usually obvious which one fits.

Will the trains on the elevated West End line over New Utrecht Avenue shake a strike or a reader loose?
+

We are enlarging our house. When should the access control wiring go in?
+

Before the walls are closed. We can walk the site with your contractor to mark the spots. The readers, strike and controller go on at the end, after the painters, and the work costs less than cutting finished walls later.

Can the yeshiva office lock every outside door with one button?
+

Yes. We wire a lockdown button to the controller so that one press cancels any scheduled unlock, stops staff cards from opening outside doors and keeps every entrance locked from the street. Our yeshiva can only close its doors for work during the summer break. How much can be done in that window?
+

Usually the whole job. Most of the time on a school project goes into door repairs, cable runs and programming, not into fitting readers, and with the building empty we can work on several doors at once. Our early minyan starts before sunrise. Should the shul door unlock on a timer or open only for members’ fobs?
+

For the first hour of the day we usually recommend fobs. A timer that unlocks the door leaves it standing open to anyone while only a few people are inside. With fobs, members let themselves in and the door relocks behind each one. Once the building is busier, the shul can choose to hold the door unlocked on a schedule. The decision is the board’s; we set it up either way.

Can one fob open both our house and the shul where my husband is a gabbai?
+

Sometimes. The house and the shul are separate systems with separate administrators, which is how it should be. If both systems use the same credential format, the shul can enroll the same fob that the family uses at home, so one fob opens both doors. Neither system can see the other’s records. If the formats differ, the simplest answer is two fobs on one ring.

Should every classroom door in our yeshiva be on the access system, or are good locksets enough?
+

For most classrooms, good locksets are enough. Readers belong on the entrances, the office and rooms such as records or equipment rooms. Classroom doors usually need a lockset a teacher can lock from inside without stepping into the hall, and the NPSE program lists internal door locksets as eligible. Putting every classroom on readers adds cost and programming without making the building much safer.

Can a reader go on a Thirteenth Avenue storefront door that sits behind a roll-down gate at night?
+

It can, but it is rarely the right door. Once the gate is down, a reader on the glass door behind it cannot be reached, so the gate itself is opened by key or its own control. For staff access we usually put the reader on the back door or the narrow street door beside the storefront, which stay reachable at all hours, and leave the storefront on its mechanical lock and gate.
