Side Door Showing Open, Keypad Beeping, or an Old Panel That Stopped Calling Out in Pelham Gardens? Same-Day Help

Plenty of houses on these blocks already have an alarm on the wall. It was put in by a previous owner, or by a national company years ago, and somewhere along the way the household stopped using it. The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Gardens Properties Need Alarm Systems

Wikipedia places Pelham Gardens in the northeast Bronx, bounded by East Gun Hill Road on the north and east, Pelham Parkway on the south, and the Dyre Avenue line of the 5 train on the west, ending at the Esplanade. Eastchester Road is its main street. It sits in Community District 11 and ZIP code 10469, and the 49th Precinct, whose station house is on Eastchester Road, patrols it. CityNeighborhoods.NYC draws the lines a little differently, with Stillwell Avenue on the southeast, and both descriptions are in use, so we treat the edges loosely and go by what the caller calls home.

The housing is the point. Wikipedia says the neighborhood is dominated by single-family homes, with some small apartment buildings and two- and three-family houses, and that many private homes have basement studio apartments while still being classified as one-family. CityNeighborhoods describes brick and stucco one- and two-family houses built mostly between the 1930s and the 1950s, in Tudor Revival, Cape Cod and Colonial styles, with pitched roofs, bay windows and private driveways. The New York Times, in a 2011 “Living In” column, referred to “numerous semidetached two-families” here. For an alarm, that adds up to houses with doors on three or four sides, side driveways that lead to the back, garages, basements with their own entrances, and often two households under one roof.

The reputation is just as real. That 2011 Times column ran under the headline “The Merits of Safe and Sound.” On Reddit’s r/bronx, people recommending places to live keep naming Pelham Gardens alongside Morris Park and Country Club as safe, quiet, single-family areas; one poster calls it nice and safe and close to Jacobi, another says anything covered by the 49th Precinct is among the safest in the borough. We read those threads as excerpts only and do not quote anyone further. Wikipedia adds that crime across the 49th Precinct fell 71.7 percent between 1990 and 2022.

And yet the precinct still logged 133 burglaries in 2022, according to the same article. Some of the ones that made the news happened right here, and they share a pattern. In August 2011 the Bronx Times reported a four-week run of house burglaries by a 19-year-old who had just moved in with a relative in the neighborhood. Officers caught him entering a house on Westervelt Avenue between Waring and Mace Avenues and arrested him on a corner of Eastchester Road; he was tied to break-ins on Adee and Seymour Avenues, his specialty was jewelry, and he told officers that stealing in Pelham Gardens was easy “because everybody kept their doors unlocked.” In September 2012, DNAinfo reported three young men taking a TV and jewelry from a residence on Seymour Avenue at about 5 a.m. In April 2021, ABC7 reported three men forcing the rear door of a home near Gabriel Drive and Mace Avenue at about 12:30 a.m., calling themselves police, and ransacking the house with two women inside.

Put those together and the risk on these blocks is not a constant stream of break-ins. It is a quiet neighborhood where people relax, and where the few burglars who come through count on that. They find a side door that nobody locks, a back door out of view of the street, a house that is empty on a weekday, or a house that is full and asleep at an hour when nobody is looking out. An alarm here earns its keep by not depending on anyone remembering to lock up.

Other recent items fill out the picture. In December 2025, News 12 showed a Pelham Gardens homeowner’s video of a man taking packages off his front porch “in less than 30 seconds,” the fourth package he had lost that season; a sign asking drivers to leave boxes behind the back gate did not help. In April 2026 News 12 reported an overnight house fire on Adee Avenue that also burned a car parked in the driveway. And the Bronx Times’ monthly crime roundup for a 28-day NYPD period ending October 5, 2025 found burglaries down 19.2 percent across the Bronx while felony assaults in the 49th Precinct rose from 31 to 61 and robberies from 16 to 35. Precinct numbers cover Allerton, Morris Park, Pelham Parkway and Van Nest as well, so we use them as context, not as a measure of any one block.

Our Approach

A Safe Block Is Not a Locked House: Designing Around the Habit Gap

Every alarm we install anywhere in the city does the same basic things. A contact on a door reports when it opens. A motion detector reports a warm body crossing a room. A panel decides, based on how it is armed, whether that report is a chime, a warning or an alarm, and then it sends a signal. What changes from one neighborhood to the next is not the hardware. It is the way people live with it. In a Pelham Gardens house, the biggest weakness we see is not a flimsy door or a hidden window. It is the hours when the house is unlocked because the block feels safe, and the alarm is off because nobody thinks to turn it on.

The 2011 case in the Bronx Times is the clearest local example we have. The burglar was not breaking down doors. He was walking into houses where the doors were not locked. A lock only works when someone remembers to use it; a door contact works every time the door moves, locked or not. That is the core of how we plan an alarm here: we assume the side door will be left on the latch, the garage will be left up, and the last person to bed will forget to arm, and we build a system that covers those moments without asking the family to become different people.

An example, not a measurement of any household. Your own row is worked out at the walkthrough from when people actually leave, come back and go to bed.

The four habits we plan for

The side door on the latch. On a detached or semi-detached house with a side driveway, the family rarely uses the front door. They come and go through the side or kitchen door from the driveway, and it is often unlocked all day while someone is home. We put the main keypad there, not in the front hall, and we give that door a chime in disarmed mode so anyone in the kitchen hears it open. In the evening, a stay mode arms the perimeter while the family moves freely inside, so an unlocked side door still reports if it opens after dark.

The garage that stays up. A garage door left open after the morning school run turns the garage into an unwatched room attached to the driveway. If the garage connects to the house, the door from the garage into the house becomes the weak point; if it is detached at the end of the driveway, the tools and bicycles inside become the target. We fit a tilt sensor to the overhead door and set the app to send a message if it is still up at a set hour in the evening. That one alert, more than any siren, is what closes garages on our customers’ blocks.

The forgotten bedtime arm. A household that is home every evening tends to arm the system only when someone remembers, and many nights nobody does. Current interactive alarm platforms offer arming reminders that tell you when the system was not armed by a chosen time, and notices when a sensor is left open too long; dealers such as Alarm System Store describe both features on Alarm.com service. Where the panel supports it, we also set an automatic night arm after the house has been quiet for a period you choose, and we program it so it never arms over a door that is standing open.

The house that is full at night and empty all day. Many Pelham Gardens households work standard hours or long hospital shifts, so the house sits empty for most of a weekday and full overnight. Those are two different alarms. Away mode, with every door and the interior motion detectors live, covers the empty day. Night mode, with the perimeter instant and the bedrooms free, covers the full house. The 2012 Seymour Avenue burglary at around 5 a.m. and the 2021 rear-door break-in after midnight both happened at hours when a house is most likely to be occupied, so we spend as much time on the night setup as on the away setup.

What we change about a standard plan

  • Keypad by the door you use. Usually the side door off the driveway, with a second keypad at the top of the stairs so arming at night does not mean walking back down.
  • Chime on the side and back doors. When disarmed, those doors announce themselves inside; you hear the door you did not lock.
  • Open-door report at bedtime. A push to two phones listing every door and window still open, before the night arm.
  • Garage tilt sensor with an evening alert. “Garage up at 9:00 p.m.” is the most useful message many of our customers ever get.
  • Separate areas for separate households. A rented floor or basement studio gets its own code and its own area, so the owner never has to choose between arming the house and letting the tenant come home.
  • A 24-hour zone for valuables. A closet or safe that reports when it is opened, armed or not.
  • Panic buttons where people are. Bedside, stair top and side door, set to silent or audible as you choose.

None of this requires a more expensive system. The devices are the same ones on our Bronx price list; the difference is in where they go and how they are programmed. That is why we ask about the household before we count windows.

Night, while the house is full

The 2021 case reported by ABC7 is the kind every household dreads: men forcing a rear door after midnight and claiming to be police. We do not want to make more of it than it was, and we will not pretend an alarm makes anyone safe from armed people already inside. What an alarm can do is make the back of the house loud the instant that door moves, give the people upstairs a way to call for help without finding a phone, and give whoever is at the front door a camera view and a reason to call 911 before opening anything.

That shapes three decisions. First, the rear and side doors are instant in Night mode, with no entry delay; the family uses the keypad or a key fob to come down in the morning. Second, panic buttons go where a frightened person actually is, not in a closet by the panel. Third, the doorbell camera is part of the alarm plan, so that when someone at the door says they are police, you can see how many people are there, whether anyone is moving around to the back, and call 911 to check while the door stays shut.

ABC7 reported in May 2021 that three men wearing items marked “Police” forced the rear door of a Pelham Gardens home after midnight. This routine is general household advice, not legal or police guidance.

Daytime, when the house is empty

The 2011 spree ran for four weeks before it ended, and the reporting describes a burglar choosing private homes, not shops. Away mode is the straightforward part of any alarm, and the details that matter here are coverage and confirmation. Coverage means every door on the ground floor and in the basement, including the cellar door off the driveway that nobody opens, plus a motion detector where anyone heading upstairs has to pass. Confirmation means a camera on the side or back door whose clip reaches the monitoring station and your phone, so the signal is a picture and not just a zone number. We cover both in detail further down.

Semi-detached two-families

One party wall, stacked or side-by-side apartments, sometimes shared front steps. Two households, and a neighbor on the other side whose half we never touch.

Houses with basement studios

A below-grade unit with its own door, often off the driveway or down a few steps at the back. It gets its own area, and nothing we install makes its way out harder to use.

Three-families and small buildings

A shared entry, a hall, and several apartments. Each apartment can have its own system; the building door and cellar belong to the owner’s area.

Materials and methods, house by house

Brick and stucco walls. We keep holes in the outside of the house to a minimum. Contacts, keypads and motion detectors all go inside. Where an outdoor siren or camera goes on the facade, we drill through mortar joints in brick rather than the brick face, and seal any penetration through stucco so water cannot get behind it. Wireless devices mean most houses need no new cable through masonry at all.

Bay windows. A three-sash bay is the most common front window in these styles. Instead of a contact on each sash, a single glass-break detector on the opposite wall listens for the sound of breaking glass across the whole bay, and contacts go only on the sashes that open.

Old wood doors and frames. Many original side and back doors have settled in their frames over seventy or more years. We use wide-gap contacts that tolerate a door that does not close the same way every time, and we check each door at the end of the day when the heat or air conditioning has been running, because that is when wood moves.

Basements. Cellar doors off the driveway, steel bulkhead doors and basement windows set in wells are covered with contacts or, for glass block, left to a motion detector inside. Wikipedia notes a low point in the terrain on Seymour Avenue; in houses where water has come in before, we keep the panel, its battery and its communicator upstairs and use replaceable wireless devices below.

Finished attics and dormers. Dormer windows on a Cape Cod or Tudor rarely need sensors unless a porch roof, a garage roof or a tree gives access. We check the roofline from the yard before deciding.

Party walls. In a semi-detached house, motion detectors are aimed away from the shared wall and from the neighbor’s windows, and no device goes on any part of the building that belongs to the other owner.

Business Alarm Systems in Pelham Gardens

The neighborhood is residential, but it is not without businesses, and most of them sit in or beside houses. Wikipedia describes a high concentration of private medical facilities in the area, drawn by the hospitals just south of Pelham Parkway, and CityNeighborhoods calls East Gun Hill Road a commercial corridor of groceries, bakeries and service businesses. That gives us four kinds of business alarm work here.

  • Home-based businesses. Contractors and food-service owners, whom Wikipedia lists among the area’s residents, often keep tools, stock or equipment in a garage or basement. Those spaces get their own area and can stay armed while the family is home.

Rules a Pelham Gardens business or house alarm has to respect

  • Siren time limit. New York City Administrative Code §24-238 says an audible burglar alarm on a building must be able to, and must, shut itself off within fifteen minutes of activation. Every siren we program here times out well inside that limit.
  • Licensed installers. Under New York General Business Law Article 6-D, security and fire alarm installers are licensed by the Department of State; the Department of State sets the training, exam and fingerprinting requirements. Our license is #12000287431.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide in rentals. HPD requires smoke detectors in dwelling units and carbon monoxide detectors in many homes with fuel-burning appliances, including one- and two-family houses that are not occupied only by the owner. A house with a rented floor or studio should have both, and we can add monitored ones to the alarm.
  • Fire alarm systems are separate. A business or building that needs an FDNY-approved fire alarm system gets one designed and filed for that purpose; a burglar alarm with smoke detectors is not a substitute. See our Pelham Gardens fire alarm page.
  • Exits stay usable. Nothing we install on a door may stop anyone inside from getting out. That applies with special force to a basement studio, where the door to the yard may be the only way out.
  • Privacy. Cameras tied to an alarm cover your own property. On a semi-detached house or a shared driveway, we aim them so they do not look into a neighbor’s windows or yard.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Pelham Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Pelham Gardens owners, landlords and families ask us, grouped by what they are really about. Each is answered here in full, and none of them is repeated in the shorter FAQ further down.

Living on a block that feels safe

Our block has felt safe for decades. Is there a real reason to arm an alarm on a street like ours?

Yes, and the reason is the feeling itself. The burglaries that have made the local news here were not the work of crews testing every door on a busy avenue. They were a newcomer walking into houses he found unlocked in 2011, a 5 a.m. entry on Seymour Avenue in 2012, and a rear door forced after midnight in 2021. Each one took advantage of a household that was relaxed, asleep or away, which is exactly what a safe block encourages. The 49th Precinct’s 133 burglaries in 2022 were spread across several neighborhoods, but they did not skip this one.

The practical answer is that an alarm is cheap insurance against your own good habits slipping. It reports a door that opens whether or not it was locked, it tells you about a garage that stayed up, and it gives you a way to get help at night without finding a phone. On a quiet street, that last one may matter most, because there is nobody walking past at 3 a.m. to notice.

We sit at home every evening and only think about the alarm at bedtime. Can the doors on the ground floor arm themselves once the house goes quiet?

On many current interactive panels, yes. The feature goes by different names, but the idea is the same: if no door has opened and no motion has been seen downstairs for a period you choose, after a time you choose, the system arms itself in its night or stay setting. We set it so the perimeter arms and the bedrooms stay free, so nobody upstairs is caught out by a motion detector on the way to the bathroom.

We add two safety rules. The system never arms itself over a door or window that is reported open; instead it sends a message listing what is open. And it sends a reminder to more than one phone if it still is not armed by a later time, because the person who usually arms it may be on a night shift. Which version of the feature you get depends on the panel and the service plan, and we name both on the quote.

My parents still leave the side door on the latch so neighbors can drop by. How do we add an alarm without asking them to change the way they live?

By leaving the daytime alone and protecting the night. While they are home, the side door simply chimes when it opens, so they hear a neighbor or anyone else come in. Nothing arms, nothing sounds, and the door can stay on the latch the way it always has. A doorbell camera on that side, if they want one, lets the family see who called.

The work happens at bedtime and when they go out. A key fob on the key ring arms the house in one press as they leave by the side door, and an automatic night arm takes care of the evening if they forget. If they never learn more than one button, the house is still covered for the hours that matter. We do the training at their kitchen table, at their pace, and leave a printed card by the keypad.

The 2011 burglar had just moved in with a relative in the neighborhood, so people may well have seen him around. Can an alarm catch someone who does not look out of place?

An alarm does not care what anyone looks like. A door contact reports that the door opened while the system was armed; a motion detector reports a body moving through a room. Neither judges whether the person is a stranger. That is one of the real advantages of an alarm over watching neighbors: on a block where people know each other, a familiar face walking up a driveway does not raise anyone’s suspicion, but it still sets off the side door if the house is armed.

Where faces do matter is at the verification step. A camera clip sent with the alarm lets you, and the monitoring operator, see who came through the door. If it is your nephew who forgot his code, you cancel with your code word. If it is someone you have seen on the block but never invited in, the clip is what you and the police will want.

Night, when everyone is home

In a two-story house, where should panic buttons go so that whoever is upstairs can reach one in a few steps?

Where people actually are when something goes wrong: beside the main bed, at the top of the stairs, and by the door the family uses. A fixed button beside the bed is the one most people reach for at night. A keypad at the top of the stairs usually has a panic key built in, which covers anyone sleeping in the other bedrooms. A key fob on the nightstand or a pendant worn by an older parent covers the rest.

Each panic point can be audible, which sounds the sirens, or silent, which goes straight to the monitoring station without a sound. We set that button by button with you. Many families want the bedside button silent, so help is on the way without announcing it, and the stair-top one audible.

Men in police-style vests forced a rear door in Pelham Gardens in 2021. How can the doorbell camera and the alarm help us check who is really outside before anyone opens up?

Real officers knock on doors at night for real reasons, and nobody should be afraid to cooperate with them. The point is to check before opening, and a camera makes that possible. With a doorbell camera at the front and a camera on the side or back, you can see from your phone or an indoor screen how many people are there, whether a marked car is parked outside, and whether anyone is moving around to the back of the house while someone talks to you at the front. Then call 911 yourself and ask the dispatcher whether officers are at your address. Use 911, not a number someone at the door gives you.

While you check, the alarm should already be in Night mode with the rear and side doors instant, so a second person trying the back door sets off the sirens. If a door is forced, a panic button sends a signal to the monitoring station that does not depend on you reaching a phone. This is general household advice, not police guidance; if real officers give you instructions, follow them.

Jewelry, cash and the rooms upstairs

Jewelry was the 2011 burglar’s main target. Is it better to put our jewelry in a safe with a sensor on it, or to make sure a burglar never finds where it is kept?

Both, but in that order of importance, the hiding comes second. A burglar in a house with a sounding alarm has only a few minutes and goes to the places everyone checks first: the main bedroom dresser, the closet shelf, the nightstands. A safe that is bolted down and has a shock sensor on it turns those minutes into noise; a contact on the closet door, set as a 24-hour zone, tells you the closet was opened even if the rest of the system was off. That gives you a record and an alert, not just a hope.

Hiding things somewhere unusual helps against a burglar who is in a hurry, but it fails the moment a family member forgets where something went or tells the wrong person. We would rather design so that the place valuables are kept is the most protected place in the house, and keep the rest of the details between you and us. We never write the location of a safe on any document that leaves your house.

If a burglar goes straight upstairs to the bedrooms while we are out, where does the motion sensor need to be to catch them on the stairs?

Usually at the top of the stairs, looking down the flight, or on the landing wall, looking across it. In a Colonial or center-hall house, the stair is the only route to the bedrooms, so one detector there covers every bedroom at once. In a Cape Cod with a narrow stair to a finished attic, a detector near the bottom of the stair can work better, because the attic space is too tight to cover well. We test the coverage by walking it with the detector in test mode before we leave.

That detector is on in Away mode and off, or set to watch only the downward direction where the model allows, in Night mode, so the family can move between bedrooms. Combined with contacts on every ground-floor door, it means a burglar who came in through an unlocked side door is caught twice before reaching a dresser: once at the door and once on the stairs.

Driveways, garages and the front step

The garage remote is clipped to the visor of the car parked in our driveway. If someone breaks into the car, can they walk into the house through the garage?

They can open the garage, and if the garage is attached with an inside door to the house, that inside door is the only thing between them and your kitchen. That makes three small changes worth doing. Put a contact on the inside door from the garage, armed whenever the house is, with no long entry delay. Put a tilt sensor on the overhead door, so the app tells you the moment it goes up at an hour when nobody should be coming home. And keep the remote out of a car that sits in the driveway overnight, or use a keychain remote instead.

For a detached garage at the end of the driveway, the risk is to what is in the garage rather than the house. The same tilt sensor and a contact on the side door, set as their own area, cover it, and that area can stay armed all day while the family is home.

CityNeighborhoods mentions private driveways as part of the housing here; garages vary from house to house, so we look before we list devices.

Package thieves on our street are gone in under thirty seconds. What can an alarm realistically do about the front step?

Very little on its own, and we would rather say so than sell you something. What helps is a doorbell camera that records and pushes a clip while it is happening, a chime inside so anyone home hears someone on the step, and changing where packages go: a lockable parcel box, a pickup locker or a store pickup, as News 12 suggested. Tenants, studios and rented floors

Our basement studio is rented to a nurse who leaves for the night shift. How does the rest of the house stay armed while she comes and goes?

By giving her part of the house its own area. Her door and her studio are one area, armed and disarmed with her own code on her own keypad by her door. What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Systems in Pelham Gardens

Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask and similar tools could not be opened from our build environment, so the claims below are not quotes from any of them. They are the generic statements we hear repeated by customers who looked things up before calling, and by cost guides such as HomeAdvisor and Angi. We answer each with what we see on Pelham Gardens houses.

“Safe neighborhoods don’t need alarms”

This is the claim most worth correcting here, because the neighborhood’s own reputation feeds it. Low crime rates are real: the 49th Precinct’s crime fell 71.7 percent from 1990 to 2022, according to Wikipedia. But a low rate is an average across thousands of households, and it says nothing about whether your side door is locked tonight.

The burglar the Bronx Times wrote about in 2011 did not choose Pelham Gardens despite its reputation. By his own account, he chose it because of the habits that reputation encourages. A safe block is one where the few people looking for an easy house can find one.

The more useful way to think about it is that an alarm on a quiet block is less about fighting crime and more about covering for normal life: the door left on the latch, the garage left up, the nights nobody arms. That is a cheaper problem to solve than people expect.

“An alarm costs about $750 installed”

National averages of that kind come from cost guides that blend self-installed kits, basic packages and full systems across the whole country. They are not wrong as averages, but they do not describe any particular house.

On our Bronx list, a basic wireless system with four to six sensors runs $600 to $1,200 all-in, and complete home protection starts at $2,500. A typical Pelham Gardens house with a side door, a back door, a basement entry, a garage and two floors lands somewhere between those, depending mostly on how many doors there are and whether there is a second household.

The number that matters more over time is monitoring. A low installation price tied to a 36- or 60-month contract can cost far more than a higher installation price with month-to-month monitoring from $19.99. We put both numbers on the quote.

“Arm your system every time you leave and it will protect you”

True, and almost nobody does it every time. Advice that depends on perfect habits fails in exactly the houses where habits are relaxed, which is the point of this whole page.

A better design assumes the habit will slip. Key fobs make arming one press at the door you use. Arming reminders and open-sensor alerts catch the times you forget. Automatic night arming, where available, covers the evenings. And chimes on the side and back doors protect you even while the system is off.

We judge a Pelham Gardens system by how often it is actually armed six months after installation, not by how many sensors it has. A smaller system that is used every night does more than a large one that sits disarmed.

“Motion detectors are the most important sensor”

Motion detectors are excellent when the house is empty and useless for most of the hours a house is occupied, because they have to be switched off for the family to move around. In a household that is home every evening and all night, they do nothing during the hours of the 2012 and 2021 incidents.

Door contacts work in every mode. In Night mode, every ground-floor door and the basement entry are instant, and the people upstairs are free to move. That is why our plans here start with contacts on the side door, the back door and the basement, and add motion afterward.

When we do add motion, it goes where it catches someone heading for the valuables, at the top of the stairs, rather than in every room.

“A doorbell camera replaces an alarm”

A doorbell camera watches one door from the outside. It records the porch thief and the delivery driver, and it is very useful for checking who is at the door at night. It does not know whether the side door opened, it does not sound a siren inside, and in most setups it does not call anyone.

The News 12 porch theft in December 2025 is a good example of what a camera does well: it caught the man on video. The 2021 rear-door break-in is an example of what it cannot do: the door that was forced was not the one with the doorbell.

We treat cameras as the alarm’s eyes, not its replacement. A camera on the side or back door whose clip travels with the alarm signal is worth far more than a second doorbell.

“Professional monitoring means police are at your door in minutes”

Monitoring means trained people receive the signal, try to reach you, and request police through 911 if nobody cancels. How fast officers arrive depends on what else is happening in the precinct at that moment, and no alarm company can promise a time. We will not.

What you can control is how quickly a real human with eyes on your house is involved. A camera clip with the signal, a neighbor near the top of the call list who can look from a window, and your own phone getting the push at the same moment all cut the time before someone knows whether it is real.

The Bronx Times’ September 2025 figures, with felony assaults and robberies up sharply over one 28-day period in the 49th Precinct, are a reminder that patrol cars have many calls. Design for the minutes before they arrive.

“Anyone can install an alarm; it’s just stick-on sensors”

Anyone can stick a sensor on a door. Making a system work for two households, a detached garage, a settled wood door, a stair motion detector that ignores the dog, and a schedule built around a night shift takes planning that a box of sensors does not include.

The family’s floors stay on a separate area with separate codes, so a staff member can never disarm the house and the family never sets off the office.

Can the keypad go inside a closet or behind a cabinet door so visitors do not see it?
+

It can, as long as whoever comes in can reach it within the entry delay and hear its beeps. Many families prefer a keypad just inside the side door where it is handy, plus a key fob so they rarely touch it. A hidden keypad works best paired with fobs for everyday use.

Can you quote from photos, or do you have to see the house first?
+

Photos get you a written price range the same business day: the outside of the house from the sidewalk and the driveway, each door the family uses, the basement entry and any old keypad. We confirm the final device list at a walkthrough before installation, and the price only changes if the house differs from the photos.

What happens on the first night if something trips by mistake?
+

For the first days we can run the system with monitoring on a test period, so a mistake reaches your phone but not the police, while you get used to the entry delay and the night mode. Once everyone arms and disarms without thinking, monitoring goes live. If a door keeps tripping, we come back and adjust it.

Our basement windows are glass block. Do they need anything on them?
+

Usually not a contact, since glass block does not open. Someone would have to break it out, which is slow and loud. A motion detector covering the basement, or a glass-break detector if the space is open, is the better answer, and a glass-block panel with a small vent window gets a contact on the vent only.
