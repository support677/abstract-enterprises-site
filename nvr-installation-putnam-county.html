The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Properties Need NVR Installation

A network video recorder — an NVR — takes the stream from every camera on a property, writes it to its own hard drives and plays it back when you need it. It is the opposite of a subscription camera that uploads each clip to a company's servers. That difference became a county argument in 2026. As license-plate-reader networks drew criticism across the Hudson Valley, Putnam's Sheriff told legislators that the county had kept its readers off commercial cloud platforms, with the data stored locally and never sold or shared with private companies. Neighbouring Dutchess went the other way: Mid-Hudson News reported in October 2026 that its sheriff and district attorney had taken the county's 64 Flock readers offline on September 25 and demanded their removal. Whatever you think of plate readers, the principle Putnam's officials described — your video, your storage, your rules for who looks — is exactly what a recorder installed in your own house gives you.

Keeping footage local only works if the recorder keeps recording, and Putnam is hard on recorders in particular ways. Power arrives on long overhead lines through woods; when Isaias came through, roughly 88% of NYSEG's Putnam customers were out the next morning and Carmel alone had more than twelve thousand customers dark. Internet varies from road to road: the state's September 2025 broadband awards set aside money for fibre to 390 unserved Putnam locations, and in Philipstown Optimum fibre only arrived in 2023 while Verizon was still reviewing a Cold Spring build. Many houses are empty midweek or shut for the winter. And a lot of the property worth watching — the dock, the boathouse, the shared beach, the gate at a private road — is a long way from the room where the recorder can live.

So an NVR job in Putnam is decided by four questions before it is decided by a brand. Which room in the house stays dry, ventilated and above freezing even when nobody is home? What keeps that room's recorder, switch and modem alive when NYSEG or Central Hudson is out for a day or more? How does each camera's cable get back to it across ledge rock, wet ground and long distances? And how will you find out, from the city, that something has gone wrong? Get those right and almost any good recorder works. Get them wrong and the recorder is the first thing to fail on the night you needed it.

The theft pattern the Sheriff described matters too. Crews that drive in from elsewhere tend to work quickly and at night: the Mahopac car break-ins in August 2026 happened in about ninety minutes before dawn, State Police reported ten vehicle break-ins across Carmel, Patterson and Southeast over one October 2020 weekend, and in January 2025 State Police warned residents of Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam about targeted residential burglaries. A recorder that captures a clear face at the door and a clear car at the end of the drive, keeps accurate time and lets you export a clip quickly is what turns that kind of night into evidence.

Many Putnam owners add floodlight cameras for the driveway and dock or keyless entry for a house that stands empty at the same time, because the same closet, battery backup and network serve all of them.

Four things worth checking first

  • Did the house lose power, or only the internet? If the app says "offline" but the lights never went out, the recorder may be recording perfectly and only the modem or router is down. Someone at the house can look at the recorder's monitor or its front lights.
  • Are the dark cameras all on one side of the property? Several cameras failing together after a thunderstorm usually points to a PoE switch, a surge-damaged port or one damaged cable, not the recorder.
  • Is the date on the screen right? A recorder that rebooted without a working time source can start stamping footage with a factory date; the video may still be there under the wrong date.
  • Is there a clip from before the storm you must keep? Say so on the phone. Saving that footage comes before any repair, restart or drive swap.

Rules business owners in Putnam should know

  • Putnam licenses low-voltage technicians. Chapter 145 of the Putnam County Code, the county's Electrical Licensing Law, covers a low-voltage or limited-data communications technician's work on "video surveillance systems" operating at 90 volts or less and five amperes, starting at an outlet installed by a licensed electrician, and says no one may engage in that trade in the county unless licensed. Consumer Affairs publishes the list of licensed low-voltage technicians.
  • Home-improvement contractors register with the county. Chapter 135 requires home-improvement contractor registration with Putnam Consumer Affairs, and the county page describes registration as mandatory, with insurance, bonding and workers' compensation.
  • New York State licenses the security trade. The Department of State's security and fire alarm installer licence (Article 6-D) covers closed-circuit and video surveillance work.
  • Audio is a separate legal question. New York's eavesdropping law treats recording a conversation without at least one party's consent as a felony, so we leave microphones off unless you decide otherwise with advice.
  • Cameras aimed at a neighbour's back yard. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a private right of action against video recording of a neighbour's residential back yard without consent, which is why we set privacy masks on any camera that can see one.
  • The FCC's July 2026 order on covered equipment. It stops new imports and marketing of previously authorised Hikvision and Dahua video gear, does not require anyone to remove equipment they already own, and restricts covered uses such as public safety and government facilities — relevant to town buildings, fire districts and associations that take public money.
  • A state credit can offset part of a shop's system. Retailers with no more than 50 employees can claim New York's Commercial Security Tax Credit, worth up to $3,000 for each retail location, and security cameras count toward it.
  • Cold Spring's historic district. Exterior changes visible from the street need the Historic District Review Board's approval; work not visible from the street is not reviewed.
Your Questions Answered

NVR Installation in Putnam County: Questions Answered

These are the recorder questions Putnam owners and businesses ask that our other pages do not already answer — about keeping footage local, lake and road associations, lightning and flooding, the county licence, the historic district and the theft patterns the county itself has described. Each answer comes first; the reasoning follows.

Keeping Putnam footage in Putnam

The Sheriff told the county legislature that Putnam keeps its plate-reader data on a local server, not a vendor's cloud — can our cameras be set up the same way?

Yes, and on a recorder that is the default rather than the exception. Every camera streams to the NVR in your house over your own cable, the recorder writes that video to its own drives, and nothing goes anywhere else unless you choose to send it. You can watch it on a monitor in the house with no internet at all.

Where owners lose that control is usually in the setup, not the hardware. Many recorders offer to create a cloud account during first boot, some cameras phone home to their maker by default, and some apps copy event clips off-site. We go through those settings with you and switch off what you have not asked for. If you want to watch from the city, there are two ways to do it: the maker's relay service, which passes live video through its servers without storing your recordings there, or a VPN to your own home router, which keeps everything between your phone and your house. The second takes a little more setup and works on most cable and fibre connections in the county.

The trade-off is the one the county accepted: if the recorder is destroyed or stolen, the footage on it goes with it. That is why we put the recorder somewhere hard to find, give it a camera of its own, and — if you want — set it to send a copy of selected clips to storage you control, such as a small network drive hidden elsewhere in the house.

Lake colonies, lake houses and associations

From ice-out to freeze-up the dock is where things go missing — can a recorder get usable night video of a boat tied up 150 feet from the house?

It can, but not from a camera on the house aimed into the dark. Water is the hardest thing to record at night: a camera's built-in infrared light reflects off the surface, fades long before 150 feet, and leaves a boat as a grey shape. The usual answer is a camera at the dock itself, or on the boathouse, close enough that its own light reaches what it watches, with a narrower lens pointed along the dock rather than out over the lake.

Getting that camera's video back to the recorder is the second half of the job. A cable in conduit up the bank works if the ground allows it; where it does not, a pair of small point-to-point wireless radios links the dock to the house without a trench, and a PoE switch or injector at the dock powers the camera from a protected outlet there. On the recorder we set the dock camera to record continuously at night, because a slow approach by boat often does not trigger motion until someone is already aboard.

Low lighting at the dock helps the camera more than anything else, and it does not have to be bright. A shielded fixture on a timer or motion sensor, aimed down at the boards rather than out over the water, gives the camera enough to see colour and detail without lighting up the cove for the neighbours.

The lake association wants cameras on its beach and dock — should they record to one member's house or to a recorder of their own?

To their own. When an association's cameras record on a member's recorder, that member ends up holding the association's footage, deciding who sees it, and paying for the power — and when the house is sold or the member falls out with the board, the arrangement falls apart. A small recorder owned by the association, in its clubhouse, pump house or a locked weatherproof cabinet with power, avoids all of that.

The association then decides, in writing, who holds the admin login (usually two officers), who gets view-only access, how long footage is kept and who may export a clip and for whom. We set the recorder to match that policy. Members can be given live view of the beach without any ability to play back or delete.

If there is no building at the beach, the cameras can still record at the association's recorder somewhere else, over a wireless link, or — for one or two cameras — on a small recorder in the cabinet itself. We price both on the walkthrough.

Our dock camera can see a neighbour's yard across the cove — what does New York law say about that view?

New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a property owner sue a neighbour who video-records their residential back yard without consent. That is the main state rule on the subject, and it is the reason we do not leave a neighbour's yard in any camera's view if we can help it.

On a lake that usually means aiming the dock camera along your own shoreline rather than across the water, and using the recorder's privacy masks — black boxes drawn over parts of the image so they are never recorded — to cover a neighbour's deck or lawn that cannot be cut out by aiming alone. Masks are set in the recorder or the camera and stay in place when the camera is rebooted.

This page is not legal advice, and §52-a has its own details and exceptions; if a neighbour has already complained, talk to a lawyer before changing anything. What we can do is show you, on the screen, exactly what each camera records once the masks are in.

Private roads, lightning and wet ground

Who should own, power and keep the footage for a camera where our private road meets the town road?

Whoever is going to answer for it — and that is usually the road association, not the household nearest the corner. A camera at a shared entrance records every resident, every visitor and every delivery. If it records to one family's recorder, that family is the custodian of everyone's comings and goings, and the first time there is a dispute about a fence, a plow or a visitor, the footage becomes a neighbour problem.

The simplest arrangement we install is a camera at the entrance powered from the nearest house that agrees to host it, with its own small recorder in a locked cabinet in that house or at the entrance, owned by the association. If no house is close enough, a solar-and-battery camera with its own storage is an alternative, though it holds fewer days and is harder to keep running through a Putnam winter.

Agree three things before the camera goes up: who pays the electricity, who holds the admin password, and who may export footage and for what. Write them into the association's minutes. We set the recorder to match, including view-only logins for each household.

Putnam took tens of thousands of lightning pulses in a single year — can a strike near a camera at the far edge of the lot reach the recorder through the cable?

Yes. Earth Networks counted 75,093 lightning pulses and 35 thunder days in Putnam in 2020. A strike does not have to hit the camera: a nearby strike can induce a surge in a long copper cable, especially one run along a fence, up a tree or across wet ground, and that surge travels straight to the recorder or PoE switch at the other end.

The protection is to keep long outdoor runs off copper where we can, and to protect them where we cannot. For a dock, gate or outbuilding a long way out, fibre or a wireless link breaks the copper path altogether. For outdoor copper runs we fit Ethernet surge protectors at the building entry and at the camera end where practical, bond them to a proper ground, and keep camera cable out of trees and away from the electric service.

None of that makes a recorder lightning-proof. It makes it much more likely that a strike costs you one camera and one port rather than the recorder, the switch and the router together.

When the power and the internet fail together

When the power and the internet both go down at the empty lake house, how do we even find out?

Most owners find out the slow way: the app says "offline" and they cannot tell whether the house lost power, the internet failed or something worse happened. A recorder cannot send a message once its own connection is gone, so the warning has to come from somewhere that notices the silence. Several recorder and camera apps can alert you when the device stops checking in; we switch that on and test it by unplugging the modem while you watch your phone.

To keep the recorder reachable through a cable, DSL or fibre outage, we add a router with a cellular backup — an LTE modem with a data plan that takes over when the wired line drops. It is not fast enough for hours of live video, but it is enough for alerts and short live checks. It needs to be on the same battery backup as the recorder, or it will go down with the power anyway.

In the parts of the county where mobile signal is weak — Senator Schumer complained in 2015 that northern Westchester and Putnam had more cell dead zones than the carriers' maps admitted — we check signal at the router's location with the carrier you choose before recommending it, and sometimes move the router or add an outside antenna.

Licences and the review board

Does Putnam's Chapter 145 cover the network cable between the recorder and each camera, or only the outlet the recorder plugs into?

Both, but in different ways. Chapter 145, the county's Electrical Licensing Law, describes the low-voltage or limited-data communications technician's trade as installing cabling for "video surveillance systems" and other systems operating at 90 volts or less and five amperes, where the work starts at an outlet or connection installed by a properly licensed electrician. It says no one may engage in that trade in Putnam unless licensed or registered. So the camera cabling is low-voltage work under the chapter.

The outlet itself is line-voltage electrical work for a Putnam-licensed electrician. If the recorder needs a new dedicated circuit, or an outlet in the barn or at the dock, an electrician installs it and the inspection follows the town's process; Kent, for example, says it issues no electrical permits and sends residents to Putnam County Consumer Affairs.

Putnam Consumer Affairs publishes downloadable lists of licensed master electricians and licensed low-voltage technicians. Ask any installer, including us, which licence covers their work in the county, and check the name on the list before anyone runs cable.

Which camera positions on a house in Cold Spring's historic district can go up without a review-board application?

Cold Spring's Historic District Review Board reviews exterior changes visible from the street; work that is not visible from the street is not subject to its review. For cameras, that splits a typical village house neatly. A camera under the rear eaves, in the back porch ceiling, inside a garage or on a side wall hidden by a fence is usually out of the board's scope. A camera on the front facade, above the front door or on a street-facing porch post is the kind of change the board looks at.

Most owners want the front door covered, so we look for positions that see it without being seen from the street: inside the porch ceiling looking out, a small camera in a recessed corner, or a doorbell camera that replaces an existing fixture. Cable runs inside the house to the recorder, so nothing new crosses the facade.

If a street-visible camera is the only way to cover what you need, we provide the product details and a photo mock-up for an application. Check with the village before installation; this page does not replace the board's own guidance.

Shops, offices and the crews the Sheriff described

The Sheriff says organized retail-theft crews travel to Putnam to target local stores — what should a shop's recorder capture so a clip is worth handing over?

Four views, recorded continuously at full resolution: a face-height view of every entrance, a view of the register or counter from behind the customer, a view of the back door or loading door, and a view of the lot that shows the car and its direction of travel. Crews that move between towns are identified by matching faces and vehicles across stores, so the face and the car matter more than a wide shot of the whole floor.

On the recorder we set those cameras to record all the time rather than only on motion, check that each one keeps detail at night under the lot lights, and make sure the recorder's clock is right so a clip from your store lines up with the next store's. We also set up export so the manager can save a few minutes of video to a USB stick or send a clip from the app without stopping the recording.

Finally, keep the recorder out of reach. Put it in a locked back room or a locked cabinet, give it a camera of its own, and never leave the monitor that shows the cameras where a customer can watch it.

Our office near I-84 and Route 312 has to keep its recorder in the building's shared telecom room — how is it kept out of other tenants' reach?

With a lock, its own network and a written agreement with the building. In a multi-tenant building the telecom room and sometimes the network switches belong to the landlord, and a camera system plugged into a shared network can be seen, or interfered with, by people outside your company. We put your recorder and cameras on their own switch, or on a separate network segment on your own equipment, with no path from the landlord's or other tenants' devices.

Physically, we install it in a locked, ventilated enclosure on its own outlet, label it as yours, and agree with building management who may open that enclosure. If the landlord has a building camera system, your cameras can stay independent of it; any sharing of footage with the landlord is then a decision you make case by case.

Before we start, we ask the building for permission to run cable through common areas, the route they prefer, and any certificate of insurance they require.

The Mahopac car break-ins happened between about 3:30 and 5 a.m. — can the recorder wake us for a person in the driveway at that hour without calling us for every deer?

Most current recorders can tell a person or a vehicle from other movement, and that is what makes a night alert usable in Putnam, where deer, foxes and the odd bear cross driveways every night. We set the driveway camera to alert only on a person or a vehicle, only inside a zone drawn over the drive and the cars, and only during the hours you choose — midnight to six, for example — with everything else recorded silently.

We then tune it over the first week. Headlights on a curving road, a flag moving in the wind or branches in a camera's view can still produce false alerts; the cure is usually a tighter zone or a small change of angle rather than turning sensitivity down until nothing triggers at all.

An alert is only useful if someone does something with it. Decide in advance what you will do: turn on the lights through the app, look at the live view, or call 911. The recorder keeps the clip either way, with the right time on it, for the police report.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About NVR Installation in Putnam County

Google's AI-generated summaries were not available to us when this page was researched, so nothing here is quoted from one. These are seven things people are routinely told about recorders — by product listings, cost articles and forum threads — set against the records Putnam itself has published: police statements, utility counts, county law and broadband awards.

"A cloud camera is safer than a recorder in the house, because nobody can steal the footage"

It is true that a thief cannot carry off a cloud server. But a cloud camera can only upload when the house has power and internet, and in Putnam those are exactly what a storm or a determined burglar takes away. After Isaias, nine in ten NYSEG customers in the county were dark the next morning; a camera that cannot reach the cloud during an outage records nothing there.

Cloud storage also moves the question of who can see your video to a company's terms of service. That was the concern residents raised about commercial plate-reader networks, and it is why Putnam's Sheriff stressed in September 2026 that the county's reader data sits on a server under regional law-enforcement control and is not searchable through a nationwide network.

A recorder answers the theft risk differently: it is hidden, locked and on camera itself, and it can copy selected clips to a second device you control. You keep the video, and you keep the decision about who sees it.

"Putnam is quiet — a recorder is overkill for a house up here"

Quiet is not the same as untouched. The Sheriff told legislators that between January and mid-August 2026 the county's readers flagged 27 known burglary-crew vehicles tied to residential burglaries across the region, many connected to New York City. In January 2025 State Police warned residents of Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam about targeted residential burglaries.

Smaller incidents are reported street by street: seven cars entered in about ninety minutes before dawn in Mahopac in August 2026, ten vehicle break-ins over one weekend in Carmel, Patterson and Southeast in October 2020, break-ins along two Philipstown roads in September 2024. In each case police asked residents for camera footage.

A recorder is not about expecting crime. It is about having a clear, correctly timed record when something does happen on a road where the nearest neighbour is out of sight — which is most roads in the county.

"A couple of Wi-Fi cameras do the same job without any wiring"

On a small lot near the router, they can. On a typical Putnam property they run into three problems. Wi-Fi does not travel well through stone foundations, across a slope to a garage or down to a dock; the camera that matters most is often the one with the weakest signal. Battery Wi-Fi cameras wake only on motion and can miss the first seconds of an approach. And every one of them depends on the home router staying up.

Wired cameras on a recorder get power and video over one cable, record continuously if you want them to, and keep recording to the drive when the internet is down. Where a camera really is too far for cable, we link it with a point-to-point wireless bridge or fibre rather than relying on household Wi-Fi.

Mixed systems are fine. Many Putnam houses end up with wired cameras on the recorder for the drive, doors and garage, plus a doorbell camera — but the recorder is what holds the evidence.

"Lightning only matters if it hits the house"

Most surge damage we see on camera systems comes from strikes that hit nothing of the owner's at all. A strike nearby induces a surge in long cables, and camera cable is often the longest copper on the property — out to the road, along a fence, down to the water.

Putnam gets plenty of chances: Earth Networks counted 75,093 lightning pulses and 35 thunder days in the county in 2020. When a surge comes in on a camera cable, the first things it meets are the PoE port, the switch and the recorder.

Fibre or wireless for the longest outdoor runs, surge protectors bonded to ground where copper leaves the building, and cable kept out of trees reduce the damage. A battery backup with surge protection on its outlets protects the power side. None of it is a guarantee, but it changes most strikes from a new system to a single replacement part.

"Your town's building department signs off on camera work"

In Putnam the licence that matters for camera cabling is county-level. Chapter 145 of the county code licenses low-voltage and limited-data communications technicians, names video surveillance systems among the work they do, and bars anyone from engaging in that trade in the county without a licence. Putnam Consumer Affairs keeps the list of licensed technicians.

Towns still matter for electrical inspections and for zoning — Kent, for example, says it issues no electrical permits and points residents to Consumer Affairs, while Patterson names the independent agencies that inspect electrical work there. And the county separately requires home-improvement contractors to register under Chapter 135.

The practical upshot: ask any installer which county licence covers their low-voltage work, which electrician will do any new outlet, and who calls for the inspection. If the answers are vague, keep asking.

"Fast internet is everywhere in the county by now"

Census figures put broadband subscriptions in Putnam high, at 94.8% of households, but subscription is not the same as a fast, reliable line at the end of every private road. The state's September 2025 broadband awards included money for Comcast to bring fibre to 390 unserved Putnam locations, and the same list included satellite service for addresses in Putnam.

In Philipstown, The Highlands Current reported in May 2023 that Optimum fibre was coming through the town while Verizon was still reviewing a Cold Spring build with no timeframe. A third-party listing for Putnam Valley shows cable widely available but fibre to only about half of addresses. Satellite and cellular services often use shared public addresses that block the inbound connections older recorder setups relied on.

So we check the actual connection at each house — the upload speed especially, because watching the recorder from the city depends on what the house can send, not what it can receive — and set remote viewing to suit it.

"Motion-only recording saves the drive and you lose nothing"

Motion recording does save space, and for some indoor cameras it is the right setting. But on a dark rural drive it can cost the moment you need: a car with its lights off rolling slowly, a person walking at the edge of the frame, or the second before the motion trigger catches up. Recorders add a pre-event buffer to help, but the buffer only works if the motion is detected at all.

Putnam nights are long and quiet, and wildlife triggers constantly, so owners often turn sensitivity down until real events are missed. The better approach on the cameras that matter — the drive, the doors, the road entrance — is continuous recording at a sensible bitrate, with person and vehicle detection used for alerts and searching rather than for deciding what gets saved.

That uses more drive space, so we size the drive for it and tell you in writing how many days each camera keeps. For a weekend house, that number has to cover the whole week you are away.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Breaker Panel, Finish at the Road

On a Putnam walkthrough the first thing I look at is not a camera position. It is the electric panel and the modem. I want to know which circuit the recorder will share, whether a generator transfer switch is there or planned, where the internet comes into the house, and whether the modem and the recorder can sit on the same battery. If those three things are in different corners of a fieldstone basement, that changes the whole plan.

Then I walk the property with a measuring wheel. Every camera the owner wants gets a route — along the foundation, up into the attic and across, overhead to the garage, in conduit down to the dock. I note where the ledge breaks the surface, where the ground stays wet in spring, where a tree has been half the camera's view for years. A route that sounds simple from the kitchen is often the one that needs a wireless link.

The last thing I do before leaving is stand at the road and ask the owner a plain question: if something happens here at four in the morning while you are in the city, what do you want to see, and how fast? The answer decides which cameras record all the time, which ones alert, and where the battery has to carry them. The recorder is chosen after that, and it is usually the easiest part.

FAQ

Putnam County NVR Installation FAQ

Scheduling, guests, storms, old drives and running costs: the everyday questions owners raise once the big decisions are made.

We have a place in the city and the house in Putnam — can both recorders show up in one app?
+

Usually, yes, if both recorders are from the same maker or support the same viewing app. Most recorder apps let one login hold several sites, so the city apartment and the Putnam house appear as two locations with their own cameras, alerts and playback. Each recorder still keeps its own video locally, and each site's internet decides how well its cameras stream. If the city system is a different brand, we can often still add both to one app that supports common camera standards, or set up the Putnam recorder so it sits beside the other in your phone with clear names. We check this before choosing the Putnam recorder, so a brand decision now does not leave you juggling two apps later.

If the router has to move into the recorder's closet, will the Wi-Fi in the rest of the house suffer?
+

It can, which is why we rarely move the router just to be near the recorder. Wi-Fi from a router shut in a closet, behind a stone wall or down in a basement often gets weaker in the rooms you actually use. The recorder only needs a single network cable to the router, so the usual answer is to leave the router where the Wi-Fi works and run one cable between the two. If both really must share a closet, a separate wireless access point on the main floor, fed by a cable from the closet, keeps the house covered. Either way the modem, router and recorder go on the same battery backup.

Can you work with the electrician installing our generator transfer switch so the recorder circuit is on the backed-up panel?
+

Yes, and it is worth doing at the same time. We tell your electrician which outlet the recorder, PoE switch, modem and router will use, and ask for it to be on a circuit the transfer switch carries. The battery backup then covers the gap between the power failing and the generator starting, and the generator carries the system for as long as it runs. The new outlet and the transfer switch are electrical work for a Putnam-licensed electrician; we handle the low-voltage side and test the whole chain together.

We rent the house to weekend guests — how do we keep them away from the recorder, and what should they be told about the cameras?
+

Put the recorder in a locked closet or a locked cabinet with ventilation, on an outlet that is not in a guest room, and give it a camera that watches the cabinet door. Guests should never be able to unplug it by accident or switch it off on purpose. Tell guests in the listing and in the house exactly where every camera is, keep cameras outside or at entrances only, and never put a camera in a bedroom, bathroom or any room guests use privately. Rental platforms have their own camera rules, so check the current rules for the one you use before guests arrive.

What happens to the hard drive inside the old recorder you take away?
+

You decide. Before we remove an old recorder we ask whether it holds footage you still need; if it does, we export it or leave the drive with you. If you want it gone, we can wipe the drive, or remove it and hand it to you to keep or destroy, so the old recordings cannot be read by whoever ends up with the box. Old recorder removal is $73 on the Putnam price list.

If someone steals the recorder, can they play back what is on it?
+

A password stops someone using the recorder's own menus, but a determined thief who takes the drive out can sometimes read it with the maker's software. That is why the defence is mostly about the recorder not leaving: hide it, lock it, put a camera on it, and copy the clips that matter to a second device elsewhere in the house or to storage you control. We also change every default password and turn off features you do not use, which makes the recorder harder to get into over the network as well.

How often does the recorder's battery backup need a new battery?
+

Every few years, depending on the model, the heat in the room and how many outages it has carried. Most battery backups run a self-test and show a warning light or sound an alarm when the battery is weak, and many can send that warning to the recorder or your phone if they are connected to the network. We note the battery model and the install date on your handover sheet, and if your system includes a maintenance visit we test the backup under load rather than trusting the light. In a county where long outages are common, a tired battery is the part most likely to let the recorder down.

What should we do to get the house ready for install day?
+

Very little. Clear a path to the electric panel, the modem or router and the room where the recorder will live, and to the attic hatch or basement areas where cable will run. Move cars away from the walls and the garage door where outdoor cameras will mount. Keep pets in a room we will not need. If the house is a weekend place, make sure the heat and power are on and someone can let us in. Have your Wi-Fi name and password ready, and the phone you want the app on. We bring ladders, drop cloths and everything else.

How much electricity does a recorder and its cameras use running all year?
+

Less than most people expect, though it varies by model. A small eight-channel recorder can be rated at only a few watts — one popular model lists six watts or less, before drives and cameras — and each PoE camera adds its own draw, which is higher at night when its infrared lights are on. We add up the figures from each spec sheet when we size the battery backup, and we can give you that total in writing so you know what the system costs to run.
