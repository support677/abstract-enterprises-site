The Neighborhood

Why Vinegar Hill Properties Need Alarm Installation

Vinegar Hill is small enough to walk end to end in a few minutes. Wikipedia describes it as stretching from the East River waterfront to Front Street and from the Brooklyn Navy Yard to Bridge Street, “roughly comprising a six block area,” with an area of about a tenth of a square mile and 2,671 residents in its 2016 estimate. It sits in Community District 2 and is patrolled by the NYPD’s 84th Precinct. Before the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway was built in the 1950s it was larger and reached south to Tillary Street; the Landmarks Preservation Commission counted that, of the 43 streets in the greater Vinegar Hill area, only 12 kept their 1885 layout after the bridges, the expressway and urban renewal.

What is left is unusual for Brooklyn. The Commission’s 1997 designation report describes three separate small groups of brick row houses from the early and middle nineteenth century: the north side of Front Street between Bridge and Gold Streets with two houses opposite, the east side of Gold Street just south of Water Street, and Hudson Avenue between Plymouth and Front Streets with corner buildings on Plymouth, Evans and Water Streets. Some of the Hudson Avenue houses were built by the shipbuilder John Jackson, who named the area after the 1798 Battle of Vinegar Hill and sold forty acres to the federal government for the Navy Yard. Hudson Avenue, Plymouth, Water and Front Streets are paved in Belgian block. To the north, Con Edison’s Hudson Avenue plant fills the skyline; to the east, an iron fence and a drop in level separate the neighborhood from the Navy Yard; to the south stand the ten towers of the Farragut Houses.

That geography is why we start a Vinegar Hill alarm with a question other neighborhoods do not raise first: who will notice? Forgotten New York called the Hudson Avenue area in 2009 “essentially a ghost town but the corpse is slowly reviving,” and The New Leaf Journal in 2021 called Hudson Avenue “quaint, quiet, and quite pretty.” A resident on Reddit’s r/AskNYC wrote of running down Gold Street “at all hours” without ever feeling unsafe. Quiet is pleasant to live with. It also means that a siren on Plymouth Street at two in the afternoon, or a window being worked on at the back of a Front Street house, may have no audience at all. So the alarms we design here are built to report: to your phone, to a central station if you choose one, and through a cellular path that does not depend on the old phone and cable lines coming in through the cellar.

The documented break-in we found inside the neighborhood shows the pattern. The Brooklyn Paper’s 84th Precinct report of August 24, 2010 describes a woman who left her house on Plymouth Street, between Hudson Avenue and Little Street, at about 10 a.m. When she came back from work about eleven hours later, the air conditioner in her bedroom window had been forced in, and a digital camera, two laptops and an iPad were gone. Nobody on the street had noticed. Precinct-wide reporting from DNAinfo in 2017 told a similar story for the 84th, which also covers Brooklyn Heights, Boerum Hill, Downtown Brooklyn and DUMBO: most of a winter spike in burglaries happened in the working week, when homes were empty.

The second question is what the house is made of. The designation report lists ground-floor stores in house after house on Hudson Avenue and Gold Street, records that in the 1970s and 1980s new owners “converted most of the storefronts to residential use, especially along Hudson Avenue,” and describes doorways sealed with plywood or cinder block, display windows sealed or rebuilt, wood-frame houses behind brick fronts, clapboard side walls left exposed when the neighbors were demolished, rear houses invisible from the street, wrought-iron fire escapes on the fronts of houses, and at 67 Hudson Avenue a sub-cellar with a doorway to a tunnel under the street. Every one of those details changes where a sensor goes, and most of them are covered in their own section below.

The third is what is going on next door. Con Edison is demolishing the old Hudson Avenue Generating Station for its Brooklyn Clean Energy Hub, a transmission substation that New York YIMBY reported in January 2024 is aiming for completion in 2029, with materials brought in by barge. The same plant had fires in April 2012 and May 2013; after the first, a member of the Vinegar Hill Neighborhood Association told DNAinfo there was “a lot of smoke damage to nearby homes.” Years of heavy work beside a quiet residential pocket, and the memory of smoke drifting over it, shape how we set up fire detection and how we check the system’s signals.

Throughout, the house stays as it is. Vinegar Hill’s owners petitioned for the historic district, and the Commission designated it on January 14, 1997, after hearings at which twenty-two people spoke in favor. Our starting point is that nothing goes on the facade. Sensors, sirens, keypads and the panel live inside, and wiring follows the paths the house already has. Most Vinegar Hill homeowners also pair the alarm with a camera inside the old shop room or at the rear, so that an alarm on the phone comes with a picture.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Vinegar Hill’s Architecture

The designation report gives an unusually exact picture of what we work in, building by building. Six types account for nearly every Vinegar Hill job. Each card below says what the report records about the type and what that means for the alarm.

Jackson-era houses with stores, 1801 to the 1830s

On Hudson Avenue between Plymouth and Front Streets, three-story brick buildings with ground-floor stores, some on the 1817 deed of John Jackson, with “simplified Federal and Greek Revival elements.” Facades often painted, storefronts modified, doorways approached by brick steps. These are the houses where the old shop room is now a living room.

Greek Revival row houses, 1830s to 1850s

237 to 249 Front Street and 69 to 75 Gold Street: brick, three stories and a basement, brownstone stoops with wrought- or cast-iron railings, iron gates under the stoop, areaways behind cast-iron fences, and in several cases security grilles at the basement and first story. The stoop door and the basement door are separate entries and need separate thought.

Wood-frame houses behind brick or clapboard

51 Hudson Avenue is recorded as a wood-frame house with clapboard siding built in the 1840s by a carpenter and shipbuilder; 53 and 55 Hudson Avenue are wood-frame houses with later brick fronts; 70 Hudson Avenue has clapboard above a stucco base. Frame walls take a drill easily, which helps wiring, and burn readily, which is why we add heat detection.

Rear houses and additions

The report notes a two-story brick house built in 1906 at the back of 51 Hudson Avenue, “not visible from the street,” a rear house at 204 Front Street, brick three-story extensions at the back of some Front Street houses, and a 1923 garage addition for five cars at 49 Hudson Avenue. These are separate buildings or wings that a front-door alarm never sees.

The 1870s apartment house and corner buildings

312 Water Street is a four-story brick apartment house built about 1869–76 by the grocer John Shutte, with security grilles at the first story and a wrought-iron fire escape. Corner buildings at Plymouth and Evans Streets also carry stores below apartments. Several households share one street door.

Firehouse, factory and lofts

The Constitution Engine Company No. 7 firehouse at 225–227 Front Street was decommissioned and turned into a home in 1976; the 1908 Benjamin Moore factory next door keeps its steel sash; west toward the Manhattan Bridge, turn-of-the-century brick factories hold lofts and workshops. Large openings, roof bulkheads and shared halls define these jobs.

Method notes by building type

Painted brick and brownstone fronts: nothing is drilled into them. Window sensors go on the inside face of the sash or frame, and the siren sits indoors, usually on the stair, where it is loud in every room and invisible from Hudson Avenue.

Old storefront glass: large panes in old wood frames are covered by a glass-break detector mounted inside on the wall or ceiling facing the glass, within the distance the maker specifies, and tested with a simulator. Contacts go only on the sashes and doors that actually open.

Doorways sealed with plywood, cinder block or brick: a sealed door can still be attacked. A shock sensor on the inside face of the infill reports a sustained attack on it before anyone is through.

Wood-frame walls: wire can often be fished through stud bays from the cellar, but old timber can hide old wiring runs and nails, so we open nothing we cannot close and use wireless devices where a run would mean cutting plaster.

Rear houses across a yard: wireless devices are tested from their final positions to the panel before we leave; if a rear house is too far or behind too much masonry, it gets a wireless repeater or a small wired expander fed through an existing conduit.

Lofts and the firehouse: wide vehicle doors and steel factory windows take heavy-duty contacts and glass-break coverage; roof bulkheads take a contact, and in shared buildings we agree with the other owners whose alarm the roof door belongs to before it goes on anyone’s system.

Former Shopfronts

The Shop That Became a Living Room: Glass at the Sidewalk, Doors That Look Like Doors

Walk Hudson Avenue and many of the ground floors still look like shops. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s report records ground-floor stores, past or present, in most of the district’s buildings on Hudson Avenue and at 69 and 77 Gold Street, in various states: historic wood storefronts modified, display windows sealed with plywood, a storefront removed in 1968 and replaced with brick and undersized windows, steel-framed display windows behind brick bulkheads. Brownstoner, writing about 50–54 Hudson Avenue in 2015, noted that like most of the older buildings in Vinegar Hill they have two floors of apartments over a storefront, and that the storefronts are “in varying degrees of original authenticity.”

In 1885 the streets around the district held 666 retail outlets, more than a third of them groceries, bakeries and fish stores, plus 110 liquor establishments, mainly saloons. By the 1970s the shops had gone, and the designation report says the new owners “converted most of the storefronts to residential use, especially along Hudson Avenue.” The result is a type of room that a standard alarm kit was not designed for: a living room, kitchen or studio whose street wall is old shop glass, at sidewalk height, often with a shop door beside the house door.

Three things follow. The glass is the weak point, so it gets acoustic glass-break detection from inside, aimed across the pane, rather than a contact that would only notice the glass if it were a window that opened. The shop door, whether it still works or was sealed, gets its own sensor, because from the street it still looks like a way in. And the motion detector moves to a back corner, looking across the room, because a detector aimed at a big pane of street glass sees sunlight and headlights sweep across the floor.

We also ask how you use the room after dark. A former shop room that is a bedroom needs to be armed while you sleep upstairs or in it; that means perimeter sensors on the glass and doors that can be armed while the motion detector is off. A room used as a studio with work in it may want a camera as well, so that an alarm arrives with a picture of what set it off. Where the old glass is single glazing in a wood frame, some owners add interior shutters or film; the detector is then re-tested through the change.

Old Walls and Back Lots

Wood Frames, Exposed Side Walls and the House Behind the House

Vinegar Hill’s houses are not all brick. The designation report describes 51 Hudson Avenue as a wood-frame, three-story house with clapboard siding, built between 1840 and 1847 by Lawrence Powers, a carpenter and ship builder who lived in the neighborhood from 1831 to 1853. It describes 53 and 55 Hudson Avenue as wood-frame houses with brick fronts added later, and 70 Hudson Avenue, on the corner of Water Street, as clapboard above a stucco-covered base. Forgotten New York called the whole enclave “a Brigadoonish enclave of frame homes, long-disused storefronts” and attached brownstones.

Demolition did the rest. Between 1903 and the 1980s house after house came down for the Manhattan Bridge approaches, factories, truck storage and parking. The report’s building entries keep noting an “exposed north side wall” or “exposed side wall” covered with stucco, asphalt shingles or clapboard: walls that were party walls until the house next door went. Some of those walls have windows that face a lot, a garden or a passage, and they are often at the side of the house where no one walking past would see someone at work.

Then there is what sits behind. At 51 Hudson Avenue the report records a two-story brick house built in 1906 at the rear of the lot, designed by Parfitt Brothers and “not visible from the street.” At 204 Front Street there is a rear house as well as the front house. At 49 Hudson Avenue a five-car garage was added in 1923. A front-door kit sees none of these. We put a rear house or a garage on the same panel as its own area, with its own codes if someone else lives there, and we test every wireless device across the yard from its final position before we leave.

Fire is part of the same conversation. A frame house, or a brick house with wooden rear additions, benefits from heat detectors in kitchens and cellars as well as smoke detectors elsewhere, all reporting to the same panel, so that a fire in the rear house at 3 a.m. reaches someone who can call FDNY. We explain which devices satisfy the city’s requirements for smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and which are extra.

Few Eyes on the Street

A Siren Nobody Hears: Designing for Blocks Where Few People Pass

The single most useful fact about Vinegar Hill for alarm design is how few people pass. North of Front Street there is no through traffic to speak of. Plymouth Street lies a block from the Con Edison site, Little Street runs a single public block, the Navy Yard fence closes the east side, Hudson Avenue’s Belgian blocks slow everything down, and the Farragut Houses and the expressway cut the neighborhood off to the south. Wikipedia describes it as “a very quiet neighborhood where its residents know one another.” That is good for neighbors and bad for a siren: a bell that rings for fifteen minutes on an empty street tells nobody anything.

So we build the alarm around reporting rather than noise. The panel reports over a cellular communicator first, because in many old houses the phone and cable lines come in through the cellar or along the facade, where they can be cut or flooded. Every adult in the house gets the app, with zones named the way you talk about the house: “old shop glass,” “rear house door,” “cellar stairs.” If you choose central station monitoring, the station has your call list and the order in which to try it. And the siren is inside, loud on the stair and in the bedrooms, where it frightens an intruder and wakes you, rather than outside where nobody is listening.

Neighbors still matter, and in Vinegar Hill people do know one another. A neighbor two doors down can be a key holder and a name on the call list without ever being given a code, and can be the person who walks over to look at the front of the house while you are on the phone with the station. We set that up when owners want it, and we write down exactly what the neighbor has agreed to do.

The documented 2010 break-in on Plymouth Street is the case that shows why. The owner left at about 10 in the morning and came home about eleven hours later to find the air conditioner forced in from the bedroom window. A sensor on that window, or a motion detector in the bedroom, would have reported within seconds of the unit going in, and the owner would have known by lunchtime, not by nine at night.

Big Neighbors

The Navy Yard Fence, the Con Edison Site and Years of Construction Next Door

Vinegar Hill’s neighbors are large. The Navy Yard is set off by an iron fence and a drop in level. To the north, Con Edison’s Hudson Avenue plant, which New York YIMBY described in January 2024 as a 92-year-old brick building that was the largest electric generator in the world when it was completed, is being demolished for the Brooklyn Clean Energy Hub: a transmission substation planned to deliver up to 1,500 megawatts from offshore wind, on a site bounded by Marshall Street, Hudson Avenue, Gold Street and Con Edison’s Farragut Station. YIMBY reported an estimated cost of $810 million and a target completion in 2029, with construction barges used to limit truck traffic.

Living beside years of demolition and construction affects an alarm in ordinary ways. Dust gets into smoke detectors and can make them oversensitive, so we choose detectors with drift compensation, check them at service visits and show you how to clean them. Heavy work can shake old window frames, so shock sensors are set to report sustained attacks rather than single knocks and are re-tested if the work nearby changes. Power interruptions are possible during utility work, so the panel’s standby battery is sized and its low-battery and AC-loss reports go to your phone.

Smoke from outside is the other memory. DNAinfo reported a fire at the plant on May 31, 2013, put out within an hour with six FDNY trucks, and an explosion on April 29, 2012 that sent up a huge cloud of black smoke; after the 2012 fire a member of the Vinegar Hill Neighborhood Association said there was “a lot of smoke damage to nearby homes.” Monitored smoke detection is set up so that the central station knows how to reach you first, and how to treat a smoke alarm when the cause may be outside, a decision covered in the questions below.

Inside the Landmark Lines

What Stays Off the Facade, and How We Keep It That Way

The historic district covers only the three small groups of buildings the Commission designated in 1997, not every lot in Vinegar Hill. If your house is inside it, changes to the exterior generally need the Commission’s approval. Our default design avoids that question by keeping every device inside the house. When an owner does want something outside, such as a camera at a rear gate, we work from the Commission’s Permit Guidebook, which steers security equipment away from primary facades unless there is no alternative, asks that it be small, discreet and painted to match, and limits visible conduit. The owner files; we supply the equipment details the application needs.

Inside, the same care applies to the house itself. Original doors, enframements and iron railings recorded in the designation report are not drilled for convenience. Contacts go on the frame side where they can be hidden, adhesive-mounted where screws would mark old wood, and the siren and panel go in a closet or on the stair. Wiring runs through the cellar and up existing chases rather than across plaster.

Houses outside the district boundaries, including most of the industrial buildings and the newer apartment buildings, are not bound by those rules, but their owners often follow the same approach because the streets are part of what they bought.

Installation Day

Installing in a Two-Hundred-Year-Old House Without Marking It

Owners of two-hundred-year-old houses usually ask the same thing before anything else: what will the house look like when you have gone? The honest answer is that a well-installed alarm in a Vinegar Hill house is hard to find. That takes planning on the visit, not tricks on the day.

Wiring goes where wiring already goes. In most of these houses the cellar ceiling is open, so cable runs along the joists to a closet corner, an old service riser or the space beside a chimney breast, and rises inside it to the floors above. Where a run would mean cutting plaster or lifting floorboards that the owner wants left alone, we use a wireless device instead and test its signal. Nothing is fixed to the front of the house.

Devices are mounted to suit old fabric. Contacts go on the hinge or frame side of a door, where screws disappear into the frame rather than an original door face, or on adhesive pads on painted sash. Glass-break detectors and motion detectors go on ceilings away from medallions and cornices. The panel goes into a cupboard on an upper floor, never into the cellar where it could take water.

Most single houses are installed in one working day; a house with a rear house or a sub-cellar may take a second morning. At the end of the day the technician walks every zone with you, sets the arming schedule around the hours the house is actually empty, and leaves a printed zone list so that anyone in the household can read the keypad.

Apartments and Lofts

Farragut Houses Apartments and the Lofts Toward Bridge Street

Not everyone in Vinegar Hill owns a nineteenth-century house. Many more residents live in the ten towers of the Farragut Houses, which Wikipedia, citing NYCHA, counts at 1,390 apartments and 3,440 residents, and others rent lofts in the converted industrial buildings toward Bridge Street and the Manhattan Bridge or apartments in the newer buildings on the edges of the neighborhood.

For a Farragut Houses apartment the rule is simple: the alarm belongs to you and must not alter NYCHA’s property. We use a wireless panel with a cellular communicator, contacts on the apartment door and any reachable windows, a motion detector, and an indoor siren, all mounted so they come off cleanly. NYCHA installed security cameras at Farragut in 2014, according to Wikipedia; your alarm protects what those cameras do not, the inside of your own door. We ask you to check NYCHA’s rules on fixtures and get any permission they require before the visit.

Lofts are a different problem. The door from a shared hall into your unit is usually the main entry, large steel or wood windows may be reachable from a roof or fire escape, and roof bulkheads are shared. We protect your unit as your own area, agree with the building who owns the roof door’s alarm, and use glass-break coverage across banks of factory windows. In the former firehouse on Front Street, converted to a home in 1976, the wide wooden vehicle door is treated like a garage door, with a heavy-duty contact rated for an overhead or swinging door.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Vinegar Hill

Vinegar Hill’s commerce is small and local. The designation report describes Hudson Avenue, Sands Street and Water Street as the busy commercial strips of the 1880s; today there are a few restaurants and cafés on Hudson Avenue, workshops and studios in the old industrial buildings, and businesses inside the Navy Yard fence a short walk away. Wikipedia records that Vinegar Hill House opened at 72 Hudson Avenue in 2008, in a former apartment turned 41-seat restaurant, the first eatery on the block since a diner closed in the 1970s, and that Cafe Gitane opened at 70 Hudson Avenue in 2018.

Hudson Avenue restaurants and cafés

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Vinegar Hill: Questions Answered

Each question below came from the way Vinegar Hill’s houses are built and the facts in the sources on this page. Answers that depend on your building are confirmed on the visit.

The shop that became a home

The ground floor of our Hudson Avenue house was a store for generations, and its old display window now lights our living room right at sidewalk level. How do you protect a room whose street wall is mostly former shop glass?

With detection that listens to the glass rather than waiting for it to open. An acoustic glass-break detector mounted inside, on the wall or ceiling facing the pane and within the distance its maker specifies, hears the particular sound of glass breaking and reports it before anyone has climbed through. Any part of the storefront that opens, a sash, a transom or a door, gets a contact as well. The motion detector goes in a back corner looking across the room, not at the glass, so sunlight and headlights on the street do not trouble it. We test the glass-break detector with a simulator from where the glass is, and show you the test.

Next to our front door there is a second, older shop doorway that was sealed shut years ago, but from the sidewalk it still looks like a way in. Does a door nobody can open belong on the alarm?

Yes, because a burglar does not know it is sealed until he is working on it. Whether the old doorway was filled with plywood, cinder block or brick, or simply screwed shut behind its old door, we fix a shock sensor to the inside face of the infill or the door. It reports a sustained attack, prying or hammering, while the intruder is still outside, and it is set so that a single knock or a truck on the Belgian blocks does not trigger it. If the doorway is only plywood, we also suggest the owner strengthen it, because no sensor makes plywood harder to get through.

Old walls, back lots and the cellar below the cellar

The house that once stood beside ours was demolished long ago, so our side wall is exposed and has windows facing an open lot. How should windows that were never meant to be reachable be handled?

As ground-floor windows, whatever floor they are on, if anything gives a foothold: a fence, a shed roof, a low addition, a parked truck on the lot. Those side windows usually face away from the street, which makes them attractive. Each one that opens gets a contact, and the room behind a group of them gets glass-break coverage. If the lot is later built on, the windows may become unreachable again, and the zones can be changed at a service visit.

There is a small second house at the back of our lot, hidden from the street behind the front house. If its alarm goes off, how do the police find a building they cannot see from the sidewalk?

By the account notes, which we write with you. The rear house goes on the panel as its own area, and the central station’s record for that area says, in plain words, that it is behind the street address, how to get there (through the side passage or gate, or through the front house), who holds the gate key or code, and who lives in the rear house if it is let. The station reads those notes to the dispatcher. Without them, a responder may check the front door, find it locked and leave.

Below our cellar there is a sub-cellar, and in one wall an old doorway that once led to a passage under the street. Does a space two levels below the sidewalk need anything?

Usually a contact on the door into it, or a motion detector if there is no door, plus a water sensor on the floor. The designation report records a sub-cellar at 67 Hudson Avenue with a doorway to a tunnel under the street. Whether such a passage is bricked up or not, a sensor at the point where the sub-cellar joins the house tells you if anyone comes up that way, and a water sensor tells you about the more likely visitor, water. The panel itself does not go down there.

Our fire escape hangs on the front of the house, over the sidewalk, rather than at the back. Does a street-side fire escape change which windows need protecting?

It changes which windows are reachable: every window on the fire escape becomes as easy to get to as a ground-floor window, so each one gets a contact and the rooms get glass-break coverage. The designation report records wrought-iron fire escapes on the fronts of several houses, including 51 and 67 Hudson Avenue and 237 Front Street. Nothing we install locks or blocks a fire escape window; the sensors only report it opening. A front fire escape is at least in view of the street, so on a quiet block we still treat it as unwatched.

A quiet corner beside big neighbors

Smoke from the 2012 fire at the Con Ed plant reached houses nearby. If our monitored smoke detectors report while we are away, how is it decided whether to send the fire department?

The central station follows the instructions on your account, and you choose them with us. On most home accounts we set up, a smoke alarm is treated as a fire and the station calls FDNY, then calls you, because a real fire cannot wait for a phone call. Owners who want a quick check first can ask for the station to call the premises and the first contact before dispatching, accepting the delay. With two or more smoke detectors in the house, the app shows which one reported, which helps you judge whether smoke came in through an open window. We will not set an account to ignore smoke alarms.

Our 1830s house has no closets and the stair runs straight up the middle. Where can the panel go so that someone who breaks in cannot find it and smash it in the first minute?

Away from the door and the keypad. The keypad by the entrance is only a control; the panel with its communicator and battery goes somewhere an intruder would not look in the first minute, such as a cabinet on an upper floor, the back of a built-in, or under the stair. We also turn on the panel’s communicator supervision, so that if the panel stops reporting in, the central station is told, and, where the panel supports it, we program it to send a signal the moment a door opens rather than only at the end of the entry delay, so smashing the keypad does not stop the report.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Vinegar Hill

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask, Answer the Public or Bing results from our build environment for “alarm installation Vinegar Hill,” so nothing below is quoted from them. Instead, these are seven claims that appear often in generic online advice about home alarms, each checked against what this page found about Vinegar Hill.

“A starter kit on the front door is enough for a small house.”

Cost guides and generated summaries tend to describe a starter system as a panel, a keypad, two or three door contacts and one motion detector, and to treat that as right for a small house. In a Vinegar Hill house the front door is often the least likely way in.

The reachable openings here are elsewhere: old shop glass at the sidewalk, a sealed shop doorway, side windows on a wall that became exposed when the neighbor was demolished, a basement door under the stoop, windows on a front fire escape, a rear house, and in the one documented 2010 case, a bedroom window with an air conditioner in it.

So we price from the house, not the box. A small apartment may really need only the starter set; a three-story former-shopfront house usually needs glass-break coverage, a shock sensor and contacts on every opening sash. The written quote lists each device and the opening it covers.

“An outdoor siren and a sign scare burglars away.”

The generic advice assumes that someone will hear the siren and that a sign on the front is harmless. On Vinegar Hill’s quietest blocks the first assumption is weak, and on a landmarked facade the second needs the Commission’s rules.

A siren matters most inside, where it is loud enough to cut a visit short and to wake the household. Outside, on a street with few passers-by, it mainly tells an intruder that nobody is coming yet.

What actually brings a response here is a report to a person: the app on your phone, a central station if you choose one, and a call list that includes someone close by. We put the money into that before anything goes on the front of the house.

“Wireless is always the best choice for an old house.”

Wireless devices are often the right answer in a nineteenth-century house, because they avoid cutting plaster and drilling old woodwork, and we use them in most Vinegar Hill jobs.

But “always” does not hold. A rear house across a yard, a sub-cellar under brick vaults, or a panel two floors from a cellar door can be at the edge of a device’s range, and the alarm is only as good as the signal from its farthest sensor.

We test every wireless device from its final position, use repeaters or a small wired expander where the signal is marginal, and run wire where a house is being renovated and the walls are open anyway. Most houses end up with a mix.

“Your phone app is all the monitoring you need.”

Self-monitoring through an app is a real option, and many owners choose it. The weakness is the moment the alert arrives: in a meeting, on the subway, asleep, or abroad.

A staffed central station works through your call list and calls the 84th Precinct or FDNY when instructed, whatever you are doing. On blocks where a neighbor may not hear anything, that difference is larger than it would be on a busy avenue.

Monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, so owners can try it, keep it for the months they travel, or drop it. The alarm itself works either way.

“You cannot put an alarm in a landmarked house, or you need a permit first.”

Generated answers sometimes run together two different things: the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s review of exterior changes in a historic district, and the installation of equipment inside a house.

Vinegar Hill’s historic district covers three small groups of buildings. In those buildings the Commission’s rules apply to exterior work, and its Permit Guidebook steers security equipment away from primary facades. An alarm installed entirely inside the house adds nothing to the facade.

That is why our default design is interior-only. Where an owner wants an outdoor camera or device, we provide the equipment details for the owner’s application and wait for approval. This is a description of how we work, not legal advice.

“Glass-break detectors cause false alarms.”

Older or badly placed glass-break detectors did respond to sharp noises. Modern acoustic detectors listen for the combination of sounds that breaking glass makes, and they work well when they are mounted at the right distance and angle from the glass they cover.

In a former shop room, the alternative is worse: a contact cannot see a fixed display pane at all, and a motion detector aimed at street glass sees sun and headlights. Glass-break detection is the device that fits that room.

We test each detector with a simulator from the glass it covers, show you the result, and adjust sensitivity at a service visit if a particular household noise turns out to be a problem.

“Any handyman can install an alarm.”

Some general summaries suggest that installing an alarm is a simple job anyone can do. Owners can install their own do-it-yourself kits, but in New York anyone who installs, services or maintains a security or fire alarm system for others must hold a Department of State installer license.

Licensing is not a guarantee of good work, but it is a minimum: the license holder has been fingerprinted, completed qualifying education and passed an exam, according to the Department of State’s licensing page.

Our work runs under license #12000287431, and we bring our insurance certificate on request. Ask any installer for both before they start drilling in a two-hundred-year-old house.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Back of the Lot, Not the Front Door

This is how our technicians are asked to survey a Vinegar Hill house. It describes our method, not a particular job. The survey does not start at the front door. It starts at the back of the lot, because that is where the parts a front-door kit misses are: the rear house, the yard gate, the windows on an exposed side wall, the back of the old shop.

From the back the technician works forward and down. Every opening is listed with what it is made of and whether it opens. Then the cellar, and if there is one, the sub-cellar, with a note of where water has stood and where the phone and cable lines come in. Only then the front: the old shop glass, the sealed doorway, the house door, the fire escape if it is on the front.

Last comes the question that decides more than any device: who will be told? The technician asks who should get the app, whether the house wants a central station, which neighbor holds a key, and what the neighbor has agreed to do. On a block where few people pass, the answers to those questions are the alarm system; the sensors only feed them.
