The Neighborhood

Why Wingate Properties Need Alarm Installation

Wingate is small enough to walk across in ten minutes and hard to pin down on a map. Wikipedia draws it from Empire Boulevard south to Winthrop Street and from New York Avenue east to Troy Avenue, and says it is sometimes counted as part of Crown Heights, East Flatbush or Prospect Lefferts Gardens. Another neighborhood guide, cityneighborhoods.nyc, runs it down to Clarkson Avenue. Older writing about the same ground calls it Pigtown, after the small animal farms that NYC Parks says lasted here well into the twentieth century, and sets the west edge at Nostrand Avenue. The name Wingate came from George W. Wingate High School and Wingate Park. The dates conflict too: NYC Parks says old Kingston Avenue Hospital buildings were demolished in 1954 to build the school, Wikipedia gives the school's opening as 1956 and cityneighborhoods.nyc as 1955; NYC Parks says the city bought the park land in 1952 and opened the park in 1957, while cityneighborhoods.nyc dates the field to 1941. The NYPD's own 71st Precinct page lists Wingate by name, alongside the southern part of Crown Heights and Prospect Lefferts.

What every version shares is the hospital district to the south. NYC Parks describes Kings County Hospital Center as standing directly opposite Wingate Park on the south side of Winthrop Street. Wikipedia lists the hospital at 451 Clarkson Avenue with 627 beds and calls it a Level I trauma center; SUNY Downstate's College of Medicine is at 450 Clarkson Avenue and reported about 8,000 faculty and staff in 2018; Kingsboro Psychiatric Center is further east at 681 Clarkson Avenue. BK Reader's 2024 profile of Wingate names Kings County as a major local employer, and cityneighborhoods.nyc says growth around these institutions from the 1920s to the 1950s drew health professionals and support workers to the surrounding blocks.

The census shows what that means for daily life. In ZIP 11203, which covers the southern half of Wingate and much of East Flatbush, 44.1 percent of employed residents work in education, health care or social assistance; across Brooklyn the figure is 29.3 percent. In the same ZIP, 20.9 percent of commuters leave for work before 5 a.m., between noon and 4 p.m., or between 4 p.m. and midnight, against 14.9 percent borough-wide. Those are the people who come home in daylight and sleep while the street is busy. The ZIPs are larger than Wingate, so treat the figures as the character of the area rather than a count of any one block.

Then look at when burglaries here began. We pulled every NYPD burglary complaint from 2021 through 2025 inside a rectangle bounded by Empire Boulevard, Troy Avenue, Winthrop Street and New York Avenue, using the city's open complaint data. There are 66 records; 38 are homes, 28 of them apartments and 10 one- or two-family houses. Nineteen of those 38 home burglaries have a starting time between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m., and nine started between midnight and 6 a.m. The starting time is when the reported window began, not a confirmed moment of entry, but the pattern is plain enough: the hours a night-shift worker is asleep with the curtains drawn are the hours most of these homes were entered.

That is the thesis of this page. A standard alarm is designed around two states: everybody out, or everybody in bed at night. A Wingate household is often in a third state, with one person asleep at noon, a phone face-down on Do Not Disturb, a partner at work, and a teenager due home at three. Add a street where ambulance sirens are part of the background, and a summer concert series a block or two away, and the design questions change: which doors stay armed while the sleeper's floor stays quiet, how an alert gets through a silenced phone, who the monitoring center calls when the only person home cannot answer, and what makes a neighbor look up when a siren sounds. The rest of the page works through those questions for the actual buildings here.

A neighborhood that changed names, not character

The Neighborhoods, a 2024 local-history newsletter, describes Pigtown as one of the last parts of Brooklyn to get paved roads and sewers, settled by squatters and farmers in the nineteenth century and by a large Italian population in the early twentieth; it places Rudy Giuliani's childhood apartment on Hawthorne Street and notes a Hasidic community closer to the Crown Heights border and a West Indian community closer to Nostrand Avenue and Winthrop Street. Forgotten New York points out that Empire Boulevard itself was Malbone Street until the subway wreck of November 1, 1918, and that a short stretch between New York Avenue and Clove Road still carries the old name. None of that changes how a door sensor works, but it explains the mix of buildings and households a technician meets on a single block.

It also explains the cross-sell we make most often here. A household that sleeps by day usually wants to see who is ringing the bell without going downstairs, so the doorbell camera and the alarm are planned together. Many Wingate owners also ask about a video intercom for a two-family street door so the upstairs tenant can answer their own visitors.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Wingate's Architecture

The city's property file, PLUTO, lets us count what stands on these blocks instead of guessing. Inside the rectangle from Empire Boulevard to Winthrop Street and New York Avenue to Troy Avenue there are 941 residential lots. Most are small brick houses; half of the homes are in a few dozen elevator buildings. Each type needs a slightly different plan.

One-family brick row houses

234 lots, mostly recorded as built between 1920 and 1939. Typically attached, two stories over a cellar, with a front door and vestibule up a short stoop and a yard door at the back. The bedroom floor is the top floor, which makes a day-sleep mode simple.

Two-family brick houses

340 lots, the most common building here. The owner usually lives on one floor and rents the other, so the panel gets two areas, and the stair hall both households share is planned before anything else.

Three-family houses and walk-ups

274 lots in PLUTO's walk-up classes, from 1920s three-family houses to walk-ups built since 1990. More doors in the shared hall, more people with keys, and often a basement unit with its own entrance.

Six-story pre-war apartment buildings

Thirteen of the 32 elevator buildings date from 1920 to 1939, most six stories, on Lefferts Avenue, Kingston Avenue, Brooklyn Avenue and Empire Boulevard. Apartment systems here are wireless and leave the building's door frame alone.

Postwar and tower apartments

Six-story buildings from the 1950s and 1960s on Albany Avenue, Fenimore Street and New York Avenue, plus a 16-floor building on Brooklyn Avenue from 1959 and a 20-floor tower on East New York Avenue from 1968, as PLUTO records them.

Newer infill and condos

154 residential lots carry a year of 1990 or later: walk-ups, small condo buildings and five- to nine-story buildings on Winthrop Street and East New York Avenue. Many have through-wall air-conditioner sleeves and lighter doors than the brick houses around them.

Method notes by building type

Brick row houses. Solid masonry party walls carry wireless signals poorly from one end of a deep house to the other, so we site the panel toward the center of the plan, not beside the front door, and check each device's reading from the exact place it will be mounted before a single screw goes in. Front doors on these houses are often original or heavy replacements with a glazed vestibule door behind them; both get contacts so the alarm knows whether someone got through the first door or both. The yard door at the back is the one most often found unlocked in our experience, and it gets a contact plus a glass-break detector for the rear room.

Two- and three-family houses. Here the shared stair hall decides the design. If the tenant's entrance is the same street door, that door belongs to neither area alone: it can chime for both and arm only when both households are out, or it can be left off the owner's area entirely and covered by the inner door to the owner's floors. We draw the choice on the survey sheet and let both households read it. A basement unit with its own entrance under the stoop gets its own area if the tenant wants protection, and nothing at all if they do not.

Pre-war elevator apartments. The apartment door is the main way in, with a fire-escape window or two at the back. A contact on the apartment door, a motion sensor that sees the hall inside it, and contacts or glass-break on the fire-escape windows cover most of these apartments. Everything is wireless, mounted with adhesive or small screws in the apartment's own trim, and comes off when you move. If the building is a co-op, we send the board the device list and our insurance certificate before the visit.

Newer walk-ups and condos. Buildings from the 1990s onward tend to have lighter hollow-metal or composite doors, sliding doors to small rear decks, and through-wall sleeves for air conditioners. Brownstoner, writing in 2011 about a block of Rutland Road between Albany and Troy Avenues, called the newer brick houses there "Fedders houses", a Brooklyn nickname that Brooklyn Paper traces to the brand name on their through-the-wall air-conditioner sleeves. A sleeve is a hole in the wall with a unit sitting in it, so we treat it the way we treat a window: a glass-break or a motion sensor covers the room it opens into, because there is no sash to put a contact on. Where the building was wired for an alarm during construction, we test the existing cable before choosing wireless.

The campus buildings. CAMBA Gardens on the Kings County Hospital grounds is supportive and affordable housing with its own management: Phase II is at 560 Winthrop Street, and PLUTO shows two hospital-owned 2012 buildings at 690 and 738 Albany Avenue whose 209 homes match the size 6sqft gives for Phase I. We install inside individual apartments there only with the tenant's and management's agreement, and building-wide systems are management's decision.

Owners who also want the street door recorded often pair the alarm with security camera installation in Brooklyn; we plan both from the same survey so the camera and the door contact see the same door.

Day-Sleep Households

An Alarm for a House Where Someone Is Asleep at Noon

Most alarm systems come with three ready-made settings: away, for an empty house; stay, for people at home; and night, for everyone in bed after dark. None of them fits a nurse who comes off a twelve-hour shift at 7:30 in the morning and sleeps until four in the afternoon while the rest of the family is out. Away would trigger the moment she walks to the bathroom. Stay usually leaves the interior unprotected and keeps the door chime on, which wakes her every time someone comes in. Night mode often assumes every bedroom is occupied and every floor below is empty, which may be right or wrong depending on who else is home.

So for a household on hospital hours we build a fourth setting, which we call a day-sleep mode, using the custom-mode or per-sensor options on the panels we install. Every exterior door and every window someone could reach from the yard, a fire escape or the stoop rail stays armed. Motion sensors stay armed on floors where nobody should be moving, usually the living floor and the cellar. The sleeper's floor goes quiet: no motion sensor there, no chime from the bedroom keypad, and a dimmed or switched-off keypad screen so the room stays dark. The entry delay on the front door is kept short, because anyone legitimately coming home has a fob or a code, and a burglar should not get thirty seconds to walk up the stairs.

Two things never go quiet. The smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detectors stay live in every mode, because a fire in the kitchen at noon is the most dangerous thing that can happen to someone asleep upstairs. And the indoor sounder on the sleeper's floor stays loud. If someone forces the yard door at two in the afternoon, the first person who should know is the person in the house, and a siren on their own landing will wake them faster than any phone.

Getting an alert through Do Not Disturb

A night-shift worker's phone is usually silenced while they sleep. That is sensible, and it is also the reason many alarm alerts go unseen. Phone makers and alarm platforms have answers for it. Alarm.com's support material describes a Critical Alerts setting in its iPhone app that lets alarm-type notifications through Do Not Disturb; SimpliSafe's help pages describe the same for iPhone and a Do Not Disturb access permission on Android, and recommend saving the monitoring center's number as a contact allowed through Focus or Do Not Disturb. Text-message alerts, both companies note, may still be muted.

We set those options at handover, on the actual phones, and then we test them: the sleeper puts the phone on Do Not Disturb, we trip a sensor in test mode, and we watch whether it rings. If it does not, we change the setting until it does. For households that would rather not be woken by a phone at all, the indoor sounder does that job and the phone alerts go only to people who are awake.

The first minute of an afternoon alarm

If the alarm trips while the only person home is asleep, the plan should already exist. The sounder wakes them. Their bedroom has a panic button or a keypad they can reach without going downstairs. The monitoring center's call list starts with the person most likely to answer at that hour, which in a day-sleep household is usually the one at work, then the sleeper on a contact that rings through, then anyone else. The family agrees beforehand that the sleeper stays upstairs behind a closed door and does not go down to look. We write that order down with you; it changes by household, and sometimes by day of the week.

Rotating Shifts

When the Rota Changes Every Week, the Alarm Cannot Run on a Timetable

Many alarm guides recommend a schedule: arm at 8:30 every weekday, disarm at 5:30. In a house where one person works three twelve-hour nights one week and four days the next, a schedule is wrong more often than it is right. It arms while someone is home, so they learn to disarm it the moment they wake up and then forget to re-arm; or it waits for a time that never comes, and the house sits unarmed. A system that keeps fighting the family's schedule tends to end up switched off, which is the worst outcome of all.

What works better is telling the system when the house has emptied, rather than when it should. Several approaches can be combined. A reminder can be sent when the last door closes and no motion follows for a set time while the system is disarmed. Some platforms can use a phone's location to notice that every enrolled phone has left, and prompt whoever left last. An arming reminder can also go to the family group at a few fixed check times, such as 9 a.m. and 9 p.m., only if the system is still off. None of these arms the house by itself unless you want it to; they make sure somebody notices.

Each person gets their own code and, if they want one, a fob. The code identifies them in the event history, so a family that works around each other can see that the house was armed at 7:12 by the day worker and set to day-sleep at 8:20 by the night worker, without anyone having to text. When someone leaves the household, or a relative who had a code moves away, that one code is deleted and nobody else's changes.

The hand-off at shift change

The busiest moment for an alarm in these houses is the morning or evening crossover, when one person is leaving for a 7 o'clock start and the other is on the way home. This is when mistakes are most likely: the day worker disarms the whole house to make breakfast, the night worker walks in to a system that is half armed, or the teenager opens the yard door during the five minutes nobody is sure about. We set up the crossover deliberately. The person leaving arms the exterior and leaves the stair motion off. The person arriving uses a fob at the door, which disarms only the entry route. Day-sleep mode then goes on from the bedroom keypad, and the alerts switch to whoever is now at work.

Next to the Hospitals

On a Street Where Sirens Never Stop, the Siren Is Not the Alarm

A 2021 StreetEasy listing for a condo on New York Avenue in Wingate warned buyers plainly that the building is near Kings County Hospital and SUNY Downstate, so street noise and sirens may be an issue. Anyone who has lived on Winthrop Street, Clarkson Avenue or the avenues leading to the emergency entrances knows it. Wikipedia describes Kings County as a Level I trauma center, and emergency traffic to it is part of daily life on the surrounding streets.

That changes what an outdoor siren is good for. On a quiet block in southern Brooklyn a siren on the front of a house makes neighbors look out of the window. Here it competes with a regular soundtrack of emergency vehicles and is easy to ignore, and New York City's Administrative Code, section 24-238, requires a building's audible burglar alarm to shut itself off within fifteen minutes in any case. So in Wingate we treat the outdoor siren as a deterrent and a locator, not as the way anyone gets help. What gets help is the monitoring center, the phones, and a person you have named.

If you want the outside of the house to say something, a flashing strobe at the door does more than volume, because it tells a neighbor which house it is. A spoken warning at the yard door ("This door is alarmed, the police have been called") is heard by the person standing there and nobody else. A named neighbor who is home by day, often a retired relative or a neighbor with a different shift, gets a phone call from the monitoring center and can look out of the window without going outside. Those cost less than a second outdoor siren and do more on this kind of street.

What we do not change because of the hospitals

Hospital traffic does not mean an alarm should be quieter inside. The indoor sounder stays loud, because it is meant for the people in the house and for the intruder. It does not mean glass-break detectors misfire either; modern glass-break sensors listen for the particular combination of a thud and breaking glass, and a passing siren is not that. Each one is tested with a simulator after it goes up.

Summer Evenings

Concert Nights at Wingate Park

Wingate Park is a 5.89-acre jointly operated playground at the southeast corner of Brooklyn Avenue and Rutland Road, with fields, a track and courts. It is best known for the summer concerts. The Borough President's office said in June 2022 that the Wingate Concert Series began in the late 1970s under Marty Markowitz, a Wingate High School graduate, and was scaled back during the pandemic before Antonio Reynoso reinstated it. BK Reader in 2016 described Monday-night concerts at 7:30 on Brooklyn Avenue between Rutland Road and Winthrop Street, opposite Kings County Hospital; Our Time Press listed August 2018 nights co-presented by WLIB and WBLS; and BK Reader in July 2025 listed three city-run Rise Up NYC concerts at the park over one weekend, with doors at 5 and shows at 6.

On those evenings the blocks around the field fill with people and parked cars, and many Wingate families go themselves. That is a reason to think about the alarm before the evening, not a reason to worry. If the whole household walks over, arm the house fully, not in stay mode, because there is nobody inside. Decide who keeps a phone in hand; in a crowd with speakers going, a buzz in a back pocket is easy to miss, so we suggest the person called first set a loud ringtone for the monitoring center's number for that night. Check the app at the break. If you stay home, the usual evening setting is fine, with the doorbell camera's motion alerts turned down so a crowd passing the stoop does not send fifty notifications.

The Public Record

What Five Years of NYPD Burglary Records Show for These Blocks

We drew a rectangle on the city's open crime data from Empire Boulevard to Winthrop Street and from New York Avenue to Troy Avenue, and pulled every complaint the NYPD classified as burglary with a start date from January 2021 to December 2025. The result is 66 records. It is a small number for five years, which is worth saying first: Wingate is not a high-burglary neighborhood by the standards of the city. But the records are consistent about when and where, and that is what an alarm is designed around.

Thirty-eight of the 66 were homes: 28 recorded as an apartment in a residential building and 10 as a house. Nineteen of the 38 have a reported starting time between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m., and fifteen between noon and 6 p.m.; nine began between midnight and 6 a.m. Of the other 28 records, ten were recorded on the street, four at construction sites, three at commercial buildings, two at houses of worship, and one each at a doctor's or dentist's office, a hospital, a small merchant, a real estate office, a public parking garage and an inside mailbox. Two of the 66 were recorded as attempts rather than completed burglaries.

Two cautions. The time is the start of the window the victim reported, so a burglary discovered at 6 p.m. by someone who left at 8 a.m. is recorded at 8 a.m.; daytime absences inflate the morning. And a burglary of an apartment where someone was asleep upstairs would be recorded the same way as one in an empty apartment. Even so, the shape fits what we see on installs: these blocks are entered while people are at work or asleep, mostly through doors, and a design that protects the house only at night misses most of it.

Published news reports of individual burglaries inside Wingate itself are scarce. The nearest we found is a DNAinfo 71st Precinct blotter from July 2016, which reported two women arrested after using a hammer to break a window from a fire escape at 501 New York Avenue at about 1 p.m.; DNAinfo filed it under Crown Heights, and New York Avenue is the street Wikipedia uses as Wingate's western edge.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Wingate: Questions Answered

Nurses, technicians, residents, security officers and their families raise the same handful of problems when we survey a Wingate house. Here they are, grouped by situation, with the answers we give at the door. The FAQ near the bottom of the page deals with other subjects.

Sleeping while the street is awake

After a night shift I sleep from mid-morning until late afternoon, often alone in the house. Which mode should the alarm be in during those hours, and what stays off?

A custom day-sleep mode, not stay and not away. Every exterior door and every window someone could reach from the yard, the stoop rail or a fire escape stays armed, and so do the motion sensors on floors where nobody should be moving, usually the living floor and the cellar. What goes quiet is your own floor: no motion sensor there, no chime from the keypad nearest the bedroom, and its screen dimmed or off.

The front door gets a short entry delay, because anyone who belongs in the house has a fob or a code. Smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detection stays on in every mode, and the sounder on your landing stays loud, because you are the first person who needs to know if a door is forced at two in the afternoon. We set the mode to one button or one tap, so arming it at the end of a shift takes a second.

My phone is on Do Not Disturb while I sleep after a shift. Will an alarm alert still get through to me?

Only if it is set up to. Alarm.com's iPhone app has a Critical Alerts setting that lets alarm-type notifications through Do Not Disturb, and SimpliSafe documents the same on iPhone and a Do Not Disturb access permission on Android. Ordinary notifications and text-message alerts can still be muted. The monitoring center's calls get through if its number is saved as a contact you allow through Focus or Do Not Disturb.

At handover we set this on your actual phone, put it on Do Not Disturb, trip a sensor in test mode and watch whether it rings. If it does not, we change the setting until it does. If you would rather your phone never wake you, we leave it silent and rely on the indoor sounder, with the phone alerts going to whoever is awake.

Who should the monitoring center call first when the alarm trips at two in the afternoon and the only person home is asleep after a night shift?

Usually the person at work, not the sleeper. At 2 p.m. the day worker can answer, see the camera clip if there is one, and give the password or ask for dispatch. The sleeper is woken by the sounder in the house and comes second on the list, on a number saved to ring through Do Not Disturb. A neighbor or relative who is home by day can be third.

The household also agrees what the sleeper does: stay upstairs behind a closed door with the phone and the bedroom panic button, and never go down to look. We write the order on the account with you, and if it differs by day of the week, for example because the person at work changes, we set the list for the most common pattern and show you how to check it.

A rota instead of a routine

Our shifts change every week, so a fixed arming schedule never fits. How does the system notice that the last person has left and nobody armed it?

By watching the house rather than the clock. A reminder can go out when the last door closes and no motion follows for a set number of minutes while the system is disarmed. On platforms that support it, a phone's location can tell the system that every enrolled phone has left, and the last person out gets a prompt. A plain check at two or three fixed times a day, sent only if the system is still off, catches anything else.

None of these arms the house by itself unless you ask for that. They make sure someone notices, and arming then takes one tap in the app. We switch on only the reminders your household will actually read, because a phone that buzzes four times a day is soon ignored.

Two of us work opposite twelve-hour shifts and cross at the front door around seven. How do we hand the house over without a false alarm?

Set the crossover up on purpose. The person leaving arms the exterior and leaves the stair motion off, so the house is protected but not hostile to the person arriving. The person arriving uses a fob at the door, which disarms only the entry route, then sets day-sleep mode from the bedroom keypad. Alerts switch automatically to whoever is now at work, because the event history knows whose fob was used.

We rehearse it once at handover with both of you, at the real time if you can manage it, and look at the event history together after the first week. Most problems at shift change come from one person disarming the whole house out of habit, and that shows up straight away in the log.

Living next to the hospitals

Our block is used to hearing ambulances day and night. How do we make our alarm something the neighbors will actually react to?

Make it specific rather than louder. A flashing strobe at the door tells a neighbor which house it is; a spoken warning at the yard door is heard by the person standing there; and a call from the monitoring center to a named neighbor who is home by day gets someone to look out of the window without going outside. Those do more on a street full of sirens than a second outdoor siren.

An outdoor siren is still worth having as a deterrent and to mark the house, and city law caps it at fifteen minutes in any case. But in Wingate we plan as if nobody will act on the noise alone, and put the money into monitoring, phones that ring through and the neighbor call.

Getting home in the morning

I get home around eight in the morning with my hands full and want to be asleep within minutes. What is the quickest safe way in?

A fob on your key ring, or a keypad mounted inside the door at a height you can reach with a bag on your arm. The fob disarms only the entry route; the rest of the house stays armed until you set day-sleep mode from upstairs, which is one button on the bedroom keypad or the fob itself. You never have to cross the living floor to a keypad by the kitchen.

If you prefer your phone, many current systems disarm from the app as you reach the door, but phones run flat after a twelve-hour shift, so we always set a fob or code as the backup. A duress code, which disarms normally but tells the monitoring center you are being forced, is worth learning too; it costs nothing to set.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Wingate

Google's AI Overview was not reachable from the environment we research in, so we quote none of it. Instead, here are seven ideas that turn up again and again in one-size-fits-all alarm advice, on national lead sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr and in chatbot answers, each tested against the Wingate records.

"An alarm only needs to be on when nobody is home."

This is the most common idea in one-size-fits-all alarm advice and AI summaries: arm it when you leave, arm it at bedtime. It assumes a house is either empty or asleep at night.

In Wingate that misses the biggest risk. Nineteen of the 38 home burglaries in the 2021–2025 NYPD record for these blocks had a reported starting time between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m., the hours a night-shift worker is usually asleep, and the census shows a far larger share of night and evening commuters in ZIP 11203 than in Brooklyn as a whole.

The answer is a mode for being home and asleep in daylight, with the doors and the floors below armed and the sleeper's floor quiet. Most panels can do it; few installs set it up unless someone asks.

"Set an arming schedule and forget about it."

Schedules are often presented as the easy fix for people who forget to arm. They are, for people with regular hours.

For a household on rotating hospital shifts, a fixed schedule arms when someone is home and waits when nobody is, and the family ends up disarming it out of habit. A system that fights the household tends to end up switched off.

Reminders based on what the house is doing, such as the last door closing with no motion after it, or every phone having left, work with a rota instead of against it, and leave the decision to arm with a person.

"The siren is what scares burglars off and brings help."

Many summaries treat the outdoor siren as the core of an alarm. A loud siren is a real deterrent, and it marks the house.

On blocks within earshot of a Level I trauma center, sirens are background noise, and a 2021 StreetEasy listing nearby warned buyers about exactly that. City law also shuts a building's burglar alarm siren off within fifteen minutes.

Help comes from the monitoring center, from phones that ring through Do Not Disturb and from a named neighbor who is home by day. A strobe and a spoken warning at the door do more to identify the house than extra volume.

"Phone alerts mean you'll always know."

App-based kits are often sold on the idea that the alert is in your pocket, wherever you are.

A night-shift worker's phone is silenced while they sleep, and ordinary notifications and texts stay silent too. Alarm.com and SimpliSafe both publish settings to get alarm alerts through Do Not Disturb, which tells you how common the problem is.

Those settings have to be switched on and tested on each phone. We do it at handover, and we keep the indoor sounder loud because the house wakes the sleeper faster than any phone.

"Wireless sensors work the same in every house."

Online cost guides tend to price an alarm by the number of sensors, as if every house were the same.

Wingate's brick row houses and two-families have solid masonry walls and deep plans, and a base station by the front door can struggle to hear a sensor at the yard door two walls away. The newer walk-ups behave differently again.

We hold every sensor in its final spot while the panel reads its signal before anything is fastened, place the panel near the middle of the house, and add a repeater only where the numbers say so.

"An apartment is too small to need an alarm."

Advice aimed at renters often says a camera or a smart lock is enough for an apartment.

In the Wingate record, 28 of the 38 home burglaries were apartments, and the one news report we found from the edge of the neighborhood involved a fire-escape window at about one in the afternoon.

A door contact, a motion sensor inside the door and coverage on the fire-escape windows protect most apartments here, wirelessly, without touching the building's door frame, and the whole system leaves with you.

"Professional installation always costs thousands."

AI answers and national lead sites tend to quote wide national ranges that sound expensive.

A basic wireless system on our published Brooklyn list is $600 and up, most landing below $1,200; if you already own a kit we install it for $200 to $400, and monitoring, if you want it at all, begins at $19.99 a month.

Bigger houses with two households, smoke and carbon monoxide detection and a day-sleep mode cost more, and the quote shows every line before any work starts.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Is Asleep Before You Ask Where the Doors Are

This describes how we survey a house where someone works nights; it is our method, not the story of one job. The technician's first question is not about doors. It is who lives here, what hours each person works, and who is likely to be asleep while he is in the house. If the answer is that someone is asleep upstairs right now, he works downstairs quietly and comes back to the bedroom floor at the end.

Then he walks the route people actually use, from the stoop through the vestibule to the stair, and asks which door the night worker comes in by at eight in the morning. That door gets the fob reader or the keypad. He looks at the bedroom: where a keypad can go that will be reachable from the bed but dark, where a panic button sits, and whether the windows in that room can be reached from outside. Only then does he count windows and doors, and hold each sensor in place while the panel reads its signal.

The last part of the visit is the phones. Each person sets their phone the way they would at night or at noon, and he trips a sensor in test mode to see whose phone rings. In our experience that ten minutes, and the written call order that comes out of it, decide whether a shift-work household is still using its alarm a year later.
