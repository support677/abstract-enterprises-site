How It Works

What Elevator Access Control Is, and the Four Ways NYC Buildings Use It

Most New York elevators were built to answer anyone who presses a button. The front door may be locked and the lobby may have a doorman, but once a person is in the car, every floor is one press away. Elevator access control closes that gap. A credential reader is added to the cab, to the lobby call station, or to both, and the elevator stops honoring a floor button until a valid credential has been shown for that floor.

There are four common ways to do it, and the right one depends on the building, not on the brand of reader:

  • Cab reader that enables the car. The rider shows a fob or card inside the car and the car becomes usable. It is the simplest version, and also the weakest, because once the car is enabled the rider can usually press any floor the car serves.
  • Floor-by-floor lockout. Every floor button is locked by default. The reader tells the access control panel who the rider is, and the panel closes a relay for each floor that person may reach, for a few seconds, so only those buttons light. This is what most co-op boards, condo associations and multi-tenant office owners mean when they ask for an elevator floor restriction system.
  • Hall-call (landing) control. The reader sits at the lobby or garage call button, so a stranger cannot even bring the car to the lobby or up from the garage without a credential. It is common at garage levels and back entrances.
  • Destination dispatch integration. In newer office towers the rider picks a floor on a lobby kiosk and is assigned a car. Here the access control system sends the elevator system a permission over a data link, instead of switching buttons with relays.

In every version the elevator itself still decides how to move. The access control system only grants or denies the right to ask for a floor. That separation is the reason the work is shared between a low-voltage security contractor and a licensed elevator contractor in New York, and it is the reason a good installation never touches the safety circuits of the car.

When people search for an elevator security system, an elevator card reader, an elevator key fob system or a lift access control system, they are usually describing one of these four designs. When they search for elevator access management, credential management or an elevator access control management platform, they mean the software side: who holds which credential, which floors that credential opens, at what hours, and where the record of each ride is kept.

Who Does What

The Line Between Low-Voltage Access Control and Licensed Elevator Work in NYC

This is the part of the job most often misunderstood, and getting it wrong is what stalls projects. In New York City, work on the elevator itself is regulated work. Since January 1, 2022, under the New York State Elevator Safety Act, anyone who performs elevator work, meaning the alteration, installation, maintenance, repair, replacement or modernization of elevators, must hold both a New York City and a New York State license, and the employer must have a Licensed Elevator Agency Director. Filings for elevator alterations go through the Department of Buildings' DOB NOW: Build system.

Abstract Enterprises Security Systems is a licensed low-voltage security contractor, not an elevator agency, and we say so on every quote. That is not a limitation in practice, because elevator access control is designed in two halves with a clean line between them:

Life Safety

Fire Recall, Power Loss and Emergency Override: Elevator Access Control That Gets Out of the Way

Every elevator in New York City follows ASME A17.1, the national elevator safety code, as modified by Appendix K of the NYC Building Code. Part of that code is firefighters' emergency operation: when a smoke detector in an elevator lobby or machine room trips, or a firefighter turns the recall key, the cars return to the designated landing and are taken out of normal service. NYC buildings also have the labeled BANK FIRE RECALL and CAR FIRE RECALL switches that the code describes.

Anything added to a cab or a hall station has to stay out of the way of that. The Department of Buildings made the point directly in Buildings Bulletin 2022-010, written about touchless call devices: an added device must not impede the normal operation, emergency operation or evacuation of the elevator, and it must become inoperative when Phase I or Phase II emergency operation begins. Floor-restriction readers fall under the same logic, so we design every system around three rules:

  • Recall wins. When firefighters' service starts, the elevator ignores the access control system completely. The interface is wired so the elevator controller, not our panel, decides what happens.
  • Know the power-loss state. We agree in writing what the floors do if the access control panel loses power. Most residential buildings choose floors that stay locked except for the lobby and the floors staff must reach, with battery backup on the panel; some commercial tenants choose otherwise. It is the owner's decision, made before installation, not discovered during an outage.
  • A manual override exists and is documented. A key switch or a programmed override lets the super, the doorman or building security free all floors at once, for moves, deliveries, a medical call or an emergency, and every use is recorded.

Accessibility counts too. A rider who uses a wheelchair needs the reader at a height and position they can reach from inside the car, and a resident who cannot easily present a fob may need a phone credential or a longer unlock window. We set those up during programming, not after a complaint.

None of this is legal advice, and your elevator contractor and, where needed, your design professional have the final word on code compliance for the elevator. Our job is to make sure the security system never becomes the reason an elevator fails an inspection.

Tenant Data Privacy

Local Law 63 and Residential Elevator Key Fobs in New York City

If your building is a residential multiple dwelling in New York City and you use key fobs, cards, phones or biometrics to let people in, NYC Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, applies to the system. It covers more than the front door. It reaches common areas reached through the smart access system, which is why an elevator floor-restriction system in an apartment building belongs inside the same privacy plan as the lobby reader.

As the Department of Housing Preservation and Development and law-firm summaries describe it, the law requires owners of these buildings to:

  • get tenants' express consent before collecting reference data such as biometrics;
  • give tenants a plain-language privacy policy that says what the system collects, who it is shared with, how it is protected and how long it is kept;
  • use the data only for granting access and monitoring entrances, exits and common areas, not to track tenants' relationships or follow them outside the building;
  • protect the data with real security safeguards; and
  • destroy a departed tenant's reference data within 90 days.

That shapes the way we program an elevator system in an apartment building. We keep the ride log to what the building actually needs, which is usually who used which credential at which reader and when. We avoid face or fingerprint readers in residential elevators unless the owner has a consent process in place. We give the property manager a clear procedure for deactivating the credential of a tenant who moves out and for clearing the record on time, and we hand over the platform's own privacy documentation so the owner can include it in the policy given to tenants.

Office buildings, hotels, hospitals and schools are not Class A multiple dwellings, so this particular law does not apply to them, but most commercial clients ask for the same discipline anyway: short retention, named administrators and an audit trail of who changed what.

Honest Limits

What Elevator Access Control Stops in a New York Building, and What It Doesn't

Elevator access control is a strong tool, but it is one layer, and a board or owner should know what it does not do before spending money on it.

What it stops well

  • Strangers who slip in behind a resident and then try to reach other floors, the roof or the mechanical floors.
  • Old keys and copied fobs that have spread over the years, once the building moves to encrypted credentials it can cancel.
  • Visitors wandering beyond the floor of the person they came to see.
  • After-hours traffic on office, clinic and amenity floors.

What it does not stop on its own

  • A person riding up with a resident to that resident's floor. Only building culture, a doorman or a camera deals with that.
  • Stairwells. Stair doors are governed by the fire and building codes for egress and re-entry, so elevator restriction is not a reason to lock stairs in a way that traps anyone, and we do not design it that way. Stair doors are a separate access control conversation with the building's design professional.
  • A lobby door propped open by a mover or a resident. Door-held alarms and cameras at the entrance cover that gap better.
  • A credential that is lent out. Time limits, named credentials and the access log make lending visible, but they cannot prevent it.

The best results in New York come from combining layers: a secure front door with an intercom, elevator floor restriction, cameras in the lobby and the cars, and a property manager who actually uses the log. We price and plan the elevator part honestly as one of those layers.

Apartment Buildings

Elevator Access Control for NYC Co-ops, Condos and Rental Buildings

In apartment buildings the elevator decides who can reach a resident's door. Floor restriction does not replace the lobby door, the intercom or the doorman; it adds a second gate that holds even when the first one is beaten by someone following a resident inside.

Co-ops and condos

Boards usually want three things: residents reach their own floor plus shared floors such as the lobby, laundry, storage and roof deck; staff reach every floor; and guests reach only the floor of the resident who let them in. Penthouse and private-floor owners often want their floor locked to everyone else, including other residents. Because a board decides these rules, we write them out as a permission chart and have it approved in writing before programming, and we leave the board with a way to change it later without a service call.

Rental buildings

Owners of rental buildings ask for elevator access control to cut down on non-residents riding to upper floors, roof access, package theft from hallways and short-term rental traffic. The main tasks are fast move-in and move-out handling, which means issuing and cancelling credentials from the property management office, and the Local Law 63 rules described above. Many owners also want the elevator credential to be the same fob or phone app that opens the front door and the package room.

Luxury and amenity buildings

Amenity floors change the design. A building with a gym, a children's playroom, a screening room and a roof terrace usually wants those floors open to residents at set hours and closed at night, while the lobby desk can open them for a booked event. Time-based floor permissions handle that without anyone switching keys.

Visitors and deliveries

A visitor who is buzzed in through the intercom should be able to reach the resident's floor and no other. With an integrated video intercom, the resident's tap on the app or in-unit monitor can open the lobby door and enable that one floor in the car for a short window. Delivery drivers can be kept to the lobby or a package room floor. This is one of the most requested upgrades in New York, because it stops the habit of residents coming down to ride up with every guest.

Building Types

Four NYC Building Situations and How Elevator Access Control Handles Each

Four New York situations come up again and again, and each needs a slightly different design.

The prewar co-op with an attended elevator

Many older co-ops still run an elevator with an operator during some hours and in automatic mode the rest of the time, or have a handman who runs the car for moves. Floor restriction has to respect both modes. When the operator is on duty, the board may want floors free, because the operator already knows every resident. When the car is in automatic mode at night, floors lock and residents use their fobs. We set that up as a schedule with a manual switch for the staff, so the operator never has to fight the security system, and we keep the reader position away from the car's original brass or wood panel wherever the board wants the interior preserved.

These buildings are also where residents are least likely to want an app. A fob or card for each resident, plus a written procedure for issuing one to a new shareholder at closing, keeps the system usable for everyone, including residents who have lived in the building for fifty years.

The loft with a keyed elevator that opens into the unit

Converted manufacturing buildings in Lower Manhattan, Dumbo, Long Island City and Williamsburg often have an elevator that opens directly into each unit, controlled by a mechanical key switch. A key that has been copied for years by contractors, cleaners and former tenants is not much of a lock. Replacing the key switch function with a reader, through the elevator contractor, gives each owner credentials they can issue and cancel themselves, and a record of who rode to their floor. Because the elevator door is effectively the unit's front door, fire egress and the power-loss state matter even more than in an ordinary building, and we settle them with the elevator contractor and the board before anything is ordered.

The garage, cellar or back-lobby stop

A building can have a doorman at the front and still be wide open from the garage. Hall-call readers at the garage and cellar landings stop the car from being called from those levels without a credential, and a reader in the car can keep someone who rides up from the garage limited to the resident's floors. For mixed-use buildings, we often separate the commercial tenants' stops from the residential floors entirely, so a store employee in the cellar cannot ride to the apartments.

Roof access, package rooms and hallway theft

Some of the most common complaints that lead to elevator access control in New York are not burglaries but roof parties, people sleeping in stairwells, and packages taken from hallway doormats. Locking the roof and mechanical floors to staff, limiting the package room floor to residents, and keeping riders to their own floors cuts the traffic that causes all three. The access log then gives the managing agent something better than guesswork when a complaint comes in.

People and Permissions

A New York building has a lot of people who are not residents or employees but still need the elevator. Planning for them is what separates a system people accept from one they defeat with a doorstop.

For residential buildings, time-limited credentials for aides and service workers are also a practical way to keep the data the system holds to what is needed.

Commercial Buildings

Elevator Access Control for NYC Office Buildings, Medical Buildings, Hotels and Campuses

Commercial buildings use elevator access control to give each tenant control of its own floors and to keep the building's back-of-house floors private. The common requests we see across Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens and the Bronx are:

  • Multi-tenant office floors. Each tenant's floors open to its own employees and stay locked to everyone else, at least outside business hours. The lobby desk issues visitor credentials that open one floor for one visit.
  • After-hours lockdown. Floors are open during the day and require a credential at night and on weekends, with a schedule the building manager can change for holidays.
  • Executive and secure floors. One floor, often the top, stays locked even to the tenant's own staff unless they are on the list.
  • Medical and professional buildings. Patients reach clinic floors during office hours, while records rooms, staff floors and the roof stay restricted.
  • Hotels. Guests' key cards open only the guest floors and amenities they have paid for.
  • Schools, campuses and public buildings. Employee-only floors, service elevators and restricted facility floors with logs for every ride.
  • Service and freight elevators. A keypad or reader so only staff and approved contractors can use the car, with a separate schedule for move-ins.

In larger Manhattan office towers the lobby often already has turnstiles, and newer towers use destination dispatch, where riders choose a floor at a kiosk before boarding. In those buildings the access control system and the elevator system share permissions over a data link, and the work becomes a coordination exercise between the security platform, the turnstiles and the elevator manufacturer's interface. We install and program the security platform side and work to the elevator manufacturer's interface specification, which the elevator contractor provides.

For tenants rather than owners: you cannot add elevator access control on your own floors without the landlord. The elevator belongs to the building. What a tenant can do is ask the landlord to add its floors to the building's existing system, or to approve an extension we install at the tenant's cost under the landlord's rules. We help tenants put that request together.

Schedules

Time-Based Floor Permissions for NYC Offices, Clinics and Amenity Floors

Time-based floor permissions let one system behave differently at different hours without anyone touching a key. These are the schedules New York buildings ask for most:

  • Office floors open from morning to evening on weekdays and locked nights, weekends and holidays, with a holiday calendar the building manager edits each year.
  • Medical floors open during clinic hours so patients can ride up without a credential, and locked when the practice closes.
  • Amenity floors such as gyms, playrooms and roof terraces open to residents at posted hours and closed overnight.
  • Move-in and move-out days, when a freight or service car runs on independent service and floor restriction is suspended for that car only.

Infrastructure

Power, Network and Machine Room Planning for Elevator Access Control in NYC

The quiet part of an elevator access control installation is the infrastructure behind the reader. Getting it right is what keeps the system running years later.

  • Power. The access control panel and interface boards need a dedicated power supply with battery backup sized for the readers and relays. Where a new outlet or circuit is needed, it goes to a licensed electrician and appears as its own line, because Abstract Enterprises Security Systems does not do electrical work.
  • Network. Cloud platforms need a reliable internet connection at the panel. In buildings where the machine room has no network, we either run cable from the building's network closet or plan a cellular connection, and we confirm the panel still makes local decisions if the internet drops, so riders are not stranded by an outage at the provider.
  • Location. The panel and interface boards sit where the elevator contractor can reach the connection point and where building staff cannot casually unplug them, usually in or next to the machine room or controller room, in a locked enclosure.
  • Cable. Reader cable to the car usually travels with the car through conductors the elevator contractor provides, and everything else runs in the building's pathways, labeled at both ends.
  • Records. At the end, the building keeps a drawing or list of every reader, panel, interface board, relay-to-floor mapping and cable run, so the next technician does not have to guess.

If the machine room is locked by the elevator company, as many are, we arrange access through the building and the elevator contractor rather than asking for a key we should not have.

Glossary

Elevator Access Control Terms Explained for NYC Boards and Building Managers

Board meetings and contractor emails about elevator access control are full of jargon. These are the terms you are most likely to hear, in plain language:

TermWhat it means
Car callA request for a floor made from inside the car, by pressing a button on the car operating panel.
Hall callA request for the car made from a landing, by pressing the up or down button at a floor.
Car operating panel (COP)The panel of floor buttons, door buttons and key switches inside the elevator car.

Anti-passbackA rule that stops a credential being passed back and used twice in a row at the same reader, used more on turnstiles than in cars.
