The Neighborhood

Why Tribeca Properties Need Soundbar Installation

Most homes in Tribeca were built to store butter, dry goods and machinery, not to hang a television, and that single fact shapes almost every soundbar job we quote below Canal Street. The Historic Districts Council's account of the neighborhood describes how the early Federal and Greek Revival houses north of the old Washington Market were turned into food warehouses, and how, over roughly fifty years from 1860 to 1910, most of them were replaced outright by store-and-loft buildings and warehouses. Those buildings, the Council notes, still dominate the neighborhood's character. Living rooms now occupy their upper floors.

The name itself came late. According to the same history, a realtor coined "Tribeca" — the Triangle Below Canal — in the 1970s, after the city adopted a Special Lower Manhattan Mixed Use District for the area south of Canal between Broadway and West Street, hoping it would follow SoHo from industrial floors to artists' lofts and apartments. It did. The Landmarks Preservation Commission then designated four historic districts: Tribeca West in May 1991, and Tribeca East, North and South in December 1992, with an extension to Tribeca South in 2002. Tribeca East alone covers 197 buildings.

What that means inside an apartment is a short list of surfaces you rarely meet elsewhere in the borough. Real-estate guides to Tribeca lofts describe the same features again and again: exposed brick, heavy timber or steel beams, tall industrial windows, wide-plank or hardwood floors, and ceilings that commonly run from about 10 to 14 feet, with some conversions reaching 15 feet or more. One guide puts the trade-off plainly: "Open volume is inspiring, yet it can also amplify sound." Another warns that original single-glazed windows and thin partitions "may transmit street or neighbor noise," and that the fixes can be shaped by building rules and landmark status. A soundbar lives with every item on that list.

The street matters too. Tribeca North sits right against the Holland Tunnel approaches. The Tribeca Trib reported on residents of 135 Hudson Street who stood on the corners with "No Honking" placards; one of them, a retired film professor, said that "all summer, if your windows are open, you get no peace, just constant, constant noise," and a neighbor pointed to the Beach and Hudson intersection, where cars leaving the tunnel rotary cross tunnel-bound traffic. In 2022 Tribeca Citizen covered a new Tribeca North Neighborhood Association formed around tunnel traffic, sketching boundaries from Canal to North Moore and from West Street to the west side of Varick. In a loft facing those streets, the bar has to make dialogue intelligible against the city, not just fill a quiet room.

And residents ask the questions you would expect. A poster in r/AskNYC who had been viewing apartments with exposed brick in the living room wanted to know whether landlords even let tenants drill into it to mount a TV. That is the right first question in Tribeca, and it comes before the bracket, the bar or the anchor: who owns the wall, who has to agree to holes in it, and what the building itself will allow — a co-op board, a condo's house rules, a landlord, or, in a building still being legalized, the process that governs it.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Tribeca's Architecture

Tribeca is small, but its buildings come from at least four different centuries of construction, sometimes on a single block. These are the types we plan around, and what each one does to the bracket, the bar and the cable route.

Cast-Iron Store-and-Loft Buildings

Five-story Italianate fronts from the 1850s are the signature of the Tribeca South district, and the "mercantile palaces" of Tribeca East carry cast-iron storefronts from the 1850s to the 1880s. Inside: brick side walls, iron or timber columns down the middle, and floors converted one by one.

Late-19th-Century Brick Warehouses

Tribeca North holds some of the city's earliest surviving industrial buildings and its largest late-1800s brick warehouses. Expect thick bearing walls, heavy beams, big window bays and loft floors where the TV wall is original masonry.

Converted Factory Condominiums

Developers keep the facade and rebuild the interior: 443 Greenwich, an 1880s book bindery, became 53 condominiums. New partitions and services sit inside an old shell, so the TV wall may be fresh drywall a few inches in front of 140-year-old brick.

Federal Row Houses

The Harrison Street row of individually designated Federal houses, built between 1791 and 1828, six of which the Historic Districts Council calls a group unique in the city: small rooms, timber framing, old plaster and the gentlest drilling we do.

New Condo Towers

56 Leonard, the Herzog & de Meuron tower of 57 stories and 145 residences, is the best known of the newer buildings. New towers generally mean concrete slabs, steel-stud partitions, big glass and a handbook telling contractors when and how to arrive.

1970s Rental Towers

Independence Plaza's towers and its nine-story blocks along Greenwich Street, which left the Mitchell-Lama program in 2004. Managed buildings with their own contractor rules, conventional apartment layouts and living rooms that look nothing like a loft.

Façade-Preserving Rebuilds

Older street walls with entirely new floors and frames behind them. These behave like new construction inside — steel studs, backer plates, predictable acoustics — but the building's landmark status still governs anything that shows from the street.

Mixed Buildings Over Shops

Lofts above restaurants, galleries and stores on Hudson, Church, Greenwich and West Broadway, where the ground-floor tenant's hours and the residents' quiet hours both shape when drilling can happen.

How we fix a soundbar to each Tribeca surface

  • Old face brick: anchors go into whole, sound bricks, not into mortar that may be original lime mortar or recent repointing. We look at the wall in raking light first, avoid spalled or cracked units, and keep the holes inside the bracket's footprint so they can be filled later with little trace. Brick and stone carry a $65 line.
  • Painted or sealed brick: the coating hides the joints, so we measure the coursing and mark the bricks before drilling rather than guessing where the mortar lies.
  • Cast-iron and timber columns: treated as the building's structure, not a mounting surface. We do not drill structural cast iron. The answer is usually a nearby wall, a floor-standing TV mount or a console, with cables run tidily down the column face where the owner wants them there.
  • Heavy timber beams and posts: sound old-growth timber holds screws well, but splits, checks and old bolt holes are common, and many owners would rather keep the wood untouched. We only fix to exposed timber with the owner's say-so, using small fixings sized for the bar, never the TV.
  • Drywall partitions inside the shell: wood or steel studs located with a scanner, a plywood backer board ($85) across two or more studs when the bracket misses them, and a check that the partition is fixed at the top and not a free-standing screen wall. Metal studs add $30.
  • Concrete in new and 1970s towers: a hammer drill and carbide bit, dust caught at the hole, and drilling booked inside the building's quiet-work window. Concrete or block adds $75.
  • Plaster in Federal houses and older walk-ups: a light, non-hammering start through the plaster, anchors chosen for whatever sits behind it, and a tap test for plaster that has let go of its backing. Plaster adds $45.

One more rule covers all of it: the building's age. Many Tribeca loft buildings predate 1960, and under the city's Local Law 1 of 2004, paint in multiple dwellings built before that year is presumed to contain lead, according to the Department of Housing Preservation and Development. We drill those walls with a vacuum at the bit, catch the dust on a drop cloth and take it away with us.

Compliance notes for Tribeca businesses

  • Music after 10 PM. The Department of Environmental Protection's summary of the Noise Code limits sound from a commercial establishment between 10 PM and 7 AM to 42 dB measured inside a nearby residence, and to 7 dB over ambient on the street 15 feet away, with a separate 6 dB(C) bass limit once ambient exceeds 62 dB(C). With lofts over almost every Tribeca storefront, the residence is usually one timber floor away.
  • Bars that project into a walkway. Under section 307.2 of the 2010 ADA Standards, an object whose leading edge is between 27 and 80 inches above the floor in a circulation path may protrude no more than 4 inches. Bar plus bracket in a gallery corridor or office lobby is measured before the height is fixed.
  • Landmark rules inside the shop. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says interior work needs its permit only when the work needs a Buildings Department permit, affects the exterior, or sits in a designated interior landmark. A bar inside a storefront usually triggers none of those — a speaker fixed to the cast-iron front or visible signage on the facade is a different matter.
  • Low-voltage versus electrical work. Administrative Code § 28-105.4.7 (effective December 21, 2025) exempts sub-50-volt communication and data wiring from an electrical permit when it is done by the people the electrical code allows. A new or moved outlet behind the screen is line-voltage work for a Licensed Master Electrician under a permit, per the Department of Buildings, and is not something we do.
  • When the drill can run. City rules allow construction work on weekdays from 7 AM to 6 PM; many commercial landlords in loft buildings shrink that or push noisy work outside trading hours for the tenants above and below. We schedule into whatever window the landlord sets.
  • Insurance paperwork. Commercial landlords typically want a certificate of insurance naming them and their managing agent before a contractor goes in. We issue it without charge and send it ahead of the visit with the technician's name.

This is general information, not legal advice; your lease, your landlord's rules and the city's codes as they apply to your premises are what count.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Tribeca: Questions Answered

These are the questions Tribeca loft owners, renters and businesses put to us before they book, grouped by what they are really trying to settle: what the old walls will hold, how a bar behaves in a room the size of a floor, what the building and its legal status allow, how the bar fits a control system, and how short-term and business setups work.

Walls the warehouse left behind

Is it better to anchor into the old brick itself or into the mortar between the bricks?

Into the brick, nearly always. In a building put up between the 1850s and the early 1900s, the joints may still be original lime mortar, which is softer than the brick and can crumble around an anchor, or they may have been repointed at some point with a harder mix. Either way the joint is the weaker, less predictable part of the wall. A whole, uncracked face brick gives a sleeve or masonry screw something consistent to bite into, and a soundbar — a few pounds for a compact model, low teens for a flagship — needs only a few good fixings.

So before drilling we look at the wall closely, ideally in raking light from a window, and mark sound bricks inside the bracket's footprint. We skip bricks that are spalled, cracked or soft at the face, and we avoid the very edge of a brick where it meets the joint. On painted or sealed brick, where the joints are hidden, we measure the coursing from a known line and mark it out first. If the wall is crumbling across the whole area, the honest answer is a different wall, the TV arm or the console.

Holes in a brick face are less forgiving than holes in drywall, so the reversibility question is worth asking up front. Keeping the holes small, few and tucked behind the bracket means a future owner, or your painter, can fill them with a matching mortar or patch with little trace. If you rent, read the lease clause on alterations first; the r/AskNYC poster who asked whether landlords even allow drilling into exposed brick was asking exactly the right question.

A cast-iron column stands where the TV ought to go — can anything be fixed to it?

Not by us, and not by drilling. Cast-iron columns in Tribeca's store-and-loft buildings are part of the structure that carries the floors above you, and cast iron is brittle rather than forgiving; a hole in the wrong place is not something anyone can take back. We treat columns, and the heavy timber posts in many warehouses, as the building's skeleton rather than as a place to hang electronics, and in a co-op or condo the column almost certainly belongs to the building rather than to you.

There are usually better places anyway. If the column sits roughly where the screen should be, a floor-standing TV mount or a low media unit placed just in front of it puts the picture where you want it, and the bar either rides on the TV mount or sits on the unit. If a wall is nearby, a full-motion arm on that wall can reach the viewing position, with the bar clamped beneath the screen so the sound follows the picture as it swings.

Columns are still useful for cable. A neat surface channel running down the face of a column, painted to match, can carry power and HDMI from a ceiling-level route or to a floor box without touching the iron itself, as long as the channel is fixed with adhesive or clamps the owner is happy with. We will show you the options on the day and leave the column exactly as we found it.

The wall our TV hangs on stops short of the ceiling — is a partition like that solid enough for a bracket?

Sometimes, and it is worth checking rather than assuming. Loft layouts often create bedrooms and media walls with partitions that stop a foot or two below a 12-foot ceiling, to keep the light and the view of the beams. A partition like that can be perfectly solid if it is framed properly, fixed to the floor and tied back to a perpendicular wall or braced at the top. It can also be a long run of studs held only at the bottom, which flexes when you push it.

So we push it. A firm hand at the top of the wall tells us a lot, and a scanner shows whether the framing is wood or light-gauge steel and where the studs fall. A soundbar on its own is a light load; the concern is a TV and bar together on an arm, which levers against the top of the wall when it is pulled out. On a well-tied partition we fix into studs or a plywood backer spread across several of them. On a wall that moves, we keep the TV and bar on a fixed, low-profile bracket or move them to the console.

Free-standing glass partitions and sliding panels are never mounting surfaces. If the partition was built during a renovation, ask your contractor or architect what is inside it and whether blocking was left at TV height; it often was, and it saves a backer board.

Sound in a room the size of a floor

Our ceiling is exposed joists, beams and pipes — do the bar's up-firing speakers have anything to bounce off?

Not much, in most Tribeca lofts. Dolby Atmos soundbars create their sense of height by firing sound up at the ceiling and letting it reflect down to the listener. That works best with a flat, fairly low, hard ceiling a predictable distance above the bar. A ceiling ten to fourteen feet up, broken by joists, beams, sprinkler pipes and ducts, scatters that reflection in every direction and adds a long delay, so the overhead effect becomes vague or disappears.

That does not make an Atmos bar a bad buy; the front, centre and side channels still do most of the work, and many flagship bars sound excellent in a loft for dialogue and music. It does mean the extra cost of up-firing drivers buys less here than in a flat-ceilinged condo. Some bars let you switch the up-firing channels off or adjust their level in the app, and their room-correction routines will at least account for the space; we run those and check the result from the sofa.

If real height effects matter to you, the answer in a loft is dedicated speakers rather than reflections — small surround or height speakers mounted on beams or walls near the seating, which is a bigger audio job. For most households a well-placed bar, set for clear speech with the up-firing channels tamed, is the better outcome.

Our 85-inch screen dwarfs a standard soundbar — does the bar need to match the TV's width?

No, but it helps if it is not tiny. Big lofts invite big screens, and an 85-inch TV is a little over six feet wide. Even the longest consumer bars are well short of that — Sonos lists its Arc Ultra at 46.38 inches. The bar does not have to fill the width, because its job is to place voices at the centre of the picture and spread effects to the sides, and a good bar's processing widens the sound beyond its own cabinet.

Where it goes wrong is a compact bar, built for a bedroom TV, centred under a very large screen in a very large room. Dialogue still sits in the middle, but the sound feels small and the stereo spread collapses, especially from a sofa a long way back. Under a screen of 75 inches or more, a full-length bar, centred exactly on the screen and as close beneath it as the TV's stand or bracket allows, looks proportionate and sounds it.

If you want width to match the picture, add rear or side speakers from the same maker rather than chasing a longer bar. We set the bar's centre line to the screen's, not to the wall or the brick coursing, and we check that the TV's own feet or bottom bezel are not shading the bar's front drivers.

We sit more than twenty feet from the TV in a long loft — will a soundbar still sound clear that far back?

It can, but distance changes the set-up. Sound level drops as you move away from the bar, and in a hard-surfaced loft the room's reflections grow louder relative to the direct sound, so voices lose their edges. The usual result is that people turn the volume up to understand dialogue, the effects become too loud, and the neighbor below hears more than you do.

The fixes are mostly placement and settings. Bring the seating closer if the room allows — many lofts have the TV on the end wall simply because it is the only wall. Otherwise, choose a full-size bar rather than a compact one, aim it straight at the main seat, and switch on its speech or dialogue enhancement so voices are lifted without the whole mix being raised. A rug in front of the seating and soft furnishings along a brick wall reduce the reflections that blur speech.

If the distance is extreme, a small pair of surround speakers near the seating, paired to the bar, brings the sound to where you sit. Before booking, tell us roughly how far the sofa is from the TV wall and what is on the floor; it changes which bar class and settings we suggest.

Loft buildings, boards and the Loft Law

Our loft is in a building still going through Loft Law legalization — does that limit putting a soundbar up?

It can affect who has to agree, though it rarely stops a soundbar outright. Article 7-C of the Multiple Dwelling Law, the Loft Law, created a category called interim multiple dwellings for former commercial and manufacturing lofts that people live in, and the New York City Loft Board oversees bringing those buildings up to the Building Code's minimum standards for residential use. During that process the owner has legalization work to do, and tenants and owners often have rights and obligations under the Loft Board's rules that go beyond an ordinary lease.

A bracket and a few anchors are a minor alteration, but in a building where walls, fire separation or electrical systems are being brought up to code, it makes sense to ask the owner or managing agent before drilling, and to keep the bar off any wall that is scheduled to be opened, rebuilt or fire-rated. Console placement or a TV-attached bracket on a TV stand avoids the question entirely, and is easy to move if legalization work reaches your unit.

We cannot advise on your rights under the Loft Law; that is a question for the Loft Board or a lawyer. What we can do is plan an install that leaves the least possible trace and does not touch the building's systems, and give you photos and a description of exactly what was fixed where.

The elevator in our building is a small keyed car that opens into the apartment — how do the installer and a long soundbar box get up?

With a little planning. Some downtown loft buildings have elevators that open directly into each unit and run only with a key or fob for that floor; Brick Underground's guide to direct-entry elevators notes, for example, a Hudson Square loft building where a key-locked car opens straight into the apartments. That means nobody can send the technician up without your key, and in some older buildings the car is small, slow or shared with a freight function.

Loft Vocabulary

Tribeca Building Terms, Explained for a Soundbar Job

Tribeca brokers, boards and architects use a vocabulary of their own. These are the terms that come up when a soundbar is being planned, and what each one means for the visit.

  • Store-and-loft building. A 19th-century commercial building with a shop on the ground floor and open loft floors above, usually with a cast-iron or masonry front. The defining building of Tribeca South and East.
  • Bearing wall. A masonry wall that carries the floors. In a warehouse loft, the long exposed-brick wall is usually one, and it is solid all the way through — no cavity for cables.
  • Repointing. Raking out old mortar and replacing it. Recently repointed joints may be harder than the original; old lime mortar may be soft. Either way, anchors go in the brick.
  • Heavy timber. Large wooden beams, girders and posts that carry the floors of many warehouses. Beautiful, structural and not ours to drill without the owner's agreement.
  • Partition. A non-structural wall built inside the loft to create rooms. Usually wood or steel studs and drywall, sometimes stopping short of the ceiling.
  • Mezzanine. A raised platform within a high loft used as a sleeping area, office or media space. It changes sight lines to the TV and where sound travels.
  • Direct-entry or keyed elevator. An elevator that opens straight into a unit and runs to it only with a key or fob. It decides how the technician gets in.
  • Interim multiple dwelling (IMD). The Loft Law's category for former commercial lofts being legalized for residential use, overseen by the Loft Board.
  • Historic district. An area designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission; Tribeca has four. The Commission regulates exterior changes, and interior work only in the cases it lists.
  • eARC. The enhanced Audio Return Channel on an HDMI port, which carries the TV's sound, including lossless formats, back to the bar and lets one remote run the volume.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Tribeca

We could not capture Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so this section tests the published claims AI summaries draw on — national cost guides from Angi and Thumbtack among them — and the general mounting advice that gets repeated everywhere. Each needs adjusting for a Tribeca loft.

"Exposed brick is the easiest wall to mount on — it's solid"

Solid, yes. Easy, not always. Generic advice treats brick as the ideal mounting surface because it does not flex and anchors hold well in it. In a modern brick veneer or a newer masonry wall, that is largely true, and a soundbar's modest weight is no challenge for a good masonry fixing.

A Tribeca warehouse wall is different. It may be more than a century old, with soft lime mortar, repointed patches, spalled faces, layers of paint or sealant, and old fixings from its commercial life. The joints are where many people instinctively drill, and they are the weakest part. Some bricks crumble under a hammer drill; others are glazed hard enough to skate the bit.

So the work is in the reading: choosing whole bricks inside the bracket's footprint, starting holes gently, and using anchors suited to old masonry. And because brick has no cavity, the cables need a plan before the first hole — a channel, a column-face route or the console. Our menu prices that reality as a $65 brick-and-stone line on top of the $129 mount.

"Hide the wires inside the wall for a clean look — it costs $75 to $300"

Angi's range for concealing TV wiring assumes a hollow wall that a cable can be fished through. In a house with drywall on wood studs, that is usually the case, and the price reflects the length of the run and whether an in-wall power kit is used.

Many Tribeca TV walls cannot be fished at all. A brick bearing wall is solid; a cast-iron column is structural; a concrete partition in a newer tower has no cavity. Cutting a chase into a historic brick wall to bury a cable is a masonry alteration few boards, landlords or owners would accept for a soundbar, and in some buildings it may raise landmark questions if it reaches an exterior wall.

Where the TV is on a hollow partition and the owner or board agrees, our in-wall concealment with a code-compliant power and cable kit is $169. On brick, the clean look comes from a painted $59 channel run on a deliberate line — along a column edge, down a corner, or straight to a console — so it reads as part of the loft rather than an afterthought.

"Get a Dolby Atmos bar for the most immersive sound"

Buying advice often steers people toward up-firing Atmos bars, and in a typical living room with an eight- or nine-foot flat ceiling, the height effect they create can be convincing. The advice is reasonable as far as it goes.

The catch is the ceiling. Up-firing drivers rely on a reflection from a flat surface above the bar. Tribeca lofts commonly have ceilings of 10 to 14 feet, often exposed joists or beams with pipes and ducts between them. The reflection arrives late and scattered, and the overhead effect weakens or vanishes, while the price premium for the extra drivers remains.

That is not a reason to avoid a flagship bar, which may still be the best-sounding choice for dialogue and music in a big room. It is a reason to buy for the room: if the ceiling is high and broken up, put the money into a full-length bar with good speech processing, or into surround speakers near the seats, rather than paying for height effects the loft cannot deliver.

"Landmark buildings need city approval before you change anything"

This one is repeated with good intentions and is often wrong in detail. People hear "historic district" and assume every hole needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission's sign-off, and some will put off a simple install for months because of it.

The Commission's own guidance says otherwise. You do not need its permit for ordinary repairs or maintenance, and interior work needs a permit only when it requires a Department of Buildings permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior has itself been designated an interior landmark. A soundbar bracket on an inside wall of a Tribeca loft normally falls under none of those.

The approvals that actually matter are the building's: a co-op's alteration rules, a condo's house rules, your lease, or, in a building being legalized, the owner's obligations under the Loft Law. Those can restrict drilling, hours and contractors whatever the Commission thinks, so they are what we ask about first.

"Expect to pay $121 to $234 to have a TV wall-mounted"

That is Thumbtack's national average for TV wall mounting, built from jobs all over the country. It describes a television on drywall over wood studs in a house where the installer parks at the curb. As a national figure it is fair.

It is not a Tribeca number. Our published Manhattan base for mounting a 32- to 55-inch TV is $244.99, and every Manhattan visit carries a flat $150 parking line — the streets around the tunnel approaches are rarely an easy place to stop a van full of tools. A soundbar mounted beneath it on the same visit adds $129, plus $84.99 if you need our Sanus bracket.

The wall then adds its own line: $65 for brick, $45 for plaster, $75 for concrete, $30 for metal studs, and $75 for a high mount above about eight feet, which some lofts need. A published, itemized number that includes all of that is more use than a national average that includes none of it.

"A soundbar bracket add-on runs $30 to $100"

Angi's TV cost guide lists a soundbar mount add-on at $30 to $100. As a price for bracket hardware alone, that is roughly in line with the market, and many bars include a basic wall bracket in the box.

What the figure leaves out is labor and the wall. In a Tribeca loft the bracket is the easy part; the time goes into reading old brick, finding the studs in a partition, checking a wall that stops short of the ceiling, or setting up a TV-attached bracket on a full-motion arm so the bar swings with the screen.

Our menu separates the parts so you can see them: $129 to mount the bar, $84.99 for a Sanus bracket only if you need one, and the wall line where it applies. If your bar came with a suitable bracket, or you already own a better one, that $84.99 line simply drops out.

"Put the subwoofer in a corner for the deepest bass"

Corner placement is classic speaker advice: room boundaries reinforce low frequencies, so a sub in a corner sounds bigger. In a detached house with a slab floor, that can be a reasonable way to get more bass from a small subwoofer.

In a Tribeca loft, more bass is usually the problem rather than the goal. Wide-plank floors on timber joists transmit low frequencies to the apartment or business below, and a sub in a corner, on bare boards, drives exactly the frequencies that travel. The neighbor hears the explosion before you do, and corner boom in a hard room also muddies dialogue.

We place subs away from corners and away from the middle of a joist span, on a dense pad or rug, and turn their level down to suit the building. Many bars also have a night mode that compresses peaks for late viewing. In a building where your floor is someone's ceiling, controlled bass is the better-sounding result for everyone.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Tribeca Loft Walls

How our installers approach a Tribeca loft before the drill comes out — standing practice, not the story of one job

The first look is up, not at the wall. Ceiling height, beams, sprinkler pipes and any mezzanine tell us where people will actually watch from and how the bar's sound will travel. Then down: bare planks, rugs, and the question of who lives or works underneath. Only then does the technician walk to the TV wall.

On brick, the light from the windows is used to read the face. Spalled bricks, old patches, fresh repointing and earlier fixings show up in raking light, and the bracket's holes are marked on whole bricks inside its footprint. On a partition, the top of the wall gets a firm push before the scanner comes out; a partition that moves changes the plan more than any stud reading.

Before anything is fixed, the remote is tried from the farthest seat and the network from the TV wall. In a long loft, the most common complaint a week later is not the mount; it is a remote that will not reach, or a bar that drops off Wi-Fi because the router is behind a hundred feet of brick and air. Those get settled while the technician is still there.

FAQ

Tribeca Soundbar Installation FAQ

What does a soundbar on an exposed-brick loft wall come to in Tribeca, with a painted channel and parking?
+

Mounting the bar is $129, brick adds $65 and a paintable cord channel is $59, so $253 before parking. The flat $150 Manhattan parking line makes it $403. If you need our Sanus bracket rather than the one in the box, add $84.99 for $487.99, and if the TV sits high on the wall so the bar goes above about eight feet, the high-mount line adds $75.

Our open loft has a TV at each end — will one remote end up controlling both soundbars?
+

It can happen, because two bars of the same brand often answer the same infrared codes, and a remote aimed down a long open floor can reach both. We pair each bar to its own TV over HDMI-CEC so each TV remote drives only its own bar, name them separately in the app, and where the maker allows it, switch the far bar's infrared response off. Then we test both remotes from every seat.

Will the remotes still reach the soundbar from a sofa far across the loft?
+

Usually, but we check rather than assume. Infrared remotes have a limited range and need a clear line to the bar's sensor, which a coffee table, a column or the TV's own stand can block. If reach is a problem, HDMI-CEC lets the TV remote run the bar's volume, the bar's app works from a phone over Wi-Fi, and a programmed universal remote at $99 is another option.

Can a TV and soundbar be mounted on a sliding barn door or a movable wall panel?
+

We advise against it and generally will not. A sliding door or pivoting panel moves, its track carries the load in ways it was not designed for, and every cable to a moving surface is a cable that eventually gets pinched or pulled. Put the TV on a fixed wall, a full-motion arm or a floor-standing mount near the panel instead, and let the door stay a door.

Can the cord cover on our brick wall be painted to blend in?
+

Yes, the channel we fit is paintable, but brick is hard to imitate with a flat strip. It usually looks best run straight along a mortar line, tight into a corner or down the edge of a column, and painted a single colour picked from the mortar or the wall's paint. Painting is not part of our price, so ask your painter, or use a sample pot yourself once it is up.

We rearranged the loft furniture — can you come back to re-aim and re-tune the soundbar?
+

Yes. Moving the sofa or the seating zone changes how the bar should be set: room calibration, dialogue level, sub placement and the remote's line of sight. If nothing is re-mounted, it is a setup-only visit, billed at $265 for the first hour in Manhattan and then in 30-minute blocks, plus the $150 parking line. If the bar has to move on the wall, the mounting lines apply instead.

Our art-style TV hangs flush on the wall like a painting — what are the low-profile soundbar options?
+

A slim bar on a tight wall bracket just below the frame, with its cable taken straight down in a painted channel or, on a hollow partition, inside the wall. Some owners prefer the bar on a low console so the wall shows only the picture. Either way we centre the bar on the frame, keep it clear of the TV's sensor, and connect it to the TV's eARC port, which on some art-style TVs is on a separate connection box.

Can a soundbar be fixed to the railing of our loft's mezzanine so people up there can hear?
+

No. A mezzanine railing is a safety barrier, and hanging electronics and cables on it is not something we do. If the mezzanine is a den with its own screen, a compact bar on a console or a small wall bracket up there is the answer. If people upstairs watch the main TV below, raise the bar's dialogue level a little and let the open volume carry the sound; it usually does.
