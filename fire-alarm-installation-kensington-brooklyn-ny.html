The Neighborhood

Why Kensington Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

For much of the twentieth century Kensington was barely treated as its own neighborhood; many residents simply called it western Flatbush. Its buildings tell a clearer story. Development began after Ocean Parkway was completed in the 1880s, and real estate syndicates carved large tracts west of Coney Island Avenue into lots. The side streets filled with brick row houses, detached houses and the stacked duplex-porch two-families that Forgotten NY calls almost the signature style of the neighborhood. Upper Ocean Parkway then traded many of its suburban villas for six-story elevator apartment houses between the late 1920s and 1941, and between 1960 and 1965 another 26 apartment buildings went up along the parkway as a whole, with rental and co-op buildings of up to 20 stories in Kensington. Church, McDonald, Coney Island and Ditmas Avenues carry stores with apartments above. Very little of that stock was built with a modern fire alarm in mind, and much of it has been rewired, subdivided or converted at least once since.

That history matters because Kensington's recent fires have rarely stayed where they started. The June 2026 fire at 188 Ocean Parkway moved into 184. The March 2024 fire at 413 East 3rd Street spread to the building beside it, which partly lost its roof. On East Ninth Street, two multiple-alarm fires hit attached homes on the same block within a month, and a separate fire there brought vacate orders for 739 and 741. In attached and adjoining buildings, a fire's first minutes may unfold in rear rooms, roof spaces or a neighbor's house that no resident can see. Detection cannot stop a fire next door, but planned with the whole building in mind it gives every household inside the earliest warning the building is able to give.

The city's records show how ordinary detector problems are. LucidRents lists 349 Ocean Parkway, a four-story, 46-unit building from 1924, with 62 HPD violations over seven years, four of them smoke or carbon monoxide detector items. That is one building, not a neighborhood figure, and we cite it only to show that detector violations sit in the same files as doors and locks. Community Board 14's 2024 enforcement overview explains that an HPD complaint inspection checks ten items in the complaining tenant's apartment, the smoke detector among them, and that a detector problem is written as a Class B violation. Kensington straddles Community Boards 12 and 14, so complaints and building records come through both.

Six conditions in Kensington that a fire alarm plan has to answer

  • Joined walls and joined roofs. Row houses on the side streets and walk-ups on the avenues share party walls, and many share a continuous roofline. The 2024 East 3rd Street fire began at the rear of a first floor and reached the building next door, with officials saying high winds made it worse.
  • Two generations of apartment houses on Ocean Parkway. Six-story elevator buildings from the late 1920s to 1941, then early-1960s buildings of up to 20 stories, many converted to co-ops. The tallest can reach the height at which the code asks for more than bells and horns.
  • Century-old walk-ups. The September 2026 fire on Avenue C hit a building about a hundred years old, with heavy smoke and flames coming from a third-floor window. Older walk-ups often have alarms inside apartments and little or nothing in the halls.
  • Stores under homes. A four-alarm fire on Coney Island Avenue was first reported on the first floor of a three-story building with a store below and apartments on the two floors above. Church Avenue's groceries, delis and restaurants occupy the same kind of building.
  • A small landmark district and a landmark boulevard. The Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces Historic District holds 32 residential buildings on two short cul-de-sacs off East 21st Street, built between 1916 and 1920, and Ocean Parkway south of Church Avenue is a scenic landmark. Interior detection generally needs no Landmarks review; anything planned for a facade in the district is checked first.
  • Batteries in every room. Firehouse.com reported in 2026 that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent that year. In homes full of phones, scooters, power tools and e-bikes, charging spots go on every survey sheet.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Kensington's Architecture

Seven kinds of building account for nearly every fire alarm call we take from Kensington. They differ in how they are framed, what they are joined to, who controls the detection and how many households a single fire can reach, and those differences settle the plan long before a model number comes into it.

The pre-war elevator house

Six stories on upper Ocean Parkway, built between the late 1920s and 1941, many now co-ops with a cellar boiler room, a lobby and long double-loaded halls.

The 1960s parkway building

Taller postwar rental and co-op buildings, some up to 20 stories in Kensington, with an original building system that may be sixty years old.

The four-story walk-up

Brick buildings from the 1910s to the 1930s on the avenues and cross streets, with a single stair, a roof hatch and a cellar.

The attached brick house and duplex-porch two-family

Row houses on the lanes and numbered streets, and two-families with stacked front porches, joined by party walls.

The detached Victorian and Foursquare

Free-standing frame houses on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, with open stairs and attics.

The store-and-apartment building

Two or three floors of homes over a grocery, deli, restaurant or boutique on Church, McDonald or Coney Island Avenue.

The terrace house

Slee & Bryson's 1916 to 1920 houses on Albemarle and Kenmore Terraces, with rear service alleys and a small apartment building at one end.

The pre-war elevator house: one cellar, six floors and dozens of households

The six-story buildings that replaced Ocean Parkway's villas were built with masonry walls, concrete or tile-arch floors, a passenger elevator and a boiler in the cellar. Many were converted to co-ops later, which means the people who now decide about the fire alarm are a volunteer board and a managing agent rather than a single owner. What we usually find is a layered system: an older panel in the cellar or lobby, hall detection or pull stations added in one era, single-station smoke and CO alarms inside apartments installed by shareholders in another, and paperwork scattered between treasurers. Before we propose anything, we trace what is really there, panel to riser to device, and compare it with whatever FDNY letters or drawings the board can find. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules draws the line that matters most to a board's budget: swapping detectors, pull stations or panels for identical parts with no design change is treated as a repair, while moving or replacing the control panel calls for new plans filed with the FDNY.

The 1960s parkway building: height, risers and the voice question

The postwar buildings are taller and more uniform, with long corridors, compactor or refuse rooms on each floor in many cases, and electrical and alarm risers stacked in closets. Height changes the code conversation. SPARC's summary of the city's bulletin for fire alarm work in existing buildings notes that one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. Some of Kensington's tallest parkway buildings may approach or pass that line, and the midrise six-story buildings are nowhere near it. Which side a building falls on matters when its old bell-and-strobe system is due for replacement, because a replacement is a filed change, and the registered design professional will have to decide what the current code asks of that building. We measure, photograph the risers and hand the designer accurate as-found conditions so the decision is made from facts.

The four-story walk-up: one stair, one roof hatch and a cellar full of tenants' things

The walk-ups built in the 1910s through the early 1930s are where a single fire touches the most families with the fewest exits. There is usually one interior stair, sometimes a fire escape at the front or rear, a roof hatch or bulkhead at the top, and a cellar with the boiler, meters and storage. The Avenue C fire in September 2026 was in a building of about that age, with smoke and flames coming from a third-floor window. In buildings like these, we look first at the stair, because it is the shared escape route and the place smoke goes, then at the cellar, because a fire there can reach the stair before anyone upstairs knows, and then at each apartment's alarms. Whether hall detection can be added as an extension of what exists, or needs a designed and filed system, depends on what the building already has and how it is classified, and we tell the owner which path applies before pricing.

The attached brick house and the duplex-porch two-family: a fire at the back, sleepers at the front

Kensington's row houses and attached two-families tend to put kitchens, rear extensions and back porches at one end and bedrooms at the other, often a floor or two apart. The East 3rd Street fire in 2024 started at the rear of the first floor; the East Ninth Street fires reached second and third floors of attached homes. For houses like these, linking is the single most valuable upgrade: an alarm in the rear kitchen or the cellar that sounds every alarm in the house, so a fire behind closed doors is heard where people sleep. In a two-family, the question is also who hears whom. We plan each apartment's alarms so the owner downstairs and the tenant upstairs are both warned of a fire in the other's home and in the shared stair, and we write down who looks after which devices, because in a rented house that division is where alarms go missing. Where solid brick walls make new cable impractical, wireless linked alarms do the same job without opening plaster.

The detached Victorian and Foursquare: open stairs, tall ceilings and attics

The free-standing frame houses on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue are the opposite of the walk-up: lots of space, few households, and a wooden structure with an open main staircase that carries smoke from the parlor floor straight to the bedrooms. Untapped Cities places the neighborhood's American Foursquares in that part of Kensington, built in the style that ran from the 1890s into the 1930s. We place alarms at the top of each flight and outside every sleeping area, link them throughout, and pay particular attention to rooms that were added or finished later, such as attic bedrooms, enclosed porches and basement playrooms, because those are the rooms most often left out of an older set of alarms. Ceilings in these houses are usually plaster, so we fish cable through existing paths where we can and use wireless linking where we cannot, and we patch what we touch.

The store-and-apartment building: a locked store under sleeping families

On Church, McDonald and Coney Island Avenues, the typical commercial building is two or three floors of apartments over a ground-floor business with its own lease, its own keys and its own hours. A fire in a closed grocery or restaurant at night threatens the families upstairs; a fire in an apartment during business hours threatens customers below. The CBS report on the four-alarm Coney Island Avenue fire describes exactly that arrangement, with the fire first reported on the first floor. The plan has to make each part of the building warn the other, and has to be written down: who owns each device, who maintains it, who receives a signal when the store is empty, and who can open the gate for a technician or a firefighter. In some buildings that means a building panel tying store and stair together; in smaller ones it means linked alarms and monitoring. The building's occupancy and size decide which.

The terrace house: two quiet courts with landmark facades

The Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces Historic District is two short cul-de-sacs off East 21st Street, on the border the research we read places between Kensington and Flatbush: 32 residential buildings designed by Slee & Bryson and built between 1916 and 1920, with a four-family apartment building at the end of Albemarle Terrace, ground-floor garages in the Kenmore Terrace houses, and narrow rear service alleys that the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report notes behind the houses. For fire alarm purposes, these are houses and one small apartment building, planned like any other. The difference is the outside. The LPC's general guidance is that interior work in a historic district normally needs no Landmarks approval, while exterior work does, so every cable stays inside the walls and nothing is fastened to a facade without the right permit. We found no LPC guidance specific to fire alarm devices in this district, and we do not pretend otherwise.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading the Neighbors Before Reading the Panel

These notes describe our technicians' routine in buildings like the ones on this page. They are practice, not a story about any particular address.

Both neighbors first. Before ringing the bell, the technician stands across the street and looks left and right. Is the building attached on one side or two? Does the roofline run unbroken into the next building? Is there a store at street level next door, a vacant lot, a bakery or a garage? None of that is ours to change, but it tells us which walls and rooms face a neighbor's risk, and it goes on the survey sheet.

Then the back. Where we can reach it, we look at the rear yard, the alley or the back of the lot: rear extensions, back porches, stored furniture, bins, a shed. Several Kensington fires started or travelled at the rear of a building, where no one on the street could see them.

The top floor under the roof. Inside, we start high. Top-floor ceilings, the roof hatch or bulkhead and any attic space show how fire near the roof could spread or hide. The Ocean Parkway fire in June 2026 was in the upper part of the building near the roof when firefighters arrived.

Every bedroom with the door closed. On the way down we note ceiling heights, bunk beds, rooms added later and rooms used by tenants, and we sound an alarm while someone listens from the farthest bedroom with its door shut. If they cannot hear it clearly, the plan changes.

The routines of the house. We ask where phones, scooters and tools charge overnight, where space heaters run, and, respectfully, whether candles burn through the night or food stays warm on a hot plate over a Sabbath or holiday. Those answers place detectors better than any rule of thumb.

The cellar last. Boiler, meters, storage, any store stock or freezers, and anything that suggests someone sleeps down there. Only after the walk do we compare what we saw with the paperwork.

Compliance notes for Kensington buildings and businesses

  • Two agencies, two tracks. NYC TPP Inspections and KomplyOS both describe fire alarm plans going to the FDNY for review separately from DOB NOW, with the Buildings Department handling construction permits and the FDNY handling fire alarm review, testing and acceptance.
  • Where a system is required. Section 907 of the NYC Building Code sets out which occupancies need a fire alarm system. Built Engineers' summary notes that sprinklers alone do not trigger a full building fire alarm, and that a required system must be monitored by an FDNY-approved central station.
  • Small buildings. The 2022 Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by 1-hour fire partitions and has direct exit to a public way, exit court or yard, which is relevant to many of Kensington's two-families.
  • What a filing needs. The NYC Business portal lists a Certificate of Occupancy or approved Schedule A, floor plans, a riser diagram, an operating sequence matrix, approval by a New York State registered engineer or architect, and a fire alarm plan involving a licensed Master Electrician and an FDNY S97 certificate holder.
  • Repair or redesign. Like-for-like replacement of detectors, pull stations or panels is a repair; relocating or replacing a control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Existing installations. The FDNY's guide to the Fire Code, which took effect March 30, 2014, says most existing businesses do not have to meet the 2014 design and installation requirements for their existing installations.
  • Not Local Law 26. County Fire's guide notes that Local Law 26 of 2004 aimed its sprinkler mandate at office buildings 100 feet or taller, not at residential walk-ups or co-ops.
  • Kitchens. The 2022 Fire Code requires sprinklers within 30 feet of commercial cooking equipment in many occupancies, which affects restaurants and bakeries on every Kensington corridor.
  • Outage limits and readiness. FC 901.7.5.2 sets thresholds for systems out of service 8 or more hours in 24, or 30 or more hours in a month, and a new system must be complete and pre-tested before the FDNY inspection is held.
The Fire Record

What Kensington's Fires From 2024 to 2026 Show About Where Detection Has to Reach

This section reads the public reporting on recent Kensington fires for one purpose: to see which parts of a building were involved. We do not know, and the reports do not say, whether any alarm sounded, how any fire was discovered, or what caused most of them; several causes were listed as undetermined or under investigation. We draw no conclusion about any owner or tenant. What the reports do show, again and again, is fire in places a resident would not be standing.

Near the roof: 188 Ocean Parkway, June 17, 2026

The fire started just after 6 p.m., and when firefighters arrived the flames were in the upper part of the building near the roof, according to News 12 and the other reports we read. It spread to 184 Ocean Parkway, the Buildings Department issued full vacate orders for both, and fifteen people, twelve adults and three children, were displaced, with no injuries reported. The Citizen incident log places the building between Church Avenue and Albemarle Road and describes it as three stories. For detection, the lesson is about the top of a building: top-floor ceilings, the space under the roof, and whatever connects one building's roof to the next. Top-floor halls and rooms are where we want early warning, and in a building with a continuous roofline, the people on the top floor are the first to need it.

At the back of the first floor: 413 East 3rd Street, March 22, 2024

Gothamist reported that this four-alarm fire broke out in a three-story building around 5:45 p.m., starting at the rear of the first floor, with officials saying high winds worsened it. It spread to a neighboring building, which had a partial roof collapse, and Fox 5 placed it on East 3rd Street between Beverley Road and Avenue C. The rear of a ground floor is often a kitchen, a back room or an extension, and the front upstairs is where people sleep. That is the classic case for linked alarms: a device at the back that sounds every device in the building.

Row after row: East Ninth Street

Brooklyn Paper reported heavy fire on the second and third floors of two attached private homes at 382 East Ninth Street, between Avenue C and Cortelyou Road, the second multiple-alarm fire on that block in under a month, after one that partly collapsed a century-old two-story brick home. News 12 reported a separate fire in which a 67-year-old resident was found trapped and a home collapsed from the second floor to the first, with full vacate orders for 739 and 741 East Ninth Street. The excerpts we read do not show the years. Attached homes share walls and often roofs, and the upper floors of the house next door are only a party wall away from your bedrooms.

Over the apartments' heads and under their feet: Avenue C, Coney Island Avenue and East Eighth Street

The September 7, 2026 fire at 717 Avenue C, in a building about a century old, sent heavy smoke and flames from a third-floor window, injured two people and forced two families out under a vacate order; its cause was undetermined, and Hoodline described it as part of a pattern that included the June fire. CBS New York reported a four-alarm fire in a three-story Coney Island Avenue building with a store on the first floor and apartments on the second and third, first reported on the first floor, and another fire on East Eighth Street in which five people were injured and flames came from fourth-floor windows. Patch reported an older fire at 622 Ocean Parkway that destroyed one apartment and badly damaged others. Between them, these cover the shared building: one apartment's fire becomes every apartment's smoke, and a store's fire becomes the tenants' emergency.

In the bakery at 2 a.m.: 1285 36th Street, December 17, 2025

ABC7 and CBS New York reported that the five-alarm fire at the Boro Park Seder Matzah Bakery started near the ovens around 2 a.m. and spread quickly to stacked matzah boxes. A child and two adults in a nearby building were hurt, a school with hundreds of children sits next door, firefighters fought the fire for nearly nine hours, and the Buildings Department issued a full vacate order for extensive damage including a partially collapsed roof, noting damage at 1287 and 1301 36th Street as well. We make no claim about that building's systems. The general lesson for any food business is the combination of heat sources running through the night, a small crew, and fuel stacked high: detection near ovens that tolerates heat, smoke detection over storage, and a signal that reaches someone outside the building.

What we do differently in Kensington because of this

  • We survey the rear of every building and the top floor under the roof, not just the rooms people use most.
  • We treat linking as the default in attached houses and two-families, so a rear or cellar alarm sounds at the front bedrooms.
  • We ask what the building is joined to and note shared roofs and party walls on the plan.
  • In store buildings, we write down who is told about a fire in the store when it is closed.
  • In bakeries and kitchens, we plan for the hours when the fewest people are awake.
Ocean Parkway Apartment Houses

Upgrading the Fire Alarm in an Occupied Ocean Parkway Co-op Without Leaving Anyone Unprotected

Ocean Parkway is 210 feet wide, with a main roadway, two landscaped malls, two service roads and two sidewalks, laid out by Olmsted and Vaux and built between 1874 and 1876. Its apartment houses face the service roads, and many of them have been co-ops for decades. When a board decides the fire alarm needs work, nobody moves out, nobody stops cooking, and the elevator keeps running. The job has to be planned around that, and it starts with paperwork rather than ladders.

Find out what the system is before deciding what it should become. We ask the board and managing agent for any FDNY letters, inspection reports, drawings, the panel's model and the central station account details, and the Certificate of Occupancy or Schedule A. Then we document the system as found: every device on every floor, the panel's condition and the meaning of its common displays, the risers, the batteries and their dates, and which apartments have their own alarms. That survey is often the first complete record the building has had, and it decides whether the work can stay a like-for-like repair or needs a registered design professional and an FDNY filing.

Keep the building protected every night. The Fire Code limits how long a system can be out of service, with thresholds at 8 hours in a 24-hour period or 30 hours in a month. So we work floor by floor or zone by zone, restore each section before the crew leaves, and arrange fire watch or temporary measures with the board and the design professional when a cutover cannot avoid an outage. The central station is notified before any testing so a test does not become a dispatch, and told again when testing ends.

Tell shareholders what to expect. A one-page notice in the lobby, and in whatever languages the board uses for other notices, saying which floors we will be on, when alarms will sound for testing, and how to tell a test from a real alarm, prevents both panic and complacency. Apartments that need entry get appointments, not knocks.

Budget in phases if needed. A board can survey and repair now, plan a filed replacement later, and keep the old system maintained in between, as long as the old system is kept working. We price each phase separately at our Brooklyn rate so the board can vote on what it can fund.

Leave the building able to run it. The super and the board get a device list that matches the labels, a sheet explaining each panel signal and who to call, and dated test results for the files. In a building where volunteer officers change every few years, the binder belongs to the building, not to whoever happened to sign the contract.

Who Signs Off on a Kensington Fire Alarm: FDNY, DOB and the Paper Trail Behind the Panel

Many Kensington owners first learn about fire alarm approvals when a project is already underway. A co-op board on Ocean Parkway approves a panel replacement, or a landlord on Church Avenue fits out a new ground-floor tenant, and it becomes clear that the city does not treat a fire alarm as one permit from one office. Two agencies are involved, each with its own role. The Department of Buildings handles permits and installation approval. The Fire Department handles testing and approval of the system itself. Fire alarm plans go to FDNY for review separately from the DOB NOW filing that owners and architects use for most other building work. A project can be correct in one system and still waiting in the other.

This split explains why fire alarm schedules in this neighborhood often run longer than owners expect. The wiring and devices may take days to install in a three-story attached house on East Ninth Street or a four-story walk-up near Coney Island Avenue. The approvals follow their own pace. A sound plan builds both tracks in from the start, so neither one becomes a surprise after the ceilings are closed.

For new construction and substantial alterations, the design standard is NFPA 72 as amended by New York City. The local amendments matter. A system designed to the national edition alone is not automatically acceptable here, and a designer working mostly outside the five boroughs may not plan for them. Kensington has relatively little new construction, but some exists. Of the 133 buildings in the neighborhood with twenty or more homes, nine went up between 1989 and 2023, and eight of those nine stand on Ocean Parkway. Those newer buildings were built within the modern code framework. The other 124 large buildings were built before 1974, so most fire alarm work on the neighborhood's big buildings is work on older structures. That raises the question of how far an existing installation has to be brought up to current standards.

The current Fire Code took effect on March 30, 2014. FDNY's own guidance says that, for permits on existing installations, most existing businesses do not have to meet the 2014 design and installation requirements. That clause carries a lot of weight in a neighborhood whose commercial corridors are Coney Island Avenue, Church Avenue, Ditmas Avenue and McDonald Avenue, and whose storefronts often sit under apartments in buildings from the 1920s and 1930s. The practical question to settle before any quote is final is what the work actually is. If it keeps and services an existing installation, the 2014 design rules may not reach it. If the scope grows into an alteration, the question changes. We would rather sort that out with the owner at the start than discover it during an inspection.

Some requirements circulate mostly through installers' own marketing material. We repeat them here with that caveat, and owners should confirm them directly with FDNY before relying on them. The claims are that only FDNY-approved companies can legally install fire alarms in New York City, that fire alarm plans must be sealed by a licensed Professional Engineer or Registered Architect, and that the FDNY base fee for a new application is $420. We treat the sealed-drawing requirement as part of any filed project, and we tell owners up front that the drawing work and the agency fee are separate from installation labor. That way the budget a co-op board approves matches what the project will actually cost.

One rule often gets mentioned wrongly in Kensington: Local Law 26 of 2004. Owners sometimes assume it obliges them to add sprinklers or to tie a new alarm into a sprinkler upgrade. It does not apply to the neighborhood's walk-ups and co-ops. Its sprinkler mandate covers office buildings 100 feet or taller, not residential occupancies. That holds even for Kensington's tallest residential buildings. 135 Ocean Parkway, a sixteen-floor co-op with 286 homes completed in 1965, and 455 Ocean Parkway, a fifteen-floor building with 147 homes completed in 1964, are residential, and Local Law 26 does not govern them. Fire alarm work in those towers stands on its own. An owner should not let a misreading of that law inflate the scope or delay a needed detection upgrade.

The Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces Historic District needs its own note. It sits on the Kensington and Flatbush border as two short cul-de-sacs off East 21st Street. Slee & Bryson designed them, and they were built between 1916 and 1920. The district holds 32 residential buildings, including a four-family apartment building at the end of Albemarle Terrace. The houses on Kenmore Terrace have garages on the ground floor, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report notes narrow rear service alleys behind the houses. The district was designated in 1978 and listed on the National Register on June 30, 1983. We looked for LPC rules specific to fire alarm devices in this district and found none. We will not invent guidance that does not exist. For any work that would touch an exterior, such as a device, a conduit run or a penetration visible from the terraces, ask the Commission before installing rather than after. Interior detection inside these houses is a separate matter from their facades, but the garages on Kenmore Terrace and the rear alleys are where an owner should slow down and check.

Taken together, a filed Kensington fire alarm project usually runs in this order:

  • Define the scope and decide whether it is work on an existing installation or a substantial alteration that brings in NFPA 72 as amended by the city.
  • Have drawings prepared and sealed by a licensed engineer or architect.
  • Submit the plans to FDNY for review, on a track separate from the DOB NOW filing.
  • Obtain the DOB permit and installation approval.
  • Install the system.
  • Have FDNY test and approve it.

Not every job in a one-family Victorian off Beverley Road needs all of these steps. Any project in a multiple dwelling, a co-op or a mixed-use building on a commercial corridor should be checked against this sequence before work begins.

We also want to be clear about what we do not know. We looked for neighborhood-level counts of DOB fire alarm permits in Kensington and could not find them. We cannot tell an owner how many fire alarm filings the neighborhood sees in a year, or whether a given block is busier than another. When an owner asks how long approval will take, the honest answer depends on the specific filing, not on a neighborhood average we lack.

Kensington's Buildings by the Numbers and What the City's Violation Files Say About Detectors and Doors

Fire alarm work in Kensington ranges widely, from a single smoke detector in a two-family brick house to a building-wide system in a sixteen-floor tower. To size that range, we used the city's 2020 Neighborhood Tabulation Area for Kensington as it appears in HPD's violation file. That area covers census tracts 484, 486, 488, 490, 492, 494, 496, 498, 500.02 and 504.02, roughly from Caton Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway south to about Avenue F and 18th Avenue, and from McDonald Avenue and Dahill Road east to Coney Island Avenue. Tract 484 straddles two community boards. Parkville, between 18th Avenue and Foster Avenue, is grouped with Ditmas Park in that scheme and is not included in these counts. Other sources draw Kensington's boundaries differently, so these numbers describe this specific area rather than every definition of the neighborhood.

Within that area, the city's land-use file shows 2,827 residential lots holding 14,410 homes. Those homes are spread across very different building types:

  • Houses and small buildings. Houses and buildings of one to four families hold 4,857 homes. They are the brick rowhouses, detached Victorians and American Foursquare houses that still line the side streets.
  • Four-story walk-ups. There are 53 lots of four-story walk-up apartment buildings holding 1,294 homes. Their average construction year is 1924.
  • Buildings with twenty or more homes. There are 133 such buildings holding 8,132 homes. Of these, 68 stand on Ocean Parkway and hold 4,270 homes.
  • Elevator buildings. There are 89 elevator buildings with 6,714 homes. They include 39 elevator co-ops with 3,229 homes (average construction year 1948, average height six and a half floors) and 46 elevator rental buildings built before 1974 with 3,335 homes.

When the elevator buildings went up mirrors the neighborhood's history. Kensington was first developed in 1885, after Ocean Parkway was completed, as real estate syndicates subdivided tracts west of Coney Island Avenue into lots. Between the late 1920s and 1941, six-story elevator apartment houses became more common on upper Ocean Parkway and replaced many of the suburban villas that had stood there. The land-use file shows ten of today's elevator buildings from the 1920s, 23 from the 1930s, 17 from the 1940s and 8 from the 1950s. Then came a postwar surge: 25 from the 1960s, holding 2,675 homes in buildings completed between 1960 and 1965, sixteen of which now operate as co-ops. Since then the pace has been slow, with three elevator buildings in the 2010s and one in the 2020s.

These figures change how a fire alarm job is planned. A 1930s six-story building on Ocean Parkway and a 1960s tower like 135 Ocean Parkway are both elevator buildings, but they were built three decades apart with different construction methods, ceiling heights and ways of running wiring through the structure. A 1924 walk-up has no elevator shaft to follow and often no hung ceiling to hide cable. The 4,857 homes in small houses raise a different issue. In attached rows, a fire in one house can threaten the next, as East Ninth Street and East Third Street have shown in recent years. There, detection is the household's own early warning, not part of a building-wide system. A contractor who quotes all of these the same way has not looked closely at the building.

Other datasets count the neighborhood differently, and the gap is useful to understand. OpenIgloo lists 3,047 apartment buildings in Kensington with fewer than fifty units. That is a different method and a different boundary from the land-use counts above, and we do not try to reconcile them. The takeaway is the same either way: most of Kensington's apartment buildings are small, and the large Ocean Parkway buildings are a minority by count even though they hold a large share of the homes. One small-building example on that list, 866 Coney Island Avenue, shows 84 open violations. That figure describes one building, not the neighborhood, but it shows how much can build up on a single record.

This is where the city's enforcement process matters for fire safety. When a tenant files a complaint through 311 and HPD sends an inspector, the complaint inspection checks ten items in the complainant's apartment. One of them is the smoke detector. A smoke detector violation is Class B, which HPD classifies as hazardous with a thirty-day correction period. In other words, a tenant complaint about anything in the apartment can lead to an inspector checking the detector. Owners of Kensington's walk-ups and smaller rentals should treat detector condition as something that can be cited at any visit, not only when a tenant specifically reports a problem with it.

Kensington's position on the map makes the public record harder to follow. The neighborhood falls under two community boards. Community Board 12 covers Borough Park, Kensington, Ocean Parkway and Midwood, and is served by the 66th Precinct. Community Board 14 covers Flatbush, Midwood, Kensington and Ocean Parkway. An owner or tenant pulling 311 and HPD records for a Kensington building may need to check under either board, and a search limited to one board can miss part of the picture.

A single building shows what these records look like up close. 349 Ocean Parkway, built in 1924, has four floors and 46 units. Over seven years it has accumulated 62 HPD violations and 45 complaints to 311. Among the violations are four smoke or carbon monoxide detector items and four door or lock items. This is one building, and we do not present it as typical of the neighborhood. We include it because it matches the kind of building that lines the parkway and Coney Island Avenue: prewar, brick and walk-up height. Its record shows detector items and door items appearing together, which is how we tend to think about fire protection in buildings like it.

The connection between doors and alarms is clearer in the neighborhood-wide figures. In HPD's violation file for the Kensington area, self-closing door violations total 2,817. Of these, 2,256 are Class C across 236 lots, 524 are Class B across 170 lots, and 37 are Class A across 21 lots. HPD gives owners fourteen days to correct a Class C self-closing door violation, a shorter window than the thirty days for a hazardous Class B item. For comparison, the same file holds 168 intercom violations in the area. Self-closing door violations outnumber them by more than sixteen to one, which signals how often the city finds fire-separation doors in Kensington buildings that do not close on their own.

This matters for anyone installing a fire alarm because detection and containment work together. An alarm tells people there is a fire. A door that closes on its own helps keep smoke out of the stairway they will use to leave. In a building with a new panel, new detectors and new horn and strobe devices but apartment doors propped open or failing to latch, the system can alert people while the escape route fills with smoke. When we walk a Kensington building before quoting, we note the condition of the doors along with the ceilings and the old wiring. If the building's HPD record carries open self-closing door items, we tell the owner, even though the doors are not part of our scope.

We also want to be clear about the limits of this record. We could not find counts of 311 fire alarm complaints for Kensington specifically, and we could not find neighborhood-level DOB fire alarm permit counts. We found no neighborhood discussion threads about fire alarms in Kensington buildings. We cannot say whether fire alarm complaints are rising here, or which blocks generate the most. What we can say comes from the sources above: the size and age of the housing stock, the way HPD inspections reach the smoke detector, the record of one representative building, and the large number of self-closing door violations across the area.

Our practical advice to owners follows from that. Before asking for a fire alarm quote, look up your own building's HPD record under the correct community board, and note any open smoke or carbon monoxide detector items and any open self-closing door items. Know your building type: a small house, a 1920s walk-up, a 1930s or 1940s elevator building, or a 1960s tower. Know whether you own it as a landlord or a co-op. Bring that information to the first conversation. A quote built on the building's actual record and construction is more accurate, and it is more likely to clear both FDNY and HPD the first time.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Kensington: Questions Answered

These are the situations Kensington owners, boards, tenants and business owners bring to us: blocks where fire has already struck, co-ops with households that keep Shabbat, houses full of children, and food and spa businesses on the avenues. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

After the fires on nearby blocks

Two attached houses on our stretch of East Ninth Street burned within a month of each other. We own one in the same row. What should we check ourselves this week, before anyone is booked?

Start with what is already on your ceilings. Press the test button on each smoke and carbon monoxide alarm, one at a time, while someone stands in the farthest bedroom with the door closed and listens. Read the manufacture or replace-by date printed on the back of each unit, and note any that are past the maker's date or have no date at all. When you test one, notice whether the others sound too; if they do not, your alarms are not linked, and a fire in the rear kitchen or the cellar may sound only in that room. Walk every level, including the cellar and any finished attic, and check that there is an alarm in each bedroom and outside each sleeping area. Look at where phones, scooters and tools charge overnight, and move any charging away from the only exit. Then send us photos of the alarms and their labels. From those we can tell you which units to replace, whether linking is practical in your walls or should be wireless, and which rooms are missing coverage.

Officials said high winds made the 2024 fire on East 3rd Street worse. Does wind change how fast our alarms need to warn us, or which kind we choose?

Wind does not change the type of alarm, but it can shorten the time between a fire starting and a fire spreading, which makes every other choice matter more. Air feeds fire, and a windy day through an open rear window or door can push a small fire along faster than anyone expects. What you can control is how close detection sits to the places a fire is likely to start, such as the rear kitchen, a back room or the cellar, and whether an alarm there wakes the people asleep at the front of the house. That is why linking is our first recommendation in attached houses. The second is practice. A household that has walked its way out once, knows to close doors behind them on the way, and has agreed where to meet outside will use those minutes well. We cannot say how the East 3rd Street fire was discovered; we can make sure your house is set up so a fire at the back is heard at the front the moment it is sensed.

Smoke from a fire down the block came in through our windows and set off the alarm in our hall, though nothing in our home was burning. What should we do the next time that happens?

Treat the alarm as real until you have checked. Get everyone up, and look for smoke or heat inside your own home before deciding the source is outside. If a neighboring building is burning, call 911 yourself rather than assuming someone already has, and be ready to leave if smoke keeps entering or firefighters ask you to go. Close the windows on the side the smoke is coming from. If your alarm has a hush button, use it once you are sure your home is clear; do not pull the battery or take the alarm down, because you may need it again within minutes. After the event, test the alarm to confirm it still works. If an alarm near a window sounds every time outside smoke drifts in, from grills or street work as well as fires, tell us; moving it a few feet into the hall, still within the required distance of the bedrooms, can stop the nuisance without giving up protection.

Three buildings near ours have burned in three years. Can the city or our insurer now make us upgrade our fire alarm?

A fire on a neighboring property does not by itself change what the law requires of your building. Requirements follow your building's own occupancy, size and history under the Building Code and the Fire Code, and HPD enforces detector requirements inside apartments in multiple dwellings regardless of what happens next door. The FDNY's guide to the 2014 Fire Code explains that most existing businesses do not have to bring existing installations up to the newer design rules, but a change such as moving or replacing a control panel brings in new plans and the current code. An insurer is different: it sets its own conditions, and some ask owners to document detection after losses in the area. Ask your broker in writing what, if anything, they want, then send it to us. We can survey the building, document what is there, and give you a written list of what we found and what we would recommend, which is often all an insurer needs.

Ocean Parkway co-ops and households that keep Shabbat

A few shareholders in our Ocean Parkway co-op will not let anyone into their apartments. Can the building's upgrade go ahead without them?

Most of a building upgrade can, because halls, stairs, the lobby, the cellar and the panel are common areas the corporation controls. What stalls is anything that has to go inside an apartment, such as a device the design places inside a unit, or a test the board wants recorded for every home. We plan the common-area work first and schedule apartments by appointment, with a written notice that says what will be done, how long it takes and who will come. Most refusals turn out to be about timing, a fear of dust or damage, or a household's schedule around Shabbat and holidays, and offering a different day solves many of them. For the rest, ask the board's counsel what the proprietary lease says about access for repairs and safety work; we do not advise on that. What we will not do is mark an apartment as covered when we have not been inside it. The record shows it as not accessed, with the date, so the board knows exactly where it stands.

Our co-op board wants to know that every apartment has working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms. How can it collect that without inspecting every home itself?

The cleanest way is a single device day, or a few of them, when we visit each apartment by appointment, test every alarm, replace expired or missing ones where the shareholder agrees, and log the result room by room. Each unit gets a dated label, and the board receives one list showing every apartment as tested, corrected, declined or not accessed. That list is far more useful than a stack of self-signed forms, because it records the date on each alarm and whether it actually sounded. HPD's detector rules put duties on both building owners and the people who live in apartments, so a board that knows the state of every unit is in a much better position if a notice ever arrives. For shareholders who would rather handle it themselves, a short form asking for the alarm's location, its date and a photo of the label is a reasonable fallback. Repeating the device day every couple of years keeps the record current.

Many families in our building keep Shabbat and holidays and will not press buttons, answer phones or use apps from Friday evening to Saturday night. How should the fire alarm plan work for them?

A fire alarm should never depend on anyone pressing a button, and that is the right standard for every household anyway. Hardwired or sealed-battery alarms sound on their own and, when linked, sound together; a building system detects and notifies without any resident's action. Where Shabbat observance changes the plan is everything around the alarm. Do not rely on an app notification or a phone call to the apartment as the only way someone learns of a fire. Choose alarms with sealed long-life batteries, so a low-battery chirp is less likely to start on a Friday night. If the building is monitored, give the central station a call list that includes someone reachable on Shabbat. And we schedule tests, inspections and noisy work away from Friday evenings, Saturdays and holidays when the board asks. Questions about what an individual may or may not do during an emergency belong to each family and their rabbi, not to us; our job is to make sure the system needs nobody's hands.

We light candles every Friday night and memorial candles that burn for a full day, and food stays warm on a hot plate through Shabbat. How should the alarms and those flames be arranged?

A small, steady candle flame rarely sets off a smoke alarm, so the real issue is not nuisance alarms but the fact that a flame and a hot plate are running while the household sleeps or is out at synagogue. Keep the detection where it belongs, outside the bedrooms and on every level, rather than moving an alarm away from the dining room to avoid the candles. Stand candles on a metal or glass tray, away from curtains, tablecloth edges and anything children or pets can knock over, and put memorial candles somewhere they can burn without being moved. For a hot plate left on for many hours, a heat alarm in the kitchen adds protection without reacting to cooking smoke. A carbon monoxide alarm is unaffected by candles and should still be near the sleeping areas. On our survey we ask where these items stand, so the plan reflects how your home is actually used on a Friday night rather than on a weekday afternoon.

Houses with big families

Our three boys share one bedroom with bunk beds, and the top bunk is close to the ceiling. Does that change where the smoke alarm should go?

The room still needs its own smoke alarm, because it is a sleeping room, but its exact position should account for the bunk. Do not mount it directly over the top bunk, where a child can reach it, knock it with a pillow or be startled awake with an alarm inches from his face; place it on the ceiling away from the bed and away from corners and air vents, following the maker's spacing instructions. The child on the top bunk sleeps nearest the ceiling, which is where smoke collects first, so early detection in that room matters more, not less. Linking that alarm to the rest of the house means a fire downstairs sounds in the boys' room and a fire in their room sounds in yours. Then run a night-time drill: sound the alarm when the children are asleep and see whether they wake. Some children sleep through alarms, and if yours do, the plan needs to include an adult who goes to get them.

All the linked alarms in our house went off together at three in the morning and there was no smoke anywhere. How do we find out which one started it?

In a linked set, one alarm senses something and the others sound because it told them to, so the goal is to find the one that started. Many current models remember this and show it with a distinct light pattern on the initiating unit for a while after the alarm stops; the manual for your brand explains the pattern, and we label which unit is which. If your alarms are older and have no memory, note which one was flashing differently while it sounded, or which room you were near when it seemed loudest. The usual causes of a false alarm at night are dust or insects inside the sensing chamber, steam from a bathroom nearby, a draft carrying something in, or a unit reaching the end of its life. Check the dates: if one unit is far older than the rest, it is the first suspect. If the same alarm repeats after cleaning, replace it, and if the set is past its date, replace them together. We can trace a hardwired interconnect in one visit.

Food, spas and machinery on the avenues

Before Passover our bakery adds overnight shifts and fills the back of the shop with stacked boxes of finished product. How do we keep detection working when the room changes for a season?

Design the detection for the busiest layout, not the quiet one. A plan drawn for an empty back room can be undone in a week when boxes rise toward the ceiling, block smoke from reaching detectors, and crowd sprinkler heads. So we ask where stock goes at the peak and how high it is stacked, and place smoke detection over storage where the stacks will not shield it, with heat detection nearer the ovens, where normal baking would set off smoke detectors. Mark the floor and the wall with the stacking limit your sprinkler contractor and the Fire Code require below the sprinkler heads, and hold staff to it during the rush. Overnight shifts mean a small crew with loud equipment, so notification has to be heard and seen over the mixers and ovens. If the bakery is near homes or a school, think about who outside the building needs to know. The December 2025 bakery fire on 36th Street is a reminder that the rush season is not the time to discover the plan was drawn for an empty room.

We are planning a spa on Coney Island Avenue with dry saunas and a steam room. What detection can work beside that much heat and moisture?

Ordinary smoke detectors do not belong inside a sauna or steam room; the heat and moisture would set them off or ruin them. Inside hot rooms, a design may call for heat detection rated for the higher temperature, or may rely on detection in the spaces around them, and in a steam room the equipment room that houses the generator is often more important than the steam room itself. Heater and boiler rooms, laundry rooms full of towels and lint, and changing areas get detection chosen for each, and any fuel-burning equipment needs carbon monoxide protection nearby. A spa is also a place where people are undressed, wet and relaxed, so notification has to reach every room, including ones with doors closed. Depending on the occupancy and size, the system may need a registered design professional and an FDNY filing; that decision belongs to the designer, and we say at the first meeting whether we expect your spa to need a filed system, so the design fee and the FDNY review are in the budget from the start.
