The Neighborhood

Why Edenwald Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Edenwald sits at the top of the Bronx, against the Mount Vernon line, and most of what stands here was built as houses for families, not as offices or apartment towers. Rob Stephenson's neighborhood history describes it plainly: beyond the park and the housing project, the neighborhood is a mix of one- and two-family homes and row houses. NeighborhoodScout reports that many of its homes were built between 1940 and 1969, with a number built before 1940. Homes.com's listings describe prewar brick rowhouses, modest duplexes, multifamily brick colonials, Colonial Revival cottages and newly renovated Cape Cod-style townhouses. Two real listings show the range: 1906 Edenwald Avenue, a two-story single-family house built in 1920, and 3924 Duryea Avenue, a two-family built in 1925 with about 2,600 square feet across two units. None of those houses was built with a network in mind, and nearly all of them now need one.

The land itself explains the housing. NYC Parks records that John H. Eden owned an estate here from 1900 to 1913 called Edenwald, which means Eden's forest in German. CityNeighborhoods notes that Edenwald stayed largely rural after the Bronx joined the city, until the New York, Westchester and Boston Railway opened in 1912 along the right-of-way that the 5 train's Dyre Avenue line still uses. Wikipedia's account of the line says the subway took it over on May 15, 1941. Houses followed the stations, lot by lot, through the 1920s and 1930s and again after the war. That is why a single block can hold a 1920s frame house, a 1950s brick two-family and a three-family from the 1960s or 1970s, side by side on narrow lots. Each was framed, lathed and wired differently, and each gives a data cable a different way up from the cellar.

We wanted to know how many of each kind there really are, so we read the city's PLUTO lot data for seven census tracts that cover Edenwald from Boston Road and East 222nd Street up to the Mount Vernon line. That study area is our own filter, not an official boundary, and the boundaries of Edenwald itself vary by source. Within it, PLUTO lists 3,178 residential lots holding 8,467 homes. Of those lots, 1,251 are one-family houses, 694 of them attached or semi-detached; 1,478 are two-family houses; 403 are three-family houses; and only 46 lots hold four homes or more. The median one-family was built in 1940, the median two-family in 1950 and the median three-family in 1965. Most residential lots, 2,027 of them, are two floors, and the median lot is 25 feet wide. Translated into cabling: the typical Edenwald job is a two-story house, often with two households, on a narrow lot, built before anyone had heard of Ethernet.

Then there is the campus that dominates every description of the neighborhood. The Edenwald Houses is, by Wikipedia's and urbanareas.net's accounts, the largest NYCHA development in the Bronx: forty buildings of three and fourteen stories on 48.88 acres, completed on October 15, 1953, bounded by Grenada Place, East 225th Street and Baychester and Laconia Avenues, on land that had been the Hebrew Orphan Asylum. Sources give its size as anywhere from about 2,034 to 2,039 apartments. In June 2023 the city closed financing on a $783 million PACT renovation, and NYC HDC's announcement lists free broadband internet among the upgrades for every apartment. The resident FAQ promises a new intercom system and enhanced security with site-wide cameras and access control, and the Mayor's Office release of October 2026 lists free development-wide WiFi and upgraded electrical and mechanical systems. That network belongs to the campus owner and its partners; it is not something a resident, or an outside contractor at a resident's request, can add to. What it does do is set expectations on the surrounding blocks, where homeowners now ask why their own house cannot have the same steady connection in every room.

The answer is that it usually can, but the house has to be read first. A two-family on a 25-foot lot has the provider's line entering at one end of the cellar, two households' routers competing for the same signal, brick or block at the party wall of a semi-detached pair, and an attic that may have been finished into a bedroom decades ago. Census Reporter's profile of ZIP 10466 shows about 60 percent of occupied homes are rented and 67 percent of housing is in multi-unit buildings, which in this part of the Bronx mostly means two- and three-family houses rather than large apartment houses. So the people who call us are owners who live on one floor and rent another, tenants who need a wired line for work or school, and families splitting one house among several generations. In every case the first question is the same: whose rooms and whose cellar will the cable pass through, and have they agreed.

The neighborhood is also building again, in small pieces. New York YIMBY reported permits filed in January 2026 for a four-story residential building with 15 homes at 3545 Edson Avenue, and in June 2026 for two three-story residential buildings with six homes, most likely condos, at 1237 East 224th Street; the same report links an earlier filing at 1978 Needham Avenue. Buildings of that size do not have the dedicated telecom rooms of a Manhattan tower, but they still need a service entrance, a riser, a cable to every home and cabling for whatever entry and camera system the owner chooses. Those decisions are cheapest while the framing is open, and the developers of small buildings often do not have a low-voltage designer on the team at all.

Finally, we want to be honest about what our research did not find. We searched for Reddit threads, local news and community posts about home networks, internet wiring or cabling disputes in Edenwald and found none. We could not see Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches, Bing, DuckDuckGo or an AI Overview from our build environment, and we found no HPD or 311 records that concern data cabling here. No source describes how the walls of a particular Edenwald house were built. Where this page talks about plaster on wood lath, metal lath, fire blocking or knee walls, it is drawing on the ages and types of houses in the public record and on what we find in houses of that kind generally. The only way to know your house is to walk it, which is the first thing we do.

When a house gets a proper panel and tested drops, the next request is almost always to put the doorbell camera, the yard cameras and, in a two- or three-family, a modern entry panel on the same cabling. Those devices use the same paths through the cellar and up the walls, so planning their cables into the first visit saves opening the same plaster twice. Five Edenwald Cabling Jobs, and What Each One Turns On

Almost every structured cabling call we take from Edenwald falls into one of five patterns. They differ less in how much cable is involved than in what decides the route: the framing of the house, the number of households, whether the building is still open, or whether a store has a back room at all. One-family houses: the stud bays decide

A two-story house from the 1920s to the 1950s usually has wood studs behind plaster, and in many of them a bay of wall runs uninterrupted from the cellar ceiling to the second floor plate, especially on interior walls and beside closets stacked one above the other. When we find a bay like that, cable can rise without opening a single wall, because we drill up through the bottom plate from the cellar and fish down from the attic or the second floor. Where a horizontal fire block sits halfway up the bay, which builders often added between floors, we cut a small, neat opening at that height, bore through the block, and patch the opening afterward. The surprise in older houses is not the plaster itself but what else is in the bay: an old electrical run, a heating pipe or a duct. We check before we drill so the data cable ends up in a bay of its own.

Semi-detached pairs: the party wall belongs to two owners

Our study area holds 694 attached or semi-detached one-family houses. In a pair like that, the wall between the two houses is shared, and it is usually masonry or a fire-rated assembly that should not be treated as a convenient route. We keep every run on your side of it and never drill through it to borrow space on the other side. The harder case is when the provider's line comes into the cellar on the neighbor's side and crosses over; that is a matter for the two owners and the provider, and we explain the options instead of moving someone else's cable.

Two-families: one house, two networks

In a two-family, the downstairs and upstairs households often have separate internet accounts, sometimes with different providers, and a structured cabling job has to respect that. The cleanest design gives each household its own path from the cellar to its own small panel or closet, so the two services never share a splitter, a switch or a cable. If the owner also wants cameras or an entry panel for the whole house, those get a third set of cables on the owner's own panel. We ask both households before we drill through a floor between units, because the hole is in someone's home on both sides.

Three-families: a small building with a hallway

Three-family houses are where an Edenwald house starts to behave like a small apartment building. There is usually a common stair, a front door shared by three households and a cellar that the owner controls. The common stair is a tempting route for cable and often the worst one, because it is the building's way out in a fire and every hole through its walls weakens it. We prefer stacked closets, a pipe chase with room to spare, or the back of the building, and we seal every floor crossing with a listed firestop product. The owner's equipment, the panel for cameras, entry and any building network, goes in a locked cabinet that no tenant needs to open.

Count the floors the cable has to cross

The single biggest factor in an Edenwald house is not how many jacks you want but how many floors each one has to travel. A jack in a first-floor living room right above the cellar is a short, simple run; a jack in a converted attic bedroom of a two-story house crosses two floors and usually a fire block. When you list the rooms, list the floor too. It helps us price fairly and choose whether one riser path can serve several rooms on the upper floors.

Think about the yard and the garage

Many Edenwald lots run long and narrow toward the back, and owners often want a camera over the driveway, a camera at the back of the yard, or a connection in a rear garage. If any of that is in your plans, tell us now. Outdoor runs need outdoor-rated cable, protection where they leave the house, and a sealed entry point; planning them with the indoor drops lets them share the same panel and the same cellar path instead of becoming a separate job later.

If you are building, send the drawings

Developers and owners of the new small buildings going up on Edson Avenue, East 224th Street, Needham Avenue and the side streets should send the floor plans and the electrical drawings, even if they are not final. From those we can mark where the service enters, where a utility or telecom closet should go, how many sleeves each floor needs, and how each home gets its own path. It is far cheaper to add a line on a drawing than to core a slab after it is poured.

Edenwald's houses went up in several waves, and each wave left a different wall for a cable to pass through. These are the types that the listings, neighborhood histories and lot data describe, and how we approach each one.

Two-story houses like the 1920 one-family on Edenwald Avenue. Wood studs, plaster on wood lath, and cellars with open joists. Often the easiest to fish once the bays are found.

Prewar Brick Rowhouses

Brick fronts and party walls, plaster inside. Runs stay on interior walls and in the cellar; nothing crosses the party wall.

Postwar Brick Two-Families

The houses of the 1950s boom, the decade with the most residential lots in our study area. Brick or block outside, plaster or early drywall inside, two households stacked.

Colonial Revival and Cape Cod Cottages

Steep roofs and finished upper floors with knee walls. The crawl space behind the knee wall is often the best path along the upper floor.

New Small Buildings

Recent filings of three and four stories. Open framing, sleeves through each floor, and a closet that should be on the drawings from the start.

Frame houses: find the bay, then the fire block

In a two-story frame house of the 1920s or 1930s, the cellar ceiling is usually open joists, which makes it easy to carry cable from the service entrance to a point directly below any wall on the first floor. From there we drill up through the bottom plate into the stud bay. If the bay is clear to the second floor, a fish rod or a pull line finishes the job; if it meets a fire block, we open a small square of plaster at that height, bore the block, pass the cable and close the opening with a patch that can be painted. Wood lath plaster is brittle at the edges, so we score openings rather than break them out, and we keep every opening small enough to patch flat. Upstairs, the attic or the space above the second-floor ceiling is often the easiest way to drop into bedrooms from above.

Brick two-families: respect the floor between households

The postwar two-families that line so many Edenwald blocks put one household above the other, with a floor that is also the line between two homes. A cable that rises from the cellar to the upstairs unit has to pass through the first-floor apartment, either inside a wall or a closet, and the downstairs household has to know and agree. Some houses of this era used plaster over metal lath instead of wood lath; it is harder to cut cleanly and holds a fish rod less predictably, so we look for closets, chases and the space beside a chimney before committing to a wall. Where an outside route makes more sense, along the back of the house rather than the front, we use outdoor-rated cable in a protective cover and seal the entry at the wall.

Cape Cod and Colonial Revival cottages: use the knee wall

Houses with a steep roof and a finished upper floor have a triangular crawl space behind the short wall of each upstairs room. That space often runs the full length of the house, and once we reach it, cable can travel from one end of the upper floor to the other without touching a finished wall. The trick is getting into it: many have a small access door, some have none, and some are packed with insulation. We check for access, wear the right protection where insulation is present, and keep cable above the insulation and clear of any recessed lights or vents.

Three-families and new buildings: think like a building

Once a house holds three households, it needs a building plan: a path from the service entrance to each unit, an owner's panel for shared systems, and sealed openings wherever cable crosses a floor or the stair enclosure. New buildings go one step further. While the framing is open we can set sleeves through each floor, run spare conduit with pull strings to every home, and size the closet where the service lands so a future provider or a building camera system has room. On a finished building, if a run has to pass through block at a stair or a closet wall, we core the block with the owner's agreement and seal the opening once the cable is through.

The takeout counters, Caribbean kitchens and fried-chicken shops along Boston Road and East 233rd Street often run their registers, kitchen printers, delivery-tablet chargers, card readers and a guest Wi-Fi network off one router behind the counter. When that router reboots or a guest's device misbehaves, everything stops at once. A structured layout gives each payment device and printer its own tested drop, puts the guest Wi-Fi on a separate network configured on the switch, and moves the equipment out of the grease and heat near the line. Kitchen walls get cable in a protective cover where it runs low, because carts and buckets will hit anything that hangs.

Converted houses: residential walls, business needs

A number of offices, programs and churches in and around Edenwald work out of buildings that began as houses. They have the stud bays, plaster and cellars of a house, but the drop count of a small office: several desks, a printer, a copier, a camera recorder, and often a room that doubles as a meeting or classroom space. We treat them as houses for the route and as offices for the network, with a small rack, a managed switch and separated networks for staff, guests and building systems. Where a congregation wants a camera at the front of the hall connected back to a booth or office, that run goes in the same plan.

Rules and practical limits for business cabling in Edenwald

  • Permits for data wiring. NYC Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, under which an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, effective December 21, 2025. A Bronx hiring guide we read says the exemption applies when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the electrical code defines it. On a commercial scope we confirm the filing requirement before work starts.
  • Fire alarm wiring stays separate. Fire alarm circuits fall outside the data exemption and carry their own licensing and filings. We do not mix fire alarm cable into a data job.
  • New receptacles are electrical work. If a cabinet, recorder or access point needs a new outlet, that circuit is for a licensed electrician, and the quote says so.
  • Jacket ratings match the space. Cable in an air-handling ceiling needs a plenum rating, cable rising between floors needs at least a riser rating, and cable leaving the building needs an outdoor rating.
  • Holes get sealed. Every penetration through a rated wall or floor is sealed with a listed firestop product suited to the cable and the assembly, and left visible for inspection.
  • Landlord approval comes first. In a leased store, the lease usually controls alterations. We ask for the landlord's agreement and any insurance certificate before the visit.
  • Across the city line. Edenwald ends at Mount Vernon, a separate city in Westchester County. A business with locations on both sides should know the rules are not the same; we quote any Mount Vernon address as a Westchester job.

Field Notes: Reading a Two-Story Edenwald House From the Bottom Plate Up

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow in the kind of house that makes up most of Edenwald: two stories, a cellar, and often two households.

We start outside, at the provider's box on the foundation, and note which side of the house the line comes in. In the cellar, we find where it passes through the wall and follow every cable from that point with a light, counting splitters and old phone blocks and photographing them as they are. Then we stand under each room that needs a jack and look up: at the joists, the bottom plates, any pipes or ducts, and any old electrical run fastened to the wood. A small bore and a flexible camera tell us whether the bay above is open, blocked or already crowded. Upstairs, we knock on walls, open closet ceilings and attic hatches, and check behind knee walls. In a two-family, we ask the upstairs household which closets they use and whether a cable may rise through one. Only after we know where each bay leads do we write the route on the plan, and only after you agree to it does anyone drill.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Edenwald: Questions Answered

Old houses, old walls and old wiring

Our 1920s house still has some original cloth-covered electrical wiring inside the walls. Can the new data cable go in the same wall cavities?

It can share a wall but should not share a bay with old electrical wiring if we can help it, and it should never be tied to it or run alongside it for any distance. Old cloth-insulated wiring may be brittle, and disturbing it while fishing a data cable can damage it; electrical wiring also carries noise that a data cable is better off avoiding. We probe each bay before drilling, choose a bay without electrical runs where one exists, and where a data cable must cross an electrical one we cross at a right angle and keep them apart. If we see electrical wiring in poor condition, we point it out to you so a licensed electrician can look at it; that work is outside our trade.

Our semi-detached house shares a cellar wall with the neighbor, and the internet line actually comes in on their side before crossing over. Can our new cable follow it?

Not through their space without their agreement, and in most cases we would not want to. A line that enters on a neighbor's side and crosses the party wall is a provider's arrangement between two properties, and any change to it belongs to both owners and the provider. What we can do is start your structured cabling at the point where the line arrives on your side, build your panel there, and route everything inside your own walls. If you would rather have a service entrance of your own, the provider has to install it; we can prepare the inside path so their technician has a clear place to land the new line.

Our Cape Cod-style house has a finished upstairs with slanted ceilings and small doors in the short walls. How do cables reach those bedrooms?

The small doors usually open onto the crawl space behind the knee walls, and that space is often the best path in the house. From the cellar, we bring cable up through a first-floor wall or closet to the floor of the upper level, then travel along the crawl space and drop through the knee wall right where each jack is wanted. The jack ends up low on the knee wall, which suits desks and TVs. If the crawl space is closed off or packed with insulation, we look at the closet under the slope or a route up through the attic above the ceiling, and we tell you on the walk-through which one your house allows.

The walls in our 1950s brick two-family feel harder than plaster usually does, and screws kept spinning when we hung pictures. Will that make cabling harder?

It may mean plaster on metal lath, which some builders of that era used, and yes, it changes the approach. Metal lath is tougher to cut, can snag a fish rod, and does not let us open a small, clean square the way wood lath does. So we lean on routes that avoid cutting finished walls: closets stacked floor over floor, the space beside a chimney or a plumbing chase, and the open cellar ceiling for horizontal runs. Where a jack must go in a solid wall, we use a cutting method suited to metal lath and a mounting bracket made for it. We confirm what the walls are on the walk-through before pricing those drops.

We want a camera at the back of the yard, and the house has vinyl siding over the original wood. How does the cable get out without causing a leak?

Through one carefully placed hole, sealed in layers. We choose an exit point under an eave or overhang where water does not run down the wall, drill through the siding and sheathing at a slight downward angle so water cannot run inward, and pass the cable through a weatherproof entry fitting that seals to the siding. Outside, the cable is outdoor-rated and protected where it runs along the house or across the yard. Inside, the hole is sealed again. Vinyl siding moves with temperature, so we leave the fitting room to shift rather than screwing it tight against the panel.

Two- and three-family houses

Our three-family still has the old intercom wire running to each apartment. Can that wire be reused for a network or for a new video intercom?

For a computer network, almost never. Old intercom wire is usually a bundle of plain conductors not twisted in pairs and not rated for data, so it cannot carry Ethernet at any useful speed. For a new entry system it depends on the product: some two-wire video intercoms are designed to reuse existing wiring, provided it tests clean, while systems that need network cable at each apartment need new runs. We test the existing wire on the walk-through and tell you which systems it could support. If new cable is needed, we pull it alongside the data drops so the building is opened once.

In our three-family, where should the shared equipment go so we never need a tenant to let us in to reach it?

In a space the owner controls, which in most Edenwald three-families means the cellar. We mount a lockable wall cabinet there holding the owner's patch panel, switch and battery backup for building systems such as cameras and an entry panel, with each apartment's own internet arriving at its own panel or closet upstairs. That way the owner can service the building network without entering anyone's home, and each household's internet stays private. If the cellar floods in heavy rain or holds a boiler that throws heat, we mount the cabinet high and away from both, or choose a space under the stairs instead.

New small buildings

We are building a four-story, fifteen-home building of the size filed on Edson Avenue. Does it need a real telecom room, or will a closet do?

A well-planned closet is usually enough at that size, but it has to be planned as a telecom space, not leftover storage. It should sit where the provider's service can enter easily, have room for at least two providers' equipment plus the owner's panel, have a dedicated outlet and some ventilation, and connect to each floor through sleeves or conduit sized with spare capacity. Each home then gets its own cable or conduit back to that closet. Building it this way means a second provider, a camera system or an entry panel can be added years later without opening finished walls. We mark all of it on the drawings before framing closes.

Our project is two three-story buildings with six condos on one lot, like the filing on East 224th Street. Should they share one internet entrance or each have their own?

It depends on how the condo will be run, and it is worth deciding before the foundation. One shared entrance with a protected path between the buildings, usually conduit underground, keeps provider equipment in one place and makes a shared camera or entry system simpler. Separate entrances make each building independent, which some owners prefer when the buildings may be managed or sold differently. Either way, put empty conduit between the two buildings while the site is open; it costs little now and saves digging later. If a link between buildings is needed, fiber is the safer choice because it does not carry electrical surges from one structure to the other.

We cannot tell you exactly what is being installed inside each apartment; the public descriptions say free broadband in apartments and development-wide WiFi, but not how the cabling is laid out. Stores and community spaces

We are opening in one of the narrow storefronts on East 233rd Street and there is no back room. Where can the network equipment go?

Up, and locked. In a store with no back office, the best spot is usually a small wall-mount cabinet high on a wall near the provider's entry point or above the stock area, out of reach of customers and clear of water and carts. Cable then runs in the ceiling to each counter position and down to the terminals in a protected cover. If the space has a dropped ceiling, we may mount the cabinet just below it and keep runs short. The landlord has to agree to the location and the holes, and if there is a roll-up gate we keep any front-wall penetration clear of its track.

After the October water main break near Strang and De Reimer avenues, our basement took water, and the cable on the floor was sitting in it. Does that cable need replacing?

Usually the stretch that was submerged should be replaced, and the connectors on anything that got wet almost certainly should. Indoor data cable is not made to sit in water; moisture can wick into the jacket and connectors, corrode the copper and cause failures that show up weeks later. We test every affected run with a cable analyzer, replace what fails or was underwater, and re-route the replacement up along the joists instead of across the floor, with the panel mounted high on the wall. CBS New York reported flooded streets and parking lots from that break; if your cellar was affected, mention it when you book so we bring enough cable to replace the runs.

Our community room wants a small computer lab with about eight computers for classes. What does the cabling look like?

Eight wired drops along the tables, one for a printer, one or two ceiling access points for laptops and phones, and a small rack with a patch panel, switch and battery backup in a closet that locks. We place the drops so cables do not cross walkways, usually in a surface raceway along the wall behind the tables or in floor boxes if the floor allows. The lab's network should be separate from the office and any guest Wi-Fi. If the room is in a converted house, as many program spaces in the neighborhood are, we plan the route through the cellar the same way we would for a home, just with more drops at the end.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Edenwald

We could not capture Google's AI Overview or People Also Ask boxes for this search from our build environment, so we will not pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about home and small-business cabling and about this corner of the Bronx, set against the public record and our field practice. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that many of those answers lean on; they give a rough idea, but they are not a plan for a house in Edenwald.

"Edenwald is a public housing neighborhood."

The Edenwald Houses are large and well known, and they appear in almost every summary of the area. But in our PLUTO study area of seven census tracts, 3,132 of 3,178 residential lots hold one, two or three homes.

Rob Stephenson's history, NeighborhoodScout and Homes.com all describe the same thing in different words: one- and two-family houses, row houses, duplexes and cottages, many from before 1970.

For cabling that distinction matters. The campus is owned and managed by others and has its own renovation network; the houses around it are where owners can actually decide to wire every room.

"The free WiFi at the Edenwald Houses means the neighborhood is covered."

The Mayor's Office release describes free development-wide WiFi as part of the Edenwald Houses renovation, and HDC's announcement lists free broadband in apartments. Those are campus services.

Nothing in those announcements describes service to the privately owned houses on Edson, Wilder, De Reimer or Murdock Avenues, or to stores on East 233rd Street.

Separately, Big Apple Connect, the city's free internet program for NYCHA residents, has been extended through 2028 according to Broadband Breakfast, and NYC's page says it runs through Optimum or Spectrum depending on location. It is for public housing residents, not a neighborhood network.

"You cannot wire an old house without tearing open the walls."

This is the claim we hear most from Edenwald owners, and it is mostly wrong for the frame houses that fill the neighborhood. Wood-framed walls have hollow bays, and many of them run straight from the cellar to the second floor.

With an open cellar ceiling below and an attic or knee-wall space above, most drops need only a hole in a bottom plate and a small, patchable opening at any fire block.

Brick party walls, plaster on metal lath and finished cellars are harder, and we say so on the walk-through. But "harder" usually means a closet route or a different wall, not tearing a house apart.

"A home network needs a server closet."

Online guides written for offices talk about server rooms, MDFs and IDFs. A two-story house in Edenwald needs none of that.

A lockable wall cabinet in the cellar, near where the provider's line enters, holds a patch panel, a small switch and a battery backup in a space smaller than a medicine cabinet. In a two-family, each household may get its own.

What it does need is a sensible location: above any water line, away from the boiler's heat, with an outlet nearby. That matters more than the size of the box.

"Data wiring rules stop at the permit."

Local Law 128 of 2024 removed the electrical permit for wiring under 50 volts for data, communication, signaling and alarm work, effective December 21, 2025. A Bronx hiring guide notes the exemption applies to work done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the code defines it.

No permit does not mean no rules. Cable jackets still have to match the space, holes through rated floors and walls still have to be sealed, and fire alarm wiring remains a separate trade with its own filings.

In a two- or three-family house, the other rules that matter are practical ones: the owner's consent, and every household knowing what is being done in their home.

"The same cable works indoors and out."

Many quick answers about yard cameras treat all network cable as interchangeable. Indoor cable run along a house's exterior or buried across a yard breaks down in sunlight, cold and moisture, and fails sooner than it should.

Edenwald's long, narrow lots mean many owners want cable to the back of the yard or a rear garage. Those runs need outdoor-rated cable, protection where they are exposed, and a sealed entry at the house.

Between two buildings, such as a house and a garage or two condo buildings on one lot, fiber or properly protected copper keeps a surge in one structure from traveling to the other.

"Cabling costs a set amount per drop."

National cost guides publish per-drop averages, and they are useful for scale. But in Edenwald, the cost of a drop depends far more on the path than on the cable.

A jack in a first-floor room above an open cellar ceiling is a short job. A jack in a converted attic of a brick two-family with plaster on metal lath, crossing a floor between households, is several times the labor for the same length of cable.

That is why we price from a written drop plan after walking the house, with each drop listed and its route described, rather than from an average that is wrong for most houses.

Our Installation Process

Every Edenwald cabling job follows the same eight steps, whether it is two drops in a one-family house or a full prewire for a new building. In a neighborhood of houses shared by several households, the second step, settling who agrees, matters as much as anything about cable.

The provider's entry, the cellar ceiling, each stud bay, fire blocks, knee walls and the attic, checked before anything is drilled.

Every drop, its cable, its route, the panel location, the sealing and the price, agreed in writing before work starts.

Each copper drop tested with a cable analyzer and each fiber strand checked; anything that fails is fixed and tested again before we leave.

The port map, test results and photos of sealed holes, plus a walk through the panel, the switch and which jack goes where.

FAQ

Edenwald Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Why does choosing Cat6A over Cat6 add so little to an Edenwald quote?
+

Because most of the cost of a drop in an Edenwald house is the labor of making the path, boring plates, getting past fire blocks, fishing the bay and sealing the holes, not the cable inside it. The cable is a small part of each line on the plan. Cat6A is thicker and a little harder to pull through tight bays, and the jacks cost more, so the difference is real but modest. We show both on the quote when it makes sense so you can decide.

Our walls were filled with blown-in insulation at some point. Can cable still be fished through them?
+

Usually, yes, but more slowly. Blown-in insulation packs the bay and grabs a fish rod, so we use stiffer rods, pull from the attic or cellar end with care, and sometimes choose an interior wall without insulation instead of an exterior one. We avoid disturbing more insulation than necessary and seal the holes we make. Tell us if you know the walls were insulated so we plan the time.

Can the new data wall plates match the style and color of the outlets we already have?
+

In most cases, yes. Data jacks fit standard keystone plates that come in the common outlet styles and colors, so we can match the look of your existing switches and receptacles in each room. Send a photo of an outlet plate with the form, and we bring matching plates to the visit.

The router our provider gave us has only four ports, but the plan has nine jacks. What connects the rest?
+

A switch in the cellar cabinet. Every jack comes back to a patch panel, the panel connects to a switch with enough ports for all the drops and some to spare, and one cable joins the switch to the provider's router. If any of the devices are cameras or access points, a PoE switch can power them over the same cable. The switch is listed on the quote, or we can install one you already own.

Is outside cable work done in winter?
+

Yes, with some limits. Outdoor-rated cable can be installed in cold weather, but sealants and some fittings need temperatures above a certain point to cure properly, and ice on a roof edge or ladder area can make work unsafe. In very cold spells we may run the indoor part of the job and schedule the exterior entry and sealing for a milder day, and we tell you in advance if that is the plan.

Our kids play online games upstairs and the router is in the cellar. Will a wired drop really help through brick and plaster?
+

It usually helps a lot. Wi-Fi from a cellar router has to pass through a floor, plaster and sometimes brick before it reaches an upstairs bedroom, and online games are sensitive to the delays and drops that causes. A tested cable to the console or computer removes that path entirely, and a cabled access point upstairs gives phones and tablets a strong signal on that floor too.
