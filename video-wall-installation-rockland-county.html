What usually goes wrong

  • The player lost its clock. A signage player that cannot reach its time server drifts, and a zmanim board that is even a few minutes out is worse than none. We set a reliable time source and a fallback.
  • One tile will not wake. Often a loose power lead or a failed power supply in that one panel, not the whole wall. We test the tile before anyone orders a replacement.
  • The layout is wrong after a power cut. The controller did not save its settings, or restarted before the panels did. We fix the start-up order and save the layout so it comes back on its own.
  • Content stopped updating. A changed Wi-Fi password, an expired software licence or a firewall change at the office. We trace it and write the fix on the handover sheet.

The Calendar Comes First

Five Calendars a Rockland Wall Has to Keep

Before we talk about panel sizes we ask which calendar the wall belongs to, because in Rockland that decides the controller, the content system, the network and even where the off switch goes. Almost every wall we plan here follows one of five.

Shabbat and Yom Tov

A shul or a Jewish school wall changes mode before candle lighting and must hold that mode until after havdalah, or across a two- or three-day Yom Tov, without anyone in the building pressing a button or changing what it shows. Everything that the screen does on those days has to be decided and loaded before they begin, and the hardware has to be chosen so that nothing reacts to people in the room. Shul signage services calculate zmanim from the building’s exact map coordinates rather than from a town name, which is why the address and the location pin matter at the survey.

Whether a screen may run at all on Shabbat, and what it may show, is a halachic question, and it is not ours to answer. Halachic reference works note that setting a timer before Shabbat to switch lights or appliances on or off is permitted, while some leading authorities, among them Rabbi Moshe Feinstein, are cited as generally opposing timers that run machines to do work on Shabbat. Communities follow their own rav. We build the wall so that whichever rule the congregation follows is easy to keep: a dark screen from candle lighting, a static Shabbat board, or a schedule that runs untouched.

The academic year

Rockland Community College’s main campus is in Suffern, with centers in Nyack and Orangeburg; the college says more than 5,300 people are enrolled in credit and non-credit courses and over 200,000 visit the campus each year for cultural, recreational and commercial events. St. Thomas Aquinas College sits on Route 340 in Sparkill, and Dominican University New York on Western Highway in Orangeburg, which became a university in May 2022. A campus wall follows terms, breaks, exam weeks and commencement, and is managed by whichever office owns the building. We set it to fall back to a holding page when a term’s content runs out, so a summer lobby never shows September’s welcome in July.

The retail year

A Route 59 store wall lives by opening hours, sale dates and holiday seasons, and by a landlord’s rules on when contractors may work. Content for a season is loaded weeks ahead and switches on by date; the wall turns off after closing so panels are not burning through the night for an empty store; and the mounts are chosen so the whole wall can come down cleanly if the store moves.

The duty roster

A dispatch or operations wall never switches off, and a training classroom wall follows the course schedule. Panels for those rooms must be rated for long daily hours, and the controller needs saved layouts that come back by themselves after a power interruption. Installation is planned in sections so the room stays usable.

The household

With an average of 3.22 people per Rockland household and nearly a third of residents under eighteen, a family-room wall is used by everyone. It needs to be safe at child height, simple to switch between a game, a film and a slideshow of a family simcha, and quiet about what it shows when guests are over.

Video Wall Installation for Rockland County’s Architecture

Rockland’s walls hang in buildings of every age, from village storefronts on Main Street in Nyack to block-walled training buildings and big-box stores on Route 59. The building decides the mount, the cable path and sometimes whether a fixed wall is possible at all.

Shuls and batei midrash

Often converted houses or purpose-built halls with a lobby, a sanctuary and a study hall; walls go where people pass and where they wait, never where they will distract from prayer. Mechitza sightlines decide placement.

Churches and parish halls

Sanctuaries with high ceilings, stained glass and old plaster, and parish halls used for everything from bingo to wakes. Floor-supported frames or masonry anchors are common, and power is rarely where the screen should go.

Catering and simcha halls

Large open rooms with dance floors, stages and changing layouts. Walls are planned for a projected picture of the chuppah or a speech, an overflow room, and sound through the hall’s own system.

College and school buildings

Block-walled classrooms, glass-fronted lobbies and older brick halls on campuses such as Suffern, Sparkill and Orangeburg, with closets and network rooms that may be in another building.

Route 59 stores and mall units

Big-box stores with open steel roofs, in-line mall units with landlord-controlled ceilings, and pad sites with glass fronts facing the highway. Each landlord sets its own contractor hours and insurance rules.

Office, lab and industrial parks

Pearl River, Orangeburg and Blauvelt offices and labs, including the multi-tenant Hudson Valley iCampus, where a wall in one tenant’s space may run on another party’s power and network.

Public-safety and training buildings

Dispatch floors, emergency-operations rooms and training classrooms built from block, with raised floors or cable trays and strict rules on who may enter and when.

Rockland houses

Family rooms and finished basements where walls are finished and cannot be opened freely, and where a wall shares space with toys, bookshelves and the rest of the home’s whole-home audio.

How the building changes the method

Converted houses used as shuls. Framed walls of uncertain age, sometimes with plaster over lath. We find the studs, add blocking or a full-width rail where the wall will carry several panels, and fish cable through closed walls rather than opening them, because a congregation using the building every day cannot live with a repair in progress.

Block-walled schools, halls and training buildings. Cable runs on the surface in conduit or raceway, and passes through block in a cored, sleeved hole. Mount anchors go into solid block or grout-filled cells, not into hollow cores, and the anchor pattern is set out before drilling so every anchor lands well.

Open-ceiling stores and halls. Long cable runs hang in J-hooks or trays from the roof structure, at heights that need a lift. Landlords usually want the route approved and labelled, and fire-stopping restored wherever a cable crosses a rated wall.

Older sanctuaries and brick buildings. When nothing may be fixed to a historic interior, the wall stands on a floor-supported frame. When masonry can take anchors, we drill into the brick rather than the mortar joints that are easier to drill and weaker to hold.

Multi-tenant parks. Before the first box goes in, we confirm whose electrical panel and whose network the wall will use, and get that in writing from the building owner and the tenant.

Rockland rules that touch a video wall

  • Licensed electrical work. Laws of Rockland County Chapter 250, Article I, requires anyone in the business of installing wiring and equipment for light, heat or power, or for signaling systems operating at 50 volts or more, to be licensed, under the supervision of a master electrician. The new outlet or circuit behind a wall is that kind of work.
  • Inspection by a licensed agency. Chapter 250, Article II, licenses electrical inspection businesses. When an inspector finds an installation conforms to the National Electrical Code, local rules and Orange & Rockland specifications, the inspector issues a certificate of approval, gives the owner a copy, and notifies the utility in writing that the connection is authorized.
  • Town and village building departments. Clarkstown, Haverstraw, Orangetown, Ramapo and Stony Point each run their own building department, as do the incorporated villages, and their permit policies for electrical work differ. In the Village of Spring Valley the county’s Office of Buildings and Codes administers the state building code.
  • Home improvement licensing. Chapter 286, the county’s rewritten home-improvement law, took effect on May 14, 2026; the county says all individuals performing home improvement work must hold a Home Improvement License. It matters for walls installed in homes.
  • Protrusion into walkways. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, a wall-mounted object with its leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may stick out no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A wall in a corridor or a busy lobby is mounted with that in mind.
  • Landlord and procurement rules. Mall management, park owners and public buyers each set their own insurance, scheduling and specification terms. The county’s 2019 bid, for example, required evidence of insurance within five days of award and a certificate of experience for the past three years.

None of this is legal advice; it is the checklist we work through on every Rockland job, and the building department or inspection agency has the final word.

Your Questions Answered

Video Wall Installation in Rockland County: Questions Answered

Grouped by the kind of building asking. Congregations, colleges, store managers, public buyers and homeowners put these to us while a Rockland wall is still on paper; none of them is repeated in the FAQ further down.

Walls That Run Through Shabbat and Yom Tov

Can a shul board switch from weekday times to the Shabbat schedule on its own before candle lighting?

Yes. The board holds two or more schedules — weekday, Erev Shabbat, Shabbat, Yom Tov — and the player moves between them at times calculated from the zmanim for your building, for example a set number of minutes before candle lighting. Once the week’s content is in, nobody needs to touch the screen from Friday afternoon until after havdalah.

What the Shabbat mode looks like is the congregation’s decision: some want a static page with the Shabbat times and no movement, some want the screen dark, some leave a rotating schedule running as it was set before Shabbat. We set it up the way the rav directs, and we test the changeover on a weekday by moving the player’s clock forward to a Friday afternoon, so you see it happen before the first real Shabbat.

How does the screen know the zmanim for our building rather than for the county as a whole?

Sunrise, sunset and the times derived from them shift with longitude and latitude, so a shul in Suffern and one in Nyack, at opposite ends of Route 59, see slightly different times. Shul signage services calculate them from the building’s precise map coordinates and its time zone rather than from a town name. At set-up we enter the exact location of the building and pick the calculation method the congregation uses, then check a week of times against the luach it already trusts before the board goes live.

What happens to a programmed Shabbat schedule on the weekends the clocks change?

If the player takes its time from the internet and its time zone is set correctly, it moves to daylight saving time and back by itself, and every zman moves with it. Trouble comes from players with a manually set clock, or from a schedule built with fixed clock times instead of times tied to zmanim. We avoid both: the player syncs to a reliable time source with a second one as backup, and each changeover is tied to a zman rather than to a clock time. Before the first change after installation, we check the following Friday’s schedule in the software.

Can a two- or three-day Yom Tov be loaded in advance as one block?

Yes, and this is where planning pays. When Yom Tov runs into Shabbat, the screen may need to hold its mode for three days. We build Yom Tov schedules for the whole year at once from the Jewish calendar, so the gabbai only reviews them, and we check that the player has enough storage and does not need the internet for any of those days. If the congregation wants different content on Chol Hamoed, that goes in as its own schedule.

Our sanctuary has a mechitza. How do both sides get a clear view of the screens?

We survey sightlines from both sections, standing and seated, before choosing a position. A mechitza, a balcony rail or a bimah in the middle of the room can hide a screen that looks perfect from the doorway. Often the answer is two smaller displays showing the same thing, one facing each section, rather than one larger wall; sometimes it is a higher mounting position that clears the partition. We mark positions on the wall with tape and ask someone to sit on each side before anything is drilled.

Will Hebrew and Yiddish text come out right-to-left on a signage player, or reversed?

Most current signage software handles right-to-left text correctly, but older players, some free apps and some fonts still reverse words or break lines in the wrong place, especially when Hebrew and English share a line. We test the actual content on the actual player before handover: names, parsha titles, mixed Hebrew-English notices, numbers inside Hebrew text. If the congregation already has a template in a word processor, we check that its fonts are installed on the player, since a missing font is the most common cause of garbled lettering.

Will your technicians schedule around Shabbat, Yom Tov and the chagim?

Yes. We do not book work in a shul, a Jewish school or a Jewish-owned business on Shabbat or Yom Tov, and we avoid Friday afternoons in winter, when Shabbat begins early. Before the Yamim Noraim and Pesach, congregations are busiest, so we suggest booking a new wall well ahead of those weeks and keeping the last visit before them to programming and checks rather than drilling.

Campus, Training and Dispatch Rooms

Can a campus wall follow the academic calendar without being reprogrammed every term?

Yes. We load the college’s published calendar into the content system as a set of dated schedules: term content, exam-week notices, breaks with a reduced holding page, and one-off days such as open houses and commencement. Each schedule has an end date, and when a schedule ends without a replacement the wall falls back to a neutral page rather than old news. Each term, the office that owns the wall uploads new content into a slot that already exists; nobody rebuilds the schedule.

Can a training-room wall keep a record of what was on screen during a drill, for the review afterwards?

Yes. The controller’s output, or any single source on the wall, can be fed to a recorder at the same time as it is shown, so the instructor can replay the maps, camera views and slides in the order they appeared. We size storage for the length of the exercises and how long recordings are kept, and put the recorder where only the people responsible can reach it.

Two cautions. If live camera feeds or caller details appear on the wall, the recording carries them too, so the department’s own rules on retention and access apply to it. And a recording of the wall is not a substitute for the dispatch system’s own logs; it shows what people saw, not what the system did.

Can a wall be built in a room that has to stay staffed around the clock?

Yes, by working in sections. We agree the order with the shift supervisor: rough-in and anchors along one part of the wall while the room works on the other, temporary screens on carts if a position must be vacated, and the cut-over from old displays to the new wall during the quietest hours. Dust and noise are kept away from consoles, and no one disconnects anything on the room’s own network without the person responsible standing next to us.

Route 59 Stores and a Changing Mall

Our store is in the Palisades Center. Does the 2026 change of owner change who approves our display work?

Your lease and the mall’s tenant rules still govern it, and the people who apply them are mall management. What can change after a sale is the paperwork: a new insurance certificate format, new contacts for scheduling contractor access, revised rules for after-hours work. Before we book a date we ask management for its current contractor requirements and send the certificate it asks for. If your store has its own landlord approval process for alterations, we prepare the drawings and the equipment list for it.

Can a holiday-season layout be loaded weeks ahead and switch itself on by date?

Yes. Seasonal content goes into the content system as its own schedule with start and end dates, so the wall changes from regular content to the season’s campaign at opening on the first day and back again afterwards. Staff can still push an urgent change, such as a sold-out item, without breaking the schedule. We also set the wall to switch off after closing, which saves the panels and the electricity on nights the store is empty.

Our Nanuet unit is part of a center being redeveloped. Should we hold off on a wall?

Not necessarily, but plan it to move. We use mounts that come off a wall cleanly and leave anchor holes that are easy to patch, run the cable on paths that can be removed without opening ceilings, and keep the controller and player in a portable rack or a cabinet rather than built into the building. If the redevelopment moves your store, most of the wall moves with you. Ask your landlord about the timetable before you sign the quote; if the move is months away, a smaller single display may be the better spend.

Licensing, Inspection and Paperwork in Rockland

What is an electrical inspection agency, and why does Orange & Rockland care about it?

Rockland licenses private businesses to inspect electrical work under Chapter 250, Article II of the county’s laws, and their inspectors must be certified by a nationally recognized program. When the inspector finds a new installation conforms to the National Electrical Code, local rules and Orange & Rockland’s own specifications, the inspector issues a certificate of approval, with a copy for the owner, and notifies the utility in writing that the connection is authorized.

For a video wall that matters when the job needs a new circuit or a new service connection. The master electrician arranges the inspection; we make sure the outlets and boxes behind the wall are in place before the inspector comes, and we keep a copy of the certificate with the wall’s handover papers.

Do Rockland’s five towns treat a new display circuit the same way?

No, and the difference is worth a phone call before the job starts. One inspection agency that works across Rockland publishes a table of how each municipality handles permits: it lists Orangetown as requiring a building permit only, the Town of Haverstraw as requiring an electrical permit for electrical work, Ramapo as requiring an electrical permit with a building permit depending on the job, Stony Point as requiring building and electrical permits for new work, and Clarkstown as requiring a building permit with electrical permits varying. Incorporated villages have their own departments. We treat that table as a starting point and confirm with the building department for the address.

Our building is in the Village of Spring Valley. Who enforces the building code there?

The county does. Rockland County’s Office of Buildings and Codes administers and enforces the New York State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code and the Energy Code within the Village of Spring Valley, rather than a village building department. If the work needs a permit, the application goes to that county office. We check the address against the right authority at the survey, because a wall a few streets away in Monsey answers to the Town of Ramapo instead.

What did Rockland’s 2026 rewrite of its home-improvement law actually change?

Chapter 286, the rewritten law, took effect on May 14, 2026. The county’s licensing page still says that all individuals performing home improvement work must hold a Home Improvement License, and it now offers three routes to one: qualifying experience in the trade, at least five years of licensure in another jurisdiction with no complaints, or a surety bond. News 12 reported tempers flaring at the April 2026 Legislature meeting where the rules were scaled back, and noted that home improvement contractors in Rockland have had to be licensed since 1986.

For a homeowner the practical point is that a licence alone says less about skill than it may once have; one Rockland Reddit user wrote that it now seemed possible to register “without any training”. The licence also does not cover connecting anything to the house wiring, which stays with a licensed electrician under Chapter 250. Ask for both, plus a written contract and proof of insurance.

Is a wall-mounted screen in a family room safe around young children?

It can be made safe, and in a Rockland house with small children it has to be. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Video Wall Installation in Rockland County

Google’s AI Overview and People Also Ask boxes could not be retrieved from our research tools for this edition, so this section tests the claims made by the vendor pages and cost guides that rank for the same searches, and says where a Rockland job differs.

“A zmanim screen costs $250 and nothing after that”

One shul signage service advertises exactly that: a $250 set-up fee for a small computer that plugs into the shul’s television, plus shipping, and nothing monthly, with zmanim calculated from the building’s coordinates. For a single existing TV in a small shul, that can be a sensible answer, and we are happy to mount and wire the screen it runs on.

It is not a video wall, though. The $250 buys the player and its service, not the commercial display, the mount, the outlet behind the screen or the network connection. A tiled wall adds a controller, commercial panels rated for long hours and a rail that carries them. On our Rockland price list a 2x2 wall of 55-inch panels is $15,600 to $23,400 installed.

For a lobby board read in passing, one large display and a player may be enough; we price both.

“Set a timer and the screen will take care of Shabbat”

Vendor pages for shul screens stress that everything runs on its own once installed, and the timing part is true: the software can switch modes at the right zman. But whether a screen may run on Shabbat, what it may display, and whether a timer may be used to change it, are questions on which halachic authorities differ. Halachipedia records that setting a timer before Shabbat for lights and appliances is permitted; Torah Musings notes Rabbi Moshe Feinstein’s view that using timers to run machines to do forbidden work on Shabbat is generally forbidden.

So the installation question comes second. First the congregation decides, with its rav, how the screen should behave from candle lighting to havdalah. Then we make the hardware do exactly that and nothing more: no motion sensors, no touch screens left active, no automatic updates scheduled for Shabbat afternoon.

The practical failure we see is not halachic but technical: a player whose clock drifts, so the board shows the wrong times. That is solved with a good time source and a test, not with a different screen.

“Any brand of commercial panel will do”

Cost guides tend to treat video-wall panels as interchangeable. Rockland’s own purchasing office does not: its 2019 request for quotes for a video wall delivered to Pomona named a specific LG panel bundle and mount, said the brand and item specified were required, and stated that an alternate brand would be rejected.

There are good reasons a buyer does that. Matching existing equipment, a control system that already speaks to one maker’s panels, a known warranty process and spare parts that fit all argue for sticking with a model. When a public buyer, a campus or a chain has a standard, we install to it.

When there is no standard, the choice still matters: bezel width, brightness, how the panel reports faults, and whether the same model will still be sold when a spare is needed. We put the alternatives and their trade-offs in the proposal.

“A low-voltage installer can hook up the power”

Some installation guides treat the outlet behind the wall as a minor detail. In Rockland it is licensed work. Chapter 250 of the county’s laws covers anyone in the business of installing wiring for light, heat or power, or signaling systems at 50 volts or more, and requires a licence held by a master electrician.

Our side of the job is the low-voltage part: data, video and control cabling, players, controllers and the mounting. The new circuit or outlet is run by a Rockland-licensed master electrician, written as its own line in our quote, and inspected the way the county requires.

Ask anyone offering a wall who will connect it to the house wiring, and ask to see that licence.

“The town permit is the only sign-off you need”

Guides written for other states often say the building permit is the whole story. In Rockland, new electrical work is also inspected by a county-licensed electrical inspection agency, which issues a certificate of approval and tells Orange & Rockland that the connection is authorized.

Which permits a town or village wants for the same job also varies. An inspection agency’s published coverage table lists different policies for Clarkstown, Haverstraw, Orangetown, Ramapo and Stony Point, and the Village of Spring Valley’s building code is enforced by the county.

We find out which path applies at the survey and show it in the proposal, so the electrician, the inspector and the building department are lined up before the first anchor is drilled.

“An online LED kit is all you need”

Cost guides quote LED kits in the region of $11,000 to $15,000 for a 10-by-5-foot P2.9 wall, and note that the price is hardware only. It leaves out the structure that holds the cabinets flat, the circuits that power them, the processor set-up and the calibration that makes the cabinets match.

Those exclusions are the expensive part in Rockland buildings: a block wall that needs a frame, a hall that needs a circuit from a panel at the other end of the building, a store whose landlord wants a drawing first. On our list a 2.5mm LED wall with a 110-inch-equivalent picture is $45,500 to $71,500 installed, including mounting, processor set-up and calibration; the electrician’s circuit and any custom structural frame are separate lines.

A kit you already own can still be installed, priced after the survey.

“Once it is installed, a video wall runs itself”

This claim is nearly true for a Rockland wall that was planned well, and that is the point of everything above. A wall runs itself when its schedule is built for the whole year, its player keeps time, its controller restores its layout after a power cut and its content has an owner.

It is false for a wall that was only hung. Content runs out at the end of a term, a holiday falls on a day nobody planned for, a password changes and the screen stops updating. An installer thread on Reddit puts the other half bluntly: one panel can always be replaced, but how different it looks depends on the owner’s tolerance.

That is why our handover includes the calendar for the coming year, a spare-panel recommendation, the logins in the owner’s name and a test of every schedule on a moved clock.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Moved-Clock Test

Every Rockland wall we hand over goes through the same last hour, whatever it cost. With the person who will run it standing beside us, we move the player’s clock forward to the next moment the wall is supposed to change by itself — a Friday afternoon twenty minutes before candle lighting for a shul, the first day of a new term for a campus, opening time on the first day of a holiday sale for a store — and watch. The wall should switch modes on time, with the right content, without anyone touching it. Then we move the clock to the end of that period and watch it switch back.

If anything fails, we fix the schedule and run it again, and we do not leave until it passes. Then we switch off the circuit feeding the wall and its equipment, wait, and switch it on again, to see that the panels wake, the controller restores its layout and the player resumes its schedule on its own.

The test takes less than an hour. It is written into the proposal, so a board, a rav, a registrar or a store manager can see before signing that the wall will be proved on the days it matters, not only on the day it was hung.

FAQ

Rockland County Video Wall Installation FAQ

Can the screen show the Hebrew date and the weekly parsha automatically?
+

Yes. Shul signage software works out the Hebrew date, the parsha and the day’s zmanim from the calendar and the building’s map coordinates, so nobody types them in each week. Your own minyan times, kiddush sponsors and notices are entered by the gabbai or the office, and we set it up so one person can do that from a phone. Before handover we compare a full week of displayed times with a printed luach the congregation trusts.

Should a lobby wall in a building used on Shabbat have a motion sensor that wakes it?
+

We advise against it. A motion or presence sensor changes the screen because someone walked past, and many congregations do not want any device reacting to people on Shabbat or Yom Tov. A fixed schedule set before candle lighting does the same job without that question. If an existing screen has such a sensor, we can disable it or limit it to weekdays. The congregation’s rav makes the call; we wire it the way he decides.

How should staff clean the screens without damaging them?
+

With the wall switched off and cool, using a dry microfibre cloth, and a cloth only lightly dampened with plain water for marks. Household glass cleaners, ammonia, alcohol wipes and paper towels can strip the anti-glare coating or scratch it, and liquid sprayed straight at a panel can run down inside the bezel. Direct-view LED surfaces are more delicate still and should be dusted with a soft brush, never wiped hard. We leave written cleaning instructions for the exact panels at handover.

Can the screen count down to the next minyan or to candle lighting?
+

Yes. Most shul signage software can show a countdown to the next minyan, to candle lighting or to the end of a fast, calculated from the same zmanim as the rest of the board. We set the countdown to stop and disappear at the moment it reaches zero, so it never shows a negative time or a stale clock, and we check it on a weekday by moving the player’s clock forward. Whether a countdown should still appear once Shabbat has begun is for the congregation to decide.

What stops someone outside the building from putting their own content on the wall?
+

Several layers, set up before handover. The player and controller sit on a network segment that does not accept connections from the internet, default passwords are replaced, each person who updates content gets their own account rather than a shared login, and only the content system, not the screens themselves, can publish. Remote-control features the building does not use, such as screen casting from phones, are switched off. A wall that hangs in a public lobby should never be one guessed password away from showing something else.

Why do some LED walls show lines or flicker when guests film them on a phone?
+

Because the wall and the camera are both refreshing faster than the eye can see. An LED wall switches its LEDs many times a second and a phone reads its sensor line by line; when the two do not line up, the video shows rolling bands that nobody in the room notices. Walls with a higher refresh rate and a processor set up for cameras show it far less, so tell us if simchas, speeches or performances will be filmed in front of the wall.

Can the wall display donor recognition or dedication panels?
+

Yes, and it is a practical use in a shul, a school or a college building. Rotating dedication slides carry more names than a fixed plaque, and new names go in without an engraver. We set donor slides to appear at a steady pace, never flash and stay up long enough to read. Many institutions keep a permanent plaque as well; the screen supplements it, and the content belongs to the institution.

Can you work around a school’s or a college’s exam weeks and quiet periods?
+

Yes. Drilling block and lifting panels is noisy, so loud work goes on days the building is closed or the room is free, and programming, calibration and training happen in regular hours. Send the academic calendar and testing dates with the purchase order and we schedule around them. In a dispatch or training building the duty roster sets the quiet times instead.

Does our IT person need to be on site on installation day?
+

Not all day, but at two points. At the start, to confirm which network the players and controller join, what addresses they get and what the firewall allows, so the wall can reach its content and its time server. At the end, to see the devices on their network and take over the logins. If your IT is outsourced, a thirty-minute call the week before saves a return visit.

Will a video wall interfere with our hearing loop or assistive listening system?
+

It can if it is placed carelessly. Some display power supplies and LED drivers give off electrical noise that an induction loop picks up as a hum, and a wall cable run beside the loop wire can do the same. We keep the wall’s power and data away from the loop cable and, before handover, listen through a loop receiver with the wall on and off. Infrared and radio listening systems are rarely affected.
