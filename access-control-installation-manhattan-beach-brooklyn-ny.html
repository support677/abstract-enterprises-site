The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Beach Properties Need Access Control

On a weekday in September 2026, at about two in the afternoon, police say a man walked through the front door of a residential building near West End Avenue and Shore Boulevard, picked up several packages that held three collectible sports cards valued at roughly $17,400, and left on foot. FOX 5 New York reported the theft on September 18 with photos of the suspect and a request for tips. Nobody was hurt, and the report does not say whether the door was unlocked, propped, held by someone else or simply failed to latch. It does not need to. For anyone who owns or runs a Manhattan Beach entrance, the useful fact is the one in the first line: at two in the afternoon, on a quiet street at the western gateway of the peninsula, the front door of a residential building was something a stranger could walk through.

That is the access control problem in Manhattan Beach, and it is not the same one you find in the denser neighborhoods to the north. Wikipedia describes a small peninsula bounded by Sheepshead Bay on the north, the Atlantic on the south and east, and Brighton Beach on the west, in ZIP code 11235 and Brooklyn Community District 15, with a 2020 population of 4,684. CityNeighborhoods describes the housing as mostly detached brick and stucco houses, many in Mediterranean or Colonial Revival styles, set back behind gardens, with some rows and low-rise apartment buildings from the 1920s to the 1940s and newer houses on lots where older cottages once stood. There are very few lobbies here and almost no storefronts. What there is, on house after house, is a property with more ways in than anyone has written down: a gate at the sidewalk, a front door, a side gate to the driveway or passage, a basement door, a garage, and on some lots a gate at the back of the garden.

The other half of the neighborhood is new. West End Avenue, at the western edge of the peninsula beside Brighton Beach, has collected a row of condominiums over the last fifteen years. Compass lists the Manhattan Beach Condominium at 129 West End Avenue as a six-story building of ten units built in 2010, with an elevator, assigned parking, a video intercom and video security. CityRealty lists 35 West End Avenue as a six-story, 24-unit condo built in 2013 with a full-time doorman, private garage parking for residents, a fitness room, a bike room and a rooftop recreation area. Apartments.com lists The Wave at 102 West End Avenue as a seven-story, 48-unit building from 2016 with a doorman, a fitness center and a roof terrace. CityRealty lists 179 West End Avenue as a seven-story, ten-apartment condo opened in 2021, with an elevator, on-site parking and a rooftop terrace. And StreetEasy lists The Bayview Condominium at 15 West End Avenue as an eight-story, 39-unit building finished in 2023, with a garage, a gym, a bike room and a roof deck, and a doorman described in a single word: "Virtual."

Those two halves need different things from a door system, but they share a habit that makes access control worth doing. Houses here are large, many have been enlarged or rebuilt, and over the years the people who held keys have included builders, landscapers, caregivers, cleaners, family members home for the summer and whoever had the garage remote before the current owner. Condos here are new, small, often self-managed and full of secondary doors, and the credentials handed out during construction and sales rarely all come back. In both cases the owner, the board or the congregation cannot say with confidence who can open which door tonight. Access control is how you get that answer back, keep it current, and prove, door by door, that each entrance shut behind the last person through it.

The institutions along West End Avenue add a third reason. The Manhattan Beach Jewish Center at 60 West End Avenue, listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2015, and the congregations, kollels and schools nearby run on calendars that do not look like an office's: early mornings, late study hours, events that fill a social hall for one evening, and long stretches when nobody is in the building. In September 2026 New York State announced a record $70 million in Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes grants to 290 nonprofit organizations, with access controls named among the eligible improvements. For a congregation here, the question is no longer whether doors can be controlled. It is which doors, for whom, at what hours, and how the plan holds up when a grant reviewer asks why each device is there.

What the materials mean for the install

The brick houses are solid where you would expect a hollow wall, so the route matters more than the wall. Cable from a front door usually drops through the floor of the vestibule into the basement rather than through the masonry, and a reader goes on the wood or steel frame of the door, not into the brick. A gate at the sidewalk is a longer job: the cable has to cross the front garden, which means a buried conduit from the gate pier to the house, laid under the path or along the edge of a planting bed, with pull boxes where it turns. On a garden that has been landscaped for decades we plan that route with the owner, because the cheapest line on paper often goes straight through someone's roses.

Stucco is the material to respect. Behind the finish there is usually masonry or a frame wall with a weather barrier, and any hole that is not sealed and flashed lets water in behind the stucco, where it does its damage out of sight. We avoid fixing devices through stucco at all; a station goes on the door casing, on the face of a masonry pier, or on a mounting block that is sealed on all sides and fixed through a joint rather than the field of the wall. Where a wire has to come through the wall, it enters at a sleeve that slopes outward and is sealed both sides. Heavy arched wood doors on these houses usually have solid jambs, which is good news for an electric strike.

The enlarged and rebuilt houses are mostly frame and masonry of recent decades, with drywall interiors, so cable runs are easier, but the doors are bigger and more varied: oversized entry doors, pivot doors, steel garage entries, sliding glass to the garden and gates in new masonry walls. The West End Avenue condos are aluminum-and-glass at the street, with hollow narrow stiles that will not take a strike in the edge; the lock goes into the frame header, the panic device or a concealed electrified lock, and the reader goes on the frame or a nearby wall. Their garages and amenity doors are steel in steel frames. The synagogue and school buildings mix all of the above, and their exit doors carry panic hardware that has to keep working exactly as it does now.

Two O’Clock on a Weekday: What the September Walk-In Says About a Lobby Door

Go back to the theft near West End Avenue and Shore Boulevard. FOX 5's account is short: on Monday, September 14, 2026, around 2 p.m., a man "walked through the front door of the building" and took multiple packages, then fled on foot, and the NYPD asked anyone who recognized him to call Crime Stoppers. The station's text is slightly inconsistent about whether the $17,400 figure covers the packages or the three cards inside them, and it does not describe the door. We do not know how that door was set, and we are not going to guess. What we can do is list the ways a residential street door in a building like the ones on West End Avenue ends up open to the sidewalk at two on a weekday afternoon, because every one of them is something an access system can either fix or expose.

The five ways a self-locking door ends up open

  • It never latched. The closer has been set soft so the door is easy to open, and on a day with wind off the bay it stops an inch short. The lock is fine; the door is not closed. This is the commonest cause we find, and it is invisible from the lobby.
  • It was left on a daytime schedule. Some buildings set the street door to stay unlocked during the hours deliveries arrive, or a contractor asks for it during a job and nobody switches it back.
  • It was held. A resident on the way out holds it for a person who seems to be coming in, or a courier props it with a box while carrying the rest.
  • It was dogged or blocked. Panic hardware on many glass doors can be "dogged" so the latch stays retracted, and once that is done the door swings freely until someone undoes it. Tape over a strike or a folded paper in the latch does the same.
  • It was released. Somebody upstairs, a remote attendant or a resident's phone app released the door for a caller who said they had a delivery.

A reader on the door, by itself, does very little about any of those. A fob reader decides who may unlock a door that is closed. It does nothing if the door is not closed, and it records nothing about a person who walks through a door that is already open. That is why the first device we add to a lobby door in a building like this is not the reader but the door position contact: a small magnetic switch in the frame that tells the controller whether the door is actually shut. Once the controller knows that, it can tell you things no key or buzzer ever could. The door opened without a credential, an exit request or a release from the station: that is recorded as a forced door. The door was open for more than the set time, say forty-five seconds: that is a held-open alert, sent to the board's phones and to the desk if there is one. The door has been unlatched since 1:52 p.m.: that shows up on the dashboard in red.

With the contact in place, the five causes stop being invisible. A closer that lets the door drift open shows up as a stream of held-open alerts on windy days, and the fix is mechanical: adjust or replace the closer, set its latch speed so the door closes softly and then snaps home, or, on a heavy door that the wind fights, fit a stronger closer with a back-check. A daytime unlock schedule shows on the schedule page with its hours, so the board can see it and decide whether it should exist at all. A dogged panic bar can be replaced with a version whose dogging is done electrically and only by schedule, or with no dogging function at all. A propped door produces an alert within a minute instead of standing open for the afternoon. And every remote release, whether by a resident's app, an indoor screen or a remote attendant, goes into the log with a time and the name of the account that pressed it.

What the log can and cannot do after a theft

Owners and boards often hope the system will name the person who took a package. It usually cannot, and it is better to know that in advance. A credential log names the people who used credentials: residents, staff and any contractor with a code. A stranger who walks through an open door, or is let in by somebody else, appears as an event rather than a name: a door opened at 2:01 with no credential, or a release from apartment 4B's indoor screen at 2:00. That is still valuable. It narrows the time to the minute, which tells whoever reviews the camera recording where to look, and it shows whether the door was open, forced or released, which is the first question the police and the managing agent will ask. Pairing the door controller's events with the lobby camera's timeline, so that clicking an event opens the matching seconds of video, is one of the most useful things a small building can buy.

The log also needs a privacy rule from day one. In a condo or co-op with renters, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to smart access in the building, and in any building residents will ask who can look at their comings and goings. We set the system so ordinary entries are kept for a short period, held-open and forced-door events are kept longer, and only named administrators can search the log. When something happens, the board exports the minutes that matter and nothing else.

Garages, Gyms and Roof Decks: The Doors Behind a West End Avenue Lobby

Read the listings for the newer condos on West End Avenue one after another and a pattern appears. The street door is only the first of many. 35 West End Avenue advertises private garage parking, a fitness room, a bike room and a rooftop recreation area. The Bayview at 15 West End Avenue lists a garage, a gym, a bike room, a roof deck, laundry in the building and a hot tub. Every one of those amenities has a door, and each door has a different answer to the question "who should open this, and when?"

In a building where the lobby is the only controlled door, all of those questions get the same answer: anyone who got past the lobby. That is a reasonable starting point for a ten-unit building, but it starts to fail once there are more doors than people who can keep an eye on them. A garage with a gate operator is an entrance in its own right, often busier than the lobby, and it opens onto a door into the building that nobody at the desk can see. A gym that is open to every resident at every hour is also open to every resident's guest and every contractor who has a fob for the week. A roof deck is a pleasure until it becomes a late-night party nobody approved. A bike room is a room full of expensive, portable property behind a door that was propped for a delivery.

The roof needs one word of caution, because the law speaks to it directly. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a says roof doors in class A multiple dwellings must be self-closing but must not be self-locking, and must open from inside without a key. In practice that means the roof door can be locked against entry from the stair at the hours the board chooses, while anyone on the roof can always get back down, and we never wire a roof door so that it locks people out on the roof. On The Bayview's roof, or any other in the row, the reader controls the way up; the way down is always free.

The garage is the second front door

Three of the five buildings above list a garage or on-site parking, and in our experience the garage is where small condos lose control of their entry first. Vehicle remotes are handed out with parking spaces and almost never collected when a space changes hands, a unit is sold or a tenant leaves. Most garage operators cannot tell one remote from another, so nobody knows how many still work. The fix is to put the garage on the same system as the lobby: the gate operator takes its open command from the access controller, and each owner's opening method, whether a fob at a post, a phone key, or a long-range tag on the windshield, is enrolled by name. When a space is sold, that owner's garage access ends in one step and the lobby access stays.

The door from the garage into the building deserves its own reader, too. A car entering on a valid tag can be followed down the ramp on foot by anyone who is waiting nearby, and the garage-to-building door is the second chance to stop them. It also tells the desk, if there is one, that someone has come in through the garage, which is otherwise the blind side of the lobby. Every gate operator keeps its own safety devices, the photo eyes and edges that reverse a closing gate, and our wiring never goes around them.

Doorman, part-time doorman or "Virtual"

The West End Avenue listings use three different words for the same desk. 35 West End Avenue lists a full-time doorman; The Wave lists a doorman; The Bayview lists one as "Virtual." A remote attendant service answers the door station by video and releases the door for couriers and guests, usually for a monthly fee; a staffed desk does the same in person. Neither one is an access control system. Both are better with one underneath them. The desk, real or remote, should release the street door through the system, so that each release is logged, and the system should keep running all the doors behind the lobby whether or not anyone is answering the station. Habitat Magazine's board forum caught the practical problem years ago, when a commenter asked how long a delivery driver will wait at an unanswered door before giving up after "5 or 10 seconds." A building that relies on a person answering should plan for the times nobody does: a package area couriers can reach with a time-limited code, and a station that rings several phones at once.

Behind the Garden Gate: Detached Houses With More Ways In Than Anyone Has Counted

Walk down any of the lettered streets and look at a house the way a door system has to. There is a gate in the front fence, sometimes with a mailbox beside it. A path leads to a front door, often up a few steps. A driveway runs down one side to a garage, attached or at the back, and on the other side there is a passage or a side gate. Steps go down to a basement door. At the back there is a garden, and on some lots a gate at the far end. That is five to seven openings on a single house, and on an enlarged or rebuilt house there are often more: a second entrance for an in-law floor, a door from the garage into the kitchen, a sliding door to a deck. Each of them has been opened over the years by a different set of people, and in most houses each has its own key.

The owners who call us are rarely worried about a particular threat. They are worried about the drawer: the one with a dozen keys nobody can identify, three garage remotes and a slip of paper with the code to a keypad installed by a contractor who has since retired. They have had landscapers with a gate key, a cleaner with the side-door key, a builder with a lockbox code during a two-year rebuild, a caregiver for a parent, adult children who come for the summer and grandchildren who lose things. The first job is not to choose a reader. It is to decide which openings matter, who should be able to use each of them, and then to end every old key, remote and code on the same day.

The gate or the door?

On a house set back behind a front garden, there are two places to meet a visitor, and they do different jobs. A station at the front door is cheaper and easier to wire, but by the time a visitor rings, they are already on your property, at your door, out of sight of the street. A station at the sidewalk gate keeps the conversation at the property line: the caller is seen and heard from inside the house, and the gate opens only if you release it. For many families the right answer is both: the gate carries the camera, the call button and the reader, and the front door carries a simple keypad or reader for the family. The gate is where you decide who comes in; the door is where the family lets itself in.

A gate is a harder place to install than a door. It moves in the wind, it sags on its hinges, and its latch meets a post or pier rather than a frame. A gate lock has to tolerate a gap that changes with the seasons, so we use locks made for gates, with a long throw or a keeper that allows for movement, and we fix the hinges first so the gate swings true. The gate also needs a mechanical way out from the garden side that does not depend on electricity, and a release button inside the house. The cable to the gate runs underground in conduit, and the reader on the street side is wired so that pulling it off the post exposes nothing that would open the gate; the controller that makes the decision is inside the house.

One list instead of three apps

The houses rebuilt over the last twenty years often arrive with a different product on every door: a smart deadbolt on the front door from one company, a keypad on the side door from another, a garage opener with its own app, and a video doorbell from a fourth. Each one works. None of them knows about the others, each has its own user list, and each may belong to the account of whoever set it up. The cleanest fix is one controller with every opening on it, a strike or electrified lock on each door, and one list of people: the cleaner's code opens the side door on Tuesday mornings and nothing else, the landscaper's code opens the side gate on Thursdays, the family's fobs open everything. Where an existing device is a good one, we connect it rather than replace it; where it can only be run from its own app, we say so and price the swap.

The garden that runs to the water

Learners who arrive one at a time

A study hall that stays open in the evening has a familiar door problem. The office closes, the staff go home, and the learners keep arriving one or two at a time for hours. Somebody leaves the side door on the latch so nobody has to come down and open it, and from then until the last person leaves, the building is open to the street. What the state and federal grants say about doors

On September 8, 2026, the Governor's office announced a record $70 million in Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes grants, administered by the Division of Criminal Justice Services, to 290 nonprofit community-based organizations, with organizations able to request up to $250,000 each. The announcement lists eligible "exterior or interior security improvements" including lighting, locks and alarms, fencing and barriers, access controls, shatter-resistant glass, public address systems and impact protection. The federal Nonprofit Security Grant Program, run in New York by the Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Services, works on a different basis: its FY2025 FAQ allows up to $200,000 per site and $600,000 per organization, requires every project to tie to a vulnerability identified in the site's assessment, and warns that projects not reflected there "will not be funded." The same FAQ says cost estimates are enough for the application, and that proposals or quotes "should not be sought without having an executed grant contract with DHSES."

Those rules have practical consequences for how a congregation plans its doors. First, the door plan should follow the vulnerability assessment, not the other way around: if the assessment names the side door that the study hall leaves on the latch, that door is the project, and a reader on a door nobody flagged is hard to justify. Second, the timing matters. For a federal grant, a congregation should not be collecting formal installer proposals before its contract with the state is signed, and it should keep its own records of when it asked for what. We are happy to explain hardware and typical Brooklyn pricing for budgeting at any point, and to provide a formal written proposal when the program's rules say the time has come. Third, grant-funded equipment still has to work for the building every day after the reviewers leave: battery backup, a building-owned administrator account and a service plan matter as much as the reader on the wall.

Exits stay exits

Every plan for a house of worship has to respect one rule above all: the doors that people leave by in an emergency must open from inside, at once, without a key, a code or a card. The NYC Building Code allows doors on an exit path to be electrically locked only on conditions, including a sensor on the inside that releases the door as someone approaches, release when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a manual PUSH TO EXIT button between 40 and 48 inches high within five feet of the door that keeps the lock released for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock on an exit needs release hardware in the door itself that works with one hand. Most of the time the simplest path is to leave the panic hardware the congregation already has, add an electrified version of it or an electric strike for entry from outside, and keep the exit side exactly as mechanical as it is today.

The Rules Behind a Manhattan Beach Door

An entry system in New York City touches several sets of rules at once. Which ones apply depends mostly on how many households share the building, whether apartments are rented, and whether the door is an exit. None of them make access control difficult. All of them shape how it should be set up, and most Manhattan Beach properties fall into one of three groups.

One-family and two-family houses

The houses on the lettered streets are mostly outside the city's multiple-dwelling rules. HPD's summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access. A one-family house is not covered, and in New York a two-family house is not a multiple dwelling either. That leaves the owner free to design the system as they like, but the law's principles are still good practice where anyone other than the owner's family lives in the house: say what the system records, keep only what you need, delete old entries, and never make a phone app the only way into anyone's home. The house still needs a door that opens from inside without a key, and a mechanical key at the front door as a backup, held by the family.

Condos, co-ops and apartment buildings

The West End Avenue condos and the older low-rise apartment buildings are multiple dwellings. Where they use fobs, key cards, apps or biometrics, HPD's summary of Local Law 63 says owners must get tenants' consent, give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and may not require smart access for a tenant to enter their own apartment. In a condo where some owners rent their units out, the board and the renting owners both have reasons to follow it. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a adds the door rules: in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after 1968, entrance doors must be self-closing and self-locking and kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, buildings of eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and a remote door release, and roof doors must be self-closing but not self-locking.

The words "kept locked except when an attendant is on duty" matter for the newer buildings here. A daytime unlock schedule on the street door of a condo without a staffed desk is hard to square with them, and a door that does not latch is not locked whatever the schedule says. In a building with a full-time doorman, the street door can be released by the desk while he is there, but when the desk is empty the door must lock itself again. We set lobby doors to self-lock with no daytime unlock unless a board decides otherwise in writing, and we put a door position contact on every street door so the system can show that it is actually latched.

If any apartments in an older building are rent-stabilized, Brick Underground reported in 2018 that state housing regulators had ruled that fobs must be provided free to all lawful occupants, with replacement fees for lost fobs capped, and that owners could not use a fob rollout to collect new identification. We plan the rollout in those buildings around those rulings before a single fob is ordered.

Houses of worship, schools and community buildings

Institutions are not covered by the tenant privacy law for their own doors, but the exit rules apply in full: the Building Code's conditions for electrically locked egress doors described above, release on fire alarm where the doors require it, and equipment listed for access control under UL 294. Where an institution's building includes an apartment, for a rabbi's or caretaker's family for example, that apartment's own entrance is treated as a home: it keeps a lock its residents control. The Manhattan Beach Jewish Center's National Register listing does not by itself restrict a private owner who uses no state or federal funds or permits; the New York State Historic Preservation Office's guidance says owners of listed properties are then free to alter them, subject to local zoning. A project that is paid for with a grant may bring its own review conditions, which the program will state.

Fail-safe or fail-secure

Every electric lock is one or the other, and the words are easy to mix up. As Construction Specifier puts it, "fail-safe products are unlocked when power is removed," while "fail-secure products are locked when power is removed." The same article notes that "most products provide free egress whether they are fail safe or fail secure," that "fail-secure strikes are typical for most applications, except when access is required upon fire alarm," and that fail-safe locks are used on stairwell doors that must allow re-entry. On a Manhattan Beach house, the front door and the gates are almost always fail-secure: a blackout should not leave the house open to the street, and the inside lever still lets everyone out. In a condo, the street door is usually fail-secure as well, while doors that must release on fire alarm, and stair doors that must allow re-entry, are set the way the code requires.

Who does what

New York State licenses the installation of security and fire alarm systems through the Department of State. AESS does the low-voltage side, the readers, locks, controllers, cable and programming, under New York State license #12000287431. The 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into, and the power for a gate operator, is electrical work for a licensed electrician, and we mark on the plan exactly where it is needed so your electrician can add it before installation day.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Manhattan Beach: Questions Answered

The condos on West End Avenue

Our lobby door is supposed to lock itself, yet a stranger walked into a building near ours on a weekday afternoon. How does that happen, and what would actually change it?

A self-locking door only locks when it is fully closed, and the commonest reason a street door is open in daylight is that it is not. A closer set soft for residents carrying groceries, a gust off the bay, a latch that has worn so it rides over the keeper, a panic bar dogged open for a delivery, a resident holding it for someone who looked like they belonged: any of these leaves a locked door that is not locking anything. A reader alone does not change it, because a reader only controls a door that is shut.

What changes it is a door position contact and an alert. Once the controller knows whether the door is latched, it can report every time the door is held open past a set number of seconds, every time it opens with no credential or release, and every release by a resident's phone or indoor screen. The first week of alerts usually shows the cause. If it is the closer, the fix is mechanical and inexpensive. If it is a habit, the board has the record to change it. And if it is a release, the log shows which account pressed it and when.

The ground-floor commercial space in our new building has not been leased. Should the door system wait for a tenant, or be set up for one now?

Set it up now, but leave the tenant's choices open. While the unit is empty and the walls are open, run cable to each of the unit's doors and to any shared back-of-house door it might use, leave power at each location, and create an empty partition on the building's controller with its own administrator slot. That costs little at this stage and saves opening finished walls later.

When a tenant signs, the partition is filled in: their staff, their customer-door hours, their alarm and camera connections if they want them, and the shared doors their lease allows. A tenant that insists on its own system can use the same cable and power. Either way, the building's residential doors never sit on the tenant's schedule, and the tenant never needs the board's login to add an employee.

We are a ten-unit condo with no managing agent. How many owners should be able to issue and cancel fobs, and what should each be allowed to do?

At least two, and never more than three with full control. One person holding the only administrator login is the most common way small buildings lose their own system: the owner sells, moves or falls ill, and nobody can cancel a lost fob. Two full administrators, ideally the board president and one other owner, can cover each other, and the system logs which of them made each change.

Below them, each unit can have a household role that adds and removes that household's own fobs and temporary codes inside limits the board sets, for example up to four fobs and no code valid for more than thirty days. A resident who handles packages or the garage can be given rights for just that. Write the roles into the house rules, put the list of administrators in the minutes, and review it at every annual meeting and every sale.

If someone steals my phone, can they use the phone key on it to get into our building?

Not easily, and you can stop it in a minute. Phone keys on current systems sit behind the phone's own lock, so a thief needs your passcode, face or fingerprint before the key works, and many systems also require the app to be opened or the phone unlocked at the reader. The bigger risk is a phone that was left unlocked or a key set to work from a locked screen for convenience.

The answer is the same as for a lost fob. Call or message any administrator, who suspends your phone key from their own phone or computer, and it stops working at every door at once. When you have a new phone, your key is reissued to it. Keep a fob or code as well, so losing a phone never leaves you on the sidewalk.

Houses on the lettered streets

Our house sits back behind a front garden with a low gate at the sidewalk. Should the reader go at the gate, at the front door, or both?

Put the decision at the gate and the convenience at the door. A video station with a reader on the gate pier means visitors are seen and spoken to at the property line, and the gate opens only when someone inside releases it. The family uses a fob or code at the gate as well. At the front door, a simple keypad or reader lets the family in without keys, and the door locks itself behind them.

If the budget allows only one, start with whichever opening people actually use. On houses where the gate stands open all day and everyone walks straight to the front door, the door comes first and the gate can join the same controller later. Where the gate is already kept closed, it is the better first device. Either way we lay the conduit to the gate on the first visit if the garden is being dug anyway.

Our rebuilt house ended up with three smart locks and a garage opener, each on a different app set up by a different trade. Can one system replace them without new doors?

Usually, yes. The doors stay; what changes is the hardware in the frames and the way people are enrolled. Each door gets an electric strike or an electrified lock wired to one controller in a mechanical room, the garage operator takes its open command from the same controller, and every person is entered once with the doors and hours they need. The battery smart locks come off, or stay as the mechanical backup if the owner likes them.

Before anything is removed, we sign in to each existing app with the owner and export or write down its users, so nobody is forgotten in the move. Accounts that belong to a builder, an electrician or a former owner are closed. Where an existing device is a good one with a standard output, such as a video doorbell that can trigger a relay, we connect it rather than throw it away.

Our gate reader is a long way from the house. Could someone pull it off the post and open the gate by touching the wires together?

Not on a system we install, because the reader at the gate makes no decisions. It only reads a credential and sends it to the controller, which is inside the house; the controller decides whether to release the gate and powers the lock from inside. Pulling the reader off exposes data wires that carry no lock power, and the controller reports the tamper. Readers that talk to the controller over an encrypted, supervised connection go further, because a swapped or disconnected reader is flagged immediately.

The weak design to avoid is the all-in-one keypad that holds the relay and the lock power inside the housing on the gate itself. Those are cheap and common, and pulling one off can indeed expose the wires that release the gate. We use them only inside a property line, never on a sidewalk gate.

If someone ever forced one of us to open the door, is there a code that would open it and quietly call for help?

Many access systems support a duress code: a code that opens the door exactly as normal but also sends a silent signal. On its own, the access system can send that signal to the phones of other family members you choose. For a signal that reaches a central station, the duress event has to go to a monitored alarm panel, which then reports it as a duress or panic alarm; that is a feature of the alarm and its monitoring, not of the door.

If you want one, tell us on the visit. We set it up with each family member, usually as their normal code with one digit changed, test that the signal arrives where it should, and write down who receives it. It should be practised once and then kept private. A duress code is not a substitute for a door you can see through before opening, which is why the gate camera matters.

Our back garden ends at the fenced strip along the bay, and there is a gate in the fence. Should that gate be on the house's system?

If it is used, yes, and if it is never used, it should at least report when it opens. A gate at the end of a long garden is the opening nobody watches: it is far from the house, out of sight of the street and easy to leave unlatched after a walk. On the same controller as the house, it can have a code or fob for the family only, a contact that sends an alert when it opens or is left open, and a free mechanical exit from the garden side.

The cable is the longest run on the property, so we usually plan it with any landscaping or fence work. Where a buried run is impractical, a battery gate lock that reports to the controller wirelessly is an acceptable compromise for a gate used a few times a week. Who has a right to use the strip itself is a question for the owner and the neighbors, not for the door system.

Synagogues, kollels and halls on West End Avenue

Our kollel stays open late on weeknights, and learners arrive one at a time after the office has locked up. How do regulars get in without the side door being left on the latch?

Give each regular their own code or fob for that one door, valid during the study hall's hours, and make the door lock itself after every entry. A call button outside rings a screen or phone inside the study hall, so a visitor can be seen before anyone gets up to let them in, and the release is logged. Nobody needs to leave the door unlatched, and nobody needs a key.

The office can review in the morning whether the door was ever held open, forced or released, and by whom. If the study hall's hours change for a season or a holiday, the schedule changes once and every learner's code follows it. Learners who stop coming are removed in one step, and a regular's code never opens the office, the sanctuary or the hall.

Our front doors have an automatic opener for wheelchair users. Can the opener and a reader work together so the doors open by themselves after hours only for people we let in?

Yes, and it is a common pairing. During open hours the push plate operates the door for anyone, as it does now. After hours the controller locks the door and disables the outside push plate, and a valid credential at the reader unlocks the door and triggers the operator in one action, so a person in a wheelchair taps a fob and the door opens for them. The inside push plate and the exit hardware keep working at all times.

The operator's own safety sensors stay in charge of the door's movement, and the lock must release before the operator starts to push, which is a wiring sequence we set and test. The reader goes within reach of a seated person, beside the push plate rather than on the door, and we check the timing so the door stays open long enough to get through.

Families who are not members rent our social hall for celebrations, and caterers arrive hours early. How do we give them the hall for one evening and nothing else?

Create a credential that exists only for the booking. The caterer gets a code that opens the service entrance and the hall from the hour they arrive until the end of the evening, on that date only; the host family's guests come in through an entrance set to unlock for the reception and lock again at its end. The office, sanctuary, classrooms and study hall stay on their own lists and are never touched by the event.

Everything expires on its own, so there is no key to collect the next morning, and the log shows when the caterer came and went if there is a question about the bill or the cleanup. The office sets it up from the booking calendar in a few minutes, and a recurring caterer can be kept on file with no active access between events.

“A building with a doorman does not need access control.”

The idea is that a person at the desk sees everyone who comes in, so electronics add nothing. On West End Avenue, listings describe a full-time doorman at one building, a doorman at another and a "Virtual" one at a third.

A doorman sees the lobby. He does not see the garage-to-building door, the gym, the bike room or the roof, and he is not there every hour of every day. When he steps away to sign for a delivery or help a resident with bags, the lobby is as open as any other door.

The two work best together. The desk releases the street door through the system, so every release is logged, and the controller keeps every other door on its own rules whether the desk is staffed or not.

“A remote video attendant is the same as a person at the desk.”

A virtual doorman is a real service: a remote attendant answers the door station by video and releases the door for couriers and guests. Listings often present it as a full replacement for staff.

It is narrower than that. It answers one station, usually the street door, and it depends on someone picking up quickly; Habitat Magazine's board forum once asked how long a delivery driver will wait if nobody answers in "5 or 10 seconds." It does not watch the garage, decide which fobs still work or close a door that did not latch.

We treat the attendant as an optional layer on a building-owned system: the doors keep working, and keep reporting, if the service is ever dropped.

“Electric locks can trap people inside in a fire.”

Construction Specifier notes that "most products provide free egress whether they are fail safe or fail secure," and the NYC Building Code sets conditions for electrically locked exit doors, including release on power failure and fire alarm and a manual PUSH TO EXIT button. FAQ

Manhattan Beach Access Control FAQ

How many digits should the codes on a Manhattan Beach house keypad be?
+

We usually set six digits for family members and workers, and never fewer than four. Longer codes are harder to guess and less likely to be worked out from worn keys, while six digits are still quick to type. We avoid birthdays, house numbers and repeated digits, give each person a different code, and lock the keypad for a short time after several wrong tries in a row. A worker's code can be shorter-lived rather than shorter, ending on the last day of the job.

How high off the ground do you mount the reader or keypad at a gate or door?
+

Within easy reach for everyone who uses it, which in practice means the operable parts sit no higher than 48 inches and no lower than 15 inches, the reach range the 2010 ADA Standards use. At a public entrance we keep to that range so a person in a wheelchair can reach it. At a house gate we also think about the family: a height a child can reach if the child should have a code, or a little higher if not, and a call button where a visitor will look for it.

After a fob is tapped, how long does the door stay unlocked, and can one person get longer?
+

Usually a few seconds, long enough to open the door, and the lock re-engages as soon as the door closes. The time is set per door. Many controllers can also give a longer unlock time to particular people, for example a resident who uses a walker or a family member who pushes a stroller through a gate, without slowing the door for everyone else. Where an automatic operator is fitted, the open time is set on the operator as well.

Can a code be set to work only once, for a single delivery or a repair visit?
+

On most current systems, yes. A single-use code stops working after its first entry, and it can also be given an expiry time so it dies at the end of the day even if it was never used. It suits a plumber coming while you are at work or a one-off delivery to a side door. For someone who will come and go several times in a day, a time-limited code is the better tool, because a single-use code will lock them out after their first trip to the van.

Is a wired reader harder to hack than a Wi-Fi smart lock?
+

Generally, yes, when it is installed properly. A wired system keeps the decision and the lock power inside the building, uses encrypted credentials and readers, and does not depend on the home Wi-Fi for the door to work. A Wi-Fi lock is only as secure as its app, its account password and the router it talks to. Whatever you choose, use encrypted fobs rather than old 125 kHz ones, keep the controller's firmware up to date, and give every administrator a strong, separate password.

Will our homeowner's insurance cost less if we install access control?
+

That depends entirely on your insurer and policy, and we do not promise a discount. Some insurers give credits for monitored alarms rather than for door systems. What we can do is give you an itemized description of what was installed, with dates and model numbers, to send to your agent, who can tell you whether it changes anything. If a policy requires a certain kind of lock on certain doors, send us the wording and we will design around it.

What does a door position contact do, and does every controlled door need one?
+

It is a small magnetic switch in the frame that tells the controller whether the door or gate is actually shut. Without it, the system knows only that it unlocked the door, not whether the door ever closed again. With it, the system can report a door held open, a door forced, or a gate left ajar after a walk in the garden. We fit one on every exterior opening we control, because it is the device that turns a lock into a lock you can check.

Can fobs, key cards and phone keys all work on the same reader?
+

Yes, on the readers we install, as long as the credentials are of a type the reader is set up for. A family can use fobs on key rings, a caregiver a card in a wallet, and adult children phone keys, all at the same gate. Stick-on tags for the back of a phone case work too. What matters is that every credential is encrypted and enrolled by name, so any one of them can be cancelled without touching the others.

Our rebuilt house has a large pivot front door. Can it take an electric lock?
+

Often, but it has to start with the door's maker. Many pivot doors have no ordinary frame on the latch side, so there is nowhere to put an electric strike, and the lock has to be an electrified version built for that door, with power brought in through the pivot or a concealed transfer. Some manufacturers offer one and some do not. Send us the brand and model; if there is a supported option we install it, and if there is not we say so and suggest another door to control.

Does someone need to be home for the whole installation?
+

Someone should be there at the start, to agree the device locations and give access to the basement or utility room, and at the end, for the walk-through and the handover of logins. In between, a house usually needs an adult on site or nearby while doors are off their locks. For a condo, a board member or the super opens the telecom room and the roof, and residents are told in advance which door will be out of action and for how long.

Can our existing alarm arm itself when the last person locks the front door with a code?
+

Often, yes, if the alarm panel accepts an input or works with the access controller. The door system can tell the panel that the last valid exit has happened, and the alarm can arm itself after a delay, or a particular code at the door can both lock up and arm. It needs care, because an alarm that arms while someone is still upstairs is worse than none. We check the panel model, agree the rules with the family and test it before handover.
