Why Bayside Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Fitted to the Door, Not the Box

Bayside was built as a suburb inside the city, and its doors still show it. The neighborhood runs from Little Neck Bay and the Long Island Sound on the north and east to the Long Island Expressway on the south, with Francis Lewis Boulevard and Auburndale on the west and the Cross Island Parkway marking the eastern edge, and most people also count Oakland Gardens and Hollis Hills below the expressway as Bayside. Inside those lines are some of the most varied residential front doors in Queens: inter-war Tudors and Colonials with heavy wood doors set into brick, post-war Capes, ranches and split-levels, garden co-op entries around landscaped courts, condominium towers on the water, and a single main street of restaurants and shops along Bell Boulevard. A smart lock that suits one of those doors can be exactly wrong for the next, which is why our Bayside work always starts with the door rather than with a product page.

Bayside Hills shows the problem best. CityNeighborhoods.NYC describes the subdivision's detached brick and stucco Tudor, Colonial and Mediterranean-style houses as going up in earnest between 1927 and 1935, many of them with slate roofs, leaded-glass windows and decorative stonework. Those houses were built with solid doors, deep masonry reveals and hardware that was meant to last a century. Some still carry their original doors, round-topped or with a small grille at eye level; others have had a modern steel or fiberglass slab fitted into an old frame. Either way, the interesting part of a Bayside Hills job is rarely the electronics. It is how the lock's outside keypad and inside battery housing sit on a door that was never designed for them, and whether the frame, after ninety-odd years of settling, still lets a motorized bolt slide home without a fight.

The second reason Bayside homeowners call is less romantic. Through June 29, 2025, the 111th Precinct, which covers Bayside, had recorded 146 burglaries for the year, 19 more than the 127 at the same point in 2024 — an increase of 15 percent, according to the CompStat figures QNS reported in July 2025. The same article described an attempt on the evening of June 28 near 202nd Street and 36th Avenue, a few blocks west of the Clearview Expressway: two masked people went into the yard of a 65-year-old man, worked on the sliding glass door at the back of the house, failed to get in, and then rang the doorbell repeatedly until neighbors noticed the open slider and shouted them away. The details matter for anyone choosing locks. The front door was not the target; the rear of the house was. And the doorbell was being used as a test of whether anyone was home.

That pattern is not new to the precinct. Back in December 2017, Patch reported that burglaries in the 111th had doubled in a 28-day November period compared with the year before, even as overall crime fell, and officers urged residents to lock windows and doors, keep yards lit and install an alarm where they could. A smart lock is not an alarm and does not replace one. What it can do, fitted properly, is make sure the doors are actually locked when the house is empty, tell you when one is not, and give you a record of every opening — which is the part of that police advice people forget most often, on the side and rear doors they use without thinking.

Then there are the multi-family buildings. Habitat Magazine reported in 2018 that nine Bay Terrace co-op sections, built in the 1950s on land owned by Cord Meyer Development and totalling 1,800 garden apartments, were buying out or otherwise acquiring their land. The lock has to work for the shareholder or owner and for the building at the same time.

Door and frame details we check on Bayside jobs

The arch, the grille and the backset. On a period door the question is not whether a smart lock will work but where it physically fits. Many smart deadbolts have an outside escutcheon taller than the old deadbolt rose, and an inside housing taller still because it holds the batteries and motor. On a round-topped door with a speakeasy grille or a glazed panel near the lock rail, we measure from the existing bore to the grille frame, to the edge of the glass and to the stop of the arch, and we check that the inside housing clears any decorative strap or knocker on the back face. Where the old bore is in the wrong place, we would rather fit a different lock than drill a second hole in a door that cannot be replaced like for like.

Doors that move with the seasons. Solid wood doors in masonry openings swell in humid summers and shrink in dry winters. Hand-turned deadbolts tolerate that; motorized bolts do not. When the strike is even slightly off, the motor stalls, the lock reports a jam and the batteries drain. We test the throw with the door pulled shut by hand, by its own weight and by the storm door closing against it, and we deepen, move or replace the strike so the bolt clears in every season. On some doors the honest answer is a trip from a carpenter first; we say so before anything is installed.

Heat behind the glass. A full-glass storm door on a sunny exposure turns the gap in front of the main door into an oven. A storm-door supplier, Storm Door Guy, warns that full-glass panels can trap heat between the storm door and the entry door, damaging paint and warping wood doors. Electronics are less forgiving than paint. Schlage's product page for its Encode Plus Wi-Fi deadbolt, for instance, lists an operating range of minus 31°F to 150.8°F for the outside lock body and 14°F to 120.2°F for the inside body. We check the exposure, and where a door bakes, we recommend swapping the glass panel for the screen in summer, choosing a model with a wide published range, or mounting the keypad side where it is not sitting in direct sun behind glass.

Corridor doors in towers and co-ops. In a multiple dwelling the city's Housing Preservation and Development fire-safety guidance says double-cylinder locks are illegal on apartment doors, and doors to interior corridors in residential buildings are required to be self-closing. So a smart lock on a Bay Club or garden co-op unit door must open from inside without a key, and must not stop the door closing and latching by itself. We check the closer, the latch and the bolt together after installation, because a motorized deadbolt that drags can keep a self-closing door from latching.

One turn out, every time. On every residential door we touch in Bayside the inside opens with a thumb-turn or lever, without a key, a code, a phone or power. We apply that on houses as well as apartments, because a fire or a medical emergency doesn't care which kind of building it is in.

Compliance callouts for Bayside business doors

  • Leaving never needs a code. Any door that serves as an exit must open from inside by a lever, push bar or thumb-turn without a key, a code or special knowledge. An outside keypad is fine; an inside keypad or an inside key cylinder on an exit door is not.
  • Decide what happens in a blackout. Wired strikes and magnetic locks are built as fail-safe (open without power) or fail-secure (stay locked). A restaurant’s exit route and a stockroom want opposite answers, and the fire code for the space has the last word, so the choice is written on the plan for each opening.
  • Bars and restaurants at closing. A lock-up check from the app confirms the bolt, but a door-position contact confirms the door is actually shut. On late-night premises we fit both, because a bolt thrown against an open door reports “locked” to some apps.
  • Apartments above the shop. Where tenants live upstairs, the residential door carries the Housing Maintenance Code's lock requirements for multiple dwellings and, if residents are given app or fob entry, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act. Business credentials stay off the residents' door.
  • Landlord's frame, landlord's say. Cutting a strike into a leased storefront frame is an alteration. We put the property manager's written approval on the work order before any frame is cut.

Storm doors and locks without keys

A full-glass storm door covers our sunny front door all summer — can the heat trapped between the two doors hurt a smart lock?

Yes, it can, and it is one of the more common reasons a keypad on a Bayside Colonial starts misbehaving in July. Storm-door suppliers warn that full-glass storm doors trap heat against the entry door; Storm Door Guy, for example, says that heat can damage paint and warp wood doors. A lock in that space is sitting in the same heat. Manufacturers publish operating ranges for this reason: Schlage lists minus 31°F to 150.8°F for the outside body of its Encode Plus and 14°F to 120.2°F for the inside body. A south- or west-facing door behind clear glass on a summer afternoon can approach the top of ranges like those.

There are three practical answers. Swap the storm door's glass panel for its screen panel from late spring to early autumn, which many full-view storm doors are designed to allow — check yours has an interchangeable panel. Choose a lock with a wide published outside range and check it before you buy. Or, on the hottest exposures, put the keyless feature on a side or rear door that sits in shade and leave the front door on a good mechanical deadbolt.

We test the door at the walkthrough by feeling the existing hardware after the storm door has been closed in the sun for a while, and we tell you which of those answers suits your house. Batteries are the other casualty of heat; locks behind hot glass tend to need fresh cells sooner.

Would a keyless-only model with no key cylinder at all be safer on our front door, or riskier?

Both, depending on what you are worried about. Keyless-only smart deadbolts exist — Yale, for instance, sells “Key-Free” versions of its Assure Lock 2 touchscreen — and removing the cylinder removes the one part of the lock that can be picked or bumped. For a household that has lost track of how many keys are out there, that has real appeal.

The trade-off is how you get in when the electronics can't help. Key-free models rely on external emergency power, typically a 9-volt battery held to contacts on the outside, when the internal batteries die. That works, but only if somebody has a 9-volt battery when they need one, and only if the fault is the batteries rather than the electronics. There is no physical override for a failed board.

Our usual advice in Bayside is a keyed smart lock on the front door, with the key kept somewhere sensible and not hidden by the door, and a key-free model only on a second door where the first one gives you a way in. If you want a key-free lock on your only entrance, we will fit it, but we will make sure you understand the emergency-power routine first.

Doorbells and the empty-house test

The pair who tried a Bayside back slider in 2025 rang the doorbell first to see if anyone was home — can our doorbell and lock answer for us when the house is empty?

They can make the house a great deal less convincing as empty. In the attempt QNS described near 202nd Street and 36th Avenue, the doorbell was rung repeatedly before neighbors raised the alarm. A plain doorbell rung at an empty house tells the person on the step exactly what they want to know. A video doorbell answers that test differently: it alerts your phone, records the person, and lets you speak through it from wherever you are, so a ring that would have been met with silence is met with a voice.

The lock is the second half. When you see who is at the door, you also want to know that the front, side and back doors are actually locked, and to lock any that aren't without going home. A connected smart lock reports its own state to the same phone, and many lock and doorbell makers allow the two to sit in one app or one smart-home platform. We set it up so the alert, the video and the lock status are on one screen.

What neither does is stop someone who decides to break a slider anyway. That is the job of a secondary bar or pin lock on the slider, a contact sensor, and an alarm — which is why we often quote the lock, the doorbell and a slider contact together for houses in the precinct.

New owners and new ways in

The house we bought near Bell Boulevard came with the seller's keypad lock but nobody knows the master code — can it be wiped and made ours?

Usually yes, because most smart locks are designed so that a factory reset needs access to the inside of the door, which you now have and a stranger outside does not. On many models the reset is done by removing the inside cover or battery pack and holding a reset button, or by a button sequence while the batteries are reconnected; the exact procedure is in the maker's manual and differs from model to model. A reset clears every user code, fingerprint and pairing, including the old owner's.

The app account is the second step. Connected locks are usually registered to a person's account with the manufacturer, and some won't join a new account until the old owner has removed them or the maker's support team has released them. After a factory reset many will simply pair as new; when they won't, we contact the manufacturer with proof of ownership of the house. Then the mechanical side: a keypad lock still has a key cylinder, and the seller's keys still fit it, so we re-key or replace the cylinder at the same visit.

Sometimes the honest answer is that the lock is not worth keeping — an old model the maker no longer supports, an app that no longer updates, or a keypad worn smooth. We tell you that before spending your money on resetting it.

Should our family use the phone-tap “home key” feature, or stick with codes?

Use both, and don't rely on the phone alone. Several current smart deadbolts let a phone or watch act as a key by tapping it on the lock, much like a transit card; Yale's Assure Lock 2 Plus with Home Keys is one example sold in hardware stores. It is genuinely convenient for adults who always carry a phone, and it means one less code to remember.

The weak points are the ones you'd expect. Phones get lost, replaced and run flat, and a phone-key setup depends on the phone platform and the lock's own batteries both behaving. Owners have posted online about being shut out when a lock's batteries died and the phone tap couldn't help, and having to buy a 9-volt battery to power the lock from outside.

So in a Bayside family house we set phone keys for the people who want them, a personal code for everyone, fingerprints where the lock supports them, and a mechanical key kept with a trusted neighbor or relative. Every person leaves the walkthrough with at least two ways in that don't depend on the same device.

Bell Boulevard and other business doors

The last bartender leaves our Bell Boulevard place after midnight — how can I check from home that the front and back doors actually latched?

By measuring the door, not just the bolt. Most smart locks report whether their own bolt is extended. That is useful, but on a busy restaurant back door it can be misleading: a bolt can be thrown while the door is still resting against the frame, unlatched, and some locks will report that as “locked”. What you actually want to know is that the door is shut and the bolt is in the strike.

We get there with a door-position sensor — a small contact in the frame, or a lock model that has one built in — so the app shows “closed” and “locked” as two separate facts. Then we set a closing-time check: if either door is open or unlocked after the time you choose, you and one manager get a notification. Each employee gets their own code, so the log shows who locked up.

On the street door of a bar, a keypad deadbolt is often enough. On a back door that takes deliveries, a keypad with an electric strike and a push-to-exit button inside can be better, because the door locks itself the moment it closes. Hardware Notes

Door Hardware Notes for Bayside Houses

The practical detail behind the equipment list: how we fit period doors, what the grade on a box means, how keypads resist guessing, how a lock fits into police burglary advice, and where its records go.

Measuring a round-topped Bayside Hills door with a speakeasy grille

On a round-topped Tudor door the new lock usually goes where the old deadbolt was, but only after measuring three things. First, the outside: most keypad deadbolts have a taller escutcheon than a plain deadbolt rose, so we measure from the existing bore up to the bottom of the grille frame and across to any glazing, and we check that the keypad will not sit over a raised panel moulding that would stop it bedding flat. Second, the inside: the battery and motor housing is the tallest part of any smart deadbolt, and on Tudor doors it has to clear the back of the grille, any decorative strap hinge or clavos, and the inside knob or lever below it. Third, the edge: the backset of a 1930s bore is not always the 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inches modern locks expect, and the door may be thicker than the lock's listed range.

If all three work, the lock goes into the existing bore and nothing about the door's face changes except the hardware. If the outside keypad would collide with the grille, a slimmer model or a fingerprint-only deadbolt often fits where a full keypad won't. If the door is too thick, many makers sell a longer tailpiece or a thick-door kit. What we avoid is drilling a second bore through a period door; once that hole is there, the door can't go back to how it was.

One more check is specific to arched doors: the arch itself. On a round-topped door the top of the leaf swings through a curve, and on some doors the inside housing of a tall lock would sit close to the curve of the jamb when the door is open. We open the door fully with a cardboard template of the inside unit taped in place before ordering anything.

What ANSI/BHMA grades measure, and why the strike matters more

The grades come from the ANSI/BHMA A156 standards that lock makers test against. They describe durability and resistance to physical attack under set tests, not how clever the electronics are. Summaries of the standard published by hardware suppliers put it this way: a Grade 1 deadbolt must survive 250,000 cycles and 10 hammer strikes, while a Grade 3 deadbolt must survive 100,000 cycles and 2 hammer strikes, with Grade 2 in between. If a smart deadbolt has been graded, the maker usually says so on the box or the spec sheet; if it says nothing, ask before you buy.

For a house front door that sees normal family traffic, Grade 2 is a sound minimum and Grade 1 is a reasonable upgrade, particularly on the side door that gets opened dozens of times a day. But the grade is only as good as what the bolt goes into. A Grade 1 bolt in a strike held by two short screws into an old soft-wood jamb will still give way before the lock does.

So on Bayside houses we put as much attention on the strike as on the lock: long screws through the strike into the framing behind the jamb, a reinforced strike plate where the frame allows it, and a check that the bolt reaches its full throw. That is where most kicked-in doors actually fail.

Wrong-code lockouts, and where codes really leak

Guessing a code at the door is slow work on a well-made lock, because manufacturers build in lockouts. Schlage's support pages, for example, say its Encode series disables the keypad for 30 seconds after three consecutive wrong codes, sounds the lock's alarm for 30 seconds after four, and disables the keypad for a minute after each further wrong entry — and, when the lock is paired with the app, the history records that the keypad was disabled. Other makers use similar schemes.

Guessing is not where codes are usually lost. Codes leak because they are short, obvious or shared: a birth year, the house number, 1-2-3-4, or a code given to a contractor two years ago and never deleted. A keypad also shows wear and grease on the buttons that are pressed most, which narrows the guess for anyone who looks closely.

So we set codes of six digits or more where the lock allows, give each person their own code so the log is meaningful, set end dates on codes for workers and visitors, and show you how to wipe the keypad face.

Where a smart lock fits into the 111th Precinct’s advice

A smart lock turns “lock up” from a habit into something you can check. The advice Patch reported from the precinct in 2017 — lock windows and doors, keep yards lit, install a home burglary alarm if possible — is still the right list. The weak point is the first item. Families lock the front door and forget the side door; the last person out assumes the first person locked the back; the teenager leaves through the kitchen.

A smart lock contributes three specific things. Auto-lock relocks the bolt a set time after the door shuts, so a door that was opened and closed is a locked door. Lock-state alerts tell you when a door has been left unlocked or has been opened when you didn't expect it. And the activity log shows which code or phone opened which door, and when, which matters both for peace of mind and for anyone you have to talk to after an incident.

It doesn't replace the lights or the alarm. Many of the locks we fit can talk to the same smart-home hub as yard lighting and alarm sensors, so “away” can mean locked doors, lights on a schedule and the alarm armed with one tap. We explain what the house's existing alarm can and can't integrate with before suggesting anything.

Where the entry log lives, and keeping it at home

Where the history lives depends on how the lock is connected. A Wi-Fi lock, or a Bluetooth lock with a Wi-Fi bridge, typically sends its events to the manufacturer's cloud so that the app can show them anywhere, and the maker's privacy policy governs what happens to that data. A lock that is Bluetooth-only keeps its log on the lock and shows it when your phone is nearby. Locks that use Matter over Thread can be controlled locally through a Thread border router on your own network, as Yale's and Bold's support pages on Matter explain, though the companion app may still use the cloud for some features.

For most households the cloud log is the point: it is what lets you see at work that the side door opened at 3:10. If you would rather keep the history at home, we can set a lock up without the cloud bridge, or on a local smart-home hub, and explain which conveniences you lose by doing so.

Whichever route you choose, the basics are the same: two-factor authentication on the account that controls the lock, a different password from your email, and administrators limited to the adults who live in the house.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Bayside

We could not open Google's AI Overview for these searches from our build environment, so the claims below are the ones that cost guides, retailers and product pages make about smart locks — the material AI summaries are trained on and tend to repeat. Each is followed by what we see on Bayside doors.

“A smart lock installs in about half an hour with a screwdriver”

Reviewers say this, and on a modern pre-bored door it is roughly true. Bob Vila's testers, for example, describe installing smart locks with little more than a Phillips screwdriver in around half an hour. Retail boxes make the same promise.

On a Bayside Hills Tudor or a Colonial door that has had three deadbolts in eighty years, the screwdriver part is still half an hour. The measuring, the strike work and the decisions about where a keypad can sit near a grille or glass are what take time, and they are where DIY installs go wrong: a bolt that drags on a swollen frame, a second bore drilled through a period door, an inside unit that hits the jamb.

The time estimate isn't wrong; it just describes a different door. Budget for the door you actually have.

“Smart lock installation costs $250 to $400”

This is a national figure. Fixr's guide, updated in January 2025, puts smart lock installation at $250 to $400 with an average of $300, for a Wi-Fi lock and an accessory, and handyman labor at $50 to $100 an hour.

It assumes one standard door, one user and nothing to fix. A Bayside job often includes a period door with an awkward bore, a strike that needs reinforcing, a storm door, a rear door, and several people to enroll. AESS doesn't publish a single figure for a house door for that reason; we quote flat after the walkthrough.

Where we do have published figures — keypads with electric strikes, new strikes and strike repairs — they are in the pricing table below at our Queens rate, and they are not the national number.

“Any smart lock fits any standard door”

Product pages hedge this more carefully than summaries do. August's retrofit lock, for instance, mounts over an existing deadbolt inside the door and is only compatible with single-cylinder deadbolts. Most smart deadbolts list a range of door thicknesses and backsets.

Bayside's older doors fall outside those ranges more often than new-build doors do: thicker solid doors, unusual backsets, mortise hardware, doors with glass or grilles near the lock rail, and round-topped leaves that limit the size of the inside unit.

The correct claim is that most smart locks fit most standard doors. Whether yours is standard is what the walkthrough is for.

“Connect it to Wi-Fi and control it from anywhere”

True once it is connected. August's own support pages say its Connect Wi-Fi bridge should be within 10 to 15 feet of the lock and needs good Wi-Fi where it is plugged in.

Brick and stucco houses with masonry arches around the front door, routers upstairs at the back of the house, and side doors at the end of a driveway are the textbook case for a bridge that can't hear the router. Tower condos add concrete and steel.

We test signal at the bridge position, not at the router, and finish every connected install by locking and unlocking from a phone on mobile data, standing on the sidewalk.

“Keypads are weatherproof”

They are weather-resistant, to a stated rating. Yale's support page, for example, says its Smart Keypad is IP55 — dust protected and effective against water jetting — and that its lock and bridge are installed inside and not intended for outdoor use.

In Bayside the rain is the easy part. The harder conditions are the sun-baked gap behind a glass storm door, salt air on the doors closest to Little Neck Bay, and side doors that face straight into wind-driven rain with no overhang. None of those are the same as a jet of water in a test lab.

We match the model to the exposure and tell you where we think the keypad side will need replacing before the rest of the lock does.

“Smart locks are easy to hack or brute-force”

This one is usually overstated. Mainstream locks lock their keypads after a few wrong codes; Schlage, for example, documents a 30-second lockout after three wrong entries on its Encode series, with an alarm after four.

The real weaknesses are human: codes that are never deleted, admin accounts protected by an email password used everywhere else, and keys handed out years ago. And every keyed smart lock still has a mechanical cylinder that is no more or less pickable than the one on any other deadbolt.

So the security work on a Bayside install is mostly in the setup: individual codes, end dates, two-factor authentication, and a cylinder you know the history of.

“A smart lock makes the house secure”

Marketing copy implies it. Police advice doesn't. The 111th Precinct's advice, as Patch reported it, was to lock windows and doors, keep yards lit and install an alarm — a lock is one item on that list.

The Bayside attempt QNS reported in 2025 was on a rear sliding glass door. A smart deadbolt on the front door would not have been involved at all. Sliders, basement windows and side doors are where the work is.

What a smart lock adds is certainty that the hinged doors are locked and a record of who used them. We plan it as part of a whole-house approach, not instead of one.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Bayside Front Doors

How our installers approach an older Bayside door — the routine we follow on period houses, not an account of one particular visit

Before touching a tool, we open and close the door a few times and listen. A door that has to be pushed or lifted to latch will make any motorized deadbolt miserable, so the first job is to make it close sweetly with the old hardware still on.

Then the template. We tape a cardboard outline of the new lock's outside keypad and inside housing to the door, open the door through its full swing, and check the arch, the grille, any glass and the inside trim. If the template hits anything, we change the model, not the door.

Then the storm door. We close it, let the sun do its work if there is any, and touch the old deadbolt. If it is too hot to hold comfortably, the conversation turns to screens, shading or putting the keyless lock on another door.

Only after that do we take out the old lock. If the bore is the wrong size or the backset is unusual, we stop and say so before drilling anything, because on a ninety-year-old door the only mistake you can't undo is a hole.

How can we tell the bolt went all the way out and didn't stop half-thrown?
+

Most smart locks report a jam if the motor can't complete the throw, and many apps show it as an alert. At installation we check full throw by hand and by motor with the door shut, so a later jam warning means something has changed, usually swelling or a strike that has moved.
