The Neighborhood

Why New Dorp Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Block by Block

Ask someone on the South Shore where New Dorp is and you may get three different answers, and all three are right. One person means the Lane — New Dorp Lane, the old shopping street that runs from Richmond Road down past the Staten Island Railway station to Hylan Boulevard and beyond. Another means the residential streets on either side of it, bounded on the maps by Richmond and Amboy Roads, Tysens Lane, Mill Road and Bancroft Avenue. A third means New Dorp Beach, the low ground between Mill Road and Lower New York Bay that the maps sometimes list on its own but most residents count as part of the neighbourhood. Those three places have very different doors, and that is the starting point for every lock we fit here.

The Lane is a working main street. When the City Council approved the New Dorp Business Improvement District in April 2017, by a vote of 50 to 0, the district covered 146 properties and 180 businesses on New Dorp Lane and the commercial streets next to it between Hylan Boulevard and Richmond Road, most of them small and family-owned. Many of those buildings are mixed-use: a store at street level and one or two apartments above, reached by a separate street door or by a hallway that runs past the shop. Behind the storefronts there is parking, including the city's municipal field at 98 New Dorp Lane by the railway station. That combination — a glass shop front, a steel rear door onto a car park, and a residential door a few feet away — means a single building can need three different kinds of lock, three sets of people with codes, and a clear line between what the shop staff can open and what the tenants upstairs can.

The blocks inland are where most New Dorp households live. The 2020 Census counted 29,083 people in the neighbourhood tabulation area, up from 27,623 in 2010, in a mix of detached frame houses, semi-attached homes and two-families, along with rental complexes such as Tysens Park Apartments, which lists studio to two-bedroom units off Mill Road and Ebbitts Street. These are the doors that face the everyday risks. In February 2026 police in the 122nd Precinct reported three men using a crowbar to try to break into a home near Benedict Road and Circle Road, and earlier, in March 2025, the Staten Island Advance reported CompStat figures showing burglaries in the precinct up 150 percent on the year, 25 against 10, early in that year. A smart lock does nothing for a door that can be levered open at the strike, so on these blocks the frame, the strike screws and the gap at the door edge get as much attention as the keypad.

Below Hylan the problem changes again. The Department of City Planning's Resilient Neighborhoods study of the East Shore records that thousands of homes in Oakwood Beach, New Dorp Beach, Midland Beach and South Beach were severely damaged or destroyed by Hurricane Sandy, and the same report carries a photograph of a privately funded home elevation in New Dorp Beach. Under the city's flood rules, living space in a rebuilt home goes up to the design flood elevation and anything enclosed beneath it is wet-floodproofed and limited to parking, storage and building access. The planners also noted the very narrow, tall houses locals call “candlestick homes” that the rules produced on small lots. So the door you use every day may be a storey up a flight of outdoor steps, while the door at ground level opens into a space designed to flood. Lock choices, battery access and where the electronics live all follow from that.

What ties the three together is that the person paying for the lock usually knows exactly which door is the problem. A shop owner knows staff wedge the back door. A family knows which door the kids actually use. A beach-block owner knows which door was underwater in 2012. We take that knowledge as the brief, then check the door itself before talking about brands.

Three New Dorps

The Lane, the Inland Blocks and the Beach: Three Door Problems in One Neighbourhood

On the Lane: who is allowed through which door

A storefront on New Dorp Lane rarely has one door. There is the customer entrance, usually glass in an aluminium frame; a rear or side door, usually steel, that opens toward parking or a service yard; and, in the mixed-use buildings, a separate door for the apartments upstairs. The owner of the business, the owner of the building and the people living above may be three different parties with three different interests. Our job is to draw the line between them before we fit anything. Staff codes open the shop doors and only the shop doors. The tenants' street door is the landlord's and the tenants' business, and the shop never gets a code to it unless everyone agrees in writing. Where a hallway is shared, we look at whether it needs a lock at all, and if it does, whose schedule it follows.

Glass shopfronts also change the choice of hardware. A residential smart deadbolt is made for a wood or steel door with a standard bore; most narrow-stile aluminium shop doors take a mortise lock in a thin frame instead, so a keyless upgrade usually means an electric strike in the frame, a keypad or reader on the wall, and a power supply, rather than a battery lock on the door. That is the work behind our published single-door figure, and it is why we look at the frame before quoting.

On the inland blocks: the strike, the frame and the door that gets used

Between the Lane and Tysens Lane, and north toward Richmond Road and the Moravian Cemetery, the housing is mostly detached and semi-attached. These families usually come in with one of two worries: someone keeps forgetting to lock up, or someone tried to force a door. The first is a code and auto-lock question. The second is a hardware question that a smart lock alone cannot answer. A deadbolt throws into a strike plate held to a jamb, and if those screws are short and the jamb is soft, a pry bar wins whatever lock is on the door. We fit long screws into the framing, a reinforced strike where the jamb allows, and check the gap between door and frame before we talk about features.

The older streets toward Richmond Road and the gates of the Moravian Cemetery deserve a word of their own. Houses there are often older than their neighbours closer to Hylan, and their front doors may be original: heavier, thicker, sometimes with glass in the upper panel and a rim or mortise lock that has been replaced more than once. We would rather keep a good old door than swap it for a hollow modern one, so on these houses we spend the time to fill old bores, true the latch side and pick a lock whose thickness range fits the door rather than forcing the door to fit the lock.

Below Hylan: doors a storey up, doors designed to flood

In New Dorp Beach, a house rebuilt to the current flood rules has its living floor at or above the design flood elevation. The front door is at the top of an outdoor stair; the ground-level door opens into a wet-floodproofed enclosure that may hold the car, the bins and the stair up. Electronics do not belong low down in that space, and neither do lock batteries you need in an emergency. We put the smart lock on the door at living level, choose a keyed or mechanical lock for the lower door unless there is a reason to make it smart, and keep any bridge or hub upstairs with the router. Older bungalows that survived are a different case again: small, close to the ground, often with thin original doors that need checking before anything is bored.

How door details change from block to block

  • Narrow-stile aluminium shopfronts: the frame, not the door, usually takes the keyless hardware. We check stile width, the existing mortise lock and whether the landlord allows cutting before suggesting a strike.
  • Steel rear doors: many already have a closer, a panic bar or a heavy deadbolt. A keypad lever or a strike may suit better than a residential smart deadbolt, and the closer must latch the door every time for auto-relock to mean anything.
  • Older wood doors inland: check for old bores from previous locks, split stiles around the latch and soft jambs; fill and reinforce before drilling anything new.
  • Doors on semi-attached homes: confirm the jamb is fixed to framing and not just to trim, because the strike screws need something solid to bite into.
  • Doors at the top of an outdoor stair: weather from three sides, no overhang, sometimes salt in the air; choose a model and finish rated for it, and position the keypad where a hand can reach it from the top step without leaning.
  • Doors into wet-floodproofed enclosures: assume they will be wet one day. Mechanical or keyed hardware, stainless or coated fasteners, and no electronics that cannot be replaced cheaply.

Compliance callouts for New Dorp business doors

  • Getting out is never optional. The 2022 New York City Building Code (section 1010.1.9) requires egress doors to open from the inside without a key, special knowledge or effort. A keypad outside is fine; a code to leave is not. We fit a lever, push bar or exit button on the inside of every keyless business door.
  • Power loss must be planned for. Electric strikes and maglocks either stay locked or release when power fails. Which one is right depends on whether the door is an exit, and a maglock on an exit door needs release devices the code recognises; we settle that before ordering hardware.
  • Your lease may decide before the code does. On the Lane and in the Hylan centres, the storefront frame usually belongs to the landlord. We ask for written approval before cutting a strike into a frame or running cable through a facade.
  • Flood-zone storefronts. City Planning notes that the Building Code requires commercial space in the floodplain to be above the design flood elevation or dry floodproofed below it. Where a shop uses removable flood barriers at the door, the lock and keypad must not stop them being fitted in a hurry.
  • Residential doors in mixed-use buildings stay residential. Tenants' entrance rights come from housing law, not from the shop's security plan. Shop codes never open the tenants' door unless the owner and tenants have agreed it in writing.
Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in New Dorp: Questions Answered

Sorted by where in the neighbourhood the asker lives or works — the Lane, the side streets, the beach blocks, the rentals — here is what comes up on New Dorp calls, answered the way we would answer it on the doorstep.

Shops on the Lane

Staff hauling deliveries in from the lot behind us keep wedging the rear door — how do we make it easier to use shut than open?

People prop a door because getting back in is a nuisance: the key is on someone else's ring, or the door locks behind them every time they carry out the rubbish. So the fix is to make the shut door the easy option. A keypad or fob reader on the outside means nobody needs a key; a closer adjusted to swing slowly enough to walk a hand truck through, yet always latch at the end, means the door looks after itself; and a hook or shelf beside the reader means nobody puts a crate down in the doorway. We also look at why people are going out — deliveries, bins, smoke breaks — and put the reader where it is reachable with full hands. A wedge usually disappears within a week once the door is easier to use shut than open.

If someone smashes the glass in our shop's front door, does a smart lock on it still do anything?

Not much for the door itself, and it is worth being honest about that. Burglaries on Staten Island shopfronts often start with broken glass, as the Advance reported when the glass front doors of a Richmond Road restaurant in neighbouring Dongan Hills were smashed in March 2025. Once the glass is gone, a lock in the frame is bypassed. What a keyless system still gives you is the record and the alert: the strike reports that the door opened without a valid credential, the app notifies you at once, and the log shows the last legitimate lock-up. The real protection for a glass entrance is in the layers around it — a security film or laminated glass, a gate where the lease allows one, cameras and an alarm with glass-break detection. We fit the lock so it works with those, not in place of them.

Can our shop's front door unlock itself at opening time and lock again at closing?

Yes, with the right hardware. An electric strike or electrified lock on a schedule can release at your opening time so customers can push in, and relock at closing, with holiday exceptions entered in advance. The setting most owners want is “first-person-in”: the door only goes into its open-hours mode after a staff member has actually arrived and entered their code, so it does not sit unlocked on a morning when nobody turns up. We always leave a manual override so a manager can lock the door instantly during the day, and we confirm that the door still opens from inside with one movement whatever the schedule says.

Our commercial landlord wants a key to the store — do we still have to give one if we go keyless?

That depends on your lease, not on the lock. Most commercial leases on the Lane give the landlord a right of entry for repairs and emergencies, and many require you to provide a key or a means of access. Going keyless does not cancel that. In practice there are two easy ways to honour it: keep a keyed override cylinder on the lock and give the landlord that key, or give the landlord their own code or credential, which shows up separately in the log. Read the clause, or ask your attorney if it is unclear; we will set the hardware up whichever way the lease requires, and we will not remove a landlord's access without the owner's written say-so.

Can the cash office or stockroom have a code that only managers know, separate from the staff door?

Yes, and it is one of the more useful things a small business can do with keyless locks. We fit a keypad lever or smart lock on the interior door to the office or stockroom, then set up access groups: everyone on staff for the rear door, managers only for the office, owners for everything. Most systems let you limit the manager group to opening hours as well. Because each person has their own code, the log tells you who opened the office and when, which settles questions faster than a shared key ever did. Interior doors are also the cheapest to convert, because there is no weather to plan for and the door is usually standard.

Houses on the inland blocks

Police reported a crowbar attempt on a house near Benedict Road — what part of a door actually stops prying, the lock or the frame?

Mostly the frame. In the February 2026 report from the 122nd Precinct, three men tried to break into a house near Benedict Road and Circle Road with a crowbar; that kind of attack goes after the gap between door and jamb, trying to spread the frame until the bolt slips out of the strike. The lock's job is to throw a full-length bolt; the frame's job is to hold it. So we check the bolt throws its full length into the strike, replace short strike screws with long ones that reach the house framing, fit a reinforced strike plate where the jamb allows, and look at a door-edge guard on doors that open outward or have a wide gap. Those parts cost far less than the lock and make more difference.

Can the lock warn us when someone keeps trying wrong codes or forces the bolt?

Many current smart locks can. Typical features include a keypad lockout after a set number of wrong codes, a tamper or “forced entry” alert when the lock senses the bolt moved without a valid unlock, and a jam alert when the bolt could not throw. Not every model has every feature, and some only send alerts when the lock is connected through Wi-Fi or a bridge, not over Bluetooth alone. At the walkthrough we ask what you want to be told about and who should get the message, then recommend a lock that does those things on your door. Too many notifications are as bad as none, so we keep the list short.

Should the lock use my phone's location to lock up after I've walked to the train?

It can, but we usually suggest it as a backup rather than the main plan. Location-based features rely on the phone's GPS, its battery-saving settings and the app running in the background; when any of those changes, the lock may not get the message. For someone who walks from the inland blocks to the New Dorp station each morning, auto-lock on a timer — the bolt throws a set time after the door closes — is simpler and more reliable, because it happens at the door whatever the phone is doing. Location features are better used for a reminder: a notification if you are a few blocks away and the door is still unlocked. We set up both and let you decide which you trust after a week.

Should we let a voice assistant unlock the front door?

Locking by voice is convenient and safe; unlocking by voice needs care. Most major assistants that work with smart locks ask for a spoken PIN before they will unlock, and you can switch voice unlocking off entirely and keep only “lock the front door”. The risk is simple: anyone who can be heard by the speaker, including someone shouting through a window or a door, might try. In a house where a smart speaker sits near the front of the house, we generally leave voice unlocking off and use the app or keypad instead. If someone in the family relies on voice control for accessibility reasons, we set a PIN that is not used anywhere else and review it with you.

Is a fingerprint reader any harder to get past than a keypad code?

They protect against different things. A keypad code can be watched, guessed or shared; a fingerprint cannot be shared casually, which is why families with older children often like it. But fingerprint readers vary in quality, struggle with wet, cold or very dry fingers, and still need a fallback, which is usually a code or key anyway. Neither one stops someone prying the door, which is the attack actually reported on New Dorp streets. Our advice is to pick whichever your household will use correctly every day, enrol each person separately so you can remove them later, and spend the rest of the budget on the strike and frame.

Is a BHMA-graded lock worth paying more for on the front door?

It is a good shortcut for comparing locks you cannot test yourself. The Builders Hardware Manufacturers Association's residential standard for deadbolts, ANSI/BHMA A156.40, rates locks for security, durability and finish separately, with letter grades, and the finish test includes a salt-spray test. A smart deadbolt carrying that certification has been tested by an independent body rather than only by its maker. It does not make a door strong on its own, but it tells you the lock body, bolt and finish have been checked. On a New Dorp door that faces weather or gets heavy daily use, we prefer certified models and will tell you which of your shortlisted locks carry it.

The beach blocks below Hylan

Wind-blown sand gets into everything on our block — will grit jam a keyway or a keypad?

It can, slowly. Fine sand works into keyways, the gap around a deadbolt, and the edges of keypad buttons, and a motor that has to push a gritty bolt draws more battery and eventually stalls. Touchscreen keypads and sealed buttons cope better than mechanical buttons; a keyway with a shutter or a cover helps; and a dry lubricant made for locks is better than oil, which holds grit. On doors close to the beach we show you how to clear the strike pocket and keyway and suggest a quick check each spring and after a storm. If the door has no overhang, a small canopy above it does as much good as any choice of lock.

Our ground floor is wet-floodproofed storage under the house — should the inside door from that space up into the living area be locked too?

Usually yes, and it is often overlooked. On an elevated house the enclosure below is for parking, storage and building access, and its outside doors — garage door, side door, flood vents — are not built to the standard of the front door. If someone gets into that space, the door or hatch at the top of the inside stair is the last barrier before the living floor. We treat that door like an exterior door: solid, properly latched, with a deadbolt or smart lock on the living side and a thumb-turn or lever so it opens from the house with one movement. Because the lock sits at living level, its electronics stay well above the flood elevation.

Renting in New Dorp

We rent in one of the garden-apartment complexes off Mill Road — can we ask management to put a smart lock on our unit, and who pays?

You can ask, and some managers say yes, especially at turnover. The lock on your unit door is part of the owner's key system and the building's obligations, so management decides what model is fitted, keeps a means of entry for emergencies, and normally pays if it is their initiative. If you want it more than they do, a common arrangement is that you pay, the owner approves the model, and it stays with the apartment when you leave. Get the agreement in writing before anything is bored. City rules also protect tenants from being forced onto app-only entry in multiple dwellings, so a keyed option should remain available to you either way.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in New Dorp

Search summaries borrow their lines from national price guides, retailer listings and product reviews; the exact AI Overview wording was not retrievable when this page was researched, so we quote those underlying sources instead. All seven statements hold up somewhere in America. Below is how each one fares against a real door on the Lane, a side street or the beach blocks.

“Smart lock installation costs about $250 to $400”

That range comes from Fixr's national cost guide, updated in January 2025, which puts a single smart lock installation at $250 to $400 with an average of $300. It describes one Wi-Fi lock and one accessory on a typical door, priced on a national average. It is a useful number if your door is a standard, already-bored wood door that latches perfectly.

Very few of the jobs we see on the Lane fit that description. A glass shopfront usually needs an electric strike, a reader, a power supply and a free-exit device rather than a battery deadbolt, and that is a different job priced from our published single-door figure of $1,650 to $2,750 on Staten Island. A steel rear door with an old panic bar and a weak closer needs the closer sorted before any keypad goes on.

Inland, the national figure leaves out the strike and frame work that matters most after a pry-bar attempt. So we quote residential locks flat after the walkthrough, with the door work listed separately, and we label any national figure as exactly that — national and not ours.

“You can do it yourself in 30 minutes with a screwdriver”

Product reviewers regularly report fitting a retail smart deadbolt with a Phillips screwdriver in about half an hour. On a door that is already bored to the lock's specification, with a bolt that lines up with its strike, that is fair.

On the doors in this neighbourhood the screwdriver part is quick; the thirty minutes before it is where the work is. A mixed-use building's residential door may have been re-bored for three different locks over the decades. A semi-attached house's jamb may be fixed to trim rather than framing. A raised house's front door may have racked slightly as the structure settled, so the bolt drags.

None of that shows up until you open the box and the bolt does not throw. When it does not, people often force it, which strips the motor or leaves a lock that works in summer and jams in winter. Checking first is cheaper than doing it twice.

“A smart lock is all a small shop needs”

Retail guides often suggest putting a consumer smart lock on a shop door to manage staff access. On a single interior door, or a solid rear door that takes a standard deadbolt, a keypad lever or smart deadbolt can be exactly right.

The front of most Lane storefronts is different: narrow aluminium stiles, a mortise lock, glass that leaves the door open to a single blow. A consumer deadbolt usually will not fit that frame, and even if an adapter makes it fit, the glass remains the weak point.

For the front door we normally recommend a strike and reader with a schedule, and for the rear door a keypad lever with a door-position sensor. Once a business has more than two doors or more than a handful of staff, the step up to proper access control is worth discussing, and we will say so rather than stretch a consumer lock beyond what it was made for.

“Auto-lock means you never have to think about the door again”

Auto-lock is the feature most buyers want, and it does remove the “did I lock it?” worry. Advice articles tend to stop there.

Auto-lock only works if the door is actually shut when the bolt throws. A rear shop door that is propped, a house door that does not quite latch in humid weather, or a door with a worn strike will have the bolt extend into empty air. Some locks report that as a jam; some report nothing at all and simply show “locked”.

That is why we fit a door-position sensor where the lock supports one, adjust the closer and strike so the door latches every time, and test auto-lock with the door slightly ajar before we leave. You should still glance at the door. The lock reduces the thinking, it does not replace it.

“When the power goes out, a smart lock stops working”

This one is usually wrong for houses and sometimes right for shops. Residential smart deadbolts run on batteries and keep working through a power cut; what stops is remote access, because the router and any bridge lose power. The keypad, fingerprint reader and the key still work at the door.

Shop hardware is different. An electric strike or maglock is mains powered through a supply, and when power fails it does what it was set to do: a fail-secure strike stays locked to the outside, a fail-safe maglock releases. Both still let people out from inside if they were fitted properly.

On the East Shore, where outages after big storms are part of local memory, we size battery backup on shop supplies to cover a short outage and talk through what the door should do in a long one. On houses, we make sure the keyed override is real and that you know where the key is.

“Flood zones don't affect door hardware”

General smart-lock advice never mentions flood rules, because most homes are nowhere near a floodplain. New Dorp Beach is. City Planning's East Shore study and the city's flood-resistant construction rules mean that a rebuilt house has its living floor at the design flood elevation and only parking, storage and building access below it.

That changes where we put things. A smart lock with a battery pack and a circuit board on a ground-level door will one day be underwater; a bridge plugged into an outlet in the enclosure will go with it. Doors and fasteners below the flood elevation should be chosen to survive being wet.

So on the beach blocks we put connected locks at living level, keep electronics upstairs, and use plain mechanical or keyed hardware below unless there is a strong reason not to. For shops in the flood zone, keypads and readers are positioned so removable flood barriers can still be installed at the door.

“A better lock is the whole security answer”

Product pages naturally focus on the lock: encryption, apps, alerts. The burglary reports from this precinct describe something more basic — a crowbar against a house door near Benedict Road, broken glass at a shop entrance on Richmond Road a few neighbourhoods north.

Against a pry bar, the strike plate, the screws and the frame decide the outcome. Against broken glass, the lock in the frame is bypassed and the alarm, cameras and glass treatment carry the load. Against a lost or copied key, a smart lock is genuinely better, because a code can be deleted in seconds.

We sell and fit smart locks because they solve real problems with keys, schedules and records. We just will not pretend they solve the problems they do not, and every New Dorp quote we write lists the door and frame work alongside the lock.

Running a Mixed-Use Block

The Lane After Hours: Keyless Doors in a Building With a Shop Below and Homes Above

The buildings along New Dorp Lane were mostly put up for the way main streets worked a lifetime ago: the shopkeeper's family often lived upstairs, everybody knew everybody, and a single key ring opened every door. Today the store and the apartments are frequently separate tenancies, sometimes with an owner who lives elsewhere. That gap is where most of our Lane conversations start.

Start by listing every door and who it belongs to

Before we talk about hardware we walk the building with the owner and write down each door: the shopfront, the shop's rear door, any side or basement door, the residential street door, any shared hallway door, each apartment's own door. Next to each we write who controls it under the lease and who needs to get through it. In a surprising number of buildings, that list exposes a door that two parties both think the other one is responsible for — usually a basement or hallway door that the shop uses for storage and the tenants use for the meters.

Keep the two systems separate

Our default is two systems that do not talk to each other: one for the business, administered by the business; one for the residential doors, administered by the building owner. A staff member who leaves loses the shop codes and never had anything else. A tenant who moves out loses the street-door code and never had a way into the shop. If the owner wants a single app for convenience, we can do that with separate access groups, but we explain what it means if that one account is ever compromised.

Think about the times nobody is there

A shop that closes at eight leaves the building to the tenants until morning. If the rear door relies on a closer and an auto-relock, test that it closes and locks on a cold night as well as a warm afternoon. If there is a shared door between the shop and the hallway, it must stay locked from the shop side overnight and open from the hallway side for escape. Alerts for a door left open should go to someone who will actually act on them at eleven at night — often the owner, not the shift manager.

Deliveries and early starts

Bakeries, cafés and delis on the Lane take deliveries before opening. A time-limited code for a supplier, valid only between, say, five and seven in the morning, lets a driver drop stock in a back room without anyone handing over a key, and the log shows when they came and went. If the delivery room has a door through to the rest of the shop, that inner door stays locked to the supplier's code.

Codes, cards or phones for staff

Shops with steady staff usually do well with codes: nothing to lose, nothing to carry. Shops with high turnover or part-timers often prefer fobs or cards, because a returned fob is a visible handover and a lost one is deleted in seconds. Phone credentials suit managers and owners who already live in the app. Many systems let you mix all three on the same door. Whatever you choose, give each person their own credential; a single shared shop code is the keyless version of a key under the mat, and it makes the log useless.

When the business changes hands

Shops on a busy street change owners. A keyless system makes the handover cleaner than a box of keys, but only if someone does it. At sale we reset the shop locks to factory settings, transfer the admin account or create a new one for the buyer, delete every staff code, and leave the residential system untouched. We give both parties a short written record of what was changed.

The Beach Blocks

Below Hylan: Locks, Flood Rules and the Houses of New Dorp Beach

New Dorp Beach has always been a little different from the rest of the neighbourhood. Its streets drop toward the bay, its oldest houses began as summer cottages, and the Cedar Grove Beach Club, founded in 1911 according to a City Limits history, kept a colony of bungalows on the shore until the city took the site in 2010. A nor'easter in December 1992 had already left 28 of those bungalows in ruins, a date residents still mark on r/statenisland. Then Sandy reshaped the whole East Shore.

What the rebuilding rules mean for doors

City Planning's East Shore report explains the framework. The 1% annual chance floodplain is split into V, Coastal A and A zones. To comply fully with Appendix G of the Building Code, a residential building elevates all living space to the design flood elevation, and any enclosed space below it is wet-floodproofed. NYC Buildings Bulletin 2022-001 limits that wet-floodproofed space to parking, storage and building access. The report notes that houses rebuilt to these rules on narrow lots often became very narrow and tall — the “candlestick homes” mentioned by residents — and recommended changes so new homes better match the existing small bungalows and cottages.

Where each kind of lock belongs on a raised house

  • Front door at living level: the smart deadbolt goes here. It is the door you use, it is above the flood elevation, and its batteries are where you can reach them.
  • Inside stair door from the enclosure: treat it as an exterior door. A deadbolt or smart lock on the living side, a solid door, and a one-movement exit.
  • Ground-level side door and garage door: mechanical or keyed locks with corrosion-resistant fasteners. If you want to know when they open, a battery door sensor is cheaper to lose than a smart lock.
  • Bridges, hubs and power supplies: upstairs, near the router, never in the enclosure.

Bungalows that survived

Not every house below Hylan was raised. Some older cottages remain close to the ground, with light frames, thin doors and screen doors in front. Before fitting anything we measure the door thickness and the space between the entry door and any screen door, and check whether the frame can hold a reinforced strike. Sometimes the honest answer is a new door first; more often a careful retrofit works, and we will say which.

Before and after a storm

If you are told to leave, take the keyed override key with you, make sure the lock has fresh batteries so remote access works once power returns, and note which codes are active in case you need to let a contractor or an adjuster in later. When you come back, check that the bolt throws freely before relying on auto-lock; doors and frames that took water can swell or shift. We are happy to come and check locks after a flood, at the standard service rate.

Before the Visit

Five Things to Gather Before We Look at Your New Dorp Doors

A walkthrough goes faster, and the written price is more accurate, when a few things are ready. None of this is required — we will work it out on site if we have to — but owners who gather it beforehand usually get a quote they can act on the same day.

1. The paperwork that decides who controls each door

For a shop, that is the lease clause on alterations and landlord access. For a flat above a store, it is your own lease or a word with the building owner. For a house, it is simply knowing whether anyone else — a tenant, a relative, a previous owner's contractor — still holds a key. Ten minutes with these documents saves a second visit.

2. A list of people and when they need in

Write down everyone who needs to open each door and roughly when: staff by shift, a cleaner on Thursdays, a supplier before opening, the family, a neighbour who checks on the house. That list becomes the code plan. On the Lane it usually splits into owners, managers and staff; at home it splits into family, regular visitors and occasional ones.

3. Photos of every door, inside and out

One photo from outside, one from inside and one of the door edge with the bolt out, plus a photo of the frame where the strike sits. 5. What has gone wrong before

If someone has tried a door, if a key went missing, if a door sticks every summer or took water in a storm, tell us. Those stories decide where the money should go: a reinforced strike, a better closer, a lock that suits a wet door, or a simple rekey of the doors you are not converting. The lock is the visible part of the job; the history of the door decides the rest of it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Read a New Dorp Door

General working practice from our installers — not a story about any particular customer or address.

On a storefront we start at the frame, not the lock. We look at the stile width, the existing mortise cylinder and the threshold, and we ask the owner whose name is on the frame. Then we go out the back and watch the rear door close on its own a few times. If it hangs open an inch, that is the first thing to fix, because no keypad will make up for a door that does not shut.

On a house inland we take the strike plate off. Short screws into soft trim tell us more about the door's real strength than any spec sheet, and long screws into the framing cost almost nothing. We push on the door edge with the bolt thrown to see how much it gives. That ten-second check is the one a pry bar makes.

On a raised house below Hylan we stand in the enclosure and look up: where the high-water line would be, where the outlets are, where the router is upstairs. Then we decide which doors get electronics and which get plain, tough hardware. Every door, whichever New Dorp it is in, must open from inside with one turn or push, and we check that ourselves before we pack up.
