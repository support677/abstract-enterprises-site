The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Long Island is a place of houses, and houses are where soundbars earn their keep. More than four out of five homes in both counties are owner-occupied, according to the Census Bureau, and most of them are single-family houses with a family room, a basement or a den, and often a fireplace that the TV ended up above. Flat-panel TVs have very little room for speakers, so dialogue that was clear on an old tube set now gets lost under music and effects. A soundbar fixes that, but only if it is placed, connected and tuned for the room it is in.

The rooms here make that harder than the showroom suggests. The post-war houses that filled Nassau and western Suffolk, Capes and ranches in Levittown, high ranches and splits a decade later, are compact, low-ceilinged and frequently expanded. Many were later opened up, so the living room now runs straight into the kitchen. Newer colonials and the custom homes on the North Shore and the East End go the other way, with two-storey great rooms, cathedral ceilings and tall stone fireplaces. A bar that sounds full in a twelve-by-nineteen-foot Levittown living room can sound small in a vaulted great room, and the up-firing drivers that make Dolby Atmos work under a flat eight-foot ceiling have little to bounce off under a sloped one.

The TV wall itself is usually the first problem. On Long Island it is very often a chimney breast of brick or stone, which means masonry anchors, no cable route inside, and a mantel that has to be worked around. Or it is an exterior wall with insulation and fire blocking, or an interior wall over a slab with nowhere for a cable to go. People call us after buying a good soundbar, putting it on the console, and finding that it sits a metre below the picture, the TV remote doesn't control it, and the cables hang down the chimney.

Then there is everything outside the living room. Finished basements are the second TV room in a large share of Long Island houses, with concrete floors, drop ceilings and bedrooms overhead. Patios, decks and pool houses get TVs and soundbars every spring, and near the South Shore bays and on the East End the air carries salt and moisture that chews through ordinary hardware. And in the summer towns, a lot of systems sit unused for months between seasons.

None of this calls for a custom home-theatre contractor. It calls for an installer who knows these houses, carries the brackets, cord covers and in-wall kits on the van, knows when to stop and call an electrician, and prices each item up front. That is what we do across Nassau and Suffolk.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Long Island's Architecture

The same soundbar goes in very differently in a slab-floored Cape, a high ranch and a cathedral-ceilinged colonial. These are the house types we see most across Nassau and Suffolk, and what each one means for where the bar goes and how the wires get there.

Levittown-Era Capes and Ranches

Compact post-war houses on concrete slabs, many with radiant heat in the floor and an attic that was once unfinished. The floor is off-limits for cable, so routes run inside the wall, through the attic over interior walls, or in a paintable cover. Living rooms are small enough that a mid-size bar fills them.

High Ranches and Split-Levels

A split entry with the main living space upstairs and a den or family room on the lower level, partly below grade, often beside the garage. The lower den is usually the better movie room; the upper living room may have a vaulted ceiling and a picture window that challenge any bar.

Centre-Hall Colonials

Two storeys, a family room off the kitchen with a fireplace, and a full basement underneath. The basement makes cable routing straightforward; the fireplace makes the TV height the main decision, and the open kitchen makes a subwoofer worth having.

New Construction and Great Rooms

Two-storey family rooms, cathedral and vaulted ceilings, wide openings to the kitchen and stairs. Up-firing Atmos drivers have nothing flat to reflect from, so height effects come from rear speakers or in-ceiling speakers instead, and the bar is chosen for width and dialogue.

North Shore Older Homes

Pre-war houses with plaster walls, deep window trim and fireplaces with original surrounds. Plaster carries a $45 surcharge for the slower, careful drilling it needs, and routing often follows existing chases rather than new holes in old walls.

South Shore and East End Shore Houses

Bay-front and ocean-side houses, many raised, with porches, outdoor showers and rooms left open all summer. Stainless hardware, outdoor-rated bars where the room is effectively outside, and a plan for the months the house is closed.

Methods and materials by wall and house type

Drywall on wood studs. The most common Long Island TV wall and the simplest: the bracket lags into studs, no surcharge applies, and the in-wall kit drops cleanly into a stud bay. We find the studs, check for pipes and wiring with a scanner before drilling, and keep the bar centred on the TV even when the studs are off-centre by using the bracket's adjustment rather than toggles in drywall.

Brick, stone and block chimney breasts. Masonry gets sleeve anchors set into solid brick or stone, never into a mortar joint, and a $65 surcharge for brick or stone ($75 for concrete or cinder block). Tile over masonry is $95 because the tile must be drilled with diamond bits to avoid cracking. Cables travel up the side wall or in a cover, never through the chimney.

Plaster. Older North Shore and village houses still have plaster on lath. It cracks if drilled carelessly, so we score, drill slowly and anchor into the framing behind. The surcharge is $45. Where the TV is heavy or the studs don't line up, a plywood backer board ($85) spreads the load and gives the soundbar bracket something solid too.

Basement ceilings and slabs. In a finished basement with a drop ceiling, cable runs above the tiles on supports, not lying on them. With a drywall ceiling, small access holes at the joists let us fish across. Basement floors are slabs, so floor routes are out; the cabinet and the TV either share a wall or are connected overhead.

Shore houses and outdoor rooms. Stainless fasteners throughout, 316 grade where hardware is exposed to salt air, drip loops on every cable so water runs off before it reaches a connection, and power only from an existing protected outdoor outlet. A new outdoor circuit is electrician's work.

Restaurants and ale houses in Riverhead, Mattituck and Greenport advertise their TVs for big games — ten screens at some, four at others, according to a northforker roundup. Seasonal crowds mean systems that are easy for staff to switch on.

The retail strip around Roosevelt Field in East Garden City, the largest mall on Long Island with 243 stores. Showrooms, salons and waiting areas use a TV and a bar for demos and background content.

The Broadhollow Road office parks in Melville and Farmingdale. Conference and training rooms need dialogue that carries to the back row of a video call, which a soundbar under the display does better than the display's own speakers.

Compliance callouts for commercial soundbar work on Long Island

  • Certificates of insurance. Landlords and property managers in plazas and office parks usually require one before a contractor can work; ours are issued free.
  • Protruding objects. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, wall-mounted objects with a leading edge between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may protrude no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. A soundbar under a TV in a corridor or waiting area is planned so it doesn't become a hazard for someone using a cane.
  • Electrical work stays with electricians. New outlets and circuits in a commercial space are done by a licensed electrician and inspected; we install the AV around them.
  • Commercial work and county licensing. Nassau County's home improvement license applies to remodeling residential homes, and the county says contractors who work only on commercial buildings don't need it — but the landlord's own contractor rules still apply.
  • Cable listing. Cable above a drop ceiling used as an air return must be plenum-rated; we use the correct listing for the space.

When a Soundbar Is the Answer

Signs a Long Island Room Has Outgrown the TV's Speakers

Nobody calls us wanting a soundbar for its own sake. They want to hear the words again. These are the situations that most often lead a Long Island household to a soundbar, and what a properly installed one changes.

  • Subtitles on for everything. Thin modern TVs aim their small speakers down or backward, and in a room with hard floors and a big window, dialogue blurs. A bar's dedicated centre channel and speech enhancement put the voices back in front.
  • The volume war. Turning it up for the dialogue and down for the action scenes is a sign of too little dynamic control. A bar with night mode and a subwoofer handling the low end keeps loud moments in proportion.
  • A new TV over the fireplace that sounds worse than the old one on the stand. Moving a TV above a mantel moves its speakers too, high and pointed at the ceiling. A bar under the screen, or on the mantel, brings the sound back down to the seats.
  • A finished basement that sounds like a cave. Concrete, drywall and a low ceiling make TV speakers boomy and harsh at once. A bar with room calibration, and a sub placed for even bass, tames it.
  • Game day in a great room. When a room opens into the kitchen and half the family is cooking, the TV's own speakers can't reach the couch. A bar with a sub and rears closes the sound around the seating area.
  • Too many remotes. A soundbar on HDMI eARC lets the TV remote control the volume, and that alone simplifies life for anyone who isn't the person who set it up.
  • A patio TV nobody can hear over the pool pump. An outdoor-rated bar under the eave does what the TV's speakers can't, without a full outdoor speaker system.

If one of these sounds familiar, a photo of the wall and the model numbers are enough for a price. If the honest answer is a pair of ceiling speakers or a receiver rather than a bar, we'll say so.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation on Long Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Long Island homeowners ask once the soundbar is out of the box and the room is in front of them. They are grouped by the decision behind them; quick questions about price, scheduling and policies are in the FAQ further down.

Great rooms, open plans and high ceilings

In a two-storey great room, what are the realistic ways to get overhead sound?

There are three, and the bar's own up-firing drivers are the weakest of them in that room. Polk Audio's Atmos guide says up-firing speakers work best with flat, rigid, reflective ceilings between 7.5 and 12 feet, and a ceiling eighteen feet up is far outside that. So the useful height effects have to come from somewhere closer to the people on the couch.

Option one is a soundbar system whose rear speakers carry their own up-firing drivers, such as Samsung's Q990 series, which puts height sound just behind the seats where the ceiling over the sofa is often a lower, flat soffit or a balcony. Option two is in-ceiling speakers mounted in any flat section of ceiling over the seating, connected through a receiver. Option three is to accept a strong front stage and good dialogue, turn the bar's height level down, and spend the money on a subwoofer instead. We'll show you what the room supports before you buy.

Our family room opens straight into the kitchen — can one soundbar carry sound across all of it?

A good bar fills the seating area. It won't fill the kitchen at the same level without being too loud on the couch, and that is the honest limit of one box at one end of a long room. In the open-plan layouts common in Long Island colonials and newer builds, sound escapes into the kitchen and up the stairwell instead of bouncing back to the listener, so bars that sounded big in the store can seem thin at home.

Three things help. First, a dedicated subwoofer, which lets the bar stop straining for bass and keeps dialogue clear at a lower volume. Second, rear speakers placed behind the seating rather than in the kitchen, so the sound field closes around the people watching. Third, if the kitchen needs its own sound, a separate speaker grouped with the bar in the manufacturer's app, which is how Sonos and similar systems are designed to work. We'll tell you which of the three your room actually needs.

With the TV mounted high on a two-storey great-room wall, where does the soundbar belong?

As low as the TV allows, and never so far below the screen that voices seem to come from the floor. When a great-room TV is mounted high, often because of a fireplace, built-ins or a window line, the instinct is to put the bar on a console far below. That separates the picture from the voices. Mounting the bar directly under the TV keeps them together, and tilting the TV slightly down does more for the viewing angle than dropping the bar.

Above roughly eight feet our high-mount charge of $75 applies, because it takes taller ladders and two people to do safely. Before anything is drilled we hold the bar at the proposed height so you can hear and see it from the couch. That two-minute test settles more arguments than any rule of thumb.

Fireplace walls and mantels

Should the soundbar sit on the mantel shelf or on the wall directly under an over-fireplace TV?

If the mantel is deep enough and sits close below the screen, the shelf is a perfectly good place, and it avoids extra holes in the chimney. The bar needs to sit right at the front edge so the mantel itself doesn't block the drivers, and if it has up-firing drivers they need a clear path to the ceiling, which a TV hanging just above can block.

When the TV is mounted well above the mantel, a bar on the shelf ends up a foot or more below the picture, and the mantel lip can throw the sound downward. In that case a bracket under the TV is better. We'll look at the gap between mantel and screen and recommend one or the other; the console price of $79 applies when the bar sits on the mantel, and $129 when it is wall-mounted.

Can the cable box and game console stay in the built-ins beside the fireplace?

Usually, and it is the tidiest way to live with an over-fireplace TV: nothing on the mantel but the bar, and every box hidden in the cabinet. The catch is the path between them. The chimney itself can't carry cable, so the HDMI has to travel through the framed wall beside it, down into the basement and back up, or along the trim in a paintable cover. A cable run of up to 25 feet is $99; a paintable cord cover is $59.

The second catch is the remote. Cabinet doors block the infrared signal most cable boxes use, so either the doors stay open, the box is controlled through HDMI-CEC from the TV remote, or a universal remote that works by radio rather than infrared is programmed for the whole system, which is $99. We test every source from the couch with the doors closed before we leave.

Does a gas insert below the TV put the soundbar at risk from heat?

It can. Bob Vila's guide puts a gas fireplace at 20,000 to 35,000 BTUs per hour and recommends mounting a TV above one only where the temperature doesn't go over 100°F. The soundbar sits closer to the fire than the TV, so it sees more of that heat. A deep mantel helps by deflecting the rising air away from the wall.

The test is simple: tape a thermometer where the bar will go, run the fire for a few hours and read it. We do a quicker reading on the day, and we'll tell you if we think the spot is too hot. Some manufacturers also decline warranty claims for heat damage, which is one more reason to check before mounting rather than after.

Subwoofers, rear speakers and finished basements

Where does the subwoofer work best in a finished-basement media room?

Usually at the front of the room, near the TV wall, and not squeezed into a corner behind the bar unless that's where it measures best. Basement rooms on Long Island tend to be long and low, with a concrete floor and often a drop ceiling. Bass builds up in corners and can boom, and a drop ceiling lets some of it escape upward into the house.

Most wireless subs that come with soundbars only need a power outlet, so we try two or three positions with you, run the bar's room calibration if it has one, and leave it where the bass is even from every seat rather than loudest from one. If the room is above a bedroom or office, a lower sub level and a night mode setting are worth more than any placement trick.

Do "wireless" rear speakers still need outlets behind the sectional?

Yes. Wireless means no speaker wire from the bar, not no power. Crutchfield's listing for Samsung's HW-Q990D says each rear speaker must be plugged into an AC outlet, and the subwoofer needs one too. The same is true of Sonos rear speakers and most other systems.

In a lot of family rooms the sectional floats in the middle of the room and the nearest outlets are on the walls behind it. Options are a short, tidy run along the baseboard to the wall outlet, stands that let the cord drop straight down, or, where the room is over a basement, power from an existing outlet routed through the floor by an electrician. Pairing the Sonos or other wireless surround set is $125.

Can wire be routed through the attic or the basement instead of across the floor?

Often, and it is one of the advantages of a single-family house. A full basement with an unfinished ceiling makes it simple to drop a cable through the floor plate behind the TV and come up behind a cabinet or a seating area. A ranch or Cape with attic access can sometimes do the same from above for a TV on an interior wall.

Each route is checked before it is promised. Exterior walls are insulated and often have fire blocking, finished basement ceilings may need a few access holes, and radiant or hot-water heat lines in the floor rule some routes out. A cable run of up to 25 feet is $99; longer or more complex runs are quoted after we look.

Long Island house types

Our house is a Levittown-era Cape on a concrete slab — how does the HDMI get from the TV to the cabinet?

Not through the floor. Levittown's houses were built on concrete slabs, and the ranch models were heated by hot-water radiant pipes in the slab, so drilling the floor is out. That leaves the walls, the attic over an interior wall, or the surface.

If the TV and the cabinet are on the same wall, an in-wall kit or a cable run inside that stud bay usually solves it. If they are on different walls, the attic over the room can work for an interior wall, and a paintable cord cover along the baseboard is the fallback. Many of these houses have been expanded and re-framed over seventy years, so we open nothing until we have found what is behind the wall.

In a high ranch, is the lower-level den or the upstairs living room the better home for the soundbar?

It depends on which room you actually watch in. In a high ranch the upper floor holds the main living space and the lower level, partly below grade, is often the den or family room next to the garage. The lower room usually wins for movies: lower ceilings suit up-firing drivers better than a cathedral living room, and bass is less likely to bother anyone sleeping.

The upstairs living room often has a vaulted ceiling and a picture window opposite the TV wall, which is harder acoustically. If the TV is upstairs for daily use and the den is for movies, a simple bar upstairs and a fuller system with a sub and rears downstairs is a common and sensible split.

Patios, pool houses and the shore

Is an indoor soundbar safe on a covered patio or in a pool house?

Not for long. Indoor soundbars are not built for damp air, condensation or driven rain. Outdoor-rated models exist: Samsung's Terrace soundbar spec sheet lists an IP55 rating against dust and rain and includes a wall-mount kit. Even an outdoor-rated bar should sit under cover and out of direct afternoon sun, because heat is the other enemy.

In a pool house that is closed up and humid for days at a time, treat it as outdoors. We mount with stainless hardware, keep connections inside a covered box, and route power from an existing protected outdoor outlet. A new outdoor outlet is electrician's work.

Does salt air on the South Shore or the East End shorten a soundbar's life?

Indoors, with the windows closed and the air conditioning on, a soundbar is fine. The damage shows up where salt air reaches hardware directly: screened porches, pool houses, garages and rooms left open all summer. Industry guidance on coastal electronics notes that salt corrodes hinges, fasteners and seams long before it reaches the electronics inside.

So the bracket and screws are the first things to fail, not the bar. For rooms near the water we use stainless fasteners, and where hardware is exposed we prefer 316 stainless, which resists chloride better than the common 304 grade. If the rest of the house is shut for the season, unplugging the bar and sub avoids storm-surge damage too.

What should happen to the system when a summer house is closed for the winter?

Second homes are a big part of the East End: a 2017 study for the Town of East Hampton found that 63 percent of its houses belonged to second-home owners. Those systems sit unused for months, then are expected to work on the first warm weekend.

Before closing, unplug the bar, sub and rear speakers, and if the house is not heated, leave them indoors rather than on a porch. In spring, plug in the bar first, then the sub and rears, and let the app check for updates before anyone tries to watch. Connections that decide whether it works

HDMI eARC is the connection to use whenever both TV and bar have it. It carries lossless and Atmos audio from every app and device on the TV to the bar, and it lets the TV remote control the bar's volume. It has to be on the TV's eARC-labelled port, with the TV's audio output set to pass the signal through, and many failed installs are simply the wrong port. HDMI ARC on older TVs carries compressed surround, including Atmos in Dolby Digital Plus from streaming apps. Optical works when HDMI won't, but it carries no Atmos and no volume control. HDMI in on the bar, where it has one, is useful for a game console when latency matters.

We test every input you use — cable box, streaming device, console, the TV's own apps — before we leave, because an eARC setup that works for Netflix and fails for the cable box is still a failed setup.

Plain-English Glossary

Soundbar Terms You Will See on a Box or a Quote

  • Channel count (5.1.2, 9.1.4, 11.1.4). The first number is the speakers around you at ear level, the second the subwoofer channels, the third the height channels. Marketing numbers sometimes count virtual channels; reviewers such as RTINGS list the channels a bar actually produces on its own.
  • Up-firing driver. A speaker angled upward to bounce sound off the ceiling for Atmos height effects. Works best with flat, reflective ceilings roughly 7.5 to 12 feet high.
  • eARC. Enhanced Audio Return Channel: the HDMI connection that sends full-quality audio, including Atmos, from the TV to the bar and lets one remote control both.
  • ARC. The older version of the same connection. Carries compressed surround, including Atmos from many streaming apps, but not lossless formats from a disc player.
  • HDMI-CEC. The control signal on HDMI that lets the TV remote turn the bar on and change its volume. Brand names include Anynet+ on Samsung and Simplink on LG.
  • Optical (Toslink). A light-based audio cable. Reliable, but no Atmos and no volume control from the TV remote.
  • Room calibration. A feature that plays test tones, listens through a microphone in the bar or your phone, and adjusts the sound to the room. Sonos calls it Trueplay; Samsung's version is SpaceFit Sound.
  • Speech or voice enhancement. A setting that lifts the frequencies of the human voice. The most useful single setting for anyone who struggles with dialogue.
  • Night mode. Compresses loud passages so late-night viewing doesn't wake the house.
  • IP rating. Ingress Protection: two digits for dust and water resistance. IP55, as on Samsung's Terrace bar, means protection from dust and from water jets, which is why outdoor bars still go under cover.
  • In-wall power kit. A code-compliant kit that carries power from an existing outlet low on the wall to a recessed plate behind the TV, without adding an outlet to the house's electrical system.
  • Backer board. A plywood panel fixed to the framing to give a bracket solid material across a span where studs don't line up.

A mantel helps; Bob Vila's guide notes that the farther it projects, the more heat it deflects. It doesn't make every fireplace safe.

Patios, Decks and Pool Houses

Outdoor Soundbar Planning for Long Island Summers

Outdoor TVs are a spring ritual on Long Island, and the sound is usually the afterthought. A TV's built-in speakers disappear outdoors, where there are no walls to reflect them and a pool filter, a breeze and a dozen conversations to compete with. An outdoor-rated soundbar under the screen is the simplest fix, and it needs the same planning as the TV.

  • Cover first. Even an IP55 bar belongs under a roof, a pergola with a solid cover or a deep eave. Rain is only part of it; summer afternoon sun on a west-facing wall heats electronics far beyond what an indoor room ever sees.
  • Power from a protected outlet. The bar and TV plug into an existing weather-protected outdoor outlet with an in-use cover. If there isn't one where the TV is going, an electrician adds it before we arrive; we route cables from it neatly and with drip loops.
  • Stainless hardware. Within reach of salt air, ordinary plated screws rust and stain the siding within a season or two. We use stainless fasteners and, where hardware is fully exposed, 316 grade.
  • Mounting surfaces. Cedar shingle, vinyl and fibre-cement siding all need to be mounted through to the framing or a backer, not to the siding alone. Brick and stone patio walls take masonry anchors and carry the $65 surcharge.
  • Signal. Wi-Fi often weakens outside the house. If the bar streams music or pairs with outdoor speakers, we check the signal at the patio before mounting and tell you if an extender or a wired connection is needed.
  • Neighbours. Sound carries across quarter-acre lots on summer evenings. Aim the bar toward the seating and away from the property line, and keep the sub, if any, close to the house wall.

Pool houses closed up and humid between uses should be treated as outdoors, with outdoor-rated equipment and the same stainless hardware. Basements and Summer Houses

Planning a Basement Media Room or a Seasonal System

Basement media rooms

The finished basement is where a lot of Long Island families actually watch films, and it rewards a little planning before the drywall goes up. If the basement is still open framing, that is the moment to run speaker wire to where rear speakers will go, a cable from the TV wall to wherever the equipment will live, and a network cable to the TV location. Wire pulled through open joists costs a fraction of wire fished through a finished ceiling later.

Basement rooms are long, low and hard-surfaced. Put the TV on a short wall so the seating faces down the length of the room, keep the main seat away from the back wall where bass piles up, and use a rug over the slab. A drop ceiling is fine for up-firing drivers if the tiles are a hard, reflective type; soft acoustic tiles absorb the overhead effect, which is exactly what Polk's Atmos guide warns about. If there are bedrooms directly above, a sub on isolation feet and a sensible night-mode habit matter more than any equipment choice.

Basements also run damp in summer. Electronics are happier with a dehumidifier running, and power strips and the sub belong off the floor in a room that has ever had water in it.

Summer houses on the East End and the shore

Closing in the fall. Unplug the soundbar, sub and rear speakers rather than leaving them on standby through the winter storms. Bring any patio or pool-house bar indoors if the house won't be heated. Note the Wi-Fi name and password, because a router replaced over the winter is the most common reason a system won't reconnect in spring.

Opening in the spring. Plug in the bar first, then the sub and rears, and let the manufacturer's app finish any updates before the first weekend guests arrive. Check that the TV is still sending audio to the bar; TV updates sometimes reset the audio output to the internal speakers. Test an outdoor bar's mounting hardware for rust before re-hanging it.

When it won't come back. A setup-only visit, $250 for the first hour in Suffolk, reconnects the system to a new router, re-pairs the sub and rears, and resets eARC after a TV replacement. Booking it for the week before you arrive avoids spending the first warm weekend on the phone with support.

Before You Call

A Short Planning Checklist for Long Island Homeowners

Five minutes with this list will get you an exact price on the first call and save a return trip.

  • The soundbar model and what came in the box. Many bars include a wall bracket; if yours did, you don't need ours. Note whether a sub and rear speakers came with it.
  • The TV model and its HDMI ports. Look for the port labelled eARC or ARC. If the TV is more than about eight years old, it may only have optical, which limits the formats the bar can play.
  • The wall. Knock on it. Drywall sounds hollow between studs; plaster sounds dull and solid; brick, stone and tile are obvious. A photo is even better.
  • Where the outlet is. An outlet directly below the TV makes in-wall concealment straightforward. If there is no outlet anywhere on that wall, an electrician may need to add one first.
  • What's below the room. A basement with an open ceiling makes routing to a cabinet easy. A slab, as in Levittown and many other post-war houses, rules floor routes out.
  • The ceiling. Flat and under about 12 feet suits up-firing Atmos drivers. Vaulted, cathedral or two-storey ceilings point toward rear speakers or in-ceiling speakers for height.
  • Where the cable box and game console live. Next to the TV, in a cabinet across the room, or in a closet? That decides whether you need a cable run.
  • Who else is in the house. Bedrooms over a basement media room or next to the family room change how we set the sub and night mode.

Room sound in plain terms

Soundbars are tuned for an average room, and Long Island rooms are rarely average. Hard surfaces — tile floors, big windows, stone fireplaces — make dialogue harsh and echoey; a rug and curtains do more for clarity than any setting. Open plans let sound escape, which is why the same bar sounds quieter at home than in the store. Low basement ceilings help up-firing drivers but can make bass boomy. Most current bars include a room calibration that listens through a microphone or a phone and adjusts; we run it with the furniture in place, because a calibration done in an empty room is wrong as soon as the sofa arrives.

Two settings matter more than any other. Speech or dialogue enhancement lifts voices without raising everything else, and it is the first thing to try if you are turning the volume up for the words and down for the explosions. Night mode compresses the loudest moments so a late film doesn't wake the house. We show you both before we leave.

Fixing Earlier Installs

Common Problems With Soundbars Someone Else Installed

A good share of our soundbar calls on Long Island are for systems that are already on the wall. These are the faults we find most often, and each one is usually a short fix.

  • Bar on the wrong HDMI port. Plugged into an ordinary HDMI input instead of the eARC port, so sound comes and goes or the TV remote can't control the volume. Moving one cable and changing two settings fixes it.
  • Bar not level with the TV. Levelled to the floor or the mantel instead of to the screen, so the eye catches the angle every evening. We re-level to the TV.
  • Bracket in drywall anchors. A bar hung on plastic anchors in drywall alone, sagging at one end. We re-anchor into studs or masonry.
  • Cables taped down the chimney. HDMI and power cords running down the brick in plain sight. A paintable cover or a route through the side wall puts them away.
  • Sub in the worst corner. Bass that booms in one seat and vanishes in another. Trying two or three positions and running room calibration evens it out.
  • Rear speakers that drop out. Usually a weak Wi-Fi signal or an outlet controlled by a wall switch. We find which and fix it.
  • Atmos that never plays. The TV set to convert everything to stereo, or the streaming device sending the wrong format. A few settings in the right order bring it back.

Most of these are handled on a setup-only visit, $225 for the first hour in Nassau and $250 in Suffolk; re-mounting a bar on the wall is quoted at the $129 wall-mount price.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Long Island Family Rooms

Mantel, chimney breast and soundbar: the checklist our technicians run on Long Island hearth walls (general practice, no single customer)

Before a drill comes out on a fireplace wall, the first thing we do is sit on the couch. Where the TV ends up above a mantel decides almost everything else: how high the bar goes, whether the mantel shelf can hold it, whether its up-firing drivers have a clear path, and whether the household will be looking up at the screen all evening. Holding the bar against the wall at two or three heights and listening from the main seat takes two minutes and avoids a second set of holes in the brick.

The second thing is the heat. A reading at the spot where the bar will sit, with the fire on if the household uses it, tells us whether the plan is sensible. If the spot is too warm, it is better to say so before mounting than to explain later why a warranty claim was refused.

The third is the cable route, which on a chimney breast is never through the chimney. We look at the side walls, the basement below and the cabinet across the room, and pick the route that leaves nothing hanging. If none of them works, a paintable cover painted to match the trim is the honest answer, and we say so up front.

What exactly is included when you install a soundbar?

Mounting or placing the soundbar, levelling it to the TV, connecting it to the TV by HDMI eARC or optical, pairing a wireless subwoofer or rear speakers if they came with the system, setting the TV to send audio to the bar and checking that the TV remote controls the volume.
