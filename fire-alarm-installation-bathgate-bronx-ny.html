The Neighborhood

Why Bathgate Buildings Need a Fire Alarm Planned Around the Next Tenant

Fire alarm installation in Bathgate is unusual for the Bronx because so much of the neighborhood was rebuilt on purpose for industry. In 1980 the city designated the blocks between Washington Avenue and Third Avenue, south of the Cross Bronx Expressway, for an industrial park on land that had been a residential district of vacant lots and empty buildings, and Bathgate Industrial Park opened in 1982 as a joint project of the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey and the New York City Economic Development Corporation. Today it covers about 20 acres on seven blocks, with eight buildings and roughly 454,000 square feet of space for light industry, distribution, offices and education. The name is older than any of it: the land west of what became Crotona Park belonged to the Bathgate family, the woods there were known as Bathgate Woods, and the park itself was nearly named Bathgate Park before it became Crotona. Claremont Parkway, the industrial park's southern edge, was mapped as Bathgate Place in 1850.

Industrial parks are built for turnover. A building that packed food for one tenant becomes a pharmacy warehouse for the next; a vocational school becomes storage; an office floor becomes a training center. Every one of those changes can alter what the NYC Building Code requires from the fire alarm, because the requirement follows occupancy, occupant load and floor area, not the shape of the building. That is the thread running through this page. When does a building need a fire alarm system in Bathgate? Most often at the moment a lease changes hands, and the owners who get hurt are the ones who find out after the lease is signed and the opening date is fixed.

The city is actively re-tenanting the park right now. In June 2025 NYCEDC published a request for proposals in the City Record offering ground leases through October 2040 on four vacant sites: a two-floor building of about 17,472 square feet at 4006 Third Avenue, a 22,220-square-foot manufacturing space with 20-foot ceilings and two loading docks at 1610 Bathgate Avenue, a former vocational school of about 39,297 square feet at 1666 Bathgate Avenue, and a former baking goods distribution center of about 33,809 square feet at 1628 Bathgate Avenue. The last two fill the block between East 172nd and East 173rd Streets, and a respondent leasing both was told it would carry the capital cost of combining them. The park sits in an M1-4 district that allows light industrial, wholesale service and storage uses. For any of those buildings, commercial building fire alarm installation is part of the capital plan, because an old school's alarm design will not match a distribution warehouse, and two buildings joined into one may need one system instead of two.

The tenants already there show the scale. The Bronx Times reported in September 2025 that Perrigo, which describes itself as the world's largest maker of private-label over-the-counter medicines, runs a plant across 1625, 1700 and 1701 Bathgate Avenue with more than 200 union employees, and that part of a two-week dispute there concerned plans to run the operation around the clock. Krinos Foods opened a 100,000-square-foot headquarters and plant at 1750 Bathgate Avenue in 2016 after buying the site from the city; when the deal was first announced, NYCEDC President Seth W. Pinsky said "this long vacant site will now be home to dozens of important jobs." Manufacturing facility fire alarm installation in plants like these is about process areas, storage racks, loading docks and shift work, and the fire alarm has to keep working as the plant changes lines.

Around the park is the other Bathgate: prewar walk-ups on Washington Avenue and Bathgate Avenue north toward 174th Street, apartment buildings facing Crotona Park West, the Claremont Consolidated NYCHA buildings on Washington Avenue, and the storefronts of Third Avenue. Bronx Community Board 3 counts Bathgate among its neighborhoods alongside Morrisania, Claremont, Crotona Park East, Melrose and Woodstock. These blocks share a fire record with Claremont next door. In January 2026 a five-alarm fire that began in a ground-floor deli on Findlay Avenue spread through a mixed-use building, and the Department of Buildings issued a full vacate order covering 32 apartments and four commercial spaces. In April 2026 a fire on the fifth floor of a mixed-use walk-up at 538 Claremont Parkway left a resident hurt and a shop owner sweeping broken glass from his storefront. News 12 reported FDNY crews fighting a building fire on Bathgate Avenue itself in July 2022, without injuries. Mixed use building fire alarm installation on these blocks is about separating a store's risk from the people asleep upstairs.

Building Specialists

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Bathgate's Building Types

Commercial fire alarm systems in Bathgate fall into five building families, and the right system for one would be wrong for the next. The NYC Building Code decides whether a fire alarm is required by occupancy group, occupant load, floor area and height: schools need one in almost every case, assembly spaces need one above an occupant threshold, factories need one above certain heights and occupant counts, larger mercantile and storage floors need one, and apartment buildings need one by height and unit count. The design professional reads the code for the specific building. Commercial fire detection systems are then chosen room by room for the hazard in each space. Here is how those rules land on Bathgate's blocks.

Industrial park buildings: one shell, many possible uses

The eight buildings of Bathgate Industrial Park are mostly one and two stories, wide, steel-framed and built for trucks. The NYCEDC offering describes 20-foot ceilings and two loading docks at 1610 Bathgate Avenue and gives past uses for the others: a vocational school, a baking goods distribution center, offices. A listing for the same campus mentions earlier tenants that included medical offices and food packaging. Each of those is a different occupancy group under the code. A storage or wholesale use might need sprinkler supervision, manual pull stations and notification; a factory with enough occupants above or below the exit level needs a full system; a school needs one regardless; a medical office has its own considerations. Commercial fire alarm system retrofit is therefore the normal starting point: the existing panel, cable and devices are surveyed, the design professional decides what can stay, and the filing describes the building as it will be used, not as it was.

The physical work suits the buildings. Open steel and high ceilings make cable routes easy but devices hard to reach, so we plan lifts, set beam detectors across open bays and keep spot detectors away from roof drains and unit heaters. Business fire detection systems for the front offices and showrooms attached to these warehouses are ordinary ceiling work. Storage facility fire alarm installation in a rack area depends on rack height and commodity, which the sprinkler design also follows, so the fire alarm designer and sprinkler designer have to read the same tenant plan.

Pharmaceutical and food manufacturing

Two of the park's best-known occupants make things you can buy in a drugstore or a supermarket. Perrigo's Bathgate Avenue plant spans three addresses, and Krinos Foods' plant and headquarters at 1750 Bathgate Avenue covers about 100,000 square feet. We do not claim to work for either company. What their presence tells an incoming tenant is that the park is built for real production: mixing rooms, packaging lines, cold rooms, palletized storage and loading docks. Industrial building fire alarm installation in that setting deals with detector contamination from powders and steam, heat from ovens and sealers, and processes that must shut down safely when the alarm operates. Industrial fire alarm systems here are usually addressable, with monitor modules on sprinkler and suppression contacts and relay modules that stop conveyors, dampers or fans as the sealed input and output matrix specifies. An industrial fire alarm contractor that has worked in occupied plants schedules device work around production rather than asking the plant to stop.

Longer operating hours matter too. When a plant moves to more shifts, the building has more people inside for more of the day, and the night shift relies on notification that can be heard over machinery. That is a reason to review audibility and strobe coverage, not a code trigger by itself; the design professional decides whether anything has to be filed.

The school campus inside a warehouse

The Bathgate Educational Campus at 1595 Bathgate Avenue is the clearest example of an industrial building changing use. According to Lehman College's Public Art in the Bronx survey, it began as a one-story, 68,000-square-foot Port Authority warehouse; John Ciardullo Associates reused the steel frame, added a second floor on piles drilled to bedrock, and opened a 140,000-square-foot campus in 2006 that fills an entire block. Building Design + Construction reported a project serving about 1,300 students, with each school given its own entrance and a dedicated stair tower and the schools sharing a ground floor with a recreation room, library, cafeteria and art and music rooms. Mott Hall Bronx High School, Validus Preparatory Academy and the Urban Assembly School for Applied Math and Science are housed there today.

Public school buildings belong to the city's school construction and facilities programs, and their fire alarm work runs through those agencies and their contractors. The lesson for a private owner nearby is the conversion itself: a warehouse became an educational occupancy, which brought full detection, manual stations, notification in every classroom, voice or tone signaling as designed, and careful treatment of the shared assembly spaces. Anyone turning a park building into a charter school space, a training center or a day program faces the same set of decisions on a smaller scale.

Walk-ups and elevator buildings around the park

North of the park and along Crotona Park West the housing is mostly prewar five- and six-story buildings, with some later infill. These are masonry buildings with plaster walls, few chases and cellars that hold the boiler and the meters. When a building-wide fire alarm is required or was installed, it typically protects the cellar, boiler room, public halls, compactor rooms and any commercial space at street level, while each apartment keeps the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms that HPD enforces. Cable runs in exposed raceway along hall ceilings, painted to match, or through old pipe chases where the design allows. Commercial smoke detector replacement in a hall painted many times over, a heat detector instead of smoke in the boiler room, and a fire alarm battery replacement in a damp cellar are typical first jobs.

The large public housing complexes to the west and south are a separate world. Bronx Community Board 3 lists Claremont Consolidated on Washington Avenue and the Butler, Morrisania and Webster/Morrisania developments of Claremont Village, which the board calls the largest assemblage of public housing in the nation. NYCHA's own design guidelines call for fire alarm systems with two dedicated lines to a central station or fire command center and elevator recall, and NYCHA work is done through NYCHA's own procurement. In 2018 City Limits reported that a Department of Investigation review at Butler Houses found 40 of 136 apartments with smoke or carbon monoxide detectors that did not work. That is the kind of record that keeps fire safety on the agenda in every Bathgate building meeting.

Field Notes: The Warehouse That Inherited Someone Else's Alarm

This is a typical call for the kind of building Bathgate has, not a story about a named address. A distributor signs a lease on a one-story industrial building that used to hold offices and a training room in the front and storage in the back. There is a fire alarm panel by the door, green power light on, so everyone assumes the alarm is done. Our technicians open the panel and pull the history: months of trouble events from the period the building sat empty, a dead battery set, a communicator still dialing a phone line that no longer exists, and smoke detectors in the old classrooms that the new floor plan turns into a pallet aisle.

The honest scope looks nothing like a reset. The tenant needs a design professional to redraw the system for storage and distribution: beam detection across the open bay instead of spot detectors on a high ceiling, sprinkler waterflow and tamper monitoring on the new risers, pull stations at the dock doors, strobes the forklift drivers can see, and a cellular communicator in place of the dead line. The old devices that fit the new layout are kept and tested; the rest come out. When the owner sees the FDNY's 20-to-24-week average beside the lease's opening date, the conversation turns to sequencing, and the filing starts the same week.

Fire Alarm Installation in Bathgate: Questions Answered

These are the questions incoming tenants, plant managers, school operators, landlords and store owners bring to a first call about a Bathgate building, grouped by who is asking. The answers are short on purpose; call (646) 490-0629 for the version that fits your address and lease.

Incoming industrial tenants

We are bidding on a vacant Bathgate Industrial Park building from the NYCEDC offer. When does the fire alarm enter the plan?

Before the bid is final. The use you propose sets the occupancy, and the occupancy decides what the fire alarm must include, so have a design professional and an installer look at the building during due diligence. A rough fire alarm scope, the existing panel's condition and the FDNY's 20-to-24-week average belong in the capital budget and the schedule you submit, not in a surprise after the ground lease is signed.

The old vocational school building on Bathgate Avenue is becoming a warehouse. Does its old school alarm carry over?

Parts of it might, but the approval does not. A school alarm was designed for classrooms, corridors and an occupant load that a warehouse will not have, and a warehouse needs things a school did not, such as detection suited to high open bays and supervision of the rack sprinklers. The design professional decides which panel, cable and devices can be reused, and the new layout is filed as the building's system going forward.

Our plant on Bathgate Avenue wants to add a second shift. Does running longer hours change anything for the fire alarm?

Usually not the filing, but it is a good moment to check the system against how the plant really runs. More people at night means notification must be heard over the machinery that runs then, strobes must be visible where the night crew works, and somebody on each shift must know how to read the panel and whom to call. If longer hours come with new equipment or a rearranged floor, the design professional checks whether the change has to be filed.

A food packaging line is moving into a park building with 20-foot ceilings. Which detectors work up there?

The design professional chooses, but the usual answers are projected beam smoke detectors across the open bay, heat detection where steam, sealers or ovens would fool smoke detectors, and air sampling in clean or cold rooms that need very early warning. Spot smoke detectors high on a warehouse ceiling are slow and hard to service, so most designs avoid them in the production area and keep them for offices and corridors.

The two buildings between 172nd and 173rd Streets are being combined. Is that one fire alarm or two?

Often one, if the buildings become a single occupancy with openings between them, but it depends on how the design professional treats the fire separations and egress. What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Bathgate

Here are seven claims we see repeated, and how they hold up on these blocks.

"If the building already has a fire alarm, a new tenant can just use it"

An existing panel is a starting point, not an approval for the new use. The FDNY approved that system for the building as it was designed and used at the time. When a vocational school becomes a warehouse, or a bakery distribution center becomes a production floor, the occupancy, the layout and the hazards all change, and the fire alarm has to be redesigned for them.

The parts that can stay are decided by the design professional after a survey. Cable in good condition and a supported panel may be reused; detectors in rooms that no longer exist, or the wrong type for the new space, come out. The new layout is filed so the approval matches what is on the walls.

Tenants who assume the old alarm is good enough find out during the fit-out inspection or, worse, after a fire. The time to ask is before the lease is signed.

"Fire alarm installation takes a few days"

The fieldwork can be short. The project is not. Drawings must be designed and sealed by a New York State PE or Registered Architect, filed with the FDNY on a TM-1, examined, corrected if there are objections, approved, installed, pre-tested and then witnessed by the FDNY. "Smoke detectors are the right detector everywhere"

Not in a plant or a warehouse. Spot smoke detectors high on a 20-foot ceiling respond slowly and are hard to test; in a bakery or packaging line, flour, steam and heat keep them in alarm; at a loading dock, truck exhaust fools them. "A fire alarm is required only in big buildings"

Size is one trigger among several. When does a building need a fire alarm system under the NYC Building Code? It depends on occupancy group, occupant load, floor area, height and number of units. Several of the park's buildings sat vacant before the 2025 offering. A panel that loses power, runs its batteries flat and drops its central station connection leaves the building unprotected.

Keeping power on, batteries tested and the communicator live between tenants costs far less than rebuilding a dead system for the next one. If a system is taken out of service on purpose, the FDNY's impairment rules apply.

Vacancy is also when copper phone lines quietly disappear. Check that the building still reports before the next tenant's walkthrough.

"Inspection is an annual box to tick"

NFPA 72, as modified by the city, sets different intervals for different tests: some functions are checked more often than once a year, many annually, some devices on longer cycles. How often should fire alarms be inspected in a Bathgate plant? As the code's tables and the building's inspection company schedule them, with records kept on the premises.

Owners who skip maintenance pay twice: once in violations, and once in a system that no longer matches the building it is supposed to protect.

FAQ

Bathgate Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Is Bathgate a separate neighborhood or part of Claremont and Morrisania?
+

Bathgate is one of the neighborhoods Bronx Community Board 3 lists alongside Morrisania, Claremont, Crotona Park East, Melrose and Woodstock, and its industrial park is often described as part of Morrisania. For a fire alarm the label does not matter: every building in it follows the same FDNY filing and acceptance process as the rest of New York City.

Does the M1-4 zoning in Bathgate Industrial Park change fire alarm rules?
+

Zoning decides which uses are allowed, such as light industry, wholesale and storage; the Building Code then decides what fire alarm each use needs. So zoning shapes the answer indirectly, by limiting which occupancies can move in, while the design professional applies the code to the specific use.

Can work start on a park building before the FDNY approves the fire alarm plans?
+

Other construction can proceed under its own permits, but fire alarm installation is done after the FDNY approves the plans, according to the FDNY's tip sheet. Rough-in that has to happen before walls close is sequenced with the design professional so nothing installed early has to be torn out.

Who holds the fire alarm records for a building leased from NYCEDC?
+

The lease decides who maintains the building systems, and the records should sit with whoever is responsible: the Letter of Approval, approved drawings, as-builts, test reports and the central station contract. Ask for copies during due diligence, because the next design professional will need them.

Do you install fire alarms inside NYCHA buildings in Claremont Village?
+

NYCHA buildings are maintained and upgraded through NYCHA's own programs and contractors. Our Bathgate work is for private owners, tenants, schools and businesses in the surrounding buildings, including the walk-ups and storefronts that share blocks with NYCHA developments.

Is your Bronx office in Bathgate?
+

No. Bronx calls are handled through our office at 460 E Fordham Rd, Bronx, NY 10458, north of Bathgate, on the direct line (646) 490-0629, and our main office is at 1282 Troy Ave in Brooklyn. Technicians come to your building, so you never need to visit an office.

Will an upgrade in a Bathgate school building happen while classes are in session?
+

Noisy work, ceiling work in classrooms and system testing are normally scheduled for after hours, weekends or breaks, and the school is told in advance when alarms will sound. The building's operator sets the rules, and a written work plan comes with the proposal.

What happens to monitoring if a park building sits vacant between tenants?
+

The approved system remains the owner's responsibility. Keep power on, keep the standby batteries tested and keep the central station account active. If the system is shut down or cannot report, the FDNY's impairment and fire watch rules apply until it is restored.

Can a Third Avenue restaurant tie its hood system into the building fire alarm?
+

Where the building has a fire alarm system, the design normally monitors the kitchen hood suppression so a discharge reports to the panel and the central station. The hood contractor and the fire alarm installer coordinate the connection, and the FDNY acceptance test proves it.

Does a Crotona Park West apartment building follow the same FDNY filing as an industrial park building?
+

Yes. Both are filed with the FDNY on a TM-1 by a design professional, installed by the certified company and electrician named on the filing, and tested in front of the FDNY Fire Alarm Inspection Unit. What differs is the design: public halls and a cellar panel in one, open bays, docks and sprinkler supervision in the other.
