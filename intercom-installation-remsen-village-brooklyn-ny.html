Why Remsen Village Properties Need Intercom Installation

That ownership pattern is the first thing an intercom has to fit. The Maid Marines neighborhood profile describes the developers' pattern here as two-story brick semi-detached homes and rowhouses, modest two-family structures and small garden apartment buildings, and it calls two-family homeownership the economic backbone of the neighborhood. A Brownstoner Building of the Day from 2013 looked at 649–677 East 91st Street, a run of detached two-family houses from 1932 by the architect Irving Kirshenblit, each with a garage in the back for every apartment. On Remsen Avenue itself, openigloo lists 300 Remsen Avenue as a two-unit, two-floor building from 1935. The newer buildings are a different scale. A two-button bell plate on a 1930s brick house and a lobby panel serving 95 homes have almost nothing in common except the job of letting the right person through the right door.

The law sorts those buildings into very different duties. Under the state's Multiple Dwelling Law, as Habitat Magazine summarizes it, buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units must have a two-way voice intercom linking the front door to each apartment. Parker Custom Security adds two points that matter here: buildings put up before 1968 fall under the same requirement once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask for it in writing, and buildings with fewer than eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement at all, although the self-closing and self-locking door requirement still applies to them. Parker also notes that New York courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service, and that failing to keep one working has been found to breach the warranty of habitability under Real Property Law §235-b. So in a Remsen Village two-family, the intercom is the owner's choice, but the street door latching every time is not. In the newer mid-rises the intercom is a legal duty that a manager has to keep up month after month, and in a small garden apartment building the answer depends on the count of apartments and the year the building went up or was converted.

Repair Calls in Remsen Village

Need Repair in Remsen Village? A Bell That Rings Downstairs but Not Up, a Release That Hums While the Door Stays Shut, or a Lobby Panel That Skips Apartments

Repair calls in Remsen Village follow the age and size of the building. In a two-family, the classic complaint is that the bell works for the owner's floor and does nothing on the tenant's. The second apartment was often wired after the house was built, sometimes by whoever happened to be handy, and its run climbs the stair hall or a closet with a splice somewhere in the middle that has loosened or corroded. The next most common call is the door release that clicks or hums at the top of the stairs while the street door stays locked. Wood frames on 1930s and 1940s brick houses shift with the seasons, the strike plate gets painted or packed with grime, and a strike that once lined up with its latch now catches just off center. A tired transformer in the basement produces the same symptom, sending enough power to make a sound but not enough to pull the keeper. In garden apartment buildings and the newer mid-rises the pattern changes: one line of apartments stops ringing, calls cut out partway, or the panel goes dark after a storm. That usually points to a single damaged run, a failed power supply or, in a networked building, a switch or setting rather than the panel at the door.

Two things are worth doing before you call. If the street door is not latching, lock it with the key or the deadbolt while the intercom is out, because an open door matters more than a silent bell. Then go to the basement and look at the transformer or power supply. If it has a light and the light is out, find the breaker or fuse that feeds it, and if it trips again after you reset it, leave it off and tell us. In a two-family, ask the tenant whether anything changed upstairs recently, such as a painted wall, a new closet shelf or a replaced light fixture near the old station, because that is often where a wire got nicked. Call (347) 934-8335 or send photos on WhatsApp. Many single faults, such as a strike that has drifted out of line, a dead transformer, a broken splice in a stair hall or a worn button, are found and fixed in one visit. Service calls and fault tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we tell you how long we expect the tracing to take before we begin. When an old system is past the point where parts are available, or a repair would cost a large share of a new system, you get both options in writing, and a working station is never thrown out just because a whole new kit would be quicker for us.

Six kinds of building produce nearly every intercom request in Remsen Village. They differ in how many doors they have, how many households share each door, how the original bell was wired, and who makes the decision: an owner who lives upstairs, an owner who lives elsewhere, or a management company. The cards name them; the notes below explain how the work changes between them.

The brick semi-detached two-family

Two-story houses joined in pairs, with a short flight of steps, a small front yard, and an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other.

The attached brick row

Houses joined along the whole block, often near-identical, with doors a few feet from the neighbors' and no open side to run cable along.

The detached two-family with garages behind

Freestanding houses like the 1932 run on East 91st Street, with a garage at the back for each apartment and a driveway beside the house.

The older two-unit on the avenue

Small buildings on Remsen Avenue and the cross avenues, some from the 1930s, closer to the sidewalk and exposed to more foot traffic.

The small garden apartment building

Low brick buildings with one front door, a handful of units and a basement where every apartment's wire meets.

The new mid-rise on Remsen Avenue

The brick semi-detached two-family: one door, two households, one basement

The semi-detached house is the building we see most. Its street door opens into a small entry or hall, with the owner's apartment on one side or one floor and a stair climbing to the tenant above. The original bell usually has one or two buttons on a small plate on the brick beside the door, with thin wire that runs through the frame, drops into the basement and climbs to the second floor through a closet or along the stair. The first question is whether that wire can be reused. We test every pair from the door to each floor, and if a pair reads clean along its whole length, a two-wire video system can often go on it with no new holes inside the house. If the upstairs run fails, the usual answer is a new cable up the same path, or up the open side of the house if the semi-detached pair leaves one wall free.

The outside of these houses is brick, and brick is forgiving if it is drilled properly. We mount into sound brick or into mortar joints, reuse the holes from the old bell plate where they line up, and size the new station to cover the marks the old one leaves. The station sits at a height that works for a visitor standing on the top step rather than on the sidewalk, and the camera is set to see a face there, not the top of a hat. Inside, the owner's station usually goes in the kitchen or the main hall, where the household actually spends its time, and the tenant's goes at the top of the stair or in the tenant's own kitchen. The release from each floor frees the street door only. Each apartment's own door stays on its own key.

The attached brick row: close doors and shared walls

On blocks of attached houses, the neighbor's door can be just a few feet away and look nearly identical to yours. That makes labeling and placement matter more than usual. A station that sits clearly on your side of the shared steps, with the house number visible above or beside it, keeps visitors from ringing the wrong family. The camera should be angled toward your own steps, not across the neighbor's door, so it shows your visitors and nobody else's comings and goings. Where two neighbors are both changing their bells at the same time, we keep the two systems entirely separate, with separate power, separate cable and separate accounts, because a shared system between two owners creates a dispute the first time one of them sells.

Inside, attached houses have no free side wall, so cable stays inside the house: through the door frame, into the basement and up the stair or a closet. The party wall is solid masonry and is not the place for a new chase. In finished houses where the owner does not want plaster opened, the best path is often the one the old bell wire already used, and if that path has been plastered over, we look for a closet, a pantry or a corner where a short, neat raceway is acceptable. We show the owner the route before drilling anything.

The detached two-family with garages behind: more doors than people expect

The detached two-families like the 1932 houses Brownstoner described on East 91st Street change the count of doors. There is a front door, often a side door along the driveway, and at the back a garage for each apartment. Families in houses like these tend to come and go by the driveway and the side door, while guests use the front. A good plan decides which entrances visitors should use and which only residents use, puts a station only where a stranger would actually ring, and keeps the others on keys. Where a household wants to know when someone is at the garage, a station or a simple call button at the garage people-door can be wired to ring only that household's floor.

Running cable to a garage at the back of the lot is the part owners worry about. Where a basement wall faces the driveway, the cable can leave the house through a sealed sleeve and run along the house wall and the fence line in an outdoor-rated raceway, or underground in conduit if the owner is already having the driveway or a walk redone. We do not cut a trench across a finished yard unless the owner asks for it, and when there is no clean path, a wireless link between the house and the garage is an honest alternative that we will price next to the wired one.

The older two-unit on the avenue: nearer the sidewalk, harder use

Small buildings directly on Remsen Avenue, Kings Highway and the other through streets get more foot traffic than the side-street houses. A local blog has called the meeting of Kings Highway, Remsen Avenue and Linden Boulevard the most pedestrian-unfriendly intersection in the city, and buildings near busy corners like that have doors that are leaned on, knocked and rained on. On these buildings we choose a metal station with a recessed camera and no protruding parts, mounted flat and tight to the brick, and we put a weather hood over it where the door has no overhang. The speaker volume is set for the street, and the microphone is placed so a resident can understand a visitor over passing traffic.

Many of these buildings, like the two-unit, two-floor building from 1935 that openigloo lists at 300 Remsen Avenue, are owned by people who do not live in them. When neither household is the owner's, the system needs to work without anyone in the building knowing how it was set up. We label the power supply and the breaker, leave a printed sheet inside the basement door with the system's model and our number, and give the owner, not a tenant, the administrator login for anything with an app.

The small garden apartment building: one front door and a basement full of pairs

Small garden apartment buildings have a single front door, a short hall or courtyard, and a handful of apartments, and the count of apartments decides what the law expects. Below eight, the building needs a self-closing, self-locking front door and no intercom by law, but tenants in a building like this almost always expect one. At eight or more, a two-way voice system is required if the building went up or was converted after 1968, and an older building comes under the same requirement once a majority of tenants ask in writing. In both cases the work looks similar: a panel at the front door with a button for each apartment, a station in each unit, and a basement where every run is tested and tagged.

The basement is where these jobs succeed or fail. In older buildings, the cellar ceiling holds decades of wire for bells, phones, cable television and old alarms, often stapled side by side with no labels. We trace each apartment's pair with tone, test it, and replace only the runs that fail. Where a new run has to cross a block partition between the boiler room and the rest of the cellar, it goes through one clean, sleeved and sealed hole. The power supply is mounted where the owner or super can reach it without going through a tenant's storage room.

The new mid-rise on Remsen Avenue: layers of entry and a hand-off from the developer

The newest buildings in the area arrive with systems already in place. The 2015 building at 106 Remsen Avenue is listed with a video intercom and a secure lobby with video surveillance, and 406 Remsen Avenue opened with gated access, an elevator and a garage. Buildings like these have several layers of entry: a vehicle gate, a garage door into the building, a pedestrian entrance, a lobby door, sometimes a package room, and the elevator. The work in these buildings is rarely about a new panel. It is about making the layers work together: making sure the lobby panel releases only the lobby door, that the garage path does not bypass the lobby, that the system keeps a record of what it released and from which apartment, and that the people who manage the building, not a departed developer or vendor, hold every login.

The other recurring task is fixing what residents dislike about a system that technically works: calls that ring too briefly, a directory nobody updated after move-in, phone forwarding that exposes residents' numbers, or households without smartphones who have no way to answer. In a building filled through a housing lottery, many households move in within a short period and the directory has to be right from the first week. We document the system, list every setting a manager can change, and recommend a short list of fixes, priced separately so the owner can choose.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Walk from the Front Step to the Basement of a Remsen Village Two-Family

Count the doors before counting the buttons. The technician starts on the sidewalk and asks where a stranger would go first. Is there a front door and a side door along the driveway? A gate in the front yard fence? A separate door under the steps? Every door a visitor might try is either given a station or made clearly not the place to ring.

Find out who answers, and from where. Which floor is the owner on, which is the tenant on, and does anyone live in the basement? Where does each household actually spend the evening: the kitchen at the back, a front room, a bedroom upstairs? Stations go where people are, not where the old handset happened to hang.

Read the frame and the latch. The door is measured, the latch and backset recorded, and the frame checked for rot, paint build-up and old strike pockets. A strike chosen from a catalog without this step is the most common reason a new release hums without opening anything.

Follow the bell wire down. In the basement, the old transformer is found, photographed and labeled, and its wire followed along the ceiling to where it turns up toward the second floor. Wires for the phone, cable television and an old alarm are separated from it and noted, so nothing gets cut by mistake.

Test every pair before trusting it. Each run from the door to each floor is tested end to end. The reading decides whether the house can take video on its existing wire or needs a new cable to the top floor.

Look at the basement ceiling for trouble. Signs of chewed insulation, water stains along a run, or a splice taped years ago are written on the survey, because they explain the faults that come back.

Cut the power and try the door. With the intercom and lock power switched off, the door is opened by hand from inside and with a key from outside, and the closer is watched to make sure the door latches on its own. The same test is done again at handover.

The Two-Family Plan

One Street Door, Two Households: How We Plan an Intercom for an Owner-Occupied Remsen Village Two-Family

Because so much of Remsen Village is owner-occupied two-families, most of our work here is a version of the same design problem: two households who share a front door but not much else. The owner wants to know who is at the door and to keep control of the building. The tenant wants to let their own visitors in without coming downstairs and without needing the owner's help. Both want packages to stop sitting on the steps. The plan below is how we resolve those wants in a building of this size.

A button and a station for each household. The outdoor station gets one button per apartment, labeled the way each household prefers, which may be the floor, an apartment letter or a family name. Each apartment gets its own indoor station, so a call for the tenant rings only upstairs and a call for the owner rings only downstairs. If the owner's family uses the basement or the yard, a second station for the owner's apartment can go there without changing anything for the tenant.

A release that frees only the street door. Both stations can open the street door. Neither opens the other household's apartment door, which stays on its own key. That keeps the tenant independent without giving them any access to the owner's space, and it means the owner never has to come down to let in the tenant's guests.

Power on the owner's side, clearly labeled. The power supply goes in the part of the basement the owner controls, on a labeled breaker, with a note on the panel cover that says what it feeds. A tenant should never need to reach the power supply, and the owner should never have to guess which breaker it is on.

Audio, video or a phone app for each household. The two households do not have to answer the same way. A two-wire video system lets one floor have a screen while the other keeps a simple audio station, and many systems also forward calls to a phone. When a household chooses a phone app, that app account belongs to the household, while the owner keeps the administrator settings, so a tenant who moves out can be removed in a minute.

Planning for the next tenant. Tenants change; houses do not. We label the button with something that survives a move, such as the floor, and keep a printed record of the settings the owner can change. When a new tenant moves in, the owner should be able to change the name on the label, reset the app, and test the station from the street in a few minutes without calling anyone.

When the owner does not live there. Some two-families here are owned by people who live elsewhere, and both apartments are rented. In that case both households get identical stations and the owner keeps the administrator role remotely. The power supply and the basement records matter even more, because the next person to look at the system may be a tenant's call to a repairman who has never seen the building.

Privacy at the Panel

Entry Panels That Dial Residents' Phones: What the Flatbush Gardens Complaints Teach a Small Remsen Village Landlord

The most specific local warning about entry systems comes from just across Community Board 17. Bklyner reported that tenants at Flatbush Gardens said a new intercom system gave out their personal phone numbers to anyone at the door. Whatever the exact cause was in that complex, the complaint describes a real risk with panels that call residents' cell phones directly: if the system is set up carelessly, the person pressing the button can learn how to reach a resident outside the building, long after the visit is over.

Phone-dial and app-based panels are useful in Remsen Village. They let a tenant who works long shifts answer the door from anywhere, and they let an owner who lives elsewhere see who is at the building. The problem is never the idea; it is the settings. Before any panel of this kind goes in, we look at three things. First, what the visitor sees on the screen while the call is placed, and whether a resident's number, name or photo can appear there. Second, what number the resident's phone shows when the call arrives, because some systems pass through the building's line and others do not. Third, who can read the list of residents' numbers in the management portal, and whether that list is held by the owner or by a vendor.

For a two-family or a small garden apartment building, the simplest safe arrangement is often a wired station in each apartment, with phone forwarding turned on only for households that ask for it, and directory entries that show a floor or an apartment rather than a full name. In the newer mid-rises, where an app is part of the building's design, we review the settings and recommend changes in writing. Residents' numbers should stay inside the system, visitors should see only what they need to place a call, and a tenant who leaves should be removed from the system the same day their keys come back. None of this costs much once it is planned. It costs a great deal of trust when it is not.

The same reports point to a second lesson: a panel is only as good as the door behind it. Tenants also told Bklyner that their building doors did not properly lock. An intercom that calls the right person is worthless if the door it guards can be pushed open. The Rules by Building Size

Two-Families, Garden Buildings and Remsen Avenue Mid-Rises: What City and State Rules Ask of the Front Door

The rules that apply to a Remsen Village entrance depend on the building, not the neighborhood. What follows is a guide to the public sources so owners, managers and tenants know what to ask, not legal advice; for any specific building, an attorney or managing agent should confirm it.

One- and two-family houses. The city's intercom requirements are written for multiple dwellings, which begin at three apartments. A one-family or two-family owner installs an intercom by choice. Once a tenant relies on a door system, keeping it working becomes part of keeping up the apartment, and courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service.

Three to seven apartments. Parker Custom Security summarizes the rule for this size: no intercom requirement, but the self-closing and self-locking door requirement still applies. HPD treats a building entrance door that fails to close by itself seriously, and owners who do not keep self-closing doors in good repair face an immediately hazardous violation. An electric strike, a closer and an intercom have to work together so the door latches after every release.

Eight or more apartments. Habitat Magazine describes the state requirement: buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units must have a two-way voice intercom linking the front door to each apartment. Parker adds that older buildings of that size come under the same requirement once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request it in writing.

Violation class. The sources disagree. Seneca Security and Parker Custom Security describe a broken intercom as a Class B violation; DOBGuard lists a non-functioning intercom under Class A. We have not confirmed which HPD applies in every case, and an owner who receives a notice should read the class and the correction instructions printed on it.

Tenants' options. Avvo's legal Q&A points tenants to 311 and to a complaint with HPD when an intercom stays broken, and notes that rent-stabilized tenants can also file a rent-reduction complaint. Parker describes the courts' treatment of intercoms under the warranty of habitability in Real Property Law §235-b.

Mounting heights. The city rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY 42-01, says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device must sit between four and five feet from the floor. On a house with front steps, the floor is the top step where the visitor stands.

Permits. One Brooklyn installer states that intercom installation does not require a special permit unless extensive wiring or structural changes are involved. We have not confirmed that with the Department of Buildings, and any new line-voltage circuit for a power supply is work for a licensed electrician.

Landmarks. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district in Remsen Village. The only city historic district in East Flatbush is on East 25th Street between Clarendon Road and Avenue D.

Getting out. Every door we fit with an electric strike or lock opens from inside without a key, a code or a button. The intercom controls who comes in, never who can leave.

Basements, cable and the old bell

Reading a basement ceiling before quoting

The basement of a Remsen Village house usually tells us more about an intercom job than the front door does. The ceiling shows how many generations of wire have been added: thin bell wire, phone wire, coaxial cable for television, sometimes the cable from an old alarm. We look at whether the bell wire is stapled straight to the joists or run in a neat bundle, whether it passes near the boiler or the water heater, and where it turns up toward the second floor. Water stains along a run, a splice wrapped in old tape, or insulation that has been chewed explain faults that keep coming back. That is why we ask for a basement photo with the first message, and why the survey always ends below the house.

When the old bell wire can carry something better

Many owners assume video means new cable throughout the house. Often it does not. Two-wire video systems carry the picture, the voice and the release over one pair of conductors, and the original bell wire in a brick two-family can sometimes do that job if it tests clean from end to end. The test matters more than the age of the wire: a 70-year-old run that reads clean can be fine, and a 10-year-old run with a bad splice will not be. Where one pair passes and another fails, we can keep the good run to one floor and pull a new cable only to the other, which keeps the job smaller and the plaster intact.

The front of a brick house

Placing the station on a short flight of steps

Most houses here have a few steps from the walk to the door, and the visitor stands on the top step to ring. The city's mounting rule measures from the floor where the visitor stands, so the button and the speaker are placed for someone on that top step, not on the sidewalk. The camera is aimed to see a face at that position, and the station goes on the side of the door the visitor approaches from, usually the side nearest the steps' handrail. Where the steps are shared with a semi-detached neighbor, the station sits clearly on your side, with your house number nearby so it is obvious which bell belongs to which house.

Front yard fences and gates

Many Remsen Village houses have a small front yard behind a low fence with a gate. If the owner wants the gate to lock, the station moves to the gate, cable has to cross the yard, and the gate needs a latch that holds and a way to open from inside. That is a bigger job and changes the look of the house from the street, so we price both arrangements and let the owner decide.

FAQ

Remsen Village Intercom Installation FAQ

When a landlord switches the street door over to fobs, should the old key cylinder be taken out?
+

We recommend keeping a mechanical key override on the street door, usually a cylinder the owner and super control. Tenant reviews from the large complex in neighboring Farragut describe a landlord removing the key lock and making tenants buy magnetic keys instead, which leaves everyone stranded if the reader or its power fails. A fob reader and an intercom can share a door with a keyed cylinder, so the building works on a bad day too. Questions about who pays for fobs in a rent-regulated building belong with an attorney or the state housing agency.

Our 1930s detached two-family has a separate garage out back for each apartment. Can a call from each garage reach only that apartment?
+

Yes. Each garage people-door can get its own small station or call button wired to ring only the household it belongs to, while the front door station keeps both buttons. The garages are on the same system as the house, so one power supply in the basement feeds everything. The cable usually leaves the house through a sealed sleeve on the driveway side and follows the house wall or fence in an outdoor-rated raceway. Where no clean path exists, a wireless link to the garages can be priced beside the wired option.

The previous owner left several old transformers on our basement wall. How do you tell which one powers the doorbell, and what happens to the rest?
+

We trace the bell wire from the door to the basement and follow it to the transformer it lands on, then confirm with a meter while the bell is pressed. The others often belonged to an old alarm, a thermostat or a long-gone chime. Each one is identified before anything is disconnected, because one of them may still feed something in use. Transformers that feed nothing are removed with the owner's agreement, and the one that stays, or its replacement, is labeled with its breaker.

Our building became apartments in the 1970s. Does the rule for buildings "converted" after 1968 apply to us?
+

It can. The state requirement described by Habitat Magazine covers buildings built or converted after 1968 that have eight or more units, so a conversion date in the 1970s counts the same way as a construction date. The second test is size: if the conversion produced fewer than eight apartments, the intercom requirement does not apply, though the self-closing and self-locking door duty still does. The building's certificate of occupancy usually shows the date and the legal number of units.

Does an intercom add much to the electric bill, and whose meter should it be on in a two-family?
+

A small door system uses very little power, far less than a typical household appliance, so the cost is minor either way. What matters more is control. We prefer the power supply on a circuit the owner controls, labeled at the breaker, so the system does not go dark if a tenant's service is shut off between tenants. Networked systems with switches and battery backup draw more, and we tell the owner which circuit they will use before installing them.

The upstairs apartment in our house has its own door down the narrow walk beside the house. Is that a good spot for a call station?
+

It can be, if the walk is lit and the station is clearly visible from the sidewalk. A visitor should not have to wander down a dark side path looking for a bell. We usually add a light over the side door on a sensor, put a small sign or number at the front pointing to the side entrance, and choose a station with a camera that sees the whole walk rather than just the doorframe. If the walk is too dark or narrow, a second button on the front station that rings upstairs may be the better answer.

Our upstairs apartment will be empty for a month between tenants. What intercom work is easiest to do while it sits empty?
+

Anything that involves opening walls, closets or ceilings upstairs. With nobody living there, new cable can be run from the basement to the tenant's kitchen or hall, an old handset can be removed and the wall patched before painting, and the station can be placed where the next tenant will actually hear it. If the owner's floor gets a new station later, the upstairs work is already done. Tell us the move-out and move-in dates so the work fits between them.

Mice chewed through the doorbell wire along our basement ceiling. How do you keep the new cable safe from them?
+

Chewed insulation is a common reason a bell works one week and fails the next. For the new run we use a sturdier jacketed cable and, along stretches where the damage was, put it inside metal or hard plastic raceway rather than stapling it bare to the joists. Holes where the cable passes through walls are sealed so they do not become a path for pests. It also helps to deal with the pests themselves, because no cable is fully safe in a basement where they keep nesting.

Our audio intercom went in during the 1990s. How do we find out whether replacement stations are still made for it?
+

Send us a photo of the station's label, usually on the back or inside the cover, and a photo of the outdoor panel. Many makers have kept compatible stations available for decades, while some older lines are gone entirely. If the original part is still sold, one failed station can be replaced on its own. If it is not, a compatible modern station sometimes works on the same wire, and if none does, we explain what a full change would involve before you decide anything.

How do you keep rain from following the new cable through our brick and into the basement?
+

Where cable enters the house through brick, we drill the hole with a slight downward slope toward the outside, line it with a sleeve, and seal both ends so water cannot run along the cable into the wall. The outdoor station is sealed at its top and sides and left open slightly at the bottom so any moisture can drain. Where possible the cable enters behind the station itself, so the hole is covered and sheltered. We check the spot during the next heavy rain if the owner asks.
