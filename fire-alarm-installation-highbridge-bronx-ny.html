The Neighborhood

Why Highbridge Buildings Need Fire Alarm Installation Planned Around the Hill

Highbridge sits on a ridge of Fordham gneiss above the Harlem River, and almost everything about its buildings follows from that. Anderson Avenue, Ogden Avenue and University Avenue climb sharply from the river's edge to the hilltop, while West 167th, 168th and 170th Streets carry dense blocks of five- and six-story apartment houses across the slope (CityNeighborhoods.nyc). Where the grade gets too steep for a street, the city built stairs. The best known is the West 167th Street step street between Shakespeare and Anderson Avenues, 132 steps that appeared in the 2019 film Joker and its 2024 sequel and now carry murals painted by local youth with CITYarts (Wikipedia, Joker Stairs; CITYarts). The neighborhood takes its name from the High Bridge, completed in 1848 to carry the Croton Aqueduct and the city's oldest standing bridge (NYC Parks).

For a building owner, the ridge shows up in the floor plans. A six-story building on Anderson Avenue can have its lobby on the avenue and a cellar door that opens at grade several floors lower on the back slope. A new building on University Avenue can present a modest face to the street and drop a long way toward the river behind it. The NYC Building Code triggers a fire alarm in apartment buildings partly by counting how far apartments sit above "the lowest level of exit discharge" (NYC Building Code 907.2.9), and the code's high-rise rules count height from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access (National Fire Sprinkler Association). On flat blocks those reference points are obvious. On Highbridge's slopes they are a design decision the engineer has to make and defend, and they change where the fire alarm control panel goes, where annunciators are needed and how the floors are labeled on the screen firefighters read.

The second reason is fire history in the older stock. Between 1910 and 1930, developers filled the hillsides with five- and six-story masonry apartment houses (CityNeighborhoods.nyc), and those buildings keep appearing in the news. In July 2019 a four-alarm fire at 917 Ogden Avenue injured five residents and six firefighters, with people escaping onto fire escapes. "We didn't hear any fire detectors, but we ended up having to come down the fire escape to get to safe ground," one resident told ABC7 New York (ABC7, July 26, 2019). In April 2025 more than 100 firefighters answered a sixth-floor fire in a building on West 162nd Street between Ogden and Summit Avenues (ABC7, April 6, 2025). In February 2026 a third-alarm fire broke out at 1040 Anderson Avenue, a six-story building where a child had been hurt in a separate fire two months earlier; more than 140 fire and EMS personnel responded and a hydrant froze in the cold (News 12 Bronx, February 6, 2026). On November 3, 2026, another apartment fire in Highbridge took firefighters about an hour to put out and left one person hurt (News 12 Bronx, November 4, 2026).

The third reason is new construction, and a lot of it is housing for people who need staff on site. Samaritan Daytop Village broke ground in December 2024 on a 422-unit tower at 1387 University Avenue, between Boscobel Place and Highbridge Park, with 190 supportive units, 106 transitional units for families, 125 affordable homes and about 100 program staff; completion is expected in June 2027 (New York YIMBY, January 24, 2025). Ogden Theatre Apartments at 1415 Ogden Avenue, 85 homes with 51 for formerly homeless residents and about 2,000 square feet of ground-floor social services, was wrapping up construction in October 2026 on a site that once held a movie theater, a bowling alley and a church (New York YIMBY, October 28, 2026). Fischer Senior Apartments opened at 97 West 169th Street in June 2026 with nine stories and 105 units, 59 of them for formerly homeless older adults (New York YIMBY, June 15, 2026). On Inwood Avenue, a two-tower, 15-story building with 195 units and a 14-story building with 114 units and more than 51,000 square feet of community facility space are rising on former auto-shop and warehouse land (New York YIMBY, October 25, 2026). Every one of those buildings needs a commercial fire alarm system designed, filed, installed, monitored and tested before people move in.

This page covers commercial and code-required fire alarm systems only: building-wide systems in apartment and mixed-use buildings and the systems in stores, offices, churches, schools and community facilities. Smoke alarms inside individual houses follow separate rules and are not part of this service.

Need Fire Alarm Repair in Highbridge? Tell Us Which Floor and Which Door

If a panel in a Highbridge apartment building, supportive residence, store or program space is in trouble, beeping or sounding with no fire, call (646) 490-0629 and we book the visit within 2 hours. Tell us the address, which street the panel is closest to, and what the screen says word for word. On a sloped lot, "the panel in the basement" can mean the cellar under the Ogden Avenue lobby or a room that opens onto the street below, and the technician needs to know which door to knock on.

While you wait, take a photo of the screen, tell the central station a technician is coming, and ask staff not to keep pressing reset, because each reset clears part of the history a technician needs. Do not pull the batteries and do not cover a detector that keeps alarming. In New York City a required fire alarm that is out of service means notifying the FDNY and keeping whatever fire watch it requires until the system is restored. Emergency fire alarm repair is about getting the system working again, not about quieting a lobby.

The calls we expect most here follow the building stock. In new supportive and senior buildings, the first year brings troubles from punch-list work, a sprinkler valve bumped by a contractor or a ventilation unit that shuts down on the wrong signal; same day fire alarm repair in those buildings is often a supervisory signal the desk staff were never shown how to read. In prewar six-story buildings on Anderson, Shakespeare and Nelson Avenues, an old panel's communicator can lose its phone line or cellular signal, which makes the visit a fire alarm communication failure emergency repair. Along Jerome Avenue, a storefront whose panel went dark after a sign, gate or awning job is a fire alarm system down repair.

People search for 24 hour fire alarm repair and after hours fire alarm service at two in the morning, and the honest promise is a booking within 2 hours, a real arrival time, and buildings that cannot report a fire put first. Each emergency fire alarm technician begins emergency fire alarm troubleshooting at the event log and then walks to the device or circuit the log names. A fire alarm trouble emergency service call in a supportive building often ends at a detector a resident covered; a fire alarm system emergency service visit in an older building often ends at a battery that aged out quietly. Urgent fire alarm repair, emergency fire alarm service, fire alarm panel failure repair and fire alarm system malfunction repair all finish the same way: the cause written on the ticket and a plain explanation of what keeps it from coming back. Commercial fire alarm emergency repair on a new building is copied to the developer, since warranty and service work are billed differently.

Building Specialists

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Highbridge's Architecture, From the River Up

Commercial fire alarm systems in Highbridge are shaped less by the age of a building than by where it sits on the slope. Here is how we approach each building type that is common here; the sealed drawings always have the final word.

Prewar six-story buildings on the side streets (Anderson, Shakespeare, Nelson, Summit)

These are the 1910 to 1930 masonry apartment houses with ornate facades, cornices and courtyard entrances that line Anderson and Ogden Avenues (CityNeighborhoods.nyc). Many were built long before today's fire alarm rules and rely on apartment smoke alarms, while others received building systems during rehabilitation or a change in their Certificate of Occupancy. Our first job in one of these buildings is to read its records and tell the owner which rules actually apply. When a system is required or chosen, fire alarm wiring installation follows existing paths first: the existing riser, the stair, a dead shaft, the hallway ceiling. On the slope, the cellar may be the floor with a street door, so we confirm with the engineer which level the panel and annunciator belong on before anything is mounted.

New mid-rise buildings (six to eleven stories)

Permit filings in the neighborhood include an 11-story, 61-unit building at 1095 Jerome Avenue, 112 feet tall with about 3,100 square feet of commercial space, replacing a two-story house built in 1925 (Marketproof, February 25, 2025), a six-story building at 955 Woodycrest Avenue (New York YIMBY, September 2025), and a seven-story building at 1295 Shakespeare Avenue that had topped out by October 2026 (New York YIMBY, October 25, 2026). For buildings like these, building fire alarm installation starts during design development. An 11-story building at 112 feet also crosses the 75-foot line that the code uses to define a high-rise (National Fire Sprinkler Association), which brings voice evacuation and other high-rise features into the design.

High-rise towers on the bluff

The tower at 1387 University Avenue is the clearest example of what the ridge does to a tall building: a 422-unit structure on a lot overlooking the river between Boscobel Place and Highbridge Park, all-electric and aiming for Passive House certification (New York YIMBY, January 24, 2025). Public reports describe its height differently (the article text says 31 stories, its summary line says 26), which is itself a reminder that on a slope, "how tall" depends on where you start counting. In a high-rise the fire alarm grows into a life-safety network: an emergency voice/alarm communication system, firefighter communication, a fire command location, elevator recall, smoke control interfaces and many booster power supplies. High rise fire alarm installation on a bluff also has to agree with the FDNY on which entrance is the response point, because ladder access on the river side is limited.

Supportive and senior residences with staff on site

Fischer Senior Apartments has an on-site resident manager, a senior center and a community center, and it runs an Enhanced Care pilot with home health aides for residents who need personal care (New York YIMBY, June 15, 2026). Ogden Theatre Apartments puts about 2,000 square feet of case management and employment services on its ground floor (New York YIMBY, October 28, 2026). The University Avenue tower plans about 100 program staff and round-the-clock security (New York YIMBY, January 24, 2025). In buildings like these, the people answering the panel are staff, not a superintendent, so assisted living fire alarm installation practices carry over, even though these are apartments: the annunciator goes where the desk is, signals are labeled in plain words, and the handover includes a training session for every shift.

Large community-facility floors in new towers

The 14-story building at 1320 Inwood Avenue includes 51,080 square feet of community facility space, and the two-tower building at 1298 Inwood Avenue includes nearly 11,000 square feet (New York YIMBY, October 25, 2026). That space can become a clinic, school or offices. When the tenant is not known at the base-building filing, we help the design professional leave the riser, the panel capacity and the power supply room for the future tenant fit out fire alarm installation, so that the next filing is an addition rather than a rebuild.

Churches, schools and older institutions

Highbridge's institutional buildings include the Romanesque Union Reformed Church on Ogden Avenue with its Tiffany glass and the Gothic former P.S. 91, now P.S. 11 (Wikipedia, Highbridge, Bronx), along with parish buildings such as Sacred Heart, founded in 1875 (CityNeighborhoods.nyc). Work in buildings like these respects decorative interiors: devices are placed so they protect people without scarring plaster or glass, cable is concealed where the engineer allows, and noisy work is scheduled around services and classes. School fire alarm installation in particular is phased around the school calendar, with the acceptance test booked when the building is empty.

Commercial Fire Alarm

Business Fire Alarm Installation on West 167th Street, Ogden Avenue, University Avenue and Jerome Avenue

Highbridge's commercial life runs along a few corridors. West 167th Street and Ogden Avenue were the commercial center of the 19th-century village of Highbridgeville (CUNY Manifold, The History of Highbridge). Jerome Avenue runs under the elevated 4 train along the neighborhood's eastern edge, where small businesses have filled storefronts for a century (CityNeighborhoods.nyc) and where new residential buildings are replacing auto shops and parking lots (New York YIMBY, October 25, 2026). University Avenue and Edward L. Grant Highway carry traffic toward the Cross Bronx Expressway and the Washington Bridge.

Business fire alarm installation on these corridors usually means one of four jobs. A storefront under apartments may need its devices tied into the building's system, or a separate system that reports to it, depending on the drawings. A restaurant fire alarm installation adds heat detection in the kitchen and fire alarm kitchen hood suppression monitoring so the building knows when the hood system discharges. An office or medical office fire alarm installation in a new community facility floor is a tenant fit-out filed on top of the base building. An office building fire alarm installation on an upper floor follows the same path. And a ground-floor space converting from an auto shop or warehouse to retail or community use is a change of use that the design professional has to file correctly before devices go up. Business fire detection systems and commercial fire detection systems in these spaces are sized for the use, not for the square footage alone.

Working under the Jerome Avenue el

The elevated 4 train is a constant soundtrack on Jerome Avenue (CityNeighborhoods.nyc), and that matters for the fire alarm. The NYC Building Code requires audible alarm notification appliances to produce at least 15 decibels above the average ambient sound level, or 5 decibels above the maximum sound level lasting at least 60 seconds, whichever is greater, in every occupiable space (NYC Building Code 907.5.2.1.1). In a store or apartment facing the tracks, the ambient level the engineer measures, and when it is measured, can change the number and size of horns or speakers. We bring a sound meter to the pre-test and take readings with trains running, so audibility is proven before the FDNY arrives rather than argued about on the day.

Compliance callouts for storefronts and community spaces

  • Design comes first. A New York State registered design professional prepares and files the plans with a TM-1 application; the installer is a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm company holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate (FDNY, Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).
  • Certificates are checkable. At least one principal of a certified fire alarm company holds an S-97 certificate of fitness, which requires NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license (FDNY, Fire Alarm Company Certificate FAQ).
  • Fees are public. The FDNY charges $420 for a new fire alarm application and $525 more where complex technical analysis is required; work cannot begin until a Project Authorization is issued (NYC Business).
  • Monitoring is part of approval. The central station connection must be active before the fire alarm inspection is scheduled (FDNY, Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).
  • Accessibility counts. Visible notification appliances, the fire alarm ADA strobe installation in public and common spaces, are part of the design wherever the code calls for them, which matters in a district where nearly one resident in five lives with a disability (FDNY Community Risk Assessment, BX04).
Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Floors From the Fire Department's Door

On a hillside lot, the first argument on any fire alarm job is about numbers. The architect's drawings call the lobby floor 1. The super calls the floor below it "the basement," even though it has windows and a door onto the lower street. The residents on that floor say they live on the ground floor. And the panel, if nobody stops it, will show whatever label the programmer typed in a hurry.

So before we pull a foot of cable on a Highbridge job, we stand at the door the FDNY will use and agree on one numbering with the engineer and the owner. That numbering goes on the riser diagram, the panel's device labels, the annunciator map, the zone chart by the desk and the central station account. When a firefighter reads "Floor B, east corridor smoke," it should send him to the right door and the right stair, not to the floor above.

It sounds like paperwork, but it decides how fast firefighters find the right stair, and it costs nothing at the start. It is also one of the first things we check when we take over an existing building: half the "nobody can read this panel" complaints we expect here are really labeling problems left over from a renovation.

Your Questions Answered

Highbridge Fire Alarm Questions From Owners, Developers, Operators and Boards

Questions we expect from Highbridge owners, developers, nonprofit operators, boards and storefront tenants.

Cost and planning

Our building on Anderson Avenue has its front door on the avenue and its cellar door several floors lower on the back slope. Why does that change the fire alarm design fee?

Because the engineer has more to decide and document. A building with entrances at two heights raises questions a flat-lot building never does: which level is the lowest level of exit discharge, which door the FDNY treats as its entrance, whether a second annunciator is needed, and how the floors below the lobby are counted and labeled. Each answer has to be shown on the sealed drawings and may draw a question from the plan examiner. That is design time, and it is why two buildings of the same size can carry different design fees.

Our construction lender on a Jerome Avenue project wants fire alarm milestones it can tie to loan draws. Which milestones make sense?

The ones that can be verified by a document. Sealed drawings filed with the FDNY; the Project Authorization; rough-in complete and inspected by your own team before drywall; panel and devices installed and programmed; the pre-test report; and the FDNY Letter of Approval after the acceptance test. Each leaves a paper trail the lender's inspector can check. Avoid milestones like "fire alarm 50 percent complete," which nobody can measure.

Height and the hill

Does a tower on the University Avenue bluff count as a high-rise if much of its height drops toward the river below the entrance?

Very likely, but the engineer has to show it. The high-rise definition is measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor, and on a lot that falls toward the river the lowest access point may sit well below the main entrance (National Fire Sprinkler Association). A building can look like a mid-rise from University Avenue and still be well over 75 feet by that measure. Once it is a high-rise, voice evacuation, firefighter communication and a fire command location become part of the fire alarm design.

Which entrance on a sloped Highbridge lot becomes the fire department's entrance, where the panel or annunciator is expected?

The one the design professional proposes and the FDNY accepts on the drawings, which is usually the main entrance with the most direct access for apparatus. On some Highbridge lots that is clear; on others, a lower street gives better truck access than the lobby street. We raise the question at the first survey, because moving a panel or annunciator after the walls close is expensive and moving it after the FDNY test is worse.

Our boiler room sits at the bottom of the slope, two floors below the lobby. Should the main fire alarm panel go down there?

Usually not. A boiler room is hot, sometimes damp and rarely visited, which is a poor home for the panel staff and firefighters need to read quickly. The panel normally belongs in a protected room near the fire department's entrance, with the boiler room covered by detectors that report back to it. If space forces the panel lower, a remote annunciator at the entrance gives responders the same information.

Noise from the el

When the designer measures ambient noise for apartments facing the Jerome Avenue el, should the reading be taken with trains running?

Yes. The code sets audibility relative to the average ambient sound and to the maximum sound level lasting 60 seconds or more (NYC Building Code 907.5.2.1.1), so a measurement taken at 3 a.m. with no trains understates the problem in rooms facing the tracks. We take readings at representative times, note them on the pre-test report, and add or relocate appliances before the FDNY acceptance test rather than discovering a quiet bedroom on the day.

Supportive and senior buildings

A home health aide program serves residents in our senior building on West 169th Street. Should the aides be part of the fire alarm plan?

They should know it, even though they do not operate it. Aides are often the first people with a resident who is slow to leave, so they need to recognize the alarm and strobes, know the building's evacuation instructions for each floor, and know who at the desk to call. We include aides and their supervisors in the handover training if the operator asks, and we write the signal guide in plain words so a new aide can follow it on the first day.

Our ground-floor social services suite locks at five. How do annual device tests reach the detectors inside it?

By scheduling them during office hours or with a staff member holding keys. NFPA 72 testing has to reach every device, including those in offices, file rooms and interview rooms, and a skipped room becomes a deficiency on the report. We agree on the test day with the program director in advance, keep client records covered and out of photos, and test the suite first so staff can lock up on time.

Program staff offices fill part of a new supportive building on University Avenue. Does the fire alarm treat those office floors differently from the apartments?

Often, yes. Offices, clinics and program rooms are a different occupancy from apartments, with different detection and notification rules, and the engineer shows each area on the drawings. Usually one building system serves them all, with the office floors reporting as their own areas on the panel so staff can tell at a glance whether an alarm is in program space or in the residences above.

Older six-story buildings

After two fires in a few months at 1040 Anderson Avenue, our board asked what a building-wide fire alarm would and would not change in a building like ours. What is the honest answer?

A building system detects smoke in hallways and common spaces, sounds throughout, and calls a central station, so a fire in the stair or corridor is reported even when nobody is home to call. It does not replace the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms inside each apartment, does not put out a fire, and does not make fire escapes or doors safer. Whether your building must have one depends on its records; whether it should is a decision the board can make with an engineer's survey in hand.

Our prewar building on Ogden Avenue has a panel whose zone labels nobody can read anymore. Is the panel the problem, or the paperwork?

Often the paperwork. Faded labels, a missing zone chart and lost drawings make a working panel useless in an emergency. We test the system, trace each zone, and rebuild the zone chart and labels; if the panel itself is failing or obsolete, the report says so with the reasons. Re-labeling a working panel is maintenance; replacing it is a filed project, and you should know which one you are paying for.

Crowds and complaints

Visitors photographing the Shakespeare Avenue stairs gather by our lobby door. Does a crowd outside change anything about the building's fire alarm?

Not the design, but it can change the upkeep. A crowd at the door does not affect detectors or horns, but a lobby that strangers wander into can see more pulled stations and damaged devices. Keep the exit doors and the path to the street clear, check lobby pull stations on every visit, and if malicious pulls start, ask us about approved protective covers rather than removing or blocking the station.

Someone keeps pulling the lobby station in our Summit Avenue building late at night. What can the owner do besides replacing the station?

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Fire Alarms in Highbridge's Hillside Buildings

Search summaries and chat assistants give quick answers about fire alarm installation, and some of those answers are simply wrong for a building on the Highbridge ridge. Here are seven common claims and what the field reality is.

"A building's height is just its number of stories"

Generic answers treat a six-story building as six stories and a 20-story building as 20. Codes do not. The apartment-building fire alarm trigger counts how far apartments sit above the lowest level of exit discharge (NYC Building Code 907.2.9), and the high-rise definition counts from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access (National Fire Sprinkler Association).

On Highbridge's slopes those starting points can sit on different streets. A building that reads as six stories from Ogden Avenue may present seven or eight on its back slope, and a tower that seems mid-rise from University Avenue can measure as a high-rise from the river side.

So the stories in a listing are not the input for fire alarm design; the engineer's section drawing is.

"The fire alarm panel always goes in the basement"

Plenty of online answers put the panel in the basement or electrical room by default. In New York City, the panel or an annunciator belongs where firefighters arrive, which is a decision shown on the drawings and reviewed by the FDNY.

In Highbridge, the "basement" may be a floor with its own street door on the low side, or a boiler room two floors below the lobby with no direct access. Putting the panel there because it is customary can force responders to hunt for information in a smoke-filled stair.

On some lots that means two annunciators on two streets.

"A standard horn is loud enough anywhere"

Product pages quote a horn's decibel rating as if that settles audibility. The code measures audibility relative to the room: at least 15 decibels above the average ambient sound, or 5 decibels above the maximum lasting 60 seconds, whichever is greater (NYC Building Code 907.5.2.1.1).

Apartments and stores facing the elevated 4 train on Jerome Avenue have an ambient level a quiet side street does not. A horn that is plenty loud on Nelson Avenue can fall short in a front room on Jerome Avenue when a train passes.

We measure with trains running and design for the real number.

"Any licensed electrician can install a building fire alarm"

AI answers often stop at "hire a licensed electrician." In New York City the FDNY allows installation by a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm company holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, and the plans must be prepared by a registered design professional (FDNY, Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).

Inspection, testing and service of an existing system are where the company certificate matters most: a company that services any component other than smoke detectors alone must be certified, and at least one principal must hold an S-97 certificate of fitness backed by NICET Level II or a NYC master or special electrician license (FDNY, Fire Alarm Company Certificate FAQ).

Ask for the certificate numbers in writing; a licensed fire alarm installer expects the question.

"Approval takes a few weeks"

Some answers suggest a fire alarm is approved within weeks of installation. The FDNY's own guidance says the initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks and that the path from plan submission to final inspection averages about 20 to 24 weeks (FDNY, Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).

For new buildings like the ones on Inwood Avenue and Ogden Avenue, that window has to be planned into the construction schedule, not added at the end. For an existing prewar building, a filed panel replacement goes through the same review.

Schedules that ignore the review are why TCOs slide.

"Supportive housing is just apartments, so the fire alarm is the same"

Generic answers treat all residential buildings alike. A supportive or senior building with offices, a senior center, security and home health aides mixes occupancies and needs.

The fire alarm has to report program spaces and residences separately, put information where staff sit, and come with training for every shift. The FDNY's risk assessment for this district found 18 percent of residents living with a disability (FDNY Community Risk Assessment, BX04), which makes visible notification and clear staff procedures more than a formality.

The panel is the same; the design around it is not.

"You can look up what a fire alarm costs online"

Cost websites publish ranges as if a fire alarm were a product. A commercial fire alarm system is a project: sealed engineering drawings, an FDNY filing, installation to those drawings, interfaces with elevators, sprinklers and fans, and a witnessed acceptance test.

On a Highbridge lot, the grade, the number of entrances, the height measurement, the el's noise and the building's staffing all move the scope. None of that appears in a national average.

How long does fire alarm installation take? Our building sits on a corner where the mailing address is on one street and the main entrance is on another. Does that matter for the fire alarm?
+

Yes. The central station dispatches the fire department to the address in its account, so that record should describe the entrance firefighters should use. On a Highbridge corner where streets run at different heights, we confirm the address on the FDNY filing, the central station account and the annunciator location all point to the same door.

Where do installers stage cable and equipment on Highbridge's steep, narrow side streets?
+

Usually inside the building. We bring material in smaller loads from the higher street, store it in a room the owner assigns, and avoid blocking the step streets and sidewalks that residents use to climb between avenues. Large deliveries such as a panel cabinet or lift are scheduled for a set window with the superintendent.

Can fire alarm cable run in exterior conduit on the back of a building that faces the river slope?
+

Only where the sealed drawings allow it. Fire alarm circuits normally run inside the building, and an exterior run exposed to weather and ice needs the right conduit, fittings and protection. In older buildings we look first for an interior shaft, a stair or a service chase before anyone proposes running pipe down the outside wall.

The central ventilation units on our new building's roof run around the clock. Do they have to shut down when the fire alarm activates?
+

Many do. Larger air handling and ventilation systems are often required to have duct smoke detection and to shut down on alarm so they do not spread smoke, and the design professional sets which units shut down in the sequence of operations. Every shutdown is then proven at the acceptance test, so the mechanical contractor needs to be on site that day.

We are converting the top floor of our Ogden Avenue building into a community room. Does that change the fire alarm?
+

It can. A room where many people gather is treated differently from apartments, and putting it at the top of the building affects exits and notification. The change of use is filed with the Department of Buildings, and any fire alarm devices it requires are designed, filed with the FDNY and tested before the room opens to the public.

Does the FDNY acceptance test for a new Highbridge building need the elevator, sprinkler and door contractors on site?
+

Usually, yes. The test proves every interface the drawings show: elevator recall, sprinkler waterflow and tamper signals, door releases and fan shutdowns. If the elevator mechanic or the sprinkler contractor is missing, those items cannot be tested and the test may not be completed. We send the schedule to every trade two weeks ahead.

Can a fire alarm survey be done in a building where families in a transitional program are living?
+

Yes, with planning. We work with the operator's staff, enter apartments and program rooms only with an escort and notice, keep resident information out of our notes and photos, and keep the visit short. Most of the survey happens in corridors, stairs, mechanical rooms and the ground-floor spaces anyway.

Do apartments below street level on the river side of a building get the same horns and strobes as the upper floors?
+

Apartments on the lower levels are part of the building, so the notification design covers them just as it covers the floors above. What changes is the routing: wire paths, exits and the floor numbering shown on the panel have to make sense for floors that sit below the main entrance, and we label them the way residents describe them.

Can fire alarm devices go on the landscaped terraces and walking paths around a new bluff-top building?
+

Outdoor terraces and paths are generally not covered with smoke detectors, which do not work well outside. Whether a terrace needs a horn, strobe or manual station depends on how it is used and how people leave it, which the design professional decides on the drawings. Exterior devices that are required are rated for weather and mounted where residents cannot easily damage them.
