The Neighborhood

Why Rockland County Properties Need Smart Lock Installation That Fits the Household

Rockland was built quickly, for families, and the families keep getting bigger. The Historical Society of Rockland County sums up the county's modern history in a few lines: by 1950 fewer than 150 farms remained, and through the 1950s homes went up at a rate of more than a thousand a year, driven by the Tappan Zee Bridge opening in 1955 and the Palisades Interstate Parkway and New York State Thruway being completed in the same decade. What went up were raised ranches, split-levels, capes and colonials on quarter-acre and half-acre lots, most of them with a front door, a door from the garage, a back or side door, and a sliding glass door onto a deck or patio. Those doors are standard, pre-bored and generally easy to convert. The households behind them are where the planning starts.

The Census Bureau's QuickFacts table for Rockland County reports 3.22 persons per household for 2020–2024 — well above the 2.99 in Nassau County and 2.93 in Suffolk — and an owner-occupancy rate of 67.9 percent. In practice that means houses where eight, ten or twelve people come and go, older children who need their own codes, grandparents, babysitters, cleaners, tutors and a long table of guests every week. A lock that holds a handful of codes, or that only works properly through one parent's phone, is outgrown in the first month. The same table counts 357,397 residents in about 110,000 housing units (July 1, 2025 estimates) and notes that 44.2 percent of residents over five speak a language other than English at home.

The single biggest difference between Rockland and every other county we serve is Shabbat. The Jewish Virtual Library puts the county's Jewish community at about 92,000 people, close to a third of the population, with the largest Orthodox and Hasidic communities in the Town of Ramapo, in and around Monsey and the villages of New Square and Kaser, which the Forward has described as more than 90 percent Hasidic. For an observant family, an electronic keypad, a fingerprint reader, a fob and a phone app are all unusable from candle-lighting on Friday until Shabbat ends on Saturday night, and the same is true on Yom Tov. Chabad.org's guide to doors on Shabbat is plain about it: doors that open with electronic key cards, fobs or keypads may not be used, and even a smart lock opened by hand from inside raises questions because of its automatic re-locking and its activity log. A smart lock job in Monsey therefore starts with a different question from one in Nanuet: which door will the family actually use on Shabbat, and how will it open?

That concern is not only about houses. On December 28, 2019, the seventh night of Hanukkah, a masked man with a large knife forced his way into a Hasidic rabbi's home in Monsey, where almost 100 people had gathered to watch the candle-lighting, and wounded five of them; one, a 72-year-old man, died in March 2020 after weeks in a coma. In the days that followed, Rockland officials announced a volunteer armed patrol for synagogues and Senator Kirsten Gillibrand urged community leaders at a roundtable in Ramapo Town Hall to apply for security grant funding for their schools and places of worship. Since then, door hardware has become part of the routine for shuls, yeshivas, mikvaos and community organisations across the county — and in September 2026 New York State announced a record $70 million in Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes grants, with locks and access controls among the eligible improvements and 99 grants totalling more than $24 million going to the Mid-Hudson region.

The fourth Rockland factor is housing pressure. Big households and high prices push families to rent out a basement apartment or a few bedrooms, and the county has spent the last four years cracking down on what that can turn into. After a fire at a Spring Valley apartment building in March 2022, county inspectors listed more than 100 violations, and the Buildings and Codes director singled out doors that did not close properly, because self-closing, fire-rated doors are what keep a fire contained. A year later, after five members of one family died in a Spring Valley house fire, inspectors cited buildings owned by the same landlord for illegal single-room occupancies and missing smoke and carbon monoxide detectors. The county's Health Department permits and inspects rooming houses every year, and its rules specifically cover door locks. A keypad on every bedroom door is not a neutral upgrade in Rockland; it is something code inspectors notice.

Door and hardware details we check on every Rockland job

Pre-bored doors and what's left in them. Most Rockland exterior doors were bored at the factory for a standard knob and a separate deadbolt. Allegion's preparation specification for Schlage's smart deadbolts, for example, calls for a door between 1-3/8 and 2 inches thick, a 2-1/8-inch or 1-1/2-inch cross bore, a 1-inch edge bore and a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4-inch backset — exactly what most of these doors already have. The trouble is what successive owners have added: a second deadbolt above the first, a chain, a surface bolt, filler plates over abandoned holes. We remove what isn't needed, fill what's left and make sure the bolt goes into solid wood.

Sidelights and glass. Colonials and newer houses often have glass sidelights beside the front door. A thumb-turn within arm's reach of a pane of glass can be reached by someone who breaks it. That's a real argument for a smart lock with an inside button rather than a long paddle in easy reach, and for keeping the auto-lock on during the week. It is never an argument for a key-operated inside cylinder, which turns a front door into a trap in a fire.

Split-level frames. Split-levels and raised ranches settle unevenly, because the lower level sits partly on a slab or foundation wall and the upper level sits on framing. A front door that latched cleanly in 1972 can now need a shoulder against it. A motorised deadbolt won't push a bolt into a misaligned strike; it strains, drains its batteries and reports a jam. We test the bolt with the door pulled shut by hand and by its own weight, and move or enlarge the strike until it throws without contact.

The mezuzah side of the frame. On a large share of Rockland front doors there is a mezuzah on the right-hand doorpost as you enter. It usually sits in the upper third of the post, well above where a deadbolt or a frame-mounted reader goes, but strike reinforcement screws, a door-position sensor or a cable run for an electric strike can come close. We look before we drill, plan around the case, and if anything on the frame has to move we say so before work starts, so the family can decide how they want it handled.

Association and landlord doors. In Rockland's garden apartments and condominiums the outside of a unit door often belongs to the association or landlord. We ask to see the alteration rules or lease clause first. Where the outside can't change, an interior retrofit over the existing deadbolt leaves the hallway side and the key untouched; where it can, we match the neighbouring doors' finish.

Leaving never needs a credential. On a Rockland house door the inside of every lock we fit is a thumb-turn, a lever or a push-button, and nothing more. The model residential code requires exit doors to be readily openable from inside without a key or special knowledge; with a dozen children and guests in a house, we would insist on it anyway.

Two-family owners and small landlords often want the front entrance sorted at the same time. For a talk-and-release entrance there is our Rockland County intercom installation service; where only the buzzer and strike need replacing, the AESS door buzzer installers in Rockland handle it.

Compliance callouts for Rockland business doors

  • Exit without a code. A door that is part of the way out of a shop, office, school or shul must open from inside without a key, code, card or special knowledge. Keypads and readers belong on the outside; the inside gets a lever, panic bar or push-to-exit device.
  • What happens in a power cut. Electrified hardware has to be told what to do without power: a fail-safe strike or magnetic lock opens, a fail-secure one stays shut. A door on the exit route and a stockroom door need different answers, and the fire code has a say, so every electrified door gets its own decision in writing.
  • Self-closing and fire-rated doors. Rockland's Buildings and Codes director told reporters after the 2022 Spring Valley apartment fire that self-closing, fire-rated doors are what contain a fire, and the county cited doors that did not close properly. We never fit hardware that holds such a door open or stops it latching.
  • Licensing. Residential home improvement work in Rockland requires a county Home Improvement Licence under Chapter 286; the county's classifications include locksmith work and low-voltage systems. A home improvement licence is not an electrical licence, so any new mains circuit for a strike power supply is an electrician's job.
  • Grant-funded work. Nonprofits using state security grant money need the work to match what was approved in the application. We itemise quotes by door and by component so they can be checked against the award.

Past three or four doors, separate keypads become hard to manage: codes have to be changed one door at a time and nothing ties the logs together. At that point a central access control system makes more sense, and our access control installation in Rockland County designs it, while commercial security cameras for Rockland businesses record what happened at each door when it opened.

Observant households and Shabbat

We keep Shabbat — what kind of lock lets the family get in and out from Friday evening until Saturday night?

One that involves no electricity at all on the door you use that day. Chabad.org's guide to doors on Shabbat says there is generally no issue with an old-fashioned lock and key, or with combination locks that are entirely mechanical, while doors that open with electronic key cards, fobs or keypads may not be used. That leaves two practical choices for the Shabbat door: a conventional keyed deadbolt, used with a key that can be carried in a permitted way, or a mechanical push-button deadbolt whose combination works through springs and tumblers rather than circuits. Several manufacturers sell battery-free push-button deadbolts; some online listings even put "Sabbath" in the product name.

For most Rockland families the answer is a split plan rather than one lock for everything. The door you use on Shabbat — usually the front door, because guests and the walk to shul both start there — gets the mechanical keypad or a plain keyed deadbolt. The garage-to-house door or side door, which the family uses during the week with cars, groceries and car seats, gets the electronic smart lock with codes, fingerprints and the app. A 2017 article by an Orthodox home-automation installer reached the same conclusion: he put his electronic smart lock on the garage entrance precisely because he doesn't use the garage on Shabbat.

Whatever goes on the Shabbat door, the inside still opens with one plain turn of a lever or thumb-turn, so getting out never involves a code, a key or a decision. We test that with you before we leave, and we label which door is which on the instruction sheet so a babysitter or a guest isn't confused on Friday night.

Our smart lock auto-locks and logs every opening — can those features be switched off for Shabbat and Yom Tov without removing the lock?

Usually some of them, but rarely all of them in one tap, and that is the problem. Chabad.org notes that some smart locks lock themselves after a set time, which creates an electrical action, and that even with auto-lock turned off most smart locks record every use and send a signal to the app whenever the door is locked or unlocked — including when it's turned by hand from inside. Its practical advice is that, ideally, smart locks should be disabled before Shabbat. The Orthodox installer who tested a popular keypad deadbolt in 2017 found the same two issues, the 30-second auto-lock timer and the activity log, and wrote that a "Shabbat mode" would be a game changer. We have not found a mainstream residential smart lock that offers one.

What can be done in practice depends on the model. Auto-lock can be switched off in the app or on the lock on most models, and switched back on after Havdalah. Logging is harder; on many locks the only certain way to stop the electronics is to cut their power. On some models that means sliding the battery pack out or flipping a battery-compartment connector, which takes seconds once you know where it is; on others it means removing the batteries entirely. We show you exactly how on your lock, note it on the instruction sheet, and if the lock makes this awkward we tell you before you buy it.

The other option is to stop fighting the electronics. Put the smart lock on a door you don't use on Shabbat, and give the Shabbat door hardware that has nothing to switch off. Families who host every week often find that easier than remembering a battery routine every Friday at candle-lighting, and it means a guest who arrives on Shabbat afternoon never touches anything electronic.

Will a keypad deadbolt or a reader on the frame crowd the mezuzah on our doorpost?

Not usually, but it is worth checking before anything is drilled. Published guidance places the mezuzah on the right-hand doorpost as you enter, in the lower part of the upper third of the post — the Chicago Mitzvah Campaign's FAQ adds that it shouldn't be higher than four inches below the lintel, and Chabad.org says that on a very tall doorway it goes at shoulder height. A residential deadbolt or a keypad on the door itself sits much lower, typically a few inches above the handle, so on most front doors the two never meet.

Where they can come close is on the frame. If the door is hinged on the left as you walk in, the latch and strike are on the right-hand post — the same post as the mezuzah. Long strike-reinforcement screws, a door-position sensor, or a cable run up to an electric strike or a frame-mounted reader can all end up in that post. We look at the mezuzah's position at the walkthrough, keep our drilling and cable routes clear of it, and mount any reader on the wall or the opposite side of the frame if the post is crowded.

If frame work genuinely requires the mezuzah to come down — say a rotted post has to be replaced — we tell you first and leave its removal and re-affixing to you, because how and when it goes back up is a question for your family and your rabbi, not for us. We never cover, drill through or re-fix a mezuzah ourselves.

Large households

With ten or more people living in the house, how many codes can a smart lock hold, and how do we keep them straight?

More than most families need, if you choose the model with that in mind. Schlage lists 100 user codes for its Encode models, and Kwikset's Z-Wave 700 locks list 250. Cheaper locks and some retrofit units hold far fewer, and a few store fingerprints and codes in separate, smaller pools. In a Rockland household with parents, eight children, a grandparent, a babysitter, a cleaner and a tutor, you can reach twenty codes before anyone has a guest — so we check the capacity, and the number of fingerprints, against your list before recommending a lock.

Keeping them straight is a naming habit, not a technology problem. Every code goes in under a person's name, never "code 7". Children get their own codes rather than sharing one family code, so the log shows who came home at what time, and so you can delete one child's code after it's been shared with a friend without changing everyone else's. Staff get scheduled codes that only work on their days. Guests get codes with an end date, which disappear on their own.

Two people should hold administrator access — usually both parents, or a parent and an older child who lives at home — so a lost phone never locks the family out of its own lock. Review the user list before every major holiday season and delete anyone who no longer needs access. We set up the first version of the list with you on installation day, and leave it printed on the instruction sheet.

Can the app tell us at bedtime which doors in the house are still unlocked?

Yes, if the locks are connected to the internet through Wi-Fi, a bridge or a hub. The app's home screen shows each connected lock as locked or unlocked, and most apps or smart-home systems can send a notification at a set time, or after a door has been unlocked for a set number of minutes. Ramapo police, after a night in April 2024 when people walked into unlocked homes in Airmont looking for car keys and valuables and tried a sliding door in New Hempstead, reminded residents to lock doors, secure windows and set alarms before bedtime — a door-status check at bedtime does exactly that job.

A status check is only as good as the lock's sense of whether the door is actually closed. A deadbolt can report "locked" with its bolt thrown into thin air if the door was left resting against the frame. Locks with a built-in door-position sensor, or a separate door contact, can tell the difference between a door that is shut and bolted and one that is just bolted. On the doors that matter most at night, we fit and calibrate that sensor.

Remember that the check covers only the doors with connected locks. A mechanical Shabbat door or a slider with a hook-bolt lock won't appear in the app. For those, an alarm door contact can report open or closed, and the family's bedtime routine — a last walk past the front door and the slider — still matters.

Raised ranches, split-levels and their doors

The sliding door from our raised ranch's kitchen to the deck only has a thumb latch — can it be made keyless and properly locked?

Yes, and it is often the weakest door in the house. Most factory slider latches are hooks operated by a small thumb lever, built to keep the door closed rather than to resist someone lifting or forcing it. In the April 2024 Ramapo incidents, police specifically mentioned an attempt to open a sliding door in New Hempstead. On a raised ranch the slider is usually on the upper level, onto a deck reached by outside stairs, which makes it less exposed than a ground-floor patio door — but not unreachable.

Ordinary smart deadbolts and retrofit units are designed for swinging doors and won't fit a slider. The keyless options are different hardware: surface-mounted keyless locks with a hook bolt that catches the frame, such as Lockey's C150, which its maker describes as mechanical, keyless and weather resistant, with a 3/8-inch hook-bolt throw for sliding glass patio doors; and electronic slider locks sold by several smaller brands. A mechanical hook-bolt lock also suits an observant household, since there's nothing electronic to disable on Shabbat.

Whatever lock goes on, we add the cheap layer that matters most on a slider: a security bar or a pin that stops the door being slid, and anti-lift blocks in the top track so the panel can't be lifted off its rollers. Where you'd like to know when the slider opens, an alarm contact covers it.

Our 1960s front door already has a knob hole and a deadbolt hole — can a smart lock reuse them without new drilling?

Almost always. Doors from Rockland's post-war building boom were bored for a separate knob and deadbolt, and smart deadbolts are designed for exactly that preparation. Allegion's specification for Schlage's smart deadbolts calls for a 1-3/8 to 2-inch door, a 2-1/8-inch or 1-1/2-inch cross bore, a 1-inch edge bore and a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4-inch backset — the standard sizes most of these doors already have. The smart deadbolt goes in the upper hole; the knob or a matching lever stays in the lower one.

Two things can complicate it. First, holes that have been enlarged or moved over sixty years: a previous owner may have added a second deadbolt, fitted a handleset with a thumb-latch grip, or patched a hole badly. We measure the backset and the bore on the day, and fill and reinforce anything the new lock won't cover. Second, the old strike: original strikes are often held by short screws into the trim. We replace them with long screws into the framing so the bolt holds against a kick.

If you'd like the lower hole to carry a matching keyless lever rather than a plain knob, most smart deadbolt lines have one. On doors where the knob is used to latch the door and the deadbolt is the lock, we leave the knob non-locking so nobody is ever locked out by the knob alone.

Our front door swings outward onto the step — does that rule out a smart deadbolt?

No. Most residential smart deadbolts work on in-swinging and out-swinging doors, and many motorised models sense the door's handing during setup. An outward-swinging door does change three things we check. The hinges are on the outside, so the pins must be non-removable or secured, otherwise the door can be lifted off regardless of the lock. The keypad faces the weather more directly, because there's no recess to shelter it. And a storm door is usually impossible, so the lock itself takes the rain.

Out-swinging doors are most common in Rockland on additions and side entrances to lower levels, where an inward-swinging door would hit stairs or a landing. On those, the step outside is often small, and anyone using the keypad stands where the door will swing into them. We position the keypad so it can be reached and read from beside the door's swing rather than in front of it.

For an outward-opening door that's also an exit from a lower-level apartment or playroom, the inside hardware has to open with one motion, without a key or code, like every other exit door we work on.

Rented rooms and accessory apartments

We let rooms in our house to boarders — what does Rockland expect of the locks on those bedroom doors?

Start with the permit, not the lock. Rockland County's Department of Health says it inspects and permits every rooming house in the county each year, and that rooming houses are subject to specific requirements on bathrooms, cooking and dining, door locks, sleeping-room floor space, smoke and carbon monoxide detector locations, and local building codes. Letting rooms to unrelated boarders can turn a house into a rooming house in the eyes of the county and your town or village, and that comes with a permit and an inspection before any lock question.

The county has been actively enforcing those rules. After the 2023 Spring Valley house fire that killed five members of one family, inspectors cited buildings owned by the same landlord for illegal single-room occupancies, illegal conversions and missing smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, and the county had already taken over code enforcement in Spring Valley the year before. A keypad lock on every bedroom door of an otherwise ordinary single-family house is one of the things that makes an inspector look twice.

So we don't fit keypad or keyed locks on bedroom doors in a house unless the owner can show us the space is permitted for that use. Where it is, the locks have to suit the permit's conditions and open from inside with one motion, never needing a key or code to get out. For a legal accessory apartment with its own entrance, we fit that entrance like any other front door: its own lock, its own codes, a plain inside release.

Shuls, yeshivas and community buildings

Our shul is applying for a state security grant — can keypads or fob readers on the doors be part of the request?

Yes. Governor Hochul's September 8, 2026 announcement of the Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes awards lists the improvements the money funds, including lighting, locks, alarms, panic buttons, fencing, barriers, access controls, shatter-resistant glass and security training. Organisations could request up to $250,000 each in that round, and 99 grants totalling more than $24 million went to the Mid-Hudson region. Keypads, readers, electric strikes, door contacts and request-to-exit hardware all fall under locks and access controls.

What matters for an application is that the door work is specific and justified. Grant reviewers and administrators want to see which doors, what hardware on each, and why — a main entrance that needs controlled entry during the week, side doors that need to be locked from outside but stay exits, a school wing that needs separate credentials. We walk the building with your board or security committee, write an itemised quote door by door, and note where hardware choices are driven by egress rules so that nothing in the plan conflicts with the fire code.

Grant programmes have their own rules about procurement, timing and when work can start; follow the programme's guidance and the award letter, and don't commission work before you're allowed to. We'll provide the documentation you need when you're ready.

How do we keep a shul's front door controlled on Shabbat, when many of the people coming in can't use a keypad or a fob?

By planning for Shabbat as its own operating mode rather than an exception. Chabad.org's guidance notes that electronic key cards, fobs and keypads can't be used on Shabbat, and suggests arranging before Shabbat to use a door with a standard key or having staff open the door. For a congregation, that usually means the main entrance is attended during services: a greeter, a security volunteer or a hired guard who sees who is coming in, with the door unlocked only while someone is watching it.

The access control system can support that without anyone touching it on Shabbat. Doors can be set on a schedule before Shabbat begins, so they unlock and relock at fixed times; whether a pre-set electronic schedule is acceptable for your community is a question for your rabbi, and we set it up whichever way the shul decides. Side and rear doors stay locked from the outside all Shabbat while remaining exits from inside with panic hardware. A camera at the entrance gives the person at the door a view of the approach.

During the week, the same doors run normally: codes or fobs for staff, teachers and regular volunteers, schedules for minyanim and classes, and a log the administrator can check. The hardware is the same; only the schedule and the people at the door change.

Shops and offices

Our Route 59 store closes before Shabbat and the closing time moves every week with sunset — can the door's schedule keep up?

Partly, and it's worth knowing which part. Most consumer smart lock apps and many small access control systems schedule by clock time and day of the week, not by sunset, so a schedule set for 3:45 p.m. on Fridays in December is wrong by April. Some smart-home platforms and larger access control systems can trigger on sunrise and sunset or hold a calendar of exception days. Candle-lighting time moves every week and Yom Tov falls on different weekdays every year, so even a sunset-aware schedule needs checking around the holidays.

The arrangement that works for most Rockland shops is simpler. The door relocks automatically every time it closes, so it is never left unlocked overnight or over Shabbat. Opening for the day is done by the first employee with a code. Closing is done by people rather than by a timer, with the last person out checking the door. Staff codes are set to stop working from Friday afternoon to Saturday night, adjusted seasonally, so nobody can open the store during that time by accident or otherwise.

If the store reopens on Saturday night or runs on Sundays, we set a separate weekend schedule. The owner gets a notification if the door is opened outside business hours, which is the alert that actually matters.

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Rockland County

First, how this section was put together. Google's AI Overview was out of reach of the research tools behind this page, so the seven claims below are not its text. They're ideas that recur in national cost guides, product pages and how-to articles, which is the material automated answers are built from, and under each one we set out what Rockland doors actually show.

“Smart lock installation costs $250 to $400”

Fixr's national cost guide, updated in January 2025, puts a typical smart lock installation at $250 to $400, with an average of $300. That figure assumes one ordinary door, one lock and a homeowner who will configure the codes.

A Rockland job is rarely that. It's commonly a split plan — a mechanical Shabbat door, an electronic garage door, a slider lock — with a frame to square up and twenty people to enrol. For community buildings and shops, a keypad with an electric strike is $1,950 to $3,250 installed at our Rockland rate.

The national figure isn't wrong; it just describes a different job. We give you a flat written figure for yours after the walkthrough.

“Most smart locks have a Shabbat mode”

It's easy to assume that a smart lock, like the rest of a connected home, can simply be switched into a Shabbat setting. We haven't found a mainstream residential smart lock that offers one. The Orthodox installer who tested a popular keypad deadbolt in 2017 wrote that a Shabbat mode "would certainly be a game changer", precisely because it didn't exist.

What the locks have are separate settings: auto-lock on or off, sounds on or off, and in some cases a privacy mode. None of them stops the lock logging events. Chabad.org's guidance is that, ideally, smart locks should be disabled before Shabbat.

That's why we plan an observant household's doors around hardware that needs no Shabbat routine at all, rather than relying on a setting the lock doesn't have.

“Go keyless and you'll never carry a key again”

Smart lock marketing promises a keyless life, but every lock we fit still has a mechanical override, and for many Rockland families the key is the Shabbat credential. Chabad.org describes the Shabbat belt, made so that the key itself fastens it, as the practical way many people take a key out on Shabbat where there is no eruv.

A key also covers the rare failure: a dead battery, a jammed motor, a keypad cracked by ice. Mechanical push-button deadbolts avoid both problems, which is one reason we recommend them on the Shabbat door.

Keep at least one key for every electronic lock in the house, and one with a trusted neighbour.

“Sliding doors can't take a keyless lock”

Ordinary smart deadbolts and retrofit units don't fit sliders, and some articles stop there. In fact there are keyless locks made for sliding glass doors, including surface-mounted hook-bolt models. Lockey's C150 is one, described by its maker as mechanical, keyless and weather resistant.

On a raised ranch's deck door, a keyless slider lock plus a security bar and anti-lift blocks is a large improvement over the factory thumb latch.

It's also the kind of door Ramapo police mentioned after the April 2024 attempted break-in in New Hempstead.

“Any handyman can put in a smart lock”

Mechanically, fitting a battery smart deadbolt to a pre-bored door is not hard. Legally, Rockland County has required home improvement contractors to be licensed since 1986, and under Chapter 286 of the Laws of Rockland County, effective May 14, 2026, anyone doing home improvement work on residential property needs a county Home Improvement Licence. The county lists locksmith work and low-voltage systems among its licence classifications.

The county's Office of Consumer Protection keeps an online database where you can check a contractor's licence, and warns that unlicensed contractors may not carry liability, disability or workers' compensation insurance.

Check before you book anyone, us included.

“Put locks on the bedrooms and the house is ready to rent by the room”

Online advice about renting rooms often treats a lock on each bedroom door as the finishing touch. In Rockland, rooming houses are permitted and inspected every year by the county's Department of Health, with rules on door locks among the requirements, and the county has cited landlords for illegal single-room occupancies after deadly fires.

A lock cannot make a rooming house legal, and a keyed or coded lock on the inside of a bedroom door can make a fire far more dangerous.

If you're considering renting rooms or an accessory apartment, talk to your town or village building department and the county first. We'll fit door hardware that matches what the space is allowed to be.

“A smart lock is all the security a door needs”

A good deadbolt that is actually locked protects its door well. That's all it protects. The 2013 daytime burglaries Clarkstown police investigated in Nanuet and New City targeted unoccupied homes during working hours, and police advised residents to lock every window and door.

What a smart lock adds is convenience and information: the bolt goes across even when someone forgets, and the app says when it hasn't. Sliders, ground-floor windows and the door in from the garage still need their own answers.

So the quote we write often has three lines: the locks, a doorbell camera on the front step, and alarm contacts for the openings a lock can't watch.

Commuter Households

Doors for Houses That Stand Empty From 7 to 6

Much of Clarkstown and Orangetown empties out on a weekday morning. Parents drive over the Mario Cuomo Bridge to Westchester, down the Palisades Parkway to the city or across the state line into Bergen County, and the house is left to the school bus, the dog walker, the cleaner and the delivery drivers. Clarkstown police's warning after the 2013 daytime burglaries in Nanuet and New City was aimed at exactly these houses: thieves were choosing homes that were empty during working hours. A smart lock does three useful jobs in a house like that.

  • It closes the door nobody locked. Auto-lock on the garage or side door means the bolt throws a set time after the last person leaves, whether or not anyone remembered on the way to the car.
  • It tells you who came home. Each child's code is logged under their name, so the parent at work sees "Daniel, 3:12 p.m." on the phone and knows the school bus delivered him. If nobody has come in by four, that is worth a call.
  • It lets the day's visitors in without a hidden key. The cleaner's code works on Wednesday mornings, the dog walker's at midday on weekdays, and a contractor gets a code that expires on Friday. The key under the planter, which anyone watching the house could find, goes back in the drawer.

The garage door is usually the one to convert: the family uses it with cars and car seats, it's out of sight of the street, and in a raised ranch or split-level it opens into the lower level. Add a doorbell camera at the front for deliveries and visitors, and an alarm contact on the slider.

One caution: alerts for every opening soon get ignored, so we set them for what matters — the first arrival after school, any opening in a quiet period, a door left unlocked. And agree with older children what the parents will see in the log before the lock goes on.

Rooms and Units

Before Locks Go on Rented Rooms or an Accessory Apartment

Rockland's code enforcement has changed since 2022. This is the checklist we go through before fitting any lock that separates one occupant from another in a house.

  • Is the space permitted? A legal accessory apartment, a two-family house or a permitted rooming house can take separate locks. A single-family house with unrelated boarders behind keyed bedroom doors is exactly what the county's inspectors have been citing.
  • Who enforces where you are? Most towns and villages run their own building departments; in Spring Valley, the county took over code enforcement in 2022. Rooming houses are permitted and inspected by the county's Department of Health.
  • Can everyone get out? Every door on the way out of a sleeping room opens from inside with one motion, without a key or code. We will not fit a keyed or coded lock on the inside of any bedroom or unit door.
  • Do the hall doors still close? In multi-family buildings, self-closing and fire-rated doors must keep closing and latching. Nothing we fit holds them open.
  • Are the detectors there? Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors were among the first things inspectors checked after the Spring Valley fires. We're not the people to install them, but we'll tell you if we can't see any.
  • Who holds the codes? An occupant's door is theirs. The owner keeps a key for emergencies under the lease, not a live view of the occupant's comings and goings.

If you're unsure whether a space is permitted, ask the building department first. We'd rather wait a week than fit hardware that has to come off after an inspection.

Community Buildings

A Door Checklist for Rockland Shuls, Yeshivas and Nonprofits

Most of the community buildings we work on in Rockland are planning door security with a grant application or award in hand. This is what we go through with the security committee.

  • Main entrance: controlled entry during the week by code or fob, a camera on the approach, and a clear plan for who is at the door on Shabbat, Yom Tov and during large events.
  • Side and rear doors: locked from outside, alarmed or monitored, and always openable from inside with panic hardware or a lever. These are exits first.
  • School or office wing: separate credentials for staff, schedules that match the school day, and visitors buzzed in rather than given codes.
  • Power failure: each electrified door specified fail-safe or fail-secure according to its role and the fire code, never by default.
  • Paperwork: an itemised quote door by door, matching the improvements listed in the application, so the award can be checked against the work.
  • Training: who adds and deletes codes, who checks the log, and what happens when a staff member leaves — written down, not kept in one person's head.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Rockland Front Doors

How a Rockland visit runs, step by step — our standing routine, not an account of any particular customer's house

We start by asking which door the family uses on a Tuesday and which on a Shabbat afternoon. In Ramapo the answer is often two different doors, and that single fact decides more about the job than any lock's feature list.

Then we look at the frame before the door. On a split-level or raised ranch we hold the door shut and look at the gap along the latch side, because a house built on two levels settles unevenly and the strike is often a sixteenth of an inch off. We look at the right-hand doorpost too, note where the mezuzah is, and plan the screws and cables around it.

Then the slider. We walk upstairs to the kitchen, lift the slider panel gently to see whether it rides up in its track, and try the thumb latch. Most of the time a bar and a pair of anti-lift blocks are needed whatever lock goes on.

Last, the list of people. We count them with the parents, children by name, staff by day, and only then choose models, because a lock that runs out of code slots in a house of eleven is the wrong lock however good its reviews are.

Do you fit purely mechanical push-button locks as well as electronic smart locks?
+

Yes. Mechanical push-button deadbolts, with a combination that works without batteries and a key override, are one of the most common locks we fit in Rockland, usually on the door a family uses on Shabbat. We fit them the same way as a smart lock: strike squared and reinforced, combination set with you, and the inside tested by hand.

Can the other locks in the house be re-keyed to match the key of the new smart lock?
+

Often. If the smart lock and your existing locks use the same keyway family, the other cylinders can be re-keyed to the smart lock's key so one key opens every door. If they don't, the alternative is to replace the other cylinders with ones that match. We check the keyways at the walkthrough and tell you which applies.

How long will our front door be out of action on installation day?
+

Usually under an hour for a single deadbolt on a pre-bored door, from removing the old lock to a working new one. The door can still be closed and latched while we work. Frame repair, filling old holes or an electric strike takes longer, and we tell you in advance which doors will be affected and for how long.

Can you work at a yeshiva or school building during the school day?
+

Yes, with the school's agreement on timing. We plan noisy work such as drilling frames and running cable for after hours or between sessions, keep a working exit on every door we touch, and leave each door secured at the end of the day. Our technicians check in at the office and follow the school's visitor procedure.

Can the keypad's beeps and voice prompts be turned down so they don't wake the children?
+

On most smart locks, yes. Many models let you lower the volume or switch sounds off in the app or from the keypad, and some keep a single quiet confirmation tone. We set the volume with you at handover and show you how to change it. Mechanical push-button locks make no electronic sounds at all.

What happens if we move house within Rockland — can the smart lock come with us?
+

It can, if your purchase contract doesn't include it with the house. We remove it, fit a standard deadbolt in its place so the house you are leaving stays secure, and install the smart lock on the new door, provided the new door's bores and thickness suit it. Delete all codes and remove the lock from your app account before you hand over the old house.
