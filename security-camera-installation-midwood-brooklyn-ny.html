The Neighborhood

Why Midwood Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Midwood is mostly houses with yards, and a house with a yard can be entered from a side nobody on the street can see. The neighborhood runs roughly from Foster Avenue south to Kings Highway and Avenue P, and from McDonald Avenue east to Nostrand Avenue, with the B and Q trains along East 16th Street and the F along McDonald Avenue. Its old farms were cut into city lots between the 1890s and the 1910s, and the lots were generous enough to take freestanding Victorians on East 19th Street, detached brick houses with bay windows and stoops, semi-detached Tudors and blocks of red-brick two-families on East 13th through East 16th Streets. Six-story prewar apartment houses line parts of Kings Highway, Avenue I and Ocean Avenue, and the shopping runs along Avenue J, Kings Highway, Coney Island Avenue and Nostrand Avenue. For a camera installer that mix means three different jobs: houses that need their backs and sides watched, buildings that need their lobbies, mailrooms and garages watched, and stores that need their counters watched.

The houses are where the local record is clearest. In the fall of 2017, CBS New York reported that detectives from the 70th Precinct were hunting a man linked to at least ten burglaries in Midwood and Flatbush since mid-September. Investigators had pieced together his route from home surveillance along East 31st and East 32nd Streets: he walked in through a front yard, moved from backyard to backyard, took out window screens and climbed in through rear windows. At one house he stacked four chairs to reach a window and was stopped by a window lock, leaving a palm print behind. At another he got in through an unlocked kitchen window and left with a bicycle and electronics. A neighbor, Moshe Loketch, gave police his own video. One resident described it this way: "Very neatly took out screens and got in through windows in the back."

Two years before that, a home camera on Avenue J recorded a man prying open a window at about 1 a.m., removing the screen, climbing in and leaving with a laptop, two credit cards and cash taken from near where the family slept. He put the screen back on before he walked away. "There were 11 people here at the time, and you know, people were awake, and nobody detected him," Yonah Gugenheim told CBS New York. Without the recording, the family might have spent days wondering where the laptop went. That is the lesson Midwood houses teach: the break-in that matters most may leave nothing out of place by morning, and only a camera that was already watching the back of the house can show what happened.

The front of the house still matters, and so do the streets. News 12 reported a package taken from a Midwood porch in 2019, caught on camera, with a neighbor chasing the thief down. In February 2026, News 12 reported that several people forced their way into a home near East 13th Street and Avenue P at about 1:35 a.m. and assaulted a resident. On the commercial side, ABC7 reported that an armed man took $6,000 from a 49-year-old employee at a Coney Island Avenue grocery around 1 p.m. on a December afternoon in 2022, and police released the store's video of him at the register. In June 2026 a man took a cellphone and wallet from a changing room at the Makki Masjid Muslim Community Center, 1089 Coney Island Avenue, and fled south on the avenue. Each of these asks something different of a camera: a face at the porch, a picture of the door in the middle of the night, hands and a face at a counter in daylight, and a view of who walked toward a private room without filming the room itself.

The numbers are moving in a better direction, and we would rather say so than sell cameras on fear. The 70th Precinct, which covers Midwood, Fiske Terrace, Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South from its station house at 154 Lawrence Avenue, recorded 80 burglaries in 2026 through November 8, against 117 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 31.6 percent. Robberies were down 33.9 percent and grand larcenies down 10.2 percent. Car thefts were down 21.8 percent for the year but up over the latest four weeks, 9 against 8. A falling count is good news; it does not mean a particular back window, driveway or register is covered. What the counts do suggest is where a camera earns its keep: the cases that still happen are the ones worth recording clearly enough to close.

The Back of the House

Covering the Route a Midwood Burglar Actually Takes: Side Gap, Backyard, Rear Window

Most camera quotes for a house begin with a doorbell. On a detached or semi-detached Midwood house, the doorbell is often the camera that matters least, because the two best-documented burglary patterns here never touched the front door. Below is how we break the back of a Midwood lot into the stretches a person has to cross, and what a camera has to see on each one.

The way in: the side gap or the driveway

On a detached house, the only way from the sidewalk to the backyard without climbing a fence is usually the strip beside the house: a side driveway, a path to the back door, or the narrow gap between two houses. The 2017 burglar's route started exactly there, through a front yard and around the side. A camera at the front corner of the house, mounted high on the wall or under the eave and aimed straight down that strip toward the back, records everyone who walks into it from the street. It does not need to show a perfect face, because a second camera will. Its job is arrival: the time, the clothing, what the person carried and whether they came alone. Where the strip is a shared driveway or the gap is only a few feet wide, the camera's frame is set to stop at your property line, so the view records your side and not your neighbor's windows.

The approach: the backyard

Once someone is behind a Midwood house, the street cannot see them, and on many blocks the yards run into one another with low fences between. That is how the 2017 burglar moved from yard to yard. A camera on the back wall of the house, mounted high and aimed out across your own yard toward the back fence, shows anyone who comes over or through it, and anyone who walks across the yard toward the house. We set that camera's motion zone on the yard itself, not on the fence line, so a cat on the fence or a branch moving in the wind does not set off an alert every few minutes. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives a neighbor the right to sue over a camera aimed at their backyard with intent to harass, annoy or alarm them, so we aim the yard camera at your yard and use the recorder's privacy masking to black out any part of the neighbor's yard that cannot be kept out of frame.

The point of entry: rear windows and the back door

Both the 2015 and 2017 cases ended at a back window. The camera that records the face is the one aimed at the windows a person can actually reach: the kitchen window over the yard, the first-floor windows at the back, the basement window wells and the back door. A face shows best when the camera is close and only a little above head height, looking at the person as they turn toward the window, so we often mount it on the back wall a few feet to one side of the windows rather than high in a corner of the eave. At night the windows themselves are dark, so the camera needs its own infrared light or a small fixed light nearby. If one camera cannot see both the kitchen window and the back door, two cameras are cheaper than an argument after the fact.

The garage and the alley side

Many Midwood lots end in a detached garage at the back, reached by the side driveway. A garage blocks the view of the corner of the yard behind it and gives a person somewhere to wait out of sight. A camera on the garage itself, or one on the house aimed at the garage door and the gap beside it, closes that blind corner. Running cable to a detached garage usually means an underground conduit or an aerial run from the house, and we price that on its own line so you can decide whether the garage camera is worth it.

The window that looks untouched in the morning

The Avenue J burglar put the screen back. Nothing looked wrong. A camera only helps in that case if someone knows to look, so we set the recorder to keep a searchable list of motion events by camera and hour. In the morning, the back-window camera's event list for the night is a few taps away on your phone: if it shows activity at 1 a.m. when everyone was asleep, you know to look further. Paired with an alarm contact on that window, you would have known at 1 a.m. instead.

Freestanding Victorians: wood, porches and a long back yard

A wood-frame Victorian is the easiest Midwood house to wire and the easiest to damage. Cable drops cleanly from the attic down through closets and interior walls, and the basement gives a route to the back of the house. The outside is the delicate part. A camera screwed straight through clapboard or shingle into nothing can crack the siding and let water behind it, so we mount on a block fixed through to the sheathing and framing, or under the porch ceiling or eave where the camera stays dry. Porch ceilings are often the best spot for the front-door camera: close to the door, out of the weather, and at a height where a face shows. At the back, the long yard and the garage usually mean two cameras, one on the back wall and one at the garage.

Detached brick houses: solid walls and basement routes

Brick changes the drilling, not the plan. Each outside camera needs a hole through solid masonry, which we drill with a masonry bit from inside the basement or a closet where possible, so the cable comes straight out behind the camera base and nothing runs on the face of the brick. We set anchors in the brick rather than the mortar when the camera has to bear weight, and seal every hole against water. The basement usually becomes the hub: a recorder there, out of sight from the front door, with runs fanning out to the front, side and back walls.

Semi-detached Tudors: one shared wall, one open side

On a semi-detached house, half the property's edges are a party wall with the neighbor, so all the exposure is on the open side and the back. The open side, usually a driveway or path, gets the arrival camera; the back wall gets the window and yard cameras. Half-timbered and stucco fronts need care: we avoid drilling through the decorative timbers and look for a plain wall section, a soffit or the underside of the entry arch instead. Cameras on the shared back wall are aimed so they record your half of the yard, and where the two households share the driveway, we talk through the view with both before anything is mounted.

Red-brick two-families: two households, one building

The two-family houses on East 13th to East 16th Streets usually have a main door, a second door for the upstairs unit or a side or basement entrance, and a yard behind. When the owner lives in one unit and rents the other, the cameras covering shared doors and the yard are the owner's, and the tenant may reasonably want to know where they point. We can set up one recorder with separate accounts, so the tenant sees the door they use and nothing else, or two small systems if the households prefer to keep things apart. A side basement door down a few steps needs a camera close in, because the stairwell is dark and narrow.

Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park district houses: the front follows the Landmarks rules

Inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in 2008 and which covers about 250 houses, cameras on the street side of a house go through the Commission. The next section covers the rules in detail. In short, staff can approve cameras, a small house can have no more than two cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage, and the cable has to be hidden behind the wall rather than run along it. Back-of-house cameras are the ones Midwood's burglary record points to anyway, and we plan them alongside the front.

Six-story buildings, a 1962 co-op and a 2020 condo

Midwood's apartment buildings range widely. CityRealty lists 1801 Ocean Avenue as a seven-story elevator co-op built in 1962, with keyless entry, an intercom, a mail room, a live-in superintendent, no doorman and an indoor garage with a waitlist. David Francis NY lists 2025 Ocean Avenue as an eight-story, 16-apartment condo built in 2020, with a virtual doorman service. The prewar six-story buildings on Kings Highway and Avenue I sit between those two. In each, the lobby camera is the easy part. The work is in the mail room, the service door, the garage ramp and the cellar, and in running cable to them through a building where people live. We agree the route with the super or the managing agent, use existing risers and ceilings where they exist, and keep the recorder in a locked room the board chooses.

Avenue J: busy counters and early mornings

On Avenue J, a bakery or a takeout counter can go from empty to a line out the door in minutes, especially before Shabbat on Fridays and before holidays. A camera plan for one of these stores starts with three positions. A face camera inside the front door, set a little above head height and aimed at the door so every person walking in faces it. A counter camera behind and above the register, aimed at the customer side, so the recording shows hands, money and the face of whoever is paying. And an overview camera high at the front looking back through the store, which ties the other two together. If there is a back room or a basement for stock, a fourth camera watches the stair or the back door. Bright winter mornings and lit display cases make faces hard to expose, so we set the door camera for the people coming in, not the street behind them.

Kings Highway: two owners, one front

Many Kings Highway stores sit under apartments that belong to a different owner. The building's landlord wants the residential door and the sidewalk covered; the store wants its own door and counter. Two systems on one facade can work if the cameras are planned together, so one does not stare into the other's door and neither ends up looking into the apartments above. Where the store owner and the building owner are the same person, one recorder with separate accounts is usually simpler. Where they are different, each gets its own system and its own recorder, and the store's lease decides what the store may mount on the outside.

Coney Island Avenue: daytime holdups and long, narrow stores

The December 2022 grocery robbery on Coney Island Avenue happened at about 1 p.m., not after closing. That is a reminder that a store camera plan has to work while the store is full of light and customers, not only when the gate is down. The counter camera matters most: close enough to the register to show a face clearly at the moment of the demand, and recording continuously rather than waiting for motion, because motion detection in a busy store is either always on or set so loosely it misses things. Many stores on the avenue are long and narrow, with racks or shelves down the middle, so the overview camera goes high at the front looking back, and the counter camera goes behind the counter looking at the customer.

Nostrand Avenue and the side streets: the back door

Smaller stores on Nostrand Avenue and on the blocks off the main avenues often have a back door onto a yard, an alley or a service area where deliveries and garbage go out. That door is where a break-in after hours most often starts, and it is also where staff come and go. A face camera just inside it, and one outside looking along the back wall, covers both. Gates and roll-down shutters at the front protect the glass; they do nothing for the back.

Historic District

Cameras on a Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park House: What the Landmarks Rules Allow

The Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District was designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission in March 2008, the 20th historic district in Brooklyn, covering about 250 houses built mostly by two local builders in the early 20th century. Many are in the Arts and Crafts, Colonial Revival and Dutch Colonial Revival styles, which often come with shingle or clapboard walls, porches and broad eaves. If your house is on one of those lots, the cameras on its street side need a permit from the Commission. That sounds heavier than it is. The Commission's own permit guidebook sets out what its staff can approve without a public hearing, and a carefully planned camera job fits inside it.

How many cameras can go on the front

The guidebook says cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and sets a count: on small residential or commercial buildings, "no more than two security cameras can be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage." Large residential, commercial and institutional buildings can have up to three per 25 feet. A house with 50 feet of street frontage would have room for up to four cameras on that side, but each one still has to be placed with the house's details in mind. We measure the frontage, mark the camera positions on a photo of the facade, and show you the plan before it goes to the Commission.

Where the cable can and cannot run

The guidebook is strict about wiring on the outside of the wall. Conduit "must be concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding behind the installation." Where that is truly impossible, staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match the background and kept off decorative surfaces. In practice, on a wood-frame district house, that means drilling through the siding directly behind the camera base and running the cable inside the wall cavity, the attic or the porch ceiling. It suits camera work well: a camera with no visible wire is also a camera that nobody can disable by cutting a cable.

Which cameras look right on these houses

The guidebook describes dome-type cameras finished to match the surrounding materials. On a shingled Arts and Crafts front, that usually means a small dome or turret, painted or ordered in a color that disappears against the trim, mounted under the eave or the porch ceiling rather than on the face of the wall. The application asks for the manufacturer's cut sheet for each camera, and we prepare those along with the photos and the marked-up facade. Replacing an existing camera in kind is listed as not needing new elevation drawings or large-scale details, which keeps a like-for-like swap simple.

The back and sides of a district house

The guidebook's counts are written around street frontage. The cameras Midwood's burglary record points to are on the side gap, the back wall and the garage, which on most district houses are not on the street face at all. We ask the Commission about any camera that would be visible from the street, and we plan the back-of-house cameras together with the front so the whole system goes in at once once the paperwork is clear.

Field Notes: Walking a Midwood Lot From the Back Fence to the Sidewalk

This describes how we approach houses here, not one particular job. We start our Midwood walk-throughs in the backyard, not at the front door. Standing at the back fence, we ask the owner to show us every window a person could reach from the ground, every door, the basement window wells and anything that could be stacked or climbed: patio chairs, a grill, garbage cans, the garage roof. Then we walk the route a stranger would take in reverse: from the back windows along the side of the house, through the gap or down the driveway, out to the sidewalk. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Midwood: Questions Answered

The 2017 burglar on East 31st and East 32nd Streets came in through a front yard and moved from one backyard to the next. Can our cameras follow someone across yards that are not ours?

No, and they should not try. Your cameras record your side gap, your yard and your windows; the neighbor's yard is theirs, and New York's backyard camera law lets a neighbor sue over a camera aimed at their yard to harass or alarm them. What worked in 2017 was several neighbors each having their own cameras and each handing their clips to the detectives, who could then string the route together house by house. If your block wants that kind of coverage, the answer is for each house to cover its own back, with recorder clocks set correctly so the clips line up in time.

In the 2015 Avenue J case, the man put the window screen back after he left. Can a camera tell us in the morning that a back window was opened overnight, even when everything looks normal?

It can tell you something happened near that window, which is enough to make you look. We set the recorder to keep a list of motion events for each camera by hour, so in the morning you open the back-window camera, see whether anything moved there at 1 a.m., and watch those few seconds. A camera cannot tell you the sash was raised. An alarm contact on the window can, at the moment it happens, which is why most Midwood houses that add back-window cameras also put contacts on those windows.

At one house in the 2017 case, the burglar stacked four chairs to reach a window and a lock stopped him. If the lock already did its job, what does a camera add?

It turns a failed attempt into evidence. A stopped break-in is still a crime, and in 2017 the attempts were part of how police tied at least ten cases to one man. Without a recording, a family who finds chairs stacked under a window in the morning has a story; with one, they have a face, a time and the clothes he wore, which detectives can match against other houses. It also tells you which window he chose, so you know where to add a second lock or move the patio furniture.

Our house in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park district has wood shingles and deep eaves. Where can a front camera go without splitting the shingles or showing a wire?

Under the eave or the porch ceiling, almost always. Both are flat, dry and out of the weather, they keep the camera off the shingled face of the wall, and the cable can go straight up into the attic or along the porch framing without ever showing outside. The Landmarks guidebook wants conduit concealed behind the wall and cameras finished to match, and a small dome under the eave in a trim color meets both. Where a camera has to sit on the wall, we fix it to a mounting block set through to the framing, so the shingles carry no weight.

Our Avenue J shop has a line out the door on Friday afternoons. How do we keep the door camera picking out faces when the store is packed?

Put it where people walk toward it one at a time: just inside the door, a little above head height, aimed straight at the doorway. A crowd at the counter blocks a high corner camera, but everyone still passes through the door singly, so that is where faces are recorded. Keep it on continuous recording, not motion, because on a Friday it would trigger all afternoon anyway. The counter camera then shows hands and payment, and the two together give you who and what.

The 2022 Coney Island Avenue grocery robbery happened around 1 p.m. with the store open. What should a counter camera capture in a daytime holdup?

A clear face at the moment of the demand, the hands and what is in them, and which way the person left. That means a camera close to the register, behind or beside it, aimed at the customer side at face height, not a high corner view of the whole counter. A second camera at the door records the person arriving and leaving. In bright daylight the window behind a customer can turn the face into a silhouette, so we set the camera's exposure for the face, and check it on site at midday.

Our Ocean Avenue condo already has a virtual doorman service watching the entrance. Can we add our own cameras without getting in its way?

Usually, yes, as long as the board and the service provider know what is going in. A virtual doorman watches the entrance and talks to visitors; it rarely covers the mail room, the service door, the garage or the roof. We look at what the existing cameras already see, add cameras only where there is a gap, run them to a separate recorder or one the building controls, and keep the doorman service's equipment and network untouched. The condo board decides who gets access to the new cameras.

Our 1960s co-op on Ocean Avenue has an indoor garage. Why would a garage camera be set up differently from the lobby camera?

A lobby is lit, people walk in slowly, and faces are close. A garage is dim, cars come in fast, and the useful details are a plate at the ramp and a person walking between cars. So the garage gets a camera at the ramp aimed and zoomed for plates, set apart from an overview camera that shows the aisles, and both are set for low light. Cable runs in a garage often go in conduit on concrete, and long aisles may need a switch partway along. All of that changes the price, so it is on its own lines in the quote.

The 2022 UJA-Federation fund for small Brooklyn synagogues named Midwood and said packages could include video cameras. What should a small shul have ready before it asks for a camera quote?

Three things make the quote fast and the paperwork simple. Photos of every entrance, including any side or back door and the door to the women's section or a basement hall if there is one. A note of who runs the building day to day and who should be able to see the cameras. And any written requirements from the funder about what the quote must show. We write the quote with every camera, mount, cable run and recorder on its own line, so a funder or a board can follow it item by item.

Federal Nonprofit Security Grant reviews stalled in early 2026. Can a shul or school put in part of a camera system now and add the rest when funding comes?

Yes, if the first phase is planned with the second in mind. We size the recorder and the switch for the full system on day one, so the later cameras plug into ports that are already there, and pull spare cable to the far entrances while the ceilings are open. The first phase covers the entrances that matter most, usually the main door and any side door people use during services. The rest follows without redoing anything.

After the June 2026 theft from a changing room at a mosque on Coney Island Avenue, where can a house of worship put cameras when the room itself has to stay private?

Outside the room, never in it. A camera in the hallway or vestibule, aimed along the corridor toward the changing room door, records everyone who goes in and comes out without seeing inside. A second camera at the building's exit closest to that room shows which way someone left; in the June case the man fled south on the avenue. Rooms where people change, wash or pray privately stay off camera entirely, and we will not install a camera that sees into them.

In February 2026, several people forced their way into a home near East 13th Street and Avenue P at about 1:35 a.m. What should our front-door camera be doing at that hour?

Recording continuously, alerting someone awake if possible, and showing faces at the door even in the dark. At night we set the door camera's alerts to the hours the household is asleep and send them to a phone that will actually be heard, or to an adult relative elsewhere who can call. The camera needs enough light at the door for a face, so we add a small fixed light or rely on the camera's infrared. And the recorder goes somewhere a person coming through the door will not find it in a minute, so the recording survives.

Our six-story building on Kings Highway has the residential door between two stores we do not run. How do we keep the cameras on our door and not the store entrances?

By framing the camera tightly and using privacy masks. The residential entrance camera goes inside the vestibule or just above the door, aimed at the doorway and the steps, not along the sidewalk. If the frame still catches part of a store's entrance, the recorder can black out that area permanently. A sidewalk camera, if the board wants one, is set to show people passing and their direction of travel, not the inside of the shops. We talk to both store tenants before mounting anything on the shared facade.

Who Sees the Footage

Police, Patrols, Boards and Neighbors: Who Gets to See a Midwood Camera

A camera you own records your property, and the recordings are yours. But in Midwood, more people than you might expect will ask about them: detectives after a break-in on the block, a community patrol collecting clips, a co-op board, a tenant, and the neighbor whose yard is next to yours. Knowing the rules before the cameras go up keeps those conversations easy.

The police

After a crime nearby, detectives from the 70th Precinct often ask in person. In January 2024, CBS New York reported that Ring had removed the option for police to request footage through its Neighbors app; police can still knock on doors and ask, post requests in the app, or get a warrant for the video from the company or the homeowner. Albert Fox Cahn of the Surveillance Technology Oversight Project called the change "a big win for privacy and civil rights" but warned that "these systems are just one court order away from being used as a policing tool." With a recorder in your basement, nothing leaves the house unless you export it. We show every owner how to copy a time window to a USB stick or a phone, so that when a detective asks, you can hand over exactly what was asked for.

Community patrols

Volunteer patrols are part of life in this part of Brooklyn. Next door, Boro Park Shomrim asks residents and businesses to register where their cameras are, so that after a crime volunteers can quickly ask the owners nearby for footage. "These cameras are perfectly situated to capture these crimes," a Shomrim representative told BoroPark24 in 2023. Registering a camera's location is not the same as giving anyone access to your recorder; it is a list of who to ask. If you want to take part in a patrol's list, the decision is yours, and nothing we install connects your system to anyone else's.

Stores and the city's camera network

For businesses, the city has built a network on the Fusus platform that lets stores send video from their cameras to the NYPD quickly. PIX11 reported in May 2024 that owners opt in through their business improvement districts and choose how and when the police can reach their cameras. Then-Mayor Eric Adams said: "This tech will allow information to be shared between businesses and the NYPD faster than ever before." If an Avenue J or Kings Highway store wants to join, the recorder we install can usually be connected once you have signed up; if not, nothing changes.

Co-op boards, condo boards and landlords

In a co-op or condo, the lobby, hallways, mail room, garage and service doors are common areas, and cameras there are a board decision. A shareholder who wants a camera at their own door usually needs permission too. In a rental, the landlord owns the common-area cameras and decides who can see them. In a two-family, the owner's cameras on shared doors and yards are the owner's, and a fair arrangement with the tenant, such as an account that shows only the tenant's own door, saves arguments later.

Neighbors

Midwood houses sit close together, and a back camera can easily take in parts of three yards. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets the owner or tenant of a home sue someone who installs a camera on neighboring property to record activity in their backyard without written consent, with intent to harass, annoy or alarm them. We aim every yard camera at your own yard, use privacy masks for anything that cannot be framed out, and are happy to show a neighbor the view on a phone before the camera goes up.

"Start with a doorbell camera at the front door."

For an apartment, or a row house with no yard, that is often right. For a detached or semi-detached Midwood house, it is usually the wrong first camera. The two burglary patterns documented here, in 2015 on Avenue J and in 2017 on East 31st and East 32nd Streets, came in through back windows.

A camera on a detached garage at the back of a deep lot means conduit under the yard or a cable span from the house. You can. Easier to put up, yes. Just as good at the back of a Midwood lot, often not. Wi-Fi from a router at the front of a house has to pass through brick walls, floors and, for a garage camera, open air, and battery cameras need recharging just when you have forgotten about them.

A wired camera gets its power and its connection through one cable, records to a recorder in the house whether the internet is up or not, and does not run flat on a winter night.
