The Neighborhood

Why South Slope Properties Need Access Control

Nobody agrees exactly where South Slope begins and ends. A listing boundary cited by a neighborhood guide runs from Ninth Street down to the Prospect Expressway between Fourth Avenue and Prospect Park West; a Brownstoner commenter in 2008 put it at Ninth to 16th Street; a 2015 Baruch College writing project used 15th to 24th Street, the area the city rezoned in 2005 as South Park Slope. For this page we use the stretch every one of those definitions shares, the numbered streets from 10th to 19th east of Fourth Avenue, and we drew our city-data counts from that window. What happens on those blocks at the street door is the subject of this page.

The housing is mostly small. Brownstoner’s house histories describe blocks that filled in during the 1870s and 1880s with brick row houses and a scattering of frame houses, such as the simplified Italianate semi-detached brick house at 199 17th Street, two stories over a basement on a 25-foot lot, standing by 1880; a commenter on another Brownstoner piece noted many frame houses between about 11th and 15th Street below Fifth Avenue. Above the shops on Fifth and Seventh Avenues are three- and four-story walk-ups. Along Fourth Avenue, the 2005 rezoning allowed larger buildings on lots that had held one-story warehouses and garages, and mid-rise condos and rentals now line the avenue. And a few big old industrial buildings were turned into homes, most famously the Ansonia Clock Company plant that once filled the block from Seventh Avenue and 12th Street to Eighth Avenue and 13th Street and, according to the Brooklyn Public Library, is now a 71-unit co-op.

That mix means the typical South Slope entry is not a staffed lobby. It is a stoop door and a vestibule door shared by two to twelve households, a door beside a storefront that leads to the apartments upstairs, an under-stoop garden-floor door, a hall door to the back yard, and a cellar door. Nobody is at a desk. The building is only as secure as the last person who let the street door close behind them, and whether it actually closed.

The city keeps a record of how often it did not. HPD’s Housing Maintenance Code violations, published on NYC Open Data, include every write-up for a door that is supposed to close by itself and does not. In our 10th-to-19th-Street window, inspectors issued 244 such violations for building entrance and vestibule doors between January 2020 and November 2026: 138 for entrance doors and 106 for vestibule doors, found at 88 buildings among the ones since closed and 23 buildings with violations still open. The count rose from 7 in 2020 to 59 in 2026, the highest year in the period with seven weeks still to go. Almost all of them, 241 of the 244, were Class C, the category HPD treats as immediately hazardous.

Those write-ups are the reason this page starts at the hinges. A fob reader on a door that drifts open, sags on its hinges or has been hooked back for the day is a record of who walked through an open doorway, not a lock. So every South Slope job we take begins with the door: does it swing shut from fully open, does the latch catch without a push, is anything holding it back. Then the reader, the keypad or the video panel goes on, with an electric strike, a door contact that knows whether the door is really shut, and an alert when it is not.

The rest is what residents ask for. A brief Brooklyn Eagle item from August 2019 described three men cutting open packages in the lobby of an apartment building near Sixth Avenue and 16th Street at about 3:10 in the afternoon, taking roughly $450 worth. On r/parkslope, a resident writing about constant vandalism in a building said they “finally forced the landlord to change the front door lock.” Owners of two-families want the garden-floor tenant to have their own code. Co-op boards want a fob list they control. Shopkeepers on Fifth Avenue want the back door to lock behind the last person out. Each of those is covered below.

What the materials mean for the install

The oldest doors on these blocks are heavy wood doors in deep wood frames, set into brick, many still carrying a mortise lock. That is good news for access control. A wood frame takes an electric strike pocket cleanly, and a mortise lock can usually stay, with the strike releasing its latch so the key and the knob keep working exactly as before. The work that takes time is everything around the strike: a frame that has racked over a century so the latch no longer meets the keeper, hinges worn enough that the door drops onto the saddle, and a closer fitted decades ago for a lighter door. We square the frame, replace or shim the hinges and fit a closer rated for the door’s weight and width before cutting the strike pocket, so the strike is set to where the latch will actually be in July and in January.

The walk-ups and condos are a different case. Newer and renovated buildings often have hollow-metal or aluminum-and-glass entrances, and the glass storefront-style doors common on Fourth Avenue lobbies have no room in their narrow stiles for a strike. Those doors usually get a magnetic lock or an electrified exit device, which brings the Building Code’s release requirements with it: a sensor or push-to-exit on the inside, release on loss of power and on fire alarm. Cable routes differ too. A row house cellar ceiling makes a short run to the stoop door; a walk-up’s stair hall may have an old buzzer conduit we can reuse; a concrete condo needs the building’s risers and a low-voltage room. We plan the route on the first visit, not on install day.

244 Street Doors That Would Not Close: What HPD’s Record for 10th to 19th Street Says Before Any Reader Goes On

We pulled this record because it answers a question no installer’s brochure does: what actually goes wrong at South Slope entrances. The source is HPD’s Housing Maintenance Code violations dataset on NYC Open Data. We selected violations whose wording includes “self-closing,” on the numbered streets from 10th to 19th in ZIP code 11215, east of Fourth Avenue by HPD’s own coordinates, with inspection dates from January 1, 2020 through November 12, 2026. Then we sorted them by what the inspector wrote: which door, and which part. The window is ours, drawn to match the blocks every definition of South Slope includes, and the coordinates are HPD’s, so treat the counts as a close pattern rather than a census.

The wording is plain. A typical entry reads “§ 27-2005, 27-2007, 27-2041.1 HMC: REPLACE OR REPAIR THE SELF-CLOSING DOORS THAT IS MISSING OR DEFECTIVE BUILDING FRONT ENTRANCE, 1st STORY.” Another reads “REMOVE DEVICE PREVENTING DOOR FROM BEING SELF-CLOSING HOOK AND EYE AT VESTIBULE DOOR AT PUBLIC HALL.” Across all door types the window produced 762 self-closing violations in the period. Apartment entrance doors were the largest group, at 342. Building entrance doors and vestibule doors, the two doors an access system is built around, came to 244 together. Roof and bulkhead doors drew 42, cellar doors 28, yard doors 23, and the rest were stair and hall doors the wording does not pin down.

How this changes the way we price and install

Read the second table again and notice that not one of those five failures is electronic. The closer, the hinges, the latch and the habit of hooking the door back are mechanical, and every one of them would defeat a fob reader as completely as it defeats a key. That is why our written price for a South Slope street door always says what we will do to the door itself, not just what we will mount next to it, and why the visit includes a close-and-latch test: we open the door fully, let go, and watch. If it does not close and latch on its own from there, in one motion, the first line of the plan fixes that.

It also changes the order of work on install day. The closer goes on and is adjusted before the strike is cut, because the closer decides where the door comes to rest. Worn hinges are replaced before the strike is set, because a door that drops an eighth of an inch moves the latch an eighth of an inch. And a contact goes on the door so the controller can tell a door that is shut and locked from a door that was released and drifted open again, which is the difference between an access system that reports problems and one that hides them.

Finally, it explains why an owner with an open door violation should not wait for the access system to clear it. The violation is about the door closing, which a closer and hinge repair fixes; the reader comes in the same visit or later. We leave dated photos of the finished door, the closer model and the work done, which is useful to whoever handles the building’s paperwork with HPD.

Fifty-Eight Doors Held Open: Fixing the Reason a Hook Goes On

The third most common street-door write-up on these blocks was not a broken part at all. It was a hook-and-eye, a wedge, a strap or some other device that someone fitted on purpose to hold a door open: 58 violations at 35 buildings since 2020, most of them on vestibule doors. A door is hooked back because, for someone, the closed door is a problem. In our experience in brownstone-scale buildings across Brooklyn, the usual someone is a resident wrestling a stroller, a cargo bike or groceries through two doors with a closer fighting them; a courier making several trips to a package pile; a tenant moving in or out; a super hauling bins; or a household that keeps losing keys and wants the inner door to stop mattering. Pull the hook off without changing any of that and it is usually back within weeks.

So we start by asking who hooks the door, and when. The answer decides the fix. Where the problem is force, the closer is the culprit: an oversized or badly adjusted closer can make a heavy vestibule door hard to push and quick to swing back. A closer sized for the door, with its opening force and closing speed adjusted and a delayed-action setting that holds the door for a few seconds before it starts to close, takes most of the fight out of a stroller trip without leaving the door open. Where the problem is time, as with a courier or a move, a schedule or a button can do what the hook did, briefly: the controller can hold the vestibule door unlocked during a delivery window, and a magnetic hold-open wired to release on fire alarm and at a set time can keep a door open for a move and let it close by itself when the day ends.

Where the problem is keys, access control itself is the fix. When the inner door takes the same fob as the street door, there is no second key to forget, and the reason to hook it back disappears. The controller can also release both doors from one tap, in sequence, so a resident with full hands does not have to fumble at the second door. And because each door carries a contact, the system knows when both doors of a vestibule are open at the same time, which is precisely the state a hook creates, and can sound a short chirp at the door and then tell the super or the owner.

What we will not do

We do not install a hold-open that keeps a required self-closing door open with no release, and we do not disconnect a closer because a client asks. Under the Housing Maintenance Code those doors have to close by themselves, and under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a the entrance doors of a class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 must be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty. A hold-open is acceptable only where it is the kind that releases on its own, and where the owner understands that the door must still close every night. If a building’s real problem is that its doors are too heavy for some residents to open, the honest answer is a power operator or a lighter door, not a hook with a nicer finish.

District Lines Around 15th Street: Readers and Panels on Landmark Blocks

Part of South Slope sits inside the Park Slope Historic District and part does not, and the line matters before any hole is drilled. The National Register of Historic Places describes the district as roughly bounded by Prospect Park West, Berkeley Place, 15th Street, and Sixth, Seventh and Flatbush Avenues. The city’s district, which is the one that governs permits, was designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission in 1973 and expanded in 2012, to about 2,575 buildings, and again in 2016, when Extension II added 292 buildings at the north end of Park Slope. So a house on 13th Street near Prospect Park West may be inside the city district while a house on 17th Street off Fifth Avenue is almost certainly not. We do not guess. The owner, or we with the owner’s address, check LPC’s district map before quoting any work on the facade.

Inside the district, the Commission’s Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety equipment is the document that shapes the job. It allows intercom panels and doorbells at staff level, meaning without a public hearing, when they meet its criteria: they “must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening,” or on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway; there is a limit of one intercom panel per door opening; and the design, size and finish should be neutral and discreet. Equipment “must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork,” and not through cast iron or decorative surfaces. Manufacturer cut sheets are required, filed through the Commission’s Portico system, and comparative drawings are not needed for an in-kind replacement of an existing intercom.

The guidebook chapter does not mention card readers or keypads by name. In practice we design a district-block entrance so that the reader is part of the one intercom panel at that door, rather than a second device on the facade, and we choose a panel small and plain enough to read as the kind of equipment the guidebook describes. Where the house has a vestibule, the reader often belongs on the inner door, out of sight from the street, with only a call station outside. We prepare the cut sheets, a photo of the existing door with the proposed location marked, and a short description of how and where the panel is fixed, which is what an owner needs to apply; the application itself is the owner’s, and the work waits for the permit.

Doors and frames on old South Slope houses

Whether or not a house is inside the district, its original door is usually worth keeping. Wood doors from the 1880s in deep frames take an electric strike in the jamb with little visible change, and the strike plate is the only new metal a passer-by would notice. We cut the strike pocket in the frame, not the door, run the cable inside the wall from the cellar or the vestibule, and leave the door’s own mortise lock and key working. On a house that is in the district, the cable is never run on the face of the building; on one that is not, we still try not to, because surface conduit on a brick front is hard to take back.

Factory Co-ops and Fourth Avenue Condos: Many Doors, One List

South Slope’s two biggest kinds of building are its oldest industrial ones and its newest residential ones, and they share a problem the walk-ups do not: more doors than anyone can keep track of by hand. The Ansonia Clock Company plant, which the Brooklyn Public Library says was “said to be the largest clock factory in the world” and now holds a 71-unit co-op, is the best-known example of the first kind. Buildings converted from industrial use tend to have several street entrances, former loading openings, long corridors, courtyards and service areas, each added or kept for a reason that made sense in a different century. The Fourth Avenue buildings that came after the 2005 rezoning are the second kind: a lobby, a package room, a bike room, a gym or roof deck, a garage, a trash room and a service door, often with a developer’s system already on some of them.

In both, the most valuable thing a board can own is one list. One controller, or one cloud account, that holds every resident, staff member, contractor and courier, with every door assigned on purpose. Without it, a converted building ends up with a fob system on the main door, a keypad with a code everyone knows on the side door, keys for the courtyard and the storage rooms, and nobody able to say who can open what. With it, a shareholder who sells loses every door in one step, a contractor’s code ends on the day the job does, and the board can see, when it needs to, which doors are actually used.

The approach is the same for both. First we walk every exterior door and every door into a shared room and put each one in one of three columns: controlled, with a reader; monitored, with a contact and an alert but no reader; or exit only, opening from inside with no way in from outside. Most converted buildings have doors in the third column that nobody has thought about in years. Then the controlled doors go onto networked door modules near each entrance, wired back to one controller in a central room, so cable runs stay short in buildings that are long. Finally the list is built once, with the board, the managing agent and the super deciding who administers it.

Taking over a developer’s system

Many Fourth Avenue condos inherited a system the developer’s contractor chose. Some of it is worth keeping; some of it depends on a login the board never received. Before we suggest replacing anything, we inventory the controller, the readers, the credential type and the software, and we tell the board which parts it already owns outright, which depend on a subscription, and whether the fobs are an encrypted type or older 125 kHz fobs, which carry no encryption and can be copied with cheap readers sold online. Often the right answer is to keep the wiring and strikes, change the credentials and take the administrator account into the board’s name.

The privacy rules in mixed buildings

Where any apartments in a building are rented, HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to fobs, apps and other smart access on the residential doors. Owners must get tenants’ consent, give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, never track tenants’ locations outside the building, and not require smart access for a tenant to get into their own apartment. Rented units in condos and co-ops are covered on those doors too. We set systems up to keep as little data as the board needs and to purge logs on a schedule it chooses.

Rules that apply to South Slope business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on an exit path must unlock on loss of power and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, a sensor on the egress side must release it, and a manual push-to-exit device, mounted 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, must cut power to the lock and leave it unlocked for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock must be released by hardware on the door that works with one hand.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • The apartments upstairs. Under Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a, the entrance of a covered class A multiple dwelling must be self-closing and self-locking, and a building with eight or more apartments needs an intercom with voice and a remote release. A shop that shares an entrance with apartments cannot put that door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Landmark blocks. A storefront or building inside the historic district follows the same Landmarks guidance as a house: equipment within or beside the door opening, fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces.
  • Electrical work. AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431. The 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician, and we tell you before the job if one is needed.
  • Your lease. Most commercial leases require the landlord’s written consent for alterations. We give you a one-page description of the devices, where they go and how they are fixed, to send with the request.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in South Slope: Questions Answered

What the street-door record means for your building

Hinges came up in 59 street-door write-ups on our blocks, and ours was one of them. Can a heavy door that drops on worn hinges still hold an electric strike in line?

Not until the hinges are fixed. A strike is set to meet the latch at one exact height. When a heavy wood or steel door wears its top hinge, the door sags toward the lock side, the latch rides lower in the frame, and over a season it starts to catch the edge of the keeper instead of dropping into it. The lock clicks, the door looks shut, and a firm push opens it. HPD’s inspectors see the same thing from the other side: a door that does not close and latch by itself.

So on a hinge write-up we replace the hinges, usually with heavier ball-bearing hinges and a fourth hinge on a tall door, tighten or re-anchor the frame where the screws have stripped, and only then cut or adjust the strike. The door contact then confirms every night that the door is actually shut, which is the early warning that a hinge has started to go again.

Forty of the street-door violations on our stretch were still open in November 2026, some from 2021. If our closer was replaced long ago, why would our violation still show, and does it hold up an access job?

In HPD’s process a violation generally stays on the record until the owner certifies that it was corrected and HPD accepts the certification, or an inspector confirms the repair; fixing the door does not clear the listing by itself. That is why a building can have a working closer and an open violation at the same time. The building’s managing agent, or HPD directly, can say what is needed for a particular violation.

It does not hold up an access job. We can fit the reader and strike whether the listing is open or closed. What helps the owner is our paperwork: dated photos of the door closing and latching, the closer and hinge models, and what was replaced, so whoever handles the building’s HPD filings has something concrete to submit.

On our blocks HPD wrote up apartment entrance doors for not self-closing more often than street doors. If the building puts fobs on the front, do the closers on our apartment doors still matter?

Yes, and for a different reason. The closer on an apartment entrance door is about fire, not burglary: a door that closes by itself keeps smoke in the apartment it starts in and out of the stair everyone uses to get out. A fob reader downstairs changes nothing about that, and the 342 apartment-door write-ups in our window are a reminder that those closers fail too.

What a building system can add is attention. When we are in a building for the street door, we will look at the apartment-door closers in the hall if the owner asks, and quote any that need replacing in the same visit. We do not put readers on apartment doors unless the owner and tenants want them, and under the Tenant Data Privacy Law a tenant cannot be required to use smart access to enter their own apartment.

Row houses and two- and three-family buildings

We live on the upper floors of our row house and rent the garden floor, whose door is under the stoop. Can the tenant’s door and our parlor door share one controller without either household seeing the other’s comings and goings?

Yes. One controller can run both doors as two separate groups. The tenant’s fobs or codes open the under-stoop door, and the yard door if the lease gives them the yard; yours open the parlor door and whatever else you choose. Each household’s entries are logged only to that household’s group, and the owner as administrator can be set up so that routine log viewing for the tenant’s door is off unless there is a reason to look, such as a forced-door alert.

Two practical points. The controller belongs on the owner’s side of the house and the owner’s electrical circuit, so a tenant never has to enter the owner’s space to reset anything. And if the tenant also has a key to the parlor door, for the shared hall or the boiler, that key is replaced with a fob that opens only what they need, so the list of who can open the owner’s door is one the owner wrote.

Our building has had months of vandalism in the hall, and nobody can say how many keys to the front door are out there. How do we find out before deciding what replaces the lock?

You usually cannot, which is the point. A standard front door key can be copied at any hardware store, and in a building with years of turnover the honest count is “more than we know.” A resident on r/parkslope who described constant vandalism said they finally forced the landlord to change the front door lock, and that is the right first step: until the lock changes, every old key still works.

The choice is what the new lock is. A new keyed cylinder restores control for as long as nobody copies the new keys. Encrypted fobs, enrolled one per person, cannot be copied at a kiosk, and a fob that goes missing is cancelled in a minute instead of starting the problem over. Where vandalism is happening inside, a camera in the hall and a contact on the door give the owner a record of when the door was opened and by whose fob, which a key never can.

Our row house has three apartments, which makes it a multiple dwelling. Does the rule that street doors close and lock by themselves apply to a building that small?

New York’s Multiple Dwelling Law covers buildings with three or more families, and section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968; in older buildings the same equipment is required when a majority of tenants ask for it in writing. The intercom requirement with voice and a remote release applies at eight or more apartments. Which of those applies to a particular three-family is a question for the owner’s attorney or HPD, because it depends on when and how the building was built and filed.

Our design answer does not wait for the legal one. A three-family’s street door that closes and locks by itself, with fobs or codes for each household and a call button for visitors, is simply the safer door, and it costs no more to build it that way from the start.

Walk-ups, condos and co-ops

Our walk-up has no live-in super and the managing agent works from an office in another borough. Who can issue or cancel a fob on a Saturday, and do they need to come to the building?

Nobody needs to come to the building, if the system is set up for it. A controller with a secure cloud or remote connection lets an administrator add, suspend or delete a fob or code from a laptop or phone anywhere. Most small buildings name two administrators, often someone at the managing agent and one owner or board member, so a weekend lockout or a lost fob does not wait until Monday.

Issuing a brand-new fob still needs the physical fob, so we leave a few spare, unassigned fobs in a locked place in the building or with a trusted resident; the administrator assigns one remotely and the resident picks it up. Phone keys skip that step entirely. And the street door keeps a restricted mechanical override for the night everything else fails.

The lobby door of our Fourth Avenue condo is a glass storefront-style door. One installer quoted a magnetic lock and another an electrified exit device. Which is right?

Either can be right, and the door decides. Narrow aluminum stiles on a glass door rarely have room for an electric strike, so the choices are a magnetic lock on the header or an electrified exit device, a push bar that the controller can release. A magnetic lock is simpler to fit, but the Building Code requires release hardware on the door that works with one hand, a sensor on the egress side, release on power loss and on fire alarm, which adds parts and wiring. An exit device keeps the door latched mechanically and always lets people out with a push, at a higher hardware price.

For a busy condo lobby we usually prefer the exit device, because the door stays latched even during a power failure and the push bar is what residents expect. Where the existing door cannot take one, a listed magnetic lock with proper release hardware is a sound choice. Ask both installers to show the release hardware in their quotes; if one is missing, the quote is incomplete.

A 2019 report described three men opening packages inside a lobby near Sixth Avenue and 16th Street in the middle of the afternoon. If people are already inside the lobby, what can access control do about the packages?

Less than people hope, at the lobby itself. Once someone is through the street door, whether by tailgating a resident, being buzzed in as a “delivery,” or finding the door unlatched, the packages on the floor are open to them. Access control does two things before that moment: the door closes and latches every time, so nobody walks in on a door that drifted open, and visitors are let in through a video panel, so a resident can see who they are buzzing.

The bigger change is where the packages go. A parcel cabinet or a small locked package room on its own reader, with a one-time code for couriers, takes the boxes out of the open lobby. In a walk-up with no room for either, the vestibule between the two doors can be the drop point if the inner door is on fobs and stays shut, so a courier reaches the vestibule and no further.

Shops, studios and the avenues

Our dance studio on Seventh Avenue runs children’s classes after school. Can parents’ codes work only around their child’s class, and can the street door stay locked while classes are on?

Yes. Each family gets a code that works on the days of its child’s class, from a set time before the class to a set time after pickup. Between those windows the street door stays locked, and a parent arriving early rings a video station that staff answer from the front desk or a tablet. Staff and teachers keep their own codes with their own hours, and a code for a family whose term has ended stops working on the last day.

The door still opens from inside with one motion, because it is an exit, and it releases on fire alarm. If the studio is on an upper floor above a shop, the street door may also serve apartments, in which case it must close and lock by itself all day and the studio’s schedule applies only to the studio’s own door.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in South Slope

These are generalizations we see repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides, including Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a South Slope door actually needs. We do not attribute a claim to a source unless we quote it.

“Access control is about the reader and the credential.”

Most guides spend their pages on fobs, cards, phone keys and software. On South Slope’s blocks, HPD’s record points somewhere else: of 244 street-door self-closing write-ups between 10th and 19th Street since 2020, the named problems were closers, hinges, hooks holding the door open, latches and doors never set up to close. None of them is electronic.

A reader bolted beside a door with a dead closer is a record of who walked through an open doorway. It is not security, and it does not satisfy an inspector.

The credential matters, and we install encrypted ones. But the job starts with a door that swings shut from fully open and latches without a push, and the price says so.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

That is the price of a lock in a box. Kisi’s cost guide puts installation at $1,200 to $2,500 per door without existing locks and $500 to $1,500 with working locks, and VIcom sums up a typical door at “$1500 to $2000 per door installed.” An IPVM forum contributor estimated four to eight hours of work for a typical door.

On our Brooklyn list one controlled door with a reader or keypad, a strike, the wiring and ten fobs is $1,500 to $2,500; a video panel with fob access replacing a buzzer is $1,500 to $3,000.

A door needing a new closer and hinges moves within that range rather than adding a surprise. We put the figure in writing after one visit, with sales tax added.

“A smart lock does the same job for less.”

On one lightly used door, a good smart lock is a fair choice, and we install them. On a walk-up’s street door used by a dozen households, couriers and a super, it is the wrong tool: battery life drops with heavy use, codes are shared, and a smart lock cannot make the door close.

It also cannot talk to the vestibule door, the yard door or the garden-floor door as one system, or keep separate lists for the owner and the tenants.

A wired controller keeps the credentials on site, runs each strike from a battery it reports on, and treats every door as part of one list. Where one door cannot be wired, a smart lock that reports to that controller fills the gap.

“Landmark buildings can’t have keypads or readers.”

Quick answers often treat a historic district as a no. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s guidebook allows intercom panels and doorbells at staff level when they sit within or beside the door opening, one panel per opening, neutral and discreet, fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork.

It does not name card readers or keypads, so the practical route on a district block is a single discreet panel with the reader built in, or a reader on the inner vestibule door, out of view.

The owner applies, with cut sheets we provide, and the work waits for the permit. Many South Slope blocks south of the district’s edge need no Landmarks review at all, which is why we check the map first.

“Fobs end the problem of propped doors.”

Fobs end one reason for propping, a second key to forget. They do nothing about a vestibule door that is too heavy to push with a stroller, a closer that slams, a courier making six trips or a moving day. On these blocks, 58 street-door violations since 2020 were for a hook or other device holding a door open.

Taking the hook off without fixing the reason it went on is how the same door is written up again a year later.

We fix the closer’s force and timing, use schedules or a fire-alarm-released hold-open for moves, and put a contact on each door so the controller knows when both vestibule doors are open at once.

“Fail-safe locks are the safe choice.”

The phrase sounds reassuring. A fail-safe lock unlocks when it loses power, so on a row house’s stoop door or a walk-up’s street door every outage leaves the building open, and a building that must keep a self-locking entrance has an unlocked one.

For most street and house doors the right choice is fail-secure: the door stays locked from outside without power and always opens from inside with the handle, so nobody is trapped.

Fail-safe hardware belongs where the Building Code requires release on power loss, such as maglocks on exit paths. Every fail-secure door we install keeps a restricted override key.

“Any fob system is more secure than keys.”

A fob system is only as secure as its credentials and its door. Older 125 kHz fobs carry no encryption and can be copied with cheap devices sold online, which makes them little better than a key from the hardware store.

What actually improves security is encrypted credentials that cannot be cloned, one per person, a list someone reviews, and a door that closes and latches every time.

In a building where the closer fails each winter, a closer service plan does more for security than any credential upgrade, and we will say so in the quote.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover a row house stoop door and a converted factory co-op. What sets South Slope apart is steps two and four: we test every door before we quote it, and we fix the closer, hinges and latch before any electronics go on.

1. Photos and any HPD notice

You send each door from outside and inside, and any violation for it, and tell us who uses it: owners, tenants, shareholders, staff, couriers or a cleaner.

2. Close-and-latch test

On the visit we let each door swing shut from fully open and write down what stops it closing and locking by itself: closer, hinges, latch or a hook.

3. Written door plan and Brooklyn price

Every lock, reader, contact and controller, what each door does in an outage, any Landmarks or board approval needed, and the price, in writing.

4. Fix the door first

Closer, hinges, latch and strike plate repaired or replaced so the door closes and locks without a push, and any hook or wedge removed and the frame patched.

5. Mount and wire

Reader or panel at the door opening, strike in the frame, controller and battery indoors, cable inside the building, never on a district facade.

6. Enroll people and set schedules

Fobs, codes and phone keys per person, separate groups for owners and tenants, hours for staff and expiry dates for helpers.

7. Test on battery and fire alarm

Each door tested on mains, on battery and with power removed, exits opened from inside, and alerts confirmed to the right phones.

8. Handover and a return visit

Administrator logins, a door list, dated photos of the finished door, and a visit after the first weeks to adjust the closer, schedules and alerts.

A five-minute door check every spring and fall

Twice a year, when the weather turns and doors swell or shrink, open the street door and the vestibule door fully and let go. Each should close and latch in one motion, without slamming. Look for a hook, strap or wedge that has appeared. Check the system for its battery status and any held-open alerts that were ignored. Then print the list of active fobs and codes and cancel any that belong to a former tenant, contractor or helper. A door that passes this check is a door an inspector will pass too.

Field Notes

Field Notes: We Let Go of the Door Before We Quote It

This describes how we start on a South Slope building, not a particular customer. Before we look at a reader or ask about fobs, we open the street door all the way and let go. Then we do it again with the vestibule door, the yard door and the garden-floor door. Each one tells us something: a door that stops an inch short has a tired closer, a door that bangs and bounces open has one set too hard, a door that closes but does not latch has a hinge or latch problem, and a door that never moves has a hook somewhere. We look for the hook too, and for the screw holes of the last one.

Then we ask who uses the door, and how. Strollers, bikes, deliveries, a super with bins, a tenant who moves out next month. The answers usually explain the hook. Only after that do we talk about readers and fobs, and by then most owners have decided that the first line of the quote should be the closer.

South Slope Access Control FAQ

How is a South Slope job priced when the door needs a new closer and hinges as well as a reader?
+

As one controlled door on the Brooklyn list, $1,500 to $2,500 with the reader or keypad, strike, wiring and ten fobs. How many hours does one South Slope street door take to finish?
+

Most single doors take most of a working day, including the closer and hinge work, the strike, the reader, the cable and testing. A vestibule pair, a door with a racked frame or a long cable run can take longer. The door stays usable with a key for most of the day, and we tell residents in advance when it will be briefly open.

Does a cloud-managed system for a South Slope co-op carry software fees, and can it run without them?
+

Cloud management usually carries a subscription set by the software maker, and we show it in the quote before you choose. A local system with the credentials stored on the controller has no monthly fee and keeps working without internet; the trade-off is that changes are made on site or over the building’s own network rather than from any phone.

Can we fit our own smart lock on the garden-floor door and have you do the street door?
+

Yes. Many owners do exactly that. If the smart lock is a model that can report to our controller, we can bring it onto the same list; if not, it stays a separate app. Either way the street door, which every household uses, gets the wired reader, strike and closer work it needs.

Will a fob system lower the insurance on a South Slope building?
+

It may or may not, and only your insurer or broker can say. Some cost guides claim discounts for access control, but we do not promise one. What we provide is a description of the system and the doors it covers, which is what a broker will ask for if the carrier offers a credit.

What should the written quote for a South Slope door actually list?
+

Every device by type, the lock and what it does in an outage, the closer and hinge work, the release hardware on any exit door, the controller and battery, the number of credentials, any software subscription, who supplies the electrical outlet, the approvals needed and the price with tax. If a quote leaves out the door hardware, ask why.

What mechanical way in stays on a South Slope street door after fobs go in?
+

A restricted-keyway cylinder whose keys cannot be cut at a hardware store, held by the owner, the super or managing agent and perhaps one resident. It exists for the night the power and the battery are both gone. As one lawyer told Brick Underground, if nobody can get into the building there needs to be a backup when the fobs do not work.

Is the closer and hinge work covered under the same warranty as the reader?
+

Products AESS supplies, including closers and hinges we fit, are covered on the terms set out in the pricing section of this page. Existing door hardware we did not supply, and damage from abuse or a door being hooked open, are not covered. Our warranty page lists every exclusion.

Can you tell by looking at our old fobs whether they can be copied?
+

Usually, by reading one with a test reader on the visit. Older 125 kHz proximity fobs carry no encryption and can be copied with cheap devices; encrypted fobs cannot. If yours are the older type, the readers and fobs can often be changed while the strike, cable and controller stay.

How many fobs come with a single controlled door, and can we order more later?
+

Ten programmed fobs come with the single controlled door line of the Brooklyn list. Extra fobs are quoted in writing at the time, and phone keys can be issued instead for residents who prefer them. Every fob is enrolled to a named person, so a lost one is cancelled rather than copied.

Will you take off the old hook-and-eye and fill the holes when the closer goes on?
+

Yes. The hook, eye and any strap or wedge come off, the screw holes in the door and frame are filled, and the closer is set so there is no reason to put the hook back. We do not patch or paint walls, so larger repairs to plaster around a frame are left for a painter.
