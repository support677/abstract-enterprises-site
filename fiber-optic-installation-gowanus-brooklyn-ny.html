The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

For most of the last century Gowanus was where Brooklyn made things and dumped what was left over. Tinware, paint, gas, electricity for the trolleys and ink all came out of buildings along a 1.8-mile canal that the Environmental Protection Agency added to its Superfund list in March 2010. Zoning kept the area industrial long after most of the industry left. When Lightstone opened its rental building at 365 Bond Street in March 2016, 6sqft reported that its two buildings were the only new residential developments under way in the neighbourhood, because the rules allowed little else.

That changed in November 2021, when the City Council approved the Gowanus rezoning. As 6sqft summarised the plan, it upzoned 82 blocks, made room for nearly 8,500 homes with roughly 3,000 of them affordable, and allowed mixed-use towers of up to 30 storeys around the canal and up to 17 storeys on Fourth Avenue. The deal carried $174 million for sewer upgrades and $200 million for nearby NYCHA developments. Five years on, the buildings are arriving: 6sqft's February 2026 guide to new Gowanus rentals lists Society Brooklyn's two 21-storey towers on Degraw and Sackett Streets, 420 Carroll's 360 apartments, and a 27-storey, 1,100-unit building planned at 175 Third Street, among others.

Every one of those buildings needs fiber, and almost none of them can take it the way a tower in Downtown Brooklyn or Long Island City does. The usual city pattern is simple: the provider's cable comes out of a street vault, enters through the cellar wall, and lands on a backboard in a cellar telecom room. City Planning's flood-resiliency summary for the rezoning says new buildings in the floodplain must meet the flood-resistant construction standards in Appendix G of the City Building Code, with homes above the flood elevation and mechanical equipment allowed on roofs. Appendix G in turn requires utilities and their attendant equipment to sit at or above the design flood elevation, or be built to keep water out. A cellar telecom room beside a tidal canal is exactly the wrong answer.

The ground is the second constraint. Many rezoned lots were once factories, coal yards and gas works, and the new buildings sit on cleaned-up soil with barriers and venting systems under the slab. When Hoodline described Maison Bond, a 14-storey building at 335 Bond Street finished in 2026, it noted remediation under the state's Brownfield Cleanup Program, including a sub-slab depressurization system against vapour intrusion. Every hole a fiber installer makes through that slab, and every pipe chase it shares, is part of somebody's environmental sign-off.

The third is the old Gowanus that never left. In October 2019 the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated five Gowanus buildings as individual landmarks, among them the 1884 Somers Brothers tinware factory on Third Street, later the American Can Company, and the Brooklyn Rapid Transit power station engine house on Second Street, since reopened as the Powerhouse Arts fabrication centre. Arts Gowanus says its open-studios weekend, held this year on October 17–18, brings more than 400 artists, businesses and venues together. Those studios, workshops and small manufacturers live in brick buildings joined to their neighbours, with floors at different heights and decades of cable nobody has labelled. Getting fiber through them is a different trade from wiring a new tower, and owners of either often pair it with structured cabling in Brooklyn for the copper on each floor.

Two Gowanuses on one block

Stand on Bond Street or Nevins Street and you can see both neighbourhoods at once: a new building of glass and brick on one side of the street, a two-storey brick workshop with a roll-up door on the other. They need fiber for opposite reasons. The new building has pathways drawn on its plans but has to keep every one of them out of the water and out of the slab. The old workshop has no pathways at all, only walls, and the network has to find its way through a building that was never meant to carry one.

Most of our Gowanus work falls into one of three shapes. A developer or general contractor needs the provider entrance, main room and riser in a new building planned before the drywall closes. An owner of an older factory complex needs its separate wings tied back to one room so tenants can share a provider. And a studio, shop or small manufacturer needs a single clean, documented link from wherever the provider landed to wherever the work actually happens. The rest of this page takes each of those in turn.

Raised Provider Entrances

Main Rooms Above the Water

Backboards, racks, splice enclosures and power for the building's main telecom room on a raised ground floor, a mezzanine or the second floor, with the slack and labels that let several providers share it.

New-Building Riser Backbones

Single-mode or OM4 cable from the main room to a closet on each residential floor, spliced into floor enclosures, firestopped at every slab and tested before the corridors are finished.

Gowanus-Mix Ground Floors

Separate strands, enclosures and labels for the light-industrial, arts and retail spaces the rezoning puts under the apartments, so a workshop tenant never shares a closet with the residential network.

Factory-to-Factory Links

Fiber between the joined or neighbouring wings of an old industrial complex, through party walls, across courtyards and over the steps where one building's floors meet another's at a different height.

Studio and Workshop Drops

One or two strands from the building's room to a single studio, shop or rehearsal space, terminated in a small wall box so the tenant can plug in a switch and stop living on Wi-Fi.

Walkway, Yard and Loading-Door Feeds

Outdoor-rated fiber in conduit to cameras, access points and lighting controllers on canal walkways, rear yards and loading doors, with enclosures mounted high enough to stay dry.

Splicing, Termination and Repair

Building Specialists

Fiber Optic Installation for Gowanus's Architecture

Six building types make up most of the neighbourhood. Each changes where the fiber can enter, where the main room can live and how cable crosses floors.

Canal-Side Towers Built Under the Rezoning

Two-tower and podium buildings on the canal blocks with public walkways along the water, ground-floor retail or arts space, and mechanical equipment pushed to the roof. Fiber follows the same logic: entrance and main room up out of the floodplain, riser closets stacked above them.

Fourth Avenue Mid-Rises

Concrete apartment buildings of roughly 11 to 13 storeys, many planned after the avenue's 2003 rezoning, some with shops at street level and some, from the first wave, with blank ground floors. Closets are often small, and the owner's own backbone usually has to share them with the providers' cable.

Nineteenth-Century Factory Complexes

Brick and timber or brick and steel buildings, often several joined together around a yard, now let as studios and workshops. No risers, few drawings, floors that don't line up between wings, and a landlord who wants every new hole explained.

Individual Landmarks

Buildings such as the 1884 tinware factory on Third Street, the 1911 flushing-tunnel pumping station on Butler Street and the 1908 Montauk Paint building on Second Avenue. Exterior conduit and new openings on the protected facades need Landmarks approval; interior routes usually decide the job.

One- and Two-Storey Industrial Sheds

Low workshops, garages and warehouses on the industrial blocks toward Second Avenue and the canal, with open steel roofs, roll-up doors and offices tucked in a corner. Fiber here is about distance and height, not floors.

Row Houses and Small Mixed-Use Buildings

Three- and four-storey brick houses and store-and-apartment buildings on the side streets toward Park Slope, Carroll Gardens and Boerum Hill, where a single cellar-to-top run and a careful exterior entrance are the whole job.

Commercial Fiber Optic Installation

Business Fiber Optic Installation in Gowanus

Gowanus business addresses fall along six stretches, and each has its own building stock and its own reason to want fiber.

Third Avenue

The neighbourhood's main commercial street, anchored by the 56,000-square-foot Whole Foods at 214 Third Street with its 20,000-square-foot Gotham Greens greenhouse. The special district requires a 17-foot total sidewalk here, and new buildings carry shops, showrooms and offices at street level.

Fourth Avenue

Rezoned in 2003 for residential buildings up to 120 feet, then given the Special Fourth Avenue Enhanced Commercial District in 2011 after complaints about blank ground floors. Shops and services under the apartments each want their own link back to the building's room.

Bond and Nevins Streets

The canal blocks themselves, where the new towers stand on former industrial lots and the remaining workshops, fabricators and storage buildings keep operating beside them. Fiber here is new-construction work on one side of the street and retrofit on the other.

Union, Carroll and Third Street Crossings

The streets that bridge the canal: the 1905 Union Street and Third Street bascule bridges and the 1889 Carroll Street retractile bridge. Businesses and studios line the streets that lead onto them, and the cleanup team warns that bridge openings for vessels can close roadways and cause detours.

Butler, Douglass and Degraw Streets

The head of the canal, where the city's main sewage-overflow tank is planned on Butler and Nevins Street parcels, the flushing-tunnel pumping station stands, and Society Brooklyn's towers rise along Degraw. Construction staging will shape access here for years.

Second Avenue to Hamilton Avenue

The southern industrial end, with the Sanitation Department facility at Second Avenue and Fifth Street named for the second overflow tank, and the city-owned Public Place site near Smith and Fifth Streets, planned as the 950-home Gowanus Green once the former gas-plant cleanup is resolved.

Compliance callouts for Gowanus fiber

  • Flood-resistant construction. In special flood hazard areas and shaded X-zones, Appendix G of the NYC Building Code applies. Section G304 places utilities and attendant equipment at or above the design flood elevation in ASCE 24, Table 7-1, unless they are built to keep water out. We design the main room and entrance on that basis and ask the architect for the project's design flood elevation in writing.
  • Firestopping. Every penetration of a rated floor or wall is sealed with a listed firestop system and photographed. In new buildings, the inspector sees those seals before the certificate of occupancy; in old factories, they are often the first rated seals the wall has ever had.
  • Environmental controls under the slab. Buildings remediated through the state Brownfield Cleanup Program or the city's E-designation process can have vapour barriers and sub-slab depressurization systems. We don't core a slab-on-grade or touch a vent riser without the owner's environmental consultant agreeing to the location and the seal.
  • Landmarks. Five Gowanus buildings became individual landmarks in 2019. New exterior conduit, boxes or openings on their protected facades need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit; we plan interior routes first.
  • Licensing. AESS holds NYS Low-Voltage licence #12000287431. Electrical circuits feeding a new main room are installed by the owner's licensed electrician; we specify what the room needs.

These notes describe how we plan work, not legal or engineering advice. The project's architect, engineer and environmental consultant have the final word on their building.

The Flood Line

Keeping Gowanus Fiber Above the Water

The canal floods. During Hurricane Sandy, the Brooklyn Eagle reported, its water "gushed over its banks", flooding nearby streets and basements, and weeks later residents were still complaining that some landlords had not pumped the cellars out. The water that came in was not clean seawater; the Eagle's report describes residents worried about high levels of bacteria and the canal's industrial contaminants. Any network equipment that sat in those cellars was finished, and any cable that stayed had to be dried, tested and in many cases replaced.

The rezoning was written with that in mind. City Planning's flood-resiliency summary for the canal corridor puts homes above the flood elevation, lets non-residential ground floors either be dry-floodproofed or raised, and allows mechanical equipment and critical systems on the roof. It also raises the land along the water: the waterfront access plan lifts the walkway area toward the height of future daily high tides. A new canal-side building, in other words, is designed so that nothing important lives low. Fiber has to follow the same rule, and in our experience that is where Gowanus projects most often go wrong — a carrier's standard drawing still shows the entrance in the cellar.

Schematic only. Heights are illustrative; the real design flood elevation comes from the project's architect and the FEMA maps Appendix G refers to.

Four decisions we make before any cable is ordered

Which frontage the providers use. Gowanus streets fall toward the canal. A lot that runs from Third Avenue down to Bond Street, or from Fourth Avenue down toward Nevins, can have one frontage noticeably higher than the other. We push for the provider entrance on the higher side and as high in the wall as the vault and the carrier's bend rules allow.

Where the main room lives. The building's main telecom room belongs on the first floor that is safely above the design flood elevation — a raised ground floor, a mezzanine or the second floor. That means agreeing a room on the architectural plans early, because once the retail and amenity spaces are let, nobody gives up a dry room for racks.

What is allowed to stay low. Some things cannot rise: the conduit from the street vault has to pass through the foundation somewhere. We make that passage a watertight sleeve with no splice, no slack loop and no equipment anywhere below the flood line, so a flooded cellar wets a sealed tube rather than a rack.

How the building's own systems reach the cellar. Cameras, leak sensors and pump alarms still need to be down there. They get a short, sealed feed from a switch above the water, with any electronics in the cellar treated as replaceable and mounted as high as the room allows.

Brownfield Ground

Working Over Cleaned-Up Ground in Gowanus

Gowanus is a patchwork of remediation. The canal itself is under an EPA cleanup that started dredging and capping the upper segment in late 2020 and, according to the cleanup team's own update for the week of July 6, 2026, is now installing bulkhead supports in the middle segment between Third Street and Hamilton Avenue. On land, many rezoned lots were cleaned up before or during construction; the former Citizens gas-plant site at Public Place, where Brownstoner reported in March 2026 that the cleanup had stalled over a dispute between National Grid and the state, shows how long that can take.

For a fiber installer the result is a building whose lowest slab is more than a floor. It can hold a vapour barrier, a gravel layer with perforated pipes, and a fan-driven sub-slab depressurization system that vents up through the building to the roof. Those parts are there because an environmental consultant designed them and a regulator accepted them. Breaking one is not a fiber problem we can splice our way out of.

Schematic only. The actual layers, and where a penetration is allowed, come from the building's remedial design and its environmental consultant.

How we keep the ground's protections intact

  • Ask first. Before the walkthrough ends we ask whether the building has a vapour barrier, a sub-slab system or a site management plan, and who the environmental consultant is. If the answer is "don't know", we treat the slab as protected.
  • Come in through walls, not floors. Provider and backbone cable enter through wall sleeves above the slab wherever possible, so the barrier underneath is never opened.
  • Keep out of vent chases. The depressurization pipe usually rises in a chase to the roof. Fiber gets its own sleeve beside it; it is never tied to the vent pipe or run through the same sealed opening.
  • Photograph every seal. Each penetration we make is photographed before and after sealing, and the photos go in the closeout file for the consultant and the owner's records.
  • Leave the yard alone unless the plan allows it. Capped rear yards and courtyards are not trenched without written approval of the method and depth, which is why most of our outdoor Gowanus work runs on walls and in surface conduit.
Your Questions Answered

Fiber Optic Installation in Gowanus: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up on Gowanus walkthroughs and that our other Brooklyn pages don't already cover. Each answer is how we handle it; the building's own architect, engineer or consultant decides where their systems are concerned.

The flood line and the street grade

Which parts of a fiber system can stay below the design flood elevation in a new canal-side building, and which have to go up?

Only the passive pathway should stay low. Appendix G of the City Building Code puts utilities and their attendant equipment at or above the design flood elevation unless they are built to keep water out, and City Planning's resiliency rules for the rezoning assume critical systems live high. We read that conservatively for telecom: the conduit or sleeve that carries the provider's cable from the street vault through the foundation can be below the line, as long as it is sealed and has no splice in it. Everything that can be harmed by water — splice enclosures, patch panels, switches, provider terminals, batteries, power supplies — goes in a room above it.

Cable itself survives a brief soaking, but connectors and splice trays do not, and floodwater beside this canal is dirty. So we also keep slack coils and service loops above the line. If a building still has to put a provider's demarcation box low because of the carrier's own rules, we ask the carrier to accept a short sealed extension to the raised room instead, and we record their answer.

Our lot runs from a higher avenue down to a lower street near the canal — which frontage should the provider's fiber come in on?

Usually the higher one. The ground in Gowanus falls toward the canal, so the same building can have its Third or Fourth Avenue side a few feet above its canal-side street. An entrance on the high side keeps the sleeve further above the flood line and shortens the distance to a raised main room. It also keeps provider work off the side of the lot where the public walkway, retail terraces and flood barriers are being built.

The deciding factor is where the providers' street vaults and manholes actually are, so we ask each carrier for its nearest structure before the foundation drawings are fixed. If the only vault is on the low street, we plan a sleeve that climbs inside the building to the raised room rather than a room that drops to meet the cable.

Schematic only; real grades vary block by block.

The shore walkway on our lot has to stay open to the public — can conduit for its lights and cameras run underneath it?

Often, but it has to be designed with the walkway rather than added later. The Gowanus waterfront access plan, written into the Special Gowanus Mixed Use District, requires shore public walkways with at least a 10-foot clear path, wider on some parcels, and raises the land along them. That landscape is built in layers over the bulkhead and its fill, with planting beds, ramps and drainage, and on many lots over remediated ground. Conduit belongs in the layers the landscape architect and civil engineer set aside for site utilities, with pull boxes in paving, not in planting beds.

We supply the conduit sizes, bend counts and pull-box spacing for the cameras, access points and lighting controllers the owner wants, plus outdoor-rated fiber and enclosures mounted above the flood line on poles or the building wall. Because the walkway is a public space the owner maintains, we keep all of it on the owner's network and label it so a future maintenance crew can find it without digging.

Brownfield buildings

The vapour-mitigation vent pipe runs up the only chase to our roof — can fiber share that shaft?

It can share the shaft, but never the pipe or its seal. A sub-slab depressurization system works by pulling soil gas out from under the building and blowing it out above the roof, so its riser pipe has to stay airtight from the slab to the fan. We run fiber in its own sleeve or innerduct beside the vent pipe, fixed to the chase wall rather than to the pipe, and we firestop our sleeve at each floor separately from the vent's own seals.

Before work starts we tell the building's environmental consultant where our sleeve will go. If the chase is too tight for both, the answer is a separate path, usually a closet stack or a corridor soffit, not squeezing cable against a pipe whose leaks would put vapour back into the building.

Can cellar flood sensors, sump-pump alarms and the vapour-mitigation fan alarm report to the super over the building's fiber?

Yes, and in a canal-side building it is one of the most useful things the backbone does. Leak sensors, pump controllers and the fan's pressure alarm are usually low-voltage devices that report to a small controller or a building-management panel. Put a switch on the building's own network in a dry spot near them, connect it to the main room over spare strands, and their alerts reach the super's phone or the management office instead of a buzzer nobody hears in an empty cellar.

We keep these systems on their own network, separate from tenants' and residents' internet, and we leave the alarm wiring and its programming to the system's installer. Our part is the dependable path: a fiber link that keeps working when the cellar floods, because the electronics at its upper end sit above the water.

Factories, studios and makers

Our complex is several old factory buildings joined together and the floors don't line up — how does a backbone cross from one section to the next?

At the stairs, or at a place where both buildings already share a level. Gowanus factory complexes grew one building at a time, so a second floor in one wing can sit between the second and third floors of the next. We look for the existing openings first: a doorway in the party wall at a landing, an old shaft, a corridor that already steps up half a flight. Cable crosses there in a short vertical jog inside a closet or along the stair wall, then continues level in the next wing.

Each wing gets its own small enclosure where the backbone enters it, so the wings can be repaired or re-let separately, and the strand map names the wings the way the landlord and tenants do. Where joined buildings have separate owners, the link needs a written agreement between them before we drill the party wall.

Schematic only; every complex is different.

Where should fiber run in a fabrication shop with an overhead crane?

Outside the crane's envelope, never across it. A bridge or gantry crane needs clear space above its rails and beneath its beam for the hook, the load and the trolley's full travel, and anything hung in that space eventually gets hit. We run fiber along the building walls below the crane rail line or above the crane's highest point at the roof steel, in armoured cable or conduit, and bring drops down the columns at the edges of the bay rather than from the middle.

We ask the shop for the crane's rated clearances and walk the travel with the operator before fixing anything. Drops to machines on the floor run in floor-mounted raceway or protected column conduit, so the network never hangs where a lifted sheet of steel or a timber beam can swing into it.

Our ceramics studio fires kilns for hours at a time — can fiber or network gear go in the kiln room?

Fiber can pass through; electronics should stay out. A kiln room gets hot, its exhaust and ventilation hoods take up the ceiling, and firings give off fumes that are hard on switches and power supplies. We route the cable along the cooler wall, away from the kiln's clearances and hood, in a metal raceway, and keep any enclosure or switch in the next room or in a ventilated cabinet outside the space.

If a kiln controller or room camera needs a connection, it gets a short copper or fiber drop from that outside switch. Cable jackets have temperature ratings, so we check the space's highest likely temperature with the studio, and we never fix anything to the kiln, its hood or its vent duct.

New towers on the canal

Our new building is going for Passive House certification — how are cable holes kept from spoiling the airtightness test?

By planning every penetration of the airtight layer and sealing it with the tapes, gaskets or grommets the envelope designer specifies. A Passive House building is pressure-tested against a strict air-leakage limit, and one unsealed sleeve through the air barrier can be enough to fail it. We send the envelope consultant our list of penetrations — the provider entrance, any roof or wall feed, and riser sleeves that cross the air barrier — before installation, and agree the seal for each.

Inside the airtight envelope, riser sleeves between floors are firestopped as usual. Where possible we pre-install spare sleeves and pull strings during construction, so later providers and tenants never need a new hole through the finished envelope. Every sealed penetration is photographed for the certifier.

Can conduit for rooftop equipment cross a green or blue roof without voiding the membrane warranty?

Yes, if it rides on the roof rather than through it. Green and blue roofs, which store or slow rainwater, sit on a waterproof membrane carrying a manufacturer's warranty, and an unauthorised fastener or penetration can void that warranty. For antennas, rooftop cameras or equipment on the roof, we run outdoor-rated fiber in conduit on non-penetrating supports above the membrane and bring it into the building through a penetration the roofer has already built.

We ask the owner for the roofing manufacturer's details and coordinate with the roofer so the crossing of planting trays or detention layers is agreed in advance. Where a new penetration is unavoidable, the roofer makes and flashes it, not us.

Matching the cable to the Gowanus job

A new tower usually gets a 24-strand OS2 backbone from the raised main room to every riser closet, because the building will host several providers and several private systems for decades. A factory complex usually gets 12 single-mode strands between wings, enough for tenants' service, the landlord's cameras and doors, and spares. A single studio or workshop usually needs two strands and a small wall box, and occasionally a short armoured run if the drop crosses a working floor.

Owner fiber, a provider circuit or more Wi-Fi

Owner fiber is the cable inside your property that you own, which any provider can use. A provider circuit is the service a carrier sells and maintains up to its own box. Wi-Fi is how devices reach the network inside a room. Gowanus clients sometimes buy one when they need another: a studio building with slow Wi-Fi on the top floor needs owner fiber to an access point up there, not a faster plan; a new building that wants residents to choose providers needs owner pathways and a shared room, not a deal with one carrier.

Beside the Gowanus Canal the basement is the part of the building most likely to flood, and the City's own flood rules expect utilities to sit at or above the design flood elevation.

Block by Block

How Fiber Work Changes Across the Gowanus Map

Gowanus is small enough to walk end to end in twenty minutes, but the job changes every few blocks. This is how we read the neighbourhood when a call comes in.

The head of the canal: Butler, Douglass and Degraw Streets

The northern tip of the canal is where the City's sewage-overflow tank work concentrates. City Limits reported in 2017 that the main 8-million-gallon tank would occupy three private parcels at 234 Butler Street and 242 and 270 Nevins Street, and that 19 businesses would be displaced. The flushing-tunnel pumping station stands on Butler Street, and Society Brooklyn's towers now rise along Degraw. For a business that stays here, the practical issues are construction access, vibration near open excavations, and providers' cable that may be rerouted as streets are rebuilt. We test a link before and after any nearby heavy work so a change in the readings is on record.

The canal blocks: Bond and Nevins between Union and Ninth

This is where old and new stand closest together. On one side of Bond Street a new building has its raised lobby, walkway and riser closets; across the street a fabricator or storage business works out of a single-storey shed. New-construction work here is mostly about getting the drawings right; retrofit work is about distances inside open buildings and keeping cable out of the way of forklifts, carts and cranes. Maison Bond at 335 Bond Street, finished in 2026, is typical of the newer pattern: Hoodline describes 73 rentals over roughly 10,900 square feet of ground-floor commercial space and 4,600 square feet of manufacturing space.

The crossings: Union, Carroll and Third Streets

The three northern bridges tie Gowanus to Carroll Gardens, and their streets carry much of the neighbourhood's foot traffic. The cleanup team's updates warn that bridge openings for vessels can close roadways and cause detours, which matters when a crew is unloading reels and ladders. We plan deliveries for these blocks with that in mind and keep vans off the bridge approaches.

Third Avenue

Third Avenue is the commercial spine, and the Special Gowanus Mixed Use District gives it a wider, 17-foot sidewalk requirement for new buildings. Shops, showrooms and offices here usually want a clean link from the building's room to the back of the store for registers, cameras and Wi-Fi, without opening finished ceilings. The older buildings on the avenue are low, so the work is horizontal: through a cellar where it is dry, or along the ceiling of the ground floor where it isn't.

Fourth Avenue

Fourth Avenue is a different building type altogether: concrete residential mid-rises from the 2000s and 2010s, with the 2021 rezoning allowing up to 17 storeys. Their closets are often tight and shared with providers, and they sit further from the canal than the waterfront blocks. A typical job is an owner's backbone for cameras, door entry and amenity Wi-Fi added to a building whose providers already occupy most of the closet space, which means finding spare sleeves, adding innerduct and labelling carefully.

The south end: Second Avenue to Hamilton Avenue

At Fourth Street the Fourth Street Basin branches east off the main canal, and south of it the land is more industrial. The Department of Sanitation facility at Second Avenue and Fifth Street was named in 2017 as the site for the second, 4-million-gallon overflow tank, and the cleanup team's 2026 updates note Huntington Street east of Smith Street closed for staging. The Public Place site, planned as Gowanus Green, waits on its cleanup. Fiber work here is mostly in workshops and warehouses: long, open runs at height, armoured cable at floor level and links to cameras on yards and loading doors.

Building During the Cleanup

Fiber Work Next to an Active Canal Cleanup

The Gowanus cleanup is not finished, and it will not be for years. EPA's July 2024 release put the middle segment's dredging and capping at an estimated $369 million over several years, with the lower segment to follow, and the City's sewage tanks are still being built. Owners planning fiber work beside all this need to think about three things that owners elsewhere in Brooklyn don't.

Access changes week to week. Streets close for staging, bridges open for barges, and bulkhead crews take parking. We check the cleanup team's weekly updates and the City's street-closure notices before scheduling, and we agree a fallback day with the client in case the block is shut.

Vibration and settlement near the bulkhead. Driving supports, excavating and dredging next to old buildings can shake them. Fiber tolerates vibration well, but connectors, enclosures on loose fixings and cable pulled tight across a crack do not. For buildings right on the canal we fix enclosures solidly, leave slack where cable crosses between old and new structures, and record test readings before nearby heavy work starts.

Paperwork reaches further than usual. General contractors on new canal-side buildings often ask for method statements, insurance naming several parties and site safety training for anyone on the job. We prepare those before mobilising, because waiting for them on site costs everyone a day.

None of this requires anything from the EPA for ordinary work inside a building. It does reward an installer who knows the neighbourhood is a construction zone and plans for it.

New Construction

When Fiber Belongs in a New Gowanus Building's Schedule

Most of the money a developer can save on fiber is saved before the building exists. A sleeve cast into a slab costs very little; the same opening cored through finished concrete, scanned first and firestopped afterwards, costs many times more. In Gowanus the stakes are higher, because the cheapest-looking place for a telecom room, the cellar, is the one place the flood rules push equipment out of. This is the sequence we suggest to developers and general contractors building under the rezoning.

A simplified sequence; real projects overlap these stages.

Design. The main telecom room is placed on the first floor above the design flood elevation and sized for several providers plus the owner's own systems. The provider entrance is put on the higher frontage, and the architect shows the route from the street vault to the room on the drawings, including which parts are sealed passive pathway below the flood line.

Foundation and slab-on-grade. Entrance sleeves and duct seals go into the foundation wall, specified with the waterproofing and, where there is one, the vapour-barrier designer. No fiber pathway is planned through the slab-on-grade unless the environmental consultant has approved it, and the sub-slab vent chase gets a neighbouring sleeve for cable.

Superstructure. Riser sleeves are cast into each floor slab in a stacked closet, with at least one spare per closet and pull strings left in. In buildings with ground-floor arts or light-industrial space, a separate sleeve to that space keeps its network apart from the residential riser.

Fit-out. Once the closets are dry and lockable, the backbone is pulled, spliced into floor enclosures and firestopped. Connectors stay capped until the dust settles, because new buildings are full of drywall dust that ruins end faces.

Occupancy. Every strand is tested and labelled, providers are given their landing positions in the main room, and the owner receives the strand map, test files and seal photographs. When the first residents move in, the building can offer them a choice of providers without anyone opening a wall.

Studios and Makers

Fiber for Gowanus Studios, Workshops and Small Manufacturers

The Special Gowanus Mixed Use District was written to let light manufacturing and housing live side by side. It rewards new buildings that include "Gowanus mix" space — arts, light industrial and similar uses — with extra floor area, and requires owners who take that bonus to record restrictions and report on the space each year. In practice that means a growing number of studios and workshops on the ground floors of new apartment buildings, alongside the long-established ones in the old factories.

Makers' networks are different from offices'. A woodshop or metal shop wants a few hard-wired connections for a CNC controller, a design workstation and a camera on the door, in a room full of dust, sparks or vibration. A ceramics studio has kilns and their ventilation. A print or textile studio has large machines that need to reach file servers. A rehearsal or performance space wants lighting and sound control alongside guest Wi-Fi. Few of them need many strands; all of them need the cable to survive the work.

So we build studio drops to be tough and simple: armoured or conduit-protected cable on working floors, enclosures off the floor and away from heat and spray, a small switch where the tenant can reach it, and labels in the tenant's own language for the room. In shared factory buildings, each tenant's strands are kept separate at the landlord's main enclosure so one studio leaving never cuts off another.

For the landlord, the same backbone usually carries building cameras and door controllers. Our commercial security camera installation in Brooklyn and access control teams work from the same strand map, so the building's own systems are never patched into a tenant's port by mistake.

Field Notes on Gowanus Buildings

How our installers approach a building near the canal — standing practice, not an account of one particular job

The first question on a Gowanus walkthrough is not about cable. It is "where did the water get to last time?" Owners and supers who were here for Sandy usually know to the step, and that answer decides where the main room goes more reliably than any map.

The second habit is looking down before drilling. In a new building that means asking about the slab and any vent pipes; in an old factory it means checking the floor level on the other side of a party wall before committing to a hole that might come out halfway up a neighbour's wall.

The third is treating every tenant's work as the reason the building exists. A studio mid-firing, a shop mid-order or a rehearsal mid-run gets our schedule, not the other way round, and the crew leaves the space as it found it.

Gowanus Fiber Optic Installation FAQ

Do you also work in Carroll Gardens, Park Slope’s Fourth Avenue blocks, Boerum Hill and Red Hook?
+

We do. The canal sits near the middle of the area our Troy Avenue crews cover, so a job on Smith Street, Fifth Avenue, Atlantic Avenue or Van Brunt Street is as routine for us as one on Bond Street, and it is priced from the same Brooklyn table.

Does fiber work inside a building beside the Superfund canal need any sign-off from the EPA or the state?
+

Ordinary cabling inside a building does not need EPA approval; the canal cleanup covers the waterway and its sediments. What can need sign-off is work that disturbs a remediated site, such as cutting a slab over a vapour barrier or digging a capped yard, which goes through the owner and its environmental consultant under the site’s cleanup plan.

Our shop ceiling is packed with fume-extraction ducts — where does the fiber go?
+

Along the walls or on its own support below or beside the ducts, never strapped to them. Exhaust ducts get cleaned, rebalanced and replaced, and some run hot. We route fiber in raceway or J-hooks fixed to the structure, keep clear of access panels and fans, and leave any switch outside the dirtiest air.

Our affordable building is HPD-assisted — does the city’s broadband guidance decide where the fiber enters?
+

It shapes the system but not the exact room. HPD’s guidance describes fiber from the street vault to the basement and risers to each floor, with at least 100 Mbps per apartment at no extra cost to tenants. In a Gowanus floodplain we follow that pattern while keeping the switch and splices above the flood elevation, and we confirm the layout with the sponsor and HPD’s broadband team.

Can a fiber link carry DMX lighting control or show control for a performance or event space?
+

Yes, once the control signal is converted. DMX is usually sent over Ethernet or through fiber converters at each end, which keeps long runs between a control booth and a stage or rigging point free of electrical noise. We install and test the fiber; the lighting company configures the control equipment.
