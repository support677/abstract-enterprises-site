The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need Alarm Installation

Carroll Gardens is a compact grid of about forty blocks in northwest Brooklyn. The usual definition runs from Degraw and Warren Streets in the north to Ninth Street or the Gowanus Expressway in the south, and from Hoyt and Smith Streets in the east to the trench of the Brooklyn–Queens and Gowanus Expressways in the west. Cobble Hill lies to the northwest, Boerum Hill to the northeast, Gowanus to the east, Red Hook to the south and the Columbia Street Waterfront District across the expressway. The neighborhood takes its name from Charles Carroll, the only Roman Catholic signer of the Declaration of Independence, whose name was already on Carroll Street and Carroll Park, and from the gardens in front of its houses. Until the 1960s most people simply called the area South Brooklyn or Red Hook.

Those gardens are not decoration added later. In 1846 the surveyor Richard Butt laid out the oldest section with unusually deep blocks so that the houses sit well back from the street, and a law of the same period required buildings between Henry Street and Smith Street to leave 33 feet 5¼ inches of courtyard between the house and the street. The Department of City Planning still describes the gardens on First, Second, Third and Fourth Place that way, and notes that on those streets the City Map is drawn 100 to 130 feet wide because the mapped street includes the deep front yards as well as the sidewalk and roadway. In practice the sidewalk and roadway together are only about fifty feet wide; everything between the sidewalk and the stoop is garden. Other guides describe the gardens as roughly 33 to 45 feet deep, edged with wrought-iron fences and shaded by trees.

The houses themselves are mostly three- and four-story single- and multi-family rowhouses, according to City Planning, with a few five-story apartment buildings, local shops on Court and Smith Streets, churches and schools. Many were built in the building boom that followed the draining of the Gowanus marshes and the digging of the canal, from the late 1860s to the early 1880s. A small part of the neighborhood is landmarked: the Carroll Gardens Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on September 25, 1973, covers 134 brownstone rowhouses on Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt, built between 1869 and 1884 with uniform setbacks, even cornice lines and fenced gardens. The famous Places west of Court Street are outside that district, so the rules about the front of a house differ from one block to the next.

The police record in Carroll Gardens points at doors, locks and windows rather than anything clever. In November 2008 the Brooklyn Paper reported that burglaries in the 76th Precinct, which covers Carroll Gardens, Cobble Hill, Red Hook and parts of Gowanus and Boerum Hill, were up 24.3 percent for the year, 97 against 78, with the activity centered in Carroll Gardens; most break-ins happened in the daytime between 8 a.m. and 5 p.m., some windows were found unlocked, and the deputy inspector called it “not a very sophisticated type of burglary” and urged residents to lock windows, not just close them. In August 2015 DNAinfo counted at least seven home burglaries in Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill in a single week and reported that precinct burglaries were up 61.7 percent for the first seven months of the year. In most of those cases a door lock was broken while the owner was away; in one, on the 300 block of Smith Street, the thief came through a back door that did not lock; in another, on Union Street, an air conditioner was pulled out of a rear window near the fire escape; and on Dennett Place, the one-block mews between Luquer and Nelson Streets, a witness watched thieves work at a door with a crowbar.

Smaller items fill in the picture. A 2020 police report described a basement apartment in a multi-family building on Union Street between Clinton and Court Streets that was broken into some time over four days. A 2011 Patch report described a burglar who climbed through an open window into a first-floor, street-facing apartment on Sackett Street between Hoyt and Smith Streets while the resident slept. Brooklyn Paper blotters have recorded a highchair taken from a stoop near Court Street, a bicycle taken from in front of a house on Union Street, and a bike chained to a vestibule banister on Smith Street that left with part of the banister. In February 2026 News 12 reported a man breaking into a Carroll Gardens home at about 1:30 in the morning while the woman who lived there was asleep. None of this describes a neighborhood under siege; the precinct ranked 37th safest of 69 patrol areas in 2010 and its crime fell more than eighty percent between 1990 and 2018. It does describe exactly which openings and habits an alarm design should start with.

Put those facts together and a Carroll Gardens alarm has a particular shape. The garden screens the front of the house from the sidewalk, so the parlor windows and the under-stoop door need the same attention most rowhouses give only to the back. The gate is where life actually happens: visitors, couriers and neighbors stop there, and a doorbell or chime belongs there rather than thirty feet away. Outdoor detection among plantings and trees has to be avoided or handled carefully. And the back of the house, with its yard door, fire-escape windows and air-conditioner sleeves, matters as much as anywhere in Brooklyn.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Carroll Gardens’ Architecture

Carroll Gardens has a short list of building types. Each asks something different of an alarm, and the garden in front changes several of them.

Set-back brownstones on the Places

The houses on First, Second, Third and Fourth Place between Henry and Smith Streets, set behind gardens of about thirty-three feet. Three or four stories with a stoop, a garden-floor door beneath it, a cellar and a rear yard. The front is hidden from the sidewalk, and the gate is far from the panel.

Historic district rowhouses

The 134 brownstones on Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt, built 1869–1884 in late Italianate and neo-Grec styles, with fenced front gardens and even stoops. Exterior work here falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission, so sirens and boxes stay inside or at the rear.

Rowhouses with shallower yards

On Union, President, Carroll and Second Streets the garden pattern continues with yards that vary in depth. A shorter garden means the gate is closer to the house, and a wireless gate device usually reaches the panel without help.

Houses with a garden apartment

Many houses are an owner’s duplex or triplex above a garden-floor rental with its own door under the stoop. One panel can be divided so each household arms its own floors, and the shared vestibule and cellar get their own zone.

Plainer rowhouses and tenements toward Hoyt and Bond

East of Smith Street the buildings are more modest, often with little or no front garden. Here the openings face the sidewalk directly, and the rear and the roof matter most.

Mixed-use buildings on Court and Smith

Shops on the ground floor with apartments above, cellars below and a back door onto a yard or passage. The shop and the apartments need separate systems or separate areas, and the cellar needs its own protection.

Mews and small lanes

Dennett Place, the one-block mews between Luquer and Nelson Streets, has small houses facing each other across a narrow lane. Doors are close to the lane and to each other, so pre-entry shock sensors and a fast-reporting communicator matter.

Churches, schools and halls

Large rooms with high ceilings, several entrances and irregular hours. Detectors are chosen for the height and width of the room, and each entrance gets its own named zone.

Newer infill townhouses and small condos

Buildings put up since the 2000s, often with open interiors and accessible walls during construction. The cheapest time to wire them is while the walls are open.

How we work in each kind of Carroll Gardens building

Brownstones with brick party walls and plaster. In an older rowhouse the garden and parlor floors can usually be wired up through the floor from the cellar, with cable dropping into closets and behind baseboards, while the upper floors use wireless devices because fishing cable through plaster and lath means opening walls. A hybrid panel accepts both, so the design does not have to choose one method for the whole house.

The distance to the gate. A wireless device thirty-odd feet out in the garden, behind a brick front wall and a stone stoop, is a long way from a panel in the cellar. We read the signal from the gate before committing to wireless, and where it is weak we add a repeater near a front window or run a cable through a conduit under the path. If the garden is being redone, a spare conduit costs very little and solves the question for good.

Original sash and old doors. Old wood windows and heavy doors need slim surface contacts, or recessed contacts set into the frame where the wood allows it, positioned so the magnet still lines up when the sash swells in summer. On double doors at the back, each leaf gets its own contact, including the one that is normally bolted.

Mixed-use and shop buildings. Drop ceilings and cellars make cable easy to hide, but walk-in coolers, compressors, heaters and ovens make motion detection harder. We choose detector types and aiming angles for those conditions and test with the equipment running.

Landmarked blocks. On Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt, we keep sirens, boxes and cable off the façade and use interior sirens. If an owner wants an exterior device, we put it at the rear where possible and supply the details needed for any Landmarks Preservation Commission application.

The Garden Setback

Thirty-Three Feet of Garden: Where a Carroll Gardens Alarm Begins

Most Brooklyn alarm advice assumes a rowhouse whose front door opens onto the sidewalk. In that kind of house, the street is a deterrent: anyone working at the front door or a front window is seen by everyone walking past, so the front gets basic protection and the money goes to the back. In the oldest part of Carroll Gardens the arithmetic changes. Between the sidewalk and the house there is a fence, a gate, a garden of trees and shrubs, and a stoop that throws the garden-floor door into shadow. From across the street you can see the house; you often cannot see what someone is doing at the parlor window or the door under the stoop.

So the first design decision is where the alarm boundary goes. It goes on the walls of the house: every door, every reachable window, the cellar. It does not go on the fence. The garden is full of things that move: branches, falling leaves, cats, squirrels, neighbors returning a stray parcel, a courier who lets himself in to put a box on the stoop, a gardener on a Tuesday. A siren that answers to the garden will go off for all of them, and a system that goes off for nothing is soon left disarmed. The gate gets something gentler: a contact that chimes inside the house, or a notification on your phone, with no power to sound the siren or call the monitoring station.

The second decision is which openings to treat as most exposed. Because the garden screens the front, the parlor windows and the under-stoop door move up the list. They get contacts like every other opening, and where the windows are large or numerous a glass-break detector covers the room. Where a door has been forced before on a block, or where the lock is old, a shock sensor on the door reports pressure and prying before the door opens. The 2015 burglaries DNAinfo described mostly started with a broken lock, and on Dennett Place a witness saw a crowbar in use; those are the moments a shock sensor is built to catch.

The third decision is where the visitor-facing devices go. A video doorbell beside the parlor door is thirty feet from the person who rang. In Carroll Gardens the doorbell, the intercom or the keypad station usually belongs on the gate post, and its signal or cable has to cross the garden. That is a design question, not an afterthought: we test wireless reach from the gate before recommending it, and when the path is being dug up anyway, a conduit under it is the best investment in the whole job.

The fourth decision is light. A garden that is pleasant by day is dark at night, and darkness is what a burglar at the front of a set-back house relies on. Stoop and garden lights that switch on when the alarm trips, or when the gate opens after dark, take that away. They also show the household and the neighbors exactly where something happened.

Finally, there is the question of who owns the garden. City Planning says the City Map counts these yards as part of the mapped street, and a 2008 Board of Standards and Appeals filing on Second Place described the adjacent gardens as land “claimed to be City-owned”. We are not lawyers and do not offer a ruling on that. It is one more reason our designs keep permanent equipment on the house itself and treat anything fixed to the fence or set in the garden as an option the owner chooses with that in mind.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation on Court Street and Smith Street

Carroll Gardens’ shops sit on two parallel streets. City Planning’s 2008 study describes commercial overlays on some blocks of Court and Smith Streets within an R6 residential district, with local retail, service businesses and houses of worship among the rowhouses, and lots owned by New York City Transit along the F and G line that runs below and along Smith Street. The rest of the neighborhood is houses, so almost every shop has apartments above it and neighbors on both sides.

Court Street is the older Italian-American shopping street. Brooklyn Magazine’s 2014 guide to the Carroll Gardens stretch named Caputo’s Bake Shop, opened in 1904 on the block between Sackett and Union Streets, D’Amico Foods near Degraw Street, in business since 1948, a social club at the corner of Fourth Place, restaurants between Fourth and Fifth Place, and the old Hamilton Savings Bank building between Luquer and Nelson Streets. G. Esposito & Sons, which moved its pork store to Court Street in 1977 after opening at Columbia and Union Street in 1922, closed in April 2023 when no buyer came forward. The street mixes century-old food shops with cafés, bookshops and restaurants, and many of them have basements, walk-in coolers and back rooms that open onto yards.

Smith Street has been a restaurant and boutique strip since the 1990s, with bars and kitchens that stay open late and staff who leave long after the street has gone quiet. Police reports from the street describe the openings that matter: the 2015 burglary through a back door that did not lock on the 300 block, shoplifting from pharmacies and shops, and the 2015 theft of a bicycle chained to a vestibule banister on the 200 block. The Carroll Street and Smith–Ninth Streets stations on the F and G bring foot traffic at both ends.

For a shop or restaurant, the alarm plan starts at the back. The front of most storefronts is glass behind a rolling gate, lit and visible to the street; the back door, the yard, the cellar hatch and the stockroom are not. We contact every door, protect the cellar, put a motion detector in the stockroom and the office, add a glass-break detector behind the display window, and place the keypad where closing staff actually leave. Opening and closing reports tell the owner who armed the system and when, and a panic button at the register reports silently.

Food shops with coolers and cellars

Walk-in coolers, compressors and heaters on Court Street. Detectors chosen and aimed to ignore cold air and cycling equipment, cellar doors and hatches contacted, and temperature sensors in the cooler if you want them.

Late-closing restaurants on Smith Street

A keypad at the exit staff really use, an arming route that does not cross the dining-room detectors, and closing reports so the owner knows the last person armed the system.

Offices, studios and practices

Upper-floor and ground-floor offices in mixed-use buildings, with their own area on a shared panel or a separate system, and user codes that can be deleted the day someone leaves.

Social clubs and community rooms

Rooms used a few evenings a week. Schedules that remind someone to arm, and alerts if the door opens outside the usual hours.

Rules that shape a Carroll Gardens business alarm

  • Who may install it. New York State requires anyone in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems to hold a Department of State security or fire alarm installer license under General Business Law Article 6-D. AESS holds New York State license #12000287431; ask any installer for theirs.
  • How long a siren may sound. NYC Administrative Code §24-238 says an audible burglar alarm on a building must be capable of terminating, and must terminate, automatically within fifteen minutes of activation. With apartments above most shops, we set the cutoff on every commercial siren and check it at handover.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. A restaurant or shop that needs a fire alarm system follows FDNY and Building Code requirements for design, installation by appropriately licensed contractors and acceptance testing. Smoke detectors on a burglar alarm panel do not replace a required fire alarm system; see our Carroll Gardens fire alarm installation page.
  • Apartments upstairs. Where the building has rental apartments, HPD rules put smoke and carbon monoxide detectors in each apartment on the owner. They can be standalone or monitored, but the shop’s alarm should not be the only thing protecting the tenants.
  • Insurance. Many commercial policies ask for a monitored alarm and a certificate. We provide the paperwork your insurer or landlord asks for.

This is general information, not legal advice; check the current rules for your building with the relevant agency.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

These are the questions Carroll Gardens owners and shopkeepers raise once the conversation moves past brand names: what to do with the garden and the gate, how to treat the openings burglars here have actually used, what to do about the things that live outside, and how to protect a shop on Court Street or Smith Street.

The front garden, the gate and the stoop

Our brownstone sits more than thirty feet back behind its front garden. Should the alarm begin at the iron gate or at the house?

At the house. The alarm boundary belongs on the walls you live inside: the parlor door, the garden-floor door under the stoop, every reachable window, the yard door and the cellar. Those are the openings where a break-in actually happens, and a sensor on each of them reports only when something opens or breaks. A boundary drawn at the fence would have to cope with everything that legitimately passes through a garden, from couriers and neighbors to falling branches, and it would trip the siren for all of them.

The gate still has a role. We usually give it a contact set to chime inside the house or send a phone notification, so you know someone has come in, without any power to sound the siren or call the monitoring station. That way the garden tells you what is going on, and the house decides when it is an alarm.

Can a contact on the garden gate chime inside the house without ever setting off the siren?

Yes. On most current panels each zone can be programmed with its own behavior, and one of those behaviors is chime or notify only. The gate contact is then ignored by the alarm logic whether the system is armed or not. It can play a chime through the keypads, send a push notification, switch on a light, or all three, and you can mute it for an afternoon from the app when the gardener or a contractor is working.

The hardware matters too. A gate moves more than a door, sags with time and spends winters under snow, so we use an outdoor-rated contact with a wide gap tolerance, mount it on the hinge side or under a rail where shovels will not reach it, and test it with the gate swung both gently and hard.

The garden hides the front of our house from the sidewalk. Which front openings does that make most important?

The parlor-floor windows and the garden-floor door under the stoop. On a street-wall rowhouse, someone working at a front window is in full view of the street; in a set-back house behind trees, a stoop and a fence, they are not. We treat those openings the way most rowhouse designs treat the back of the house: a contact on every window that opens, a glass-break detector for a room with several large panes, and a shock sensor on any door with an old lock.

The garden-floor front room also gets a motion detector where it makes sense, aimed along the room and away from the windows, so that movement inside is caught even if a window was left unlatched and opened quietly.

Our video doorbell is at the parlor door, but visitors stand at the gate thirty feet away. Where should the doorbell go, and how does it reach the house?

On most Carroll Gardens houses it belongs on the gate post, at the height of someone standing on the sidewalk. Then you see and speak to the visitor before they come into the garden, and couriers can be told where to leave a parcel. A doorbell at the parlor door only sees people who have already walked the length of the garden and climbed the stoop.

Getting it there is the real question. A battery doorbell on Wi-Fi may reach from the gate, but a brick front wall, a stone stoop and thirty feet of garden often weaken the signal; we measure it at the gate before recommending that route. The dependable option is a low-voltage cable in a conduit under the path, which also powers the doorbell. If the path is being relaid, a conduit costs little; if it is not, a narrow trench along the edge of a bed is usually less disruptive than people expect.

Will anyone outside even hear an indoor siren from a house set back that far from the sidewalk?

Some passers-by will, especially at night, but that is not really what the siren is for. An indoor siren is meant to make staying inside unbearable for an intruder and to wake the household. The person who must hear about the alarm is you or the monitoring station, and that happens through the communicator and the app, not through the air.

If you want a sounder outside as well, the rear wall is usually the better place: it faces the yards where neighbors are, it is not part of the street view, and on landmarked blocks it avoids the front façade. Any building siren in New York City must cut off automatically within fifteen minutes under Administrative Code §24-238, and we program that on every system.

Should the alarm switch on the garden and stoop lights when it trips?

We think it is one of the best additions for a set-back house. A dark garden is the cover a burglar at the front relies on; light takes it away at once and shows you, the neighbors and any camera exactly what is happening. Many current panels can switch a light through a smart switch or a relay when an alarm zone trips, and a floodlight camera can turn on by itself when it detects movement.

We set lighting rules carefully so the garden does not light up all night for cats: lights for an alarm event, optionally lights for the gate opening after dark, and nothing for general motion among the plantings unless you want it.

Doors and windows, the way burglars here have used them

In the 2015 burglaries most thieves started by breaking a door lock. Can the alarm react while someone is still prying at the door, before it opens?

Yes, with a shock or vibration sensor on the door. An ordinary contact only reports when the door actually opens; a shock sensor reports the impact of a crowbar, a hammer on the cylinder or a shoulder against the frame. On an armed system that can sound the siren while the person is still outside, which is often enough to end the attempt.

Shock sensors need tuning. A heavy front door that slams or a house next to a busy delivery route can produce bumps, so we set the sensitivity on site, test it with knocks of different strengths and keep the sensor on the door frame rather than the glass. For most houses we put one on the parlor door and the garden-floor door, the two the 2015 pattern points to.

How long should the entry delay be at our front door, and should the garden-floor door get a shorter one?

The entry delay is the time between opening an armed door and the siren sounding, so the household can reach the keypad. It should be just long enough for the slowest regular user to walk from that door to the keypad and enter a code, and no longer. In a Carroll Gardens house that usually means one delay for the parlor door, whose keypad sits just inside, and possibly a slightly longer one for the garden-floor door if the keypad is up a flight.

Doors nobody uses to come home, such as the yard door, the cellar door and the double doors at the back, get no entry delay at all, so opening them while armed sounds the alarm at once. The gate, being chime-only, needs no delay.

Our windows are usually shut but not always latched. Will a window contact notice a window that is closed but unlocked?

No. A contact reports whether the window is open or closed, not whether the latch is turned. A closed but unlatched window looks normal to the alarm until someone slides it up, at which point the contact reports it opening, which is still the moment you need. That is why every window that can be reached from outside should have a contact.

What the alarm can do is refuse to arm, or warn you, if any window is open even a little when you leave. The 76th Precinct’s advice in 2008, when some daytime burglaries came through unlocked windows, was to lock windows, not just close them. A contact cannot do that for you, but it does make sure an unlatched window cannot open silently.

In 2015 a burglar on Union Street pulled an air conditioner out of a rear window to get in. How do you protect a window that holds an AC unit?

A window unit holds the sash partly open all summer, so an ordinary contact on that sash would read open. We handle it in two layers. First, the sash gets a contact positioned for the height it sits at with the air conditioner in place, so lifting it further reports. Second, a motion detector or a glass-break detector covers the room, so the unit being pushed in or pulled out and someone climbing through is caught either way.

A rear window by a fire escape is a priority because the fire escape makes it reachable. The window must still open freely from inside for escape in a fire, so we never add anything that locks it; we only add detection.

Our garden-floor kitchen opens onto the back yard through a pair of glass doors, one of them usually bolted. Does each leaf need its own sensor?

Yes. The bolted leaf is often the weaker one, because its bolts into the floor and head are easy to work loose from outside. Each leaf gets its own contact, and a glass-break detector in the kitchen covers the glass, since a pair of glass doors can be entered without opening either leaf.

If the doors are old wood with a gap between the leaves, we fit contacts that tolerate the movement and test them with the doors pushed from outside, not just opened and closed from inside.

Can the alarm tell us that a door has been left unlocked, not just open?

An ordinary contact cannot; it knows only whether the door is open. A compatible smart lock or a lock with a bolt-position sensor can report whether the deadbolt is thrown, and many alarm platforms show that next to the door’s open or closed state and can send a reminder if the house is armed while a door is unlocked.

For a back door whose lock has stopped working properly, the first fix is the lock itself. The 2015 Smith Street burglary came through exactly such a door. Until the lock is repaired, a contact with no entry delay on that door means it cannot be opened silently while the system is armed.

The things that live outside

Bikes and strollers stay in our front garden or the vestibule. Can the alarm watch them?

Partly. Police blotters in the 76th Precinct have recorded bikes taken from in front of a house on Union Street and from a Smith Street vestibule where the thief removed part of the banister along with the bike, and a highchair taken from a stoop near Court Street. A burglar alarm protects the inside of the house, so for things kept outside we use different tools.

In a vestibule, the outer door gets a contact and the space can have its own small motion detector on a zone that notifies rather than sounds the siren while the household is home. In the garden, a gate chime and a camera covering the bike rack or stroller spot are more useful than an alarm sensor, and anything left overnight should be locked to something fixed, not to a rail that can be unscrewed.

The City Map counts our front garden as part of the street. Does that limit what can be mounted on the fence or in the garden?

We cannot give you a legal answer, and we have not found a published rule that speaks specifically to alarm devices in these gardens. What is documented is that City Planning describes the mapped streets on these blocks as including the front yards, and that at least one filing before the Board of Standards and Appeals described gardens on Second Place as land claimed to be City-owned. On landmarked blocks, anything on the exterior also falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

Our approach is cautious: permanent equipment goes on the house, and a gate contact, gate doorbell or garden light is offered as an option that can be removed without trace. If you are planning anything larger in the garden, check with the Department of Buildings before you build.

Shops on Court Street and Smith Street

Our Court Street food shop has walk-in coolers and a basement prep room. Will the compressors and cold air confuse motion detectors?

They can, if the wrong detector is pointed the wrong way. Passive infrared detectors react to changes in heat moving across their view, so a blast of cold air from a cooler door, a heater cycling or a compressor fan can trigger a cheap unit. We use detectors with dual technology or good temperature compensation in those rooms, aim them away from cooler doors, vents and heaters, and walk-test them with the equipment running.

The basement prep room usually needs its own zone, with a contact on the cellar door or sidewalk hatch and a detector at the bottom of the stairs. If you want it, a temperature sensor inside the walk-in can report a failing cooler on the same app as the alarm.

Our Smith Street restaurant closes after midnight and the last person leaves through the back. Can the alarm be armed at the back door without walking past the dining-room sensors?

Yes. We put a keypad by the back exit, give that door the exit route, and arrange the zones so the dining room and bar are armed while the kitchen route to the back door stays in its exit delay. The last person arms at the back, walks out, and the door closing completes the arming.

The owner gets a closing report naming who armed the system and when, and an alert if the restaurant is still unarmed at a set time. The front glass behind the gate keeps its glass-break detector, and the siren is set to cut off within fifteen minutes because there are almost always apartments upstairs.

Choosing between wired and wireless in a Carroll Gardens house

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Carroll Gardens

We could not view Google’s AI Overview answers from the environment this page was researched in, so the claims below are not quotations from it. They are generalizations owners repeat to us after asking chatbots or reading national cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, each checked against the houses, gardens and rules of Carroll Gardens.

“Outdoor motion sensors are the best way to protect a front yard.”

For a suburban lawn with nothing on it, perhaps. A Carroll Gardens front garden is a small, planted space with trees overhead, a fence along the sidewalk and a steady stream of legitimate visitors. An outdoor motion sensor there will react to branches, leaves, animals, couriers and neighbors, and an alarm that reacts to all of them is soon switched off.

The garden is better handled with a gate contact that only chimes, a doorbell or camera at the gate, and lights that come on with the alarm. The alarm itself belongs on the house, where an opening door or a breaking window means something.

If an owner does want detection in the garden, we use narrow-beam or curtain detectors aimed along a path at a specific height, on a notification zone, and test them through a full day of normal garden life before they are allowed near the siren.

“Professional alarm installation costs a few hundred dollars.”

National averages are built from every kind of job across the country. HomeAdvisor, for example, has published an average of about $747 with a range of roughly $55 to $2,500 for installing an alarm system. Those figures include small apartments, self-installed kits and basic packages.

A three- or four-story brownstone with a cellar, a rear yard, original windows and a gate thirty feet from the door is not an average job. Our published Brooklyn figures put a basic wireless system from $600 and a typical three-story brownstone at $2,500 to $5,000 installed.

The difference is not a premium for the neighborhood; Carroll Gardens pays our Brooklyn base rate. It is the number of openings, the floors, the cable routes and the garden devices a real house needs.

“Every Carroll Gardens brownstone is landmarked, so nothing can go outside.”

Only part of the neighborhood is a historic district. The Carroll Gardens Historic District, designated in 1973, covers 134 rowhouses on Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt. City Planning’s 2008 study confirms those two blocks are inside the district and treats the Places as separate blocks.

On landmarked blocks, exterior equipment falls under the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and we keep sirens and boxes inside or at the rear. On other blocks the front is not landmarked, though many owners still prefer nothing visible on the façade.

Either way, a good alarm needs very little outside: the gate contact and gate doorbell are small, removable devices, and everything else lives indoors.

“A video doorbell does the job of an alarm.”

A doorbell sees whoever comes to the door it is mounted on, records them and lets you talk to them. It does not know when the yard door opens, when a rear window is lifted out around an air conditioner or when someone pries at the under-stoop door out of its view.

In a set-back house, a doorbell at the parlor door often sees nothing until a visitor has walked the whole garden. Moved to the gate, it becomes very useful, but it still watches one spot.

The two work best together: the doorbell at the gate for visitors and parcels, the alarm on every opening of the house, both in one app.

“Burglars avoid houses set back from the street.”

The record does not support that. In 2008 the 76th Precinct reported a run of daytime burglaries centered in Carroll Gardens, and in 2015 DNAinfo described a week of break-ins across Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill, most through broken door locks while owners were away, one with a crowbar in use on Dennett Place.

A setback can work in a burglar’s favor, because the garden and stoop screen the front of the house from the sidewalk. That is precisely why we treat the front openings of a set-back house as exposed rather than safe.

What deters is evidence the house will react: lights that come on, a siren that sounds before the door gives way, and a system that tells someone at once.

“Anyone handy can install an alarm for you.”

An owner may install equipment on their own property, but in New York State anyone who installs, services or maintains security or fire alarm systems as a business must hold a Department of State license under General Business Law Article 6-D, which requires qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting.

The license matters less as a piece of paper than as a sign that the installer knows panel programming, entry delays, communicator testing and the rules about sirens. Ask for the license number and check it.

AESS holds New York State license #12000287431 and carries insurance for work in occupied homes, rentals and shops; certificates are available for co-op boards and landlords.

“An alarm siren rings until somebody shuts it off.”

Not legally in New York City. Administrative Code §24-238 requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to terminate automatically within fifteen minutes of activation. A siren that rings for an hour while the owner is away breaks the rule and wakes the block.

Every system we install has the cutoff programmed, and we check it at handover. In houses close together and shops with apartments upstairs, it is also simple courtesy.

The siren’s job is done in the first minutes. After that, what counts is that the monitoring station or the owner has already been told, which is why the communicator test matters more than the decibel rating.

Field Notes: Test From the Gate Inward

This is a note on how we finish a job, not a story about any particular customer or house. On a set-back brownstone, the last half hour matters most. With the system in test mode, one technician starts at the gate and walks inward, opening the gate, ringing the doorbell, climbing the stoop, opening the parlor door, then the garden-floor door, then every window and the rear doors floor by floor, while a second person watches the keypad and the app and reads off what each device reported and how strong its signal was.

  • Brooklyn Paper, “Cops see dormant burglary spike” (November 17, 2008); 76th Precinct reports of May 8, 2019 and October 28, 2020; June 27, 2018 blotter
  • DNAinfo, “String of Burglaries Hit Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill Homes, Police Say” (August 12, 2015) and crime blotter of September 15, 2015
