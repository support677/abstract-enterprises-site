The Neighborhood

Why Bushwick Properties Need Network Cable Repair

Almost nothing in Bushwick was built with a data cable in mind. Brooklyn Community Board 4's own history of the neighborhood says two- to six-family houses were built throughout the area, that the few large apartment houses date from the 1920s, and that from then on almost no new housing went up in any quantity until the 1980s. Bushwick Avenue was developed between 1880 and 1913, and the blocks around Irving Avenue between 1900 and 1913. Every Ethernet run in those houses is an afterthought, added by a provider's installer, a landlord's handyman, a tenant with a spool from a hardware store or, more and more often, all three over the years. That history matters for a repair, because the first job on almost every call is working out where the cable actually goes, and the second is working out which of the several people who touched it made the mistake that just showed up.

The buildings have changed hands and uses faster than the wiring has. Community Board 4 counted 39,992 housing units in the district in 2010 and 47,292 in 2020, an increase of 18.3 percent, while the population rose from 112,634 to 120,747. A lot of that growth came from dividing larger houses into more apartments and from converting the old factory and warehouse buildings in the northern part of the neighborhood. Each change added a layer of cable. A house that once had one telephone line now may have a provider's coax in the hallway, an Ethernet run to each apartment, a camera cable on the front wall and a cable for a doorbell or intercom, all following the same holes through the same joists. When one of them fails, the ones beside it are often the reason it is hard to find.

The connections into Bushwick buildings have never been as simple as the advertising suggests. Verizon's 2008 agreement with the city required Fios to be available to every residence by June 30, 2014, and a city audit the following year found it had not happened in many buildings; a Verizon executive told Technical.ly that "it is getting access into buildings that is the challenge." An Untapped Cities map of Brooklyn internet speeds from 2016 and 2017 described "the underserved blocks of Bushwick and Williamsburg" and noted that lower-income neighborhoods like Bushwick were typically given fewer options. Many residents now get service from a mix of fiber, cable, fixed wireless, community networks with antennas on the roof and, in public housing, the city's Big Apple Connect program. Every one of those ends at a box somewhere, and everything from that box to the wall jack in your bedroom is ordinary building cable that someone has to look after when it fails.

Then there is water, which is the single biggest reason network cable fails in this neighborhood. Community Board 4 says street and property flooding happen more often during rain, and names the commercial and residential corridors along Knickerbocker Avenue and Wilson Avenue. During Ida in September 2021, News 12 reported flooding that moved cars and hit businesses on Knickerbocker Avenue, and residents elsewhere in Bushwick were evacuated after a basement wall collapsed. In October 2025, Knickerbocker businesses were bracing again ahead of a nor'easter. In February 2025 the city announced a $390 million project to replace nearly three miles of sewers starting on Knickerbocker Avenue and covering Wilson, Central and Evergreen Avenues, but construction is estimated to begin in 2029. Until then, cellars on those blocks will keep taking water, and the router, the switch and the cables that run along the cellar ceiling or down to a shelf by the floor will keep getting wet.

Fire is the other recurring cause. On May 8, 2026, a fire on Weirfield Street spread through several older, attached wood-frame buildings through a shared cockloft, and Brownstoner has reported the fire that damaged the Lipsius-Cook Mansion on Bushwick Avenue and left its tenants waiting to return. Even when a building is not touched by flames, the smoke, heat and water from firefighting in the house next door can travel through the same cockloft and walls that carry the network cables. After a fire, what looks like an internet problem is very often a cable that was heated, soaked or cut during the cleanup. We test those runs before anyone replaces the router a second time.

Same-Day Repair in Bushwick

Same-Day Calls in Bushwick: Wet Cellars, Dead Loft Ports and Cameras Offline After Rain

Urgent calls from Bushwick usually come in one of four shapes. The first comes the morning after heavy rain: the router in the cellar is dead or blinking oddly, or it works but the apartments upstairs cannot reach it, or the camera recorder on a shelf by the boiler has lost half its cameras. Water got into a jack, a splitter or a switch, or it ran along the cable and into an end that was never sealed. The second shape is the new tenant in a loft or a freshly renovated apartment who finds that the wall ports were never connected to anything, or were connected to a switch in a telecom room that nobody has a key to. The third is a storefront, bar or restaurant on Myrtle, Wyckoff, Knickerbocker or Broadway where the card reader, the ordering screen or the cameras stopped talking to the network during service. The fourth is the slow one: a link that works, drops for a few seconds, then comes back, often worse in damp weather or when a heater kicks on, which almost always means one damaged pair inside a cable rather than a clean break.

Before you call, try three things that take two minutes. Swap the short patch cord between the wall jack and your device for one you know works. Move the cable at the router or switch to a different port. And look at the light on that port: no light at all usually means no connection on the cable, while a light that blinks on and off in a regular rhythm can mean the link is failing and reconnecting. If the trouble began after water came in, unplug the switch or router from power before touching anything that is still wet, and do not plug it back in until it has dried. Then call (347) 934-8335 with what you saw. We book within 2 hours, and if the fault is your provider's line or their box, we tell you before anyone travels. If you are a business with a dinner service or a delivery window coming up, say so on the call so the repair can be planned around it.

What We Install

Network Cable Repair Services in Bushwick

Each service below is part of our Brooklyn network cable repair work and is billed at the same hourly rate. A typical Bushwick visit uses two or three of them: the fault is found, the right fix is made, and the result is proven with a test.

Ethernet cable fault location

Wiremap and distance-to-fault readings that show which pairs failed and roughly how many feet along the run, so a cable that climbs from a cellar through three floors of a walk-up is opened at one spot instead of five.

Pathway fault location and correction

Finding where the route itself caused the damage, such as a cable lying in a cellar puddle, pinched by a door frame or stretched across a cockloft joist, and moving it onto a dry, supported path.

Cat6 cable retermination

Cutting back a corroded, crushed or badly crimped end to clean copper and terminating it properly, which fixes a large share of the jacks left behind by earlier tenants and quick installs.

Network jack repair

Replacing wall jacks and keystones that were pulled out, painted over, soaked in a leak or punched down in the wrong color order, matched to the cable's category and to the wall plate already there.

Patch panel repair

Re-punching loose or mis-wired ports in the patch panel of a loft building's telecom room, a co-working floor or a storefront's back-office cabinet, and replacing panels that took water.

Certified network cable testing

Testing repaired and existing runs against the standard for their category and handing over the results, for loft tenants moving in, landlords turning over an apartment or businesses that need proof.

PoE circuit troubleshooting

Checking the power that travels on the same cable to cameras, access points and door stations, and finding whether the switch, the injector, the cable or the device is the reason it keeps dropping.

Network closet mapping and labelling

Matching every jack in the building to its port, labelling both ends and leaving a written map, so the next problem in a divided Bushwick house or a shared loft floor starts with an answer.

Building Specialists

Network Cable Repair for Bushwick's Architecture

Seven kinds of building produce most of the cable calls we get from Bushwick. They differ in where the cables run, what they pass through, how wet or hot those spaces get and who has to say yes before a wall or ceiling is touched. Those differences decide how a fault is found and fixed long before anyone talks about parts.

The two- to six-family walk-up

The most common Bushwick building: brick or frame, three or four stories, a cellar with the meters and the boiler, and apartments stacked above one stair. Cables usually leave the cellar up one chase or one hallway wall.

The attached frame house row

Wood-frame houses built in rows with a shared roof space. Cables to the top floor or the roof often travel through that cockloft, beside the neighbors' wiring and in the same space a fire or leak can reach.

The 1920s apartment house

One of the few larger buildings from the 1920s, with a stair or elevator core, a cellar telecom corner and apartments wired at different times by different providers and landlords.

The divided Bushwick Avenue house

A large single house from the 1880s to the 1910s split into apartments, with high ceilings, deep cellars and plaster walls, where one run can cross several tenants' rooms.

The converted factory loft

Brick and concrete industrial buildings turned into residential lofts, with exposed ceilings, a telecom room on the ground floor or cellar and long horizontal runs to each unit.

The co-working and studio building

Old industrial buildings like those on Bogart, Grattan, Cook and Decatur Streets, renovated for offices, studios and shared desks, with many tenants on one network and a closet run by the landlord.

The mixed-use corner building

A store, bar or restaurant on the ground floor of Myrtle, Wyckoff, Knickerbocker or Broadway with apartments above, where the shop's network and the tenants' service share a cellar and sometimes a closet.

The two- to six-family walk-up: one cellar, one chase and a cable for every floor

In a Bushwick walk-up, the provider's line usually enters at the cellar, the router or the provider's box sits on a shelf by the meters, and from there cables climb to each apartment. In the best case they go up a single chase beside the stair or a pipe. More often they were pulled at different times along different routes: one up the stairwell behind a molding, one through the floor of the first-floor apartment, one out the back wall and up the outside of the building to a third-floor window. When the top-floor tenant loses the connection, the fault can be anywhere along forty or fifty feet of vertical cable, and three of those feet are often inside someone else's apartment.

The repair starts at the two ends, because the cheapest fix is the one at the cellar shelf or the jack in the apartment. If both ends test good, distance-to-fault readings tell us roughly how far up the building the damage sits, and a tone generator and probe confirm which cable in a bundle of five or six is the one we want. In walk-ups the damage tends to cluster in three places: where the cable leaves the cellar through the ceiling, where it is pinched at a floor penetration or a door frame, and at the top where an earlier installer left a loop of slack behind a radiator. Where the run is beyond saving, the replacement follows the cleanest available path, and if that path crosses another apartment, we arrange it with the landlord and that tenant first rather than on the day.

The cellar end is where water does its damage. A router or switch sitting on the floor, a splitter screwed to a joist above the water heater or a cable that dips low enough to lie in a puddle will fail after the next heavy rain on Knickerbocker or Wilson Avenue. We move what we can up the wall, put drip loops on cables that come down to a device, and leave a short list of what else in the cellar looked at risk so the landlord can deal with it before the next storm.

The attached frame house row: the cockloft is the hallway nobody walks

Bushwick has rows of attached wood-frame houses, many with vinyl or asphalt siding over the original clapboard, built close enough that their roof spaces connect. That shared cockloft is where a lot of top-floor and roof cables travel, because it is the easiest way to reach the front of the house from a closet at the back or to bring a cable down from an antenna on the roof. It is also where trouble concentrates. On May 8, 2026, an FDNY official told News 12 that the fire on Weirfield Street spread through several of these buildings because of a shared cockloft. Even a smaller leak or a heat buildup in summer affects every cable lying across those joists.

When a cable in a frame house fails near the top, we first find out whether the fault is in the part of the run inside the house or in the part that crosses the roof space. That decides who needs to be involved. A fix inside the house is between the owner, the tenant and us. A fix in a cockloft that crosses into the house next door may need the neighbor's agreement, and we never go into a space that belongs to someone else without it. Where the run must be replaced, we route it inside the house instead of through the shared space whenever the building allows, so the next problem does not depend on next door.

The 1920s apartment house: several providers, several eras, one cellar corner

The larger apartment buildings that went up in Bushwick in the 1920s have a cellar or ground-floor corner where the providers' lines arrive, and from there a mixture of old telephone wiring, coax and newer Ethernet climbs through the building. Some apartments are served by a provider's own cable; some by a cable the landlord ran; some by a run a tenant added years ago and left behind. When a jack in one of those apartments dies, the first question is whose cable it is, because a provider's line is the provider's to fix and a cable the landlord installed is the landlord's to approve.

We sort that out by testing from the apartment to the cellar corner and identifying where the cable actually lands. If it lands on a provider's box, we tell you and you call the provider with a clear description of the fault. If it lands on a switch, a panel or a loose end on a backboard, it is building cable, and we repair it with the landlord's or managing agent's agreement. Along the way we label what we find, because the next tenant will have the same question.

The divided Bushwick Avenue house: grand rooms, deep cellars and one cable through several homes

The large houses along Bushwick Avenue and the nearby side streets were built as single homes between the 1880s and the 1910s, and many have since been divided into apartments. They have tall ceilings, thick plaster on lath, decorative moldings and cellars that run the full depth of the house. A cable from the cellar to a third-floor apartment at the back can be eighty feet long and pass through two other units on its way, often along a route chosen because it was easy on the day rather than because it was protected.

In houses like these the repair is planned around the plaster and the tenants. Distance-to-fault testing narrows the problem to a few feet, and a tone probe through the wall confirms the spot before anything is touched. Where the fault is behind plaster in another tenant's room, we stop and arrange access through the landlord instead of opening the wall from the side that happens to be convenient. Where an opening is needed, it is small and in the least visible place, and the plaster is left ready for patching. We do not patch or paint walls ourselves, so we tell you in advance if an opening will need a plasterer afterward.

Some of these houses have also been through fires or long vacancies. After the fire at the Lipsius-Cook Mansion that Brownstoner reported, tenants were left in limbo while the house was repaired. When a damaged house like that is brought back into use, every surviving cable should be tested before it is trusted again, because heat and water can leave a cable looking intact while its pairs no longer carry a clean signal.

The converted factory loft: concrete, steel and long horizontal runs

In Bushwick's former factory and warehouse buildings, residential and studio lofts are usually fed from a telecom room on the ground floor or in the cellar. From there, cables run horizontally along exposed ceilings or in metal raceway to each unit, sometimes a hundred feet or more. Because there is no drop ceiling, cables are often fastened directly to concrete or steel, and they get damaged when tenants hang lights, studio equipment or shelving from the same beams, or when renovations in one unit disturb the route to the next.

A failed run in a loft building is a different repair from one in a walk-up. The distance to fault matters more, because the cable is long and the damage could be in a shared corridor, inside another tenant's loft or at the panel in the telecom room. The access is controlled by the building manager rather than the tenant. And the faults are more often at the ends: a patch panel port that was never punched down, a cable that was cut short during a renovation and never reconnected, or a switch that was removed when an earlier tenant left. We test from your unit to the telecom room first, which tells us in minutes whether the cable is broken or simply not connected to anything.

Concrete also changes the repair options. A damaged section in a concrete ceiling cannot be fished out like a cable in a wood joist. Where the run has to be replaced, it usually goes in surface raceway along the existing route, and we agree the route and the look with the building manager before starting.

The co-working and studio building: many tenants, one closet, one landlord

The renovated industrial buildings that CityRealty described, The Bogart at 100 Bogart Street, The Anx at 13 Grattan Street, The Box Factory at 1519 Decatur Street and 199 Cook Street, are typical of the co-working, office and studio space that has filled Bushwick's industrial blocks. In buildings like these, one network often serves many companies and freelancers. The landlord or operator controls the closet, the switches and the main runs, and each tenant controls the cables inside their own space, if anything.

When a desk, a conference room or a studio loses its wired connection, we work out which side of that line the fault is on. If it is on the building side, we work with the operator, who may already have an IT contact. If it is inside the tenant's space, a quick test, a new jack or a re-terminated end usually fixes it. Either way, the result is documented so the operator and the tenant can see what was wrong and who fixed it, which avoids the usual argument about whose network is at fault.

The mixed-use corner building: the shop's network and the tenants' lines share a cellar

On Myrtle, Wyckoff, Knickerbocker and Broadway, the typical commercial building has a store, a restaurant, a bar or a salon at street level and apartments above. The shop's router, camera recorder and payment equipment usually live in a back office or the cellar, beside the residential tenants' provider boxes. The two networks are supposed to be separate, but in practice the cables are bundled together, run through the same holes and get damaged by the same leak, the same delivery dolly or the same renovation.

A repair in these buildings has to respect both sides. We confirm which cables belong to the business and which to the apartments, fix the one that failed, and label the rest so the next technician does not unplug the wrong thing. If the business's equipment is on the cellar floor in a block that floods, we recommend moving it up and protecting the cable ends, because the next storm will find it there.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Bushwick Cellar Before Touching a Cable

This is how a technician approaches the cellar of a typical Bushwick walk-up or mixed-use building on a cable call, written in the order the checks happen. It is our standard routine, not the story of one job.

The first look is at the floor, not the equipment. A tide line on the walls, rust on the bottom of the boiler jacket, a dehumidifier running, or a pump in a pit says the cellar has taken water, and on blocks near Knickerbocker and Wilson Avenues the answer is usually yes. If the router, the provider's box, a small switch or a camera recorder sits on the floor or on the lowest shelf, that is where the check starts, because corrosion on the ports of a device that got wet can make every cable plugged into it look broken.

Next comes the ceiling. In a walk-up the cables usually leave the cellar in one or two places, often beside a water pipe, a gas line or the electrical feed going up. We follow each cable from the equipment to where it disappears and look at what it touches on the way. A cable lying across a hot pipe, sagging under its own weight between staples, pinched where a joist was notched, or stapled flat through its jacket will fail in that spot sooner or later. A cable that dips down to a device and back up again is a water path, and its end is where we expect to find green copper.

Then the labels, or more often the lack of them. We count the cables, note which ones go to the provider's box and which to the router or switch, and tag them as we go. In a divided house it is common to find six or eight cables coming out of the cellar ceiling, of which two are dead runs left by earlier tenants, one is the old telephone line and one is the camera cable nobody mentioned. Knowing which is which before testing saves a great deal of time upstairs.

Only then does the tester come out. The run is unplugged at both ends, the far end gets a remote, and the wiremap shows whether all eight conductors arrive in the right order. If they do not, the distance-to-fault reading tells us how many feet from the cellar the problem sits. Most of the time that distance lands at one of the places we already looked at on the way up: the spot where the cable left the cellar, the floor penetration where a radiator pipe goes through, or the top end where a jack was crimped by hand.

Before anything is cut, the customer hears what was found. If the problem is a wet end in the cellar, the repair is quick and the advice is to get the equipment off the floor. If it is a crushed section inside another apartment's wall, the plan has to include that tenant and the landlord. If it is something we did not expect, such as a cable that was never connected at the top at all, we say so plainly and agree the next step before going further.

How Bushwick Cables Fail

Water, Fire, Rodents and Shortcuts: How Bushwick Network Cables Break and What Each Repair Involves

Over the years, Bushwick cable faults have settled into a few recognizable patterns. Each one has a typical location, a typical symptom and a typical repair. Knowing which pattern you are probably looking at helps you describe the problem on the phone and helps us bring the right parts.

Water from below: flooded cellars and wet ends

Community Board 4's district statement says street and property flooding happen more often during rain, especially along Knickerbocker and Wilson Avenues. The city's own February 2025 announcement described the $390 million sewer project as meant to "alleviate chronic flooding in Bushwick, Brooklyn," with construction not expected until 2029. In practical terms, that means cellar water is a normal part of the year for many buildings here, and network equipment in those cellars lives with it.

Water rarely cuts a cable in the middle. What it does is get into the ends: the jack on a wall near the floor, the plug in a switch on a low shelf, a splitter or a coupler hanging below a joist. The copper inside corrodes, slowly at first, so the link may keep working for weeks after the flood and then start dropping in damp weather. A tester shows pairs with high resistance or intermittent contact rather than an open break. The repair is to cut back to clean copper, re-terminate in a dry place, replace any jack or panel port that took water, and move the termination point higher. If the cable itself lay in water for a long time, the section in the cellar is replaced, because water can travel inside the jacket.

Water from above: leaks, roofs and the floor upstairs

The other kind of water comes down. A roof leak in a frame house, a burst pipe in the apartment above, a bathtub overflow in a divided house or the water used to fight a fire next door all run along the same joists and down the same chases as the network cables. Cables that run through those spaces may look fine from the outside and still fail a test, and jacks on the floor below a leak often fill with water from the inside of the wall.

After a leak from above, we test every run that passes near the wet area, not only the one that stopped working, because a cable that still connects can be carrying a damaged pair that will fail later. The repair is the same as for cellar water: clean ends, new jacks where needed, and replacement of any section that sat wet.

Heat and smoke: fires in the building or next door

Bushwick has had several serious fires in attached buildings, including the May 8, 2026 fire on Weirfield Street that News 12 reported spread through a shared cockloft. Even a fire that does not reach your unit can heat the cables in a shared roof space or wall, and the cleanup afterward often involves cutting, moving or soaking whatever is in the way. When residents come back, the internet may work in some rooms and not others, or work and then drop when the heat comes on.

A cable that has been heated may have softened insulation that lets pairs touch or shift, which shows up on a test as crosstalk, shorts or a link that only runs at a lower speed. We test every run that passed through the affected area, replace any section that shows heat damage, and keep the replacement out of the shared space where the building allows. If a fire-rated wall or floor was opened for the original cable, the new penetration is sealed again with firestop material.

Teeth: mice and rats in old walls

Rodents chew cable. It is not limited to old buildings, but old buildings with gaps around pipes, shared cocklofts and cellars give them more places to do it. Reports from elsewhere show the scale it can reach: the BBC reported in 2023 that nesting rats chewed through several cables and cut off internet service in Tring, England, and Openreach engineers said the casing was tough enough that you would normally need a drill to pierce it. In a Bushwick house, the damage is usually smaller and more local: a cable near a radiator chase or behind a kitchen cabinet with a bite through the jacket and one or two pairs.

Chewed cable produces an intermittent fault that often gets worse over time, and the tester usually shows it as one or more open or shorted pairs at a specific distance. Because the damage is local, the repair can often be a short replacement section with a proper, rated connection at each end rather than a whole new run. We also tell you where the damage is so the landlord or exterminator can close the gap the animals are using, because otherwise the new section will be the next meal.

Shortcuts: cheap cable, hand crimps and stapled jackets

A large share of what we fix in Bushwick was installed in a hurry. A box of copper-clad aluminum cable bought online, plugs crimped onto solid cable meant for jacks, staples driven straight through the jacket, couplers used to join two short cables behind a couch, or a jack punched down in the wrong color order are all common. TrueCable describes copper-clad aluminum as something people regret running, with terminations that fail and conductors that break inside walls. These installations often work at first and fail months or years later, usually after the cable has been moved.

The fix depends on how bad the original work is. A single bad end is re-terminated properly. A run of copper-clad aluminum that keeps failing is replaced with solid copper cable, because no amount of re-terminating will make it last. Staple damage is cut out and repaired with a proper connection or replaced. Either way, we tell you what the original installation used, because it explains why the other jacks in the house may be next.

When the problem is power, not data

Cameras, Wi-Fi access points, door stations and some phones get their power over the same network cable through Power over Ethernet. When they drop at night, in the cold or when a camera's infrared lights switch on, the problem is often the power budget rather than the data. A long run of thin or copper-clad aluminum cable loses too much voltage, a switch runs out of power to share, or an injector is failing. We test the power at the device end under load, check the switch's budget and the cable's resistance, and fix whichever part is short. On storefront cameras along Bushwick's shopping streets, this is one of the most common reasons a camera goes dark after dusk.

These are the questions renters, landlords and business owners raise in forums, tenant guides and our own calls when a wired connection fails in a building like Bushwick's, grouped by the situation they come from and answered for this neighborhood.

Renting, lofts and landlords

Do I need my landlord's permission before a jack is opened or a new section is run?

For anything beyond unplugging and testing, usually yes. Replacing a wall jack, opening a small hole to reach a damaged section or running a new piece of cable changes the building, and most leases leave that to the landlord. In practice, a short text or email from the landlord or managing agent saying the work is approved is enough, and we can send them a description of the repair in advance. Testing from your jack to wherever the cable ends does not change anything and can be done first, so you can show the landlord exactly what failed before asking.

None of the ports in our new loft unit work, even though the listing mentioned wired internet. Was the cable cut?

Usually not. In converted loft buildings, the most common reason a whole unit's ports are dead is that the cables were never connected at the other end: they run to a patch panel in the telecom room that was never punched down, or to a panel that is not patched to any switch, or to a switch that a previous tenant took with them. A test from your unit to the telecom room shows in minutes whether the cables arrive intact. If they do, the fix is in the closet and needs the building manager to let us in. If they do not, the distance reading tells us where the break is, often at a spot where a renovation in a neighboring unit cut through the route.

We rent desks in a converted factory and the building's network closet is locked. Can our runs still be fixed?

The part inside your space can. We can test your jacks, re-terminate damaged ends, replace jacks and fix the cable as far as your walls or ceiling allow. If the test shows the fault is in the closet or on the building's side of the route, the operator has to unlock it and agree to the work, or have their own IT contractor make the fix. Either way, you get a written result showing which side the problem is on, which usually ends the back-and-forth about whose network is broken.

Fire, smoke and attached houses

A fire next door sent smoke and heat into our shared cockloft. Does the cable up there have to be replaced?

Not necessarily, but it has to be tested before you trust it. Heat can soften the insulation inside a cable without visibly damaging the jacket, and that shows up as shorts, crosstalk or a link that only runs at a lower speed. Smoke alone usually does little to a cable, but the water used to fight the fire and the cleanup afterward often do more. We test every run that passed through the affected space. Those that pass are left alone, and those that fail are replaced, preferably along a route inside your own house so the next problem in the roof space does not become yours.

Roofs, outside walls and community networks

The cable from our rooftop community network antenna stopped working. Can you repair a run like that?

Yes. Community networks like NYC Mesh describe a typical install as one cable from a member's apartment up to equipment on the roof, powered from the apartment by a PoE injector, with more cables run back down the building to other members. That cable is ordinary network cable on a long, partly outdoor route, and it fails the same way others do: a cracked jacket where it crosses the roof edge, water at an unsealed end, or a power problem at the injector. We repair the cable and its power, and leave the rooftop equipment and its settings to the network's own volunteers, so check with them first that the antenna itself is not the issue.

Does cable fixed to the outside of a frame house need a different jacket from the one indoors?

Yes. Indoor cable is not made for sun, rain and freezing nights, and TrueCable's guidance says indoor cable exposed to moisture and sunlight will usually fail in a matter of months. Outdoor-rated cable has a jacket designed for ultraviolet light and water and can last as long as the network does if it is installed properly. When an outdoor run on a Bushwick frame house fails, we replace it with outdoor-rated cable, bring it into the house through a sealed entry with a drip loop, and check whether water has been following the old cable into the wall.

Cheap cable, odd jacks and speed

A previous tenant wired our place with a cheap box of cable. Could that be why the jacks keep failing?

Quite possibly. Look at the printing on the cable jacket. If it says CCA, or copper-clad aluminum, the conductors are aluminum with a thin copper coating, and they break and loosen at terminations far more easily than solid copper. Those runs often work when they are new and then fail one jack at a time. Re-terminating them buys a little time, but the reliable fix is to replace the runs that keep failing with solid copper cable of the right category. We check the jacket on the first visit and tell you which runs are worth keeping.

The wall jack looks like a phone outlet. Can it carry Ethernet?

It depends on what is behind it. Phone jacks are smaller, with four or six contacts, and Ethernet jacks are wider, with eight. Many Bushwick apartments have old phone outlets wired with phone cable that cannot carry a network connection at all. Some have newer network cable behind an old phone faceplate, which can be converted to a proper Ethernet jack in a few minutes. We open the plate, check the cable and tell you which one you have. If it is phone cable, the jack cannot become a network jack without a new run.

Does a section that has been repaired still carry full gigabit speed?

When the repair is done properly, yes. A new termination, a replacement jack or a short replacement section joined with connectors rated for the cable's category adds very little loss. Twisting wires together, using electrical tape or using a cheap coupler does not. That is why we test the repaired link end to end after the fix, and certify it where you need proof. If the run is long or has several repairs already, the test shows whether it still meets its category, and if it does not, we tell you that replacement is the better answer.

Is there a safe way to stay connected until the repair visit?
+

Is a damaged cable that powers a camera dangerous to touch?
+

Power over Ethernet uses low voltage and is not a shock hazard the way an outlet is. Still, unplug the switch or injector before handling a damaged end, mainly to protect the equipment, and leave anything touching building power wiring to an electrician.

What if the cable passes through a fire-rated wall or floor?
+

Where a repair or a replacement section goes through a fire-rated wall or floor, the opening around the cable is sealed again with firestop material so the wall or floor keeps its rating. This comes up most in loft and mixed-use buildings.

Do you repair coax as well as Ethernet cable?

Network cable repair covers twisted-pair data cable like Cat5e and Cat6. If the cable in question is coax for a camera system or a provider's line, tell us on the call and we will say whether it belongs with our camera repair work or with your provider.

What does the printing on the cable jacket tell you?
+

It shows the cable's category, such as Cat5e or Cat6, its fire rating for the space it runs in, and whether it is solid copper or copper-clad aluminum. Those three facts decide whether a damaged run is worth repairing or should be replaced with the right cable.
