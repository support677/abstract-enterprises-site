The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Heights Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Planned Door by Door

Most smart-lock advice is written for a single-family house with a new door. Very little of Brooklyn Heights looks like that. The Brooklyn Heights Association describes the neighbourhood as overwhelmingly low-rise brick and brownstone row houses with a smattering of apartment buildings, bounded by Old Fulton Street, Atlantic Avenue, Cadman Plaza and Court Street, and the Promenade above the East River. The houses on those blocks went up from the 1820s onwards. The doors on them were built by hand, by joiners who never imagined a battery pack, and many of them are still the original leaves, refinished a dozen times and carrying a mortise pocket, a centre knob or a brass escutcheon that an owner wants to keep.

The second thing that makes the neighbourhood different is who owns those doors. The Association's own profile counts 22,593 housing units in the Heights, 57 percent of them renter-occupied and 40 percent owner-occupied. Behind those figures sits a patchwork that a lock installer meets every week: whole houses still in single-family use, houses split into a handful of rental flats with the owner on the parlour floor, rowhouses converted into small co-ops or condominiums of five to eight apartments, and large pre-war elevator buildings with a board, a managing agent and a super. A listing reported by 6sqft, for example, describes 114 Remsen Street as a four-storey Italianate brownstone operating as a seven-unit co-op. A smart lock on the street door of a building like that is a decision for a board, not a household.

Third is the landmark itself. On November 23, 1965 the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated Brooklyn Heights as New York City's first historic district. Almost everything that touches the outside of a building inside the boundary is governed by the Commission's rules. The good news for owners is that the Commission's own permit guidebook lists repairing or replacing hardware such as hinges, knobs, locks and handles among the work that may not need a permit at all. The care is needed elsewhere: in a reader or keypad fixed to the masonry beside the door, in the cable that feeds it, in a replacement door, and in anything on the stoop or the areaway fence. We plan those parts first, so the lock itself is the easy bit.

Finally there is the street. Montague Street is the neighbourhood's one main shopping street, its business improvement district serving more than 100 businesses between Court Street and Hicks Street; Henry Street and Atlantic Avenue carry restaurants, shops and offices; the blocks around Borough Hall and Court Street are full of law offices and professional suites. Those businesses need staff codes, early deliveries and a record of who opened up, on doors that often sit in landmarked buildings with apartments above. Brooklyn Heights is also famously busy with film and television crews, which brings its own visitors to the door. We build the code plan for whoever actually needs to get in, and nobody else.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Brooklyn Heights' Architecture

The historic district takes in Federal, Greek Revival, Gothic Revival, Italianate and Anglo-Italianate houses, carriage houses, churches and large pre-war buildings. Each brings a different door, and a different answer.

Federal Wood-Frame Houses

The oldest houses in the Heights are small wood-frame Federal buildings; 24 Middagh Street, built around 1824, is generally described as the oldest. Doors here are often narrower and lighter than later rowhouse doors, frames have been patched more than once, and there is rarely a spare inch for a bulky interior unit. We measure stiles and backset first and favour compact hardware.

Greek Revival Rowhouses

Brick houses with stone trim, a modest stoop and a deep doorway. The HDC singles out the Greek Revival style as particularly notable in the district. Doorways frequently have sidelights and a transom, which pushes any reader off the glass and onto the masonry, and makes the cable route the main design question.

Italianate and Anglo-Italianate Brownstones

Taller stoops, heavy double doors and a vestibule, or a low English-basement entrance in the Anglo-Italianate rows. These are the doors that most often carry an original mortise lock and a centre knob, and where the choice between the outer pair and the inner door matters.

Gothic Revival Houses

Pointed-arch doorways and heavier detailing, often with original panelled doors that owners will not drill. Interior-side retrofit locks that leave the outside untouched are frequently the right answer here.

Carriage Houses on the Mews

Two- and three-storey former stables on Grace Court Alley, Hunts Lane, Love Lane and College Place, with tall wooden stable doors and, as City Lore notes of Grace Court Alley, hay cranes still in place. Most are homes now, and the everyday door is a smaller pedestrian door rather than the carriage doors.

Rowhouse Co-ops and Condos

Single houses divided into five to eight apartments and run by their own small board. One shared street door, a vestibule, unit doors on every landing and nobody on staff to manage keys.

Converted Hotels and Pre-War Elevator Buildings

The Hotel St. George complex, the Bossert on Montague Street and other large pre-war buildings: long corridors, heavy unit doors, a super, house rules for contractors and, often, a building-wide key or fob system that a unit lock must not undermine.

New Conversions and Towers

Residential conversion of the Hotel Bossert and new construction around Borough Hall bring sponsor-installed hardware and building apps. Unit-door decisions there run through the sponsor or the board, and we coordinate with both.

Door details we check on every Heights house

  • Original mortise pockets. Many older doors carry a mortise lock in a deep pocket in the edge. A mortise-format smart lock can sometimes use it; often the better answer is to keep the mortise lock for its latch and add a compact deadbolt or an interior retrofit above it.
  • Centre knobs. A decorative knob in the middle of a leaf usually turns nothing. It stays, and the smart hardware goes at the lock edge where the keeper and frame can take the bolt.
  • Thickness and backset. Nineteenth-century doors range widely in thickness and in how far the old bore sits from the edge. We measure both before recommending a model, because a lock that is listed for the wrong range simply will not seat.
  • Glass in and around the door. Sidelights, transoms and glazed inner doors change where a thumb-turn is safe and where a reader can sit. Any drilling near original glass is done from the protected side with the panel taped and supported.
  • Paint build-up. Doors refinished many times often bind at the strike. We ease and re-align before the motorised bolt goes in, so the motor is not fighting the paint every time it throws.
  • Masonry and mortar. Where a reader or bridge has to go on brick or brownstone, fixings go into mortar joints, never into carved stone, and conduit runs are kept short and painted to match.

Many Heights owners pair this with a burglar alarm. The same door contact that tells the app a door is open can arm and disarm an alarm when the last person locks up — see alarm installation in Brooklyn Heights.

Business doors in the Heights tend to sit in landmarked buildings with homes above, on streets that are busy all day and quiet at night. These are the corridors we work on most.

Shops, cafés and banks sit at street level with apartments and offices above, so the street door and the shop door are often side by side in one landmarked facade.

The neighbourhood's southern edge and a BID corridor of its own on the city's directory. Antique dealers, galleries and specialist shops here care about strength ratings and opening logs as much as convenience.

Court Street and Borough Hall

Law firms, accountants, therapists and other professional suites in the office buildings and converted houses around Borough Hall and the Court-Livingston-Schermerhorn BID. Typical requests are keypads on suite doors, a code for the cleaner, and a door that can be released for an appointment.

Clark Street

Home to the Clark Street subway station, whose only entrance is through the ground floor of the old Hotel St. George, and to the shops along that block. Residential and retail doors here see heavy foot traffic all day.

Churches, Schools and Institutions

Houses of worship such as Plymouth Church on Orange Street, a National Historic Landmark since 1961, along with schools and cultural buildings. Side doors used by staff, volunteers and community groups are good candidates for scheduled keypad codes.

Compliance callouts for Heights doors

  • Landmarks. Inside the historic district, work on the exterior needs an LPC permit unless it falls into the exceptions the Commission publishes. Replacing locks and handles is listed among work that may not need one; readers, intercom panels, cameras and their conduit follow the health, safety and utility equipment rules in LPC Rules section 2-20. When in doubt, the Commission's own advice is to contact LPC at 212-669-7817 before work starts.
  • One motion out. Apartment and business exit doors must open from the inside without a key, code or tool. HPD states it is illegal to install double-cylinder locks on apartment doors. Every lock we fit is tested by hand from the inside before we leave.
  • Multiple dwellings. A building with three or more households is a multiple dwelling under New York's Multiple Dwelling Law. Tenants may add their own lock and must give the owner a duplicate key on request (MDL §51-c); the owner must provide the entrance lock and keys required by the Housing Maintenance Code.
  • Tenant data. Owners who introduce app, fob or biometric entry in a multiple dwelling are bound by the city's Tenant Data Privacy Act (Local Law 63 of 2021): no requiring a smart access system to enter the tenant's own unit, consent before collecting data, limits on what is kept and for how long.
  • Self-closing doors. Doors to interior corridors and stairs in apartment buildings must be self-closing under Local Law 111 of 2018. A smart lock must never be propped, wedged or programmed in a way that defeats the closer.

This page explains how the rules affect a lock installation; it is not legal advice. Where a question turns on your lease, your proprietary lease or a permit, we say so and point you to the right office.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Brooklyn Heights: Questions Answered

Questions we are asked in the Heights that a general smart-lock guide does not answer, grouped by the kind of building they come from.

Carriage houses and the doors on the mews

Our carriage house on the mews still has its tall wooden stable doors — where does a smart lock actually go?

Almost never on the stable doors themselves. On most converted carriage houses on Grace Court Alley, Hunts Lane or Love Lane the big leaves are kept for their looks and are bolted from the inside with cane bolts or a bar, while daily life goes through a smaller pedestrian door set into the facade or cut into one of the leaves as a wicket. That smaller door is where a keypad or fingerprint deadbolt belongs. It is closer to a normal door in size and weight, it swings on its own hinges, and a lock fitted to it does not have to hold a nine-foot leaf shut against the wind.

If the pedestrian door is a wicket cut into a carriage leaf, we look closely at the stile around it. The wood is often narrow and has been altered at least once, so we size the lock to the timber that is actually there and add a reinforced keeper on the frame side. Where the household also parks inside, the carriage doors can be monitored with a door contact and left mechanically bolted, which keeps them out of the keyless system entirely and avoids a motorised bolt trying to pull a sagging leaf into line.

Because these houses are inside the historic district and face the street directly, anything mounted on the facade — a reader, a camera, a doorbell — follows the Commission's equipment rules. A lock on the door itself is the simplest route, and that is usually where we start.

We want a reader beside the doorway rather than on the door — how does its cable get there without marking the brownstone?

A wall reader needs power and a data cable, and on a landmarked facade the cable is the part to plan. The LPC guidebook allows staff to approve equipment on a primary facade only where there is no other reasonable location, and asks that it sit in a discreet spot: within or beside the door opening, beneath a band course, under the stoop or behind the areaway wall. Where conduit has to show, staff may approve roughly ten inches of it, painted to match and fixed into mortar joints rather than decorative surfaces.

In practice we try to keep the cable inside. On a rowhouse the route is usually from the cellar or the vestibule ceiling, through the door frame or the reveal, and out at the reader's back box, so nothing runs across the face of the brownstone at all. Where the masonry is too thick to core, the reader can often move into the vestibule or onto the frame inside the door opening, which is easier to approve and better sheltered.

We photograph the facade, the reveal and the proposed position before quoting, mark up the route, and give you the manufacturer cut sheet for the reader. Those are the same materials the Commission asks for in a permit application, so if one is needed you or your architect have most of the package already.

The mail carrier, the post and the street door

The mail carrier opens our building's street door with a postal key in the old intercom — what happens to that when the door goes keyless?

Many small apartment buildings give the letter carrier access through a postal lock in the intercom panel or a separate key keeper beside the door. The Postal Service calls the key that opens these an arrow key and uses it for apartment panels, collection boxes and parcel lockers; the USPS Office of Inspector General has reported that the number of arrow keys in circulation is unknown and that lost keys were not adequately tracked. That is a reason to keep the postal lock separate from your new system, not to fold it in.

When we replace an intercom or add a reader, we do not remove or rewire the postal lock on our own. The building, not AESS, arranges any change to it with the local post office, and the carrier's access has to keep working through the change. The usual solution is to keep a postal key keeper or a lock cylinder in the new panel that releases the door the same way the old one did, while residents switch to codes or fobs.

On a landmarked building the Commission's rules also cover mailboxes and mail key lockboxes: within or beside the door opening, on a gate or door under the stoop, on a plain stoop wall, or on a post in the areaway. We position the new reader so the postal lock box can stay where it is or move to one of those permitted spots.

Brownstones that have become small co-ops

Our brownstone is a self-managed co-op of six or seven apartments — who decides on a keypad for the shared street door, and who holds the master admin?

The street door belongs to the corporation, so the decision is the board's, normally by the voting rule in your by-laws, and it should be recorded in the minutes. We are happy to come to a board meeting or send a written proposal with the hardware, the cost and how codes will work. What we will not do is fit a shared-door keypad at the request of a single shareholder, however keen.

Admin rights are the part small co-ops most often get wrong. One owner sets the system up on their own phone, sells two years later, and the building is locked out of its own door. We set up the account in the corporation's name with a shared email the board controls, give two officers admin rights, and give every other resident a user credential only. The written register we leave lists who holds what, so the next treasurer inherits it with the books.

Each household should still hold a working key for the street door, and every apartment keeps its own lock. If your proprietary lease requires the corporation to hold a duplicate key to each unit, as many do, that does not change because one shareholder fits a smart lock on their own door; the board simply receives a key or an agreed emergency arrangement for it.

The laundry, bike room and storage cages in our co-op all run on one old key everyone has copied — can those doors go keyless too?

Yes, and in a small building this is often the most useful work of the lot. A shared brass key that has been copied for twenty years has no record of who holds it. A keypad deadbolt or a small reader on the cellar door, the bike room and the roof door gives each resident a code that can be removed when they move out, and a log the board can look at after something goes missing.

The doors themselves need checking first. Cellar doors in older rowhouses are often thin, warped or fitted with a hasp; the lock is only as good as the door and frame it is on, and we will tell you if one should be replaced before it is made keyless. If a door is on a route someone might use to leave the building, such as a cellar door leading to the yard, it must still open from the inside without a key or code.

For the roof, we follow the building's existing arrangement for fire department access and do not fit anything that would stop a door that must open from the inside doing so. The cheapest sensible setup is usually one battery keypad deadbolt per door, all on the same app as the street door so the board has one place to remove a departing resident.

Old fabric: glass, plaster and brick

Our inner vestibule door still has its original etched glass — how do you fit a lock without putting the glass at risk?

Carefully, and usually without drilling near it at all. Most original glazed inner doors have a solid lock rail below the glass, and an interior-side retrofit lock or a compact deadbolt can sit in that rail using the existing bore. Where new boring is unavoidable, we tape and support the panel, drill from the side the glass is bedded on, and use a sharp hole saw at low speed so the door is not jarred.

We also think about what happens after installation. A motorised bolt that slams into a tight keeper sends a small shock through the door every time it throws. Easing the strike and setting the lock to a gentle throw protects the glass for years. If the glass is cracked or loose in its putty before we start, we will point it out and photograph it so everyone knows its condition.

If a thumb-turn sits within reach of the glass, a broken pane would let someone reach through and turn it. On an inner vestibule door behind a locked outer pair that risk is small; where the glazed door is the first line of defence, a keypad lock with the thumb-turn disabled when you are away, or a different door for the smart lock, is the safer plan.

Our walls are original plaster on brick — can a wired reader or door sensor be fished to the doorway without opening the walls?

Often, yes, but not always, and we say which before we price it. In many rowhouses the plaster is applied directly to the brick party wall with no cavity to fish through, so the route has to use the spaces that do exist: the cellar ceiling, the void behind the vestibule's wooden trim, the door frame and its reveal, and the run of an old bell or intercom wire if one is still in place.

Where a run has to cross a plastered wall, we prefer a small surface raceway painted to match, inside, over cutting a chase in old plaster that may crack well beyond the cut. For a lock on the door itself the question rarely arises: battery smart deadbolts need no wiring, and a door contact for monitoring can be wireless.

A wired strike and reader on a street door is the case where cable is unavoidable. Here we look first for an existing intercom pair or bell wire that can be reused or used as a draw wire. If a route cannot be found without damage, we will recommend a battery keypad lock on the door instead of a wired reader beside it.

Visitors, crews and checking your own security

A production company wants our house for a few days of filming — how should their access work?

Brooklyn Heights has been a favourite with productions for years; in 2012 the then Council Member Stephen Levin asked the city to put the whole neighbourhood on its filming hot-spot list, and in April 2025 the Brooklyn Eagle photographed a Law & Order shoot on the Promenade. If your house is the location, the location manager will want keys for prep, shoot and wrap days. Codes are a far better answer than keys.

We set a separate code for the location team that works only on the agreed dates and hours, on the doors they are allowed to use, and expires on its own when they wrap. The app log then shows exactly when the house was opened and by which code. Private rooms can be closed off with their own lock or a door kept on a different code, and a camera at the door gives you a view of arrivals while you are away.

Two practical points: keep your own household codes unchanged and private so you never need to share them, and ask for a walk-through with the location manager before and after the shoot. If the crew needs the street door of a co-op building, that is a board decision, and the board should issue its own time-limited code rather than a resident's.

Should we book the NYPD's free security survey before or after the smart lock goes in?

Before, if you can. The NYPD offers free security surveys through each precinct's crime prevention officer — the 84th Precinct covers Brooklyn Heights — and the officer evaluates locks, doors, lighting, windows and alarm systems and suggests corrective steps. Having that report in hand means the lock plan can answer it: a reinforced keeper where the frame is weak, a different door made keyless, better lighting at the stoop.

Book through the precinct or through 311. The survey is advice, not a specification. We read the report with you at the walkthrough and price the items that are within our work, such as locks, strikes, readers, door contacts and cameras, separately from anything a carpenter or mason should do.

If the lock is already in, the survey is still worth having. Officers often spot the things that defeat a good lock: a garden door with a weak frame, a cellar window without a grille, a side gate left on the latch.

Shops and offices on the Heights' main streets

Our Montague Street shop carries valuable stock — how does a smart deadbolt's strength rating compare with the lock it replaces?

Look for certification to the BHMA standards rather than marketing words. The current residential standard, ANSI/BHMA A156.40, specifies durability, operation and security tests for residential deadbolts, including seventeen security tests, one of which loads the door against the bolt with 1,350 pounds of force. Many lock makers still describe products by the older grade 1, 2 and 3 scale, with grade 1 the commercial-duty end.

Compare like with like. If your current lock is a heavy commercial mortise lock or a high-security cylinder, many residential smart deadbolts will be a step down in physical strength even though they add codes and logs. In that case a commercial smart lock in mortise or narrow-stile format, or a reader and electric strike working with the existing high-security cylinder as key override, keeps the strength and adds the convenience.

The rest of the opening matters as much as the lock: the frame, the strike plate and its screws, the gap at the edge, the glass, and the roll-down gate if you have one. We check all of them at the walkthrough and price any strike reinforcement separately so you can see what it adds.

Types of Smart Locks We Install

Interior Retrofit Smart Locks

Mount on the inside over an existing single-cylinder deadbolt and leave the outside, the key and the historic face of the door untouched. The first thing we consider on a landmarked front door.

Compact Keypad Deadbolts

For Federal-era and carriage-house pedestrian doors where stiles are narrow and interior space is tight. Chosen by measured backset and thickness, not by catalogue photo.

Mortise-Format Smart Locks

For heavy rowhouse doors with a mortise pocket in the edge, and for shop doors that need commercial-duty hardware with lever trim and a keypad or reader face.

Fingerprint Deadbolts

What AI Gets Wrong About Smart Lock Installation in Brooklyn Heights

We could not open live AI Overview, People-Also-Ask or forum results from our build environment, so the claims below are taken from published cost guides and manufacturer pages that AI answers commonly summarise. Each is followed by what we find on Brooklyn Heights doors.

“Smart lock installation runs about $250 to $400”

That range is a real national figure: Fixr's cost guide, updated in January 2025, puts smart lock installation at $250 to $400 with an average of $300, for a Wi-Fi lock with one accessory on a standard door. It describes a modern pre-bored door, one household and no wiring.

A Heights job can be that simple, and when it is we quote it flat at the walkthrough. But the national figure does not cover reconditioning a painted-shut keeper on an 1850s door, building around an original mortise pocket, or a reader and electric strike for a co-op street door. Those have their own published AESS prices, set out in the pricing section below.

The useful takeaway is to separate the lock from the door. If the door is sound and standard, expect something close to the national range. If the door is original, the building is shared or the facade is involved, the plan matters more than the price of the hardware.

“It's a 30-minute DIY job with a screwdriver”

Product testers say so, and on a new door it can be true. Bob Vila's testers describe fitting smart locks with little more than a Phillips screwdriver in about half an hour.

On a two-hundred-year-old door the half-hour goes on measuring. Backsets vary, doors are thicker or thinner than the lock is listed for, and the frame may have been patched with three generations of wood. A lock that is slightly out of line will drain batteries and jam, and a wrong bore in an original door is permanent.

DIY is fine for an interior-side retrofit on a sound modern deadbolt. For an original door, a shared street door or anything wired, have it measured first.

“A retrofit lock leaves the outside untouched, so landmark rules never come into it”

The first half is right. August describes its retrofit lock as mounting inside on the existing deadbolt and leaving the outside untouched, with your keys unchanged. It only works with single-cylinder deadbolts, and some doors need clearance for it.

The second half is too broad. A retrofit lock on the door is rarely a landmarks issue, and the Commission lists replacing locks and handles among work that may not need a permit. A keypad, reader, camera or doorbell fixed to the facade beside the door is different: it is equipment on the building and follows section 2-20 of the LPC Rules.

So the honest answer is that the lock is usually free of landmark paperwork and the things around it may not be. Plan both together.

“Any smart lock fits any front door”

Smart locks are designed around a modern bored deadbolt. Owners of older doors have asked for years whether anything fits their mortise locks; a SmartThings community thread started in 2014 by an owner of metal brownstone doors ran for nearly a decade without a simple drop-in answer.

Heights doors include mortise pockets, centre knobs, narrow Federal stiles, glazed vestibule doors and wicket doors in carriage leaves. Each has a workable smart option, but not the same one, and some need a carpenter before they need a locksmith.

Tell us the make of the existing lock and send photos of the edge of the door. That one picture rules out half the products on the shelf.

“Keep the Wi-Fi bridge within 10 to 15 feet and you're set”

August's own support guidance is to place its Connect bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock and check for full Wi-Fi bars at that spot. The distance is right; the walls are the problem.

In a rowhouse the router is often on the top floor at the back and the lock is on the parlour floor at the front, with brick party walls, plaster and floors in between. A bridge 12 feet from the lock can still be on the wrong side of a wall that stops the signal dead.

We test the signal at the door with the bridge in its proposed spot before we leave, and add a mesh node or move the router if needed. A lock that only works when you are standing next to it is not finished.

“Matter means any lock works with any app”

Matter makes it easier for a lock to work with more than one ecosystem, but it does not remove the hardware underneath. Yale's guidance for its Matter-over-Thread lock is that a Thread border router has to be set up first, and that it must be the same brand as the app you are using.

Bold's knowledge base explains Thread as a low-power self-healing mesh needing a border router such as a Nest Hub (2nd gen) or HomePod mini, with a lock able to join up to five ecosystems.

In a co-op with several households on different phones and speakers, choose one ecosystem for the shared door and let individual apartments do what they like on their own doors.

“A smart lock only makes sense for a single-family house”

Most product pages assume one household. In Brooklyn Heights, more than half of homes are rented and many owner-occupied homes are apartments in small co-ops and condos, so the commonest buyer is a board or a landlord as often as a family.

Shared buildings are where keyless entry does the most good: a departed resident is removed in seconds, a contractor's code expires on its own, and nobody needs to re-key a street door after a lost key. They are also where the rules bite, from the Multiple Dwelling Law's duplicate-key provision to the Tenant Data Privacy Act's limits on app-only entry.

Plan shared doors as building systems, with an owner of the account, a written register and a key backup for every resident.

Landmark Rules

Working Inside the Landmark Rules, Step by Step

Owners in the Heights know that the Landmarks Preservation Commission matters, but the detail is easy to lose. The Commission's guidebook says that with few exceptions, owners need an LPC permit before doing work that affects the exterior of a landmark property, including work inside the boundaries of a historic district and work in front, side and rear yards. It also lists what may not need a permit, including repairing or replacing hardware such as hinges, knobs, locks and handles, and ordinary maintenance and repair of existing health, safety and utility equipment.

For a smart-lock project that gives a simple order of work. First, keep as much as possible on the door: a lock on the door is hardware. Second, if something must be mounted beside the door, use the published criteria: within or adjacent to the door opening, as small as possible, neutral and discreet, one intercom panel per door opening, fixings and conduit into plain masonry and mortar joints rather than decorative stone or cast iron. Third, if the plan involves a new door, the Commission looks for a design that matches the historic door's materials, operation and details, and the original fabric around the doorway is to be kept.

We do not file permits on owners' behalf, and we do not tell anyone a permit is unnecessary when the Commission is the one who decides. What we do is shape the job so it sits within the published rules wherever it can, and hand you the cut sheets, photos and drawings that an application calls for. If you are unsure, the guidebook's own advice is to call the Commission at 212-669-7817 or write to info@lpc.nyc.gov before work starts.

Before You Buy a Lock: Five Photos That Save a Visit

Most of the questions that decide a smart lock on an older Heights door can be answered from a phone camera before anyone travels. One: the edge of the door, with the door open. This shows the faceplate of the existing lock, whether it is a mortise lock in a pocket or a bored deadbolt, and the screws that hold it. Key Backup

Keys Still Matter After the Door Goes Keyless

A smart lock replaces the habit of reaching for a key, not the key itself. Every good smart deadbolt we fit keeps a mechanical override, and every household that uses the door should hold a working key for it. Batteries go flat, phones get lost, and a firmware update can leave a lock waiting for a reboot. A key in a drawer at home and another with a trusted neighbour solves all three.

In shared buildings the key has a legal side as well. Tenants in multiple dwellings are entitled to the entrance-door key the Housing Maintenance Code requires the owner to provide, and owners may not make a smart access system the only way into a tenant's home. Co-op proprietary leases commonly require a duplicate key to each apartment for emergencies. A keyless plan in the Heights therefore always includes a key plan: who holds which key, where the spare for the street door lives, and who is called if a key is lost.

We record the keyway and the number of keys cut for each lock on the handover register, alongside the codes and app users, so that when the board, the owner or the next tenant asks who can open the door, there is one complete answer.

Glossary

A Short Glossary of Heights Door Hardware

Mortise lock. A lock body set into a deep rectangular pocket cut in the edge of the door, usually with a latch and a deadbolt in one case. Common on older rowhouse and apartment doors. Some smart locks are made in mortise format; others work alongside an existing mortise lock.

Bored deadbolt. The modern type: a round hole through the face of the door and a smaller one into the edge. Most consumer smart locks are designed for this preparation, measured by its backset and the door's thickness.

Backset. The distance from the edge of the door to the centre of the bore. Older doors do not always follow modern standard sizes, which is why we measure it before recommending a lock.

Keeper and strike. The plate and pocket on the frame that receive the bolt. A misaligned or weak keeper is the most common reason a smart lock jams, drains its batteries or can be forced.

Electric strike. A keeper with a release mechanism, wired to a reader, keypad or buzzer. When it is energised (or de-energised, depending on type) the door can be pushed open while the lock itself stays locked. The usual hardware on a shared street door.

Retrofit lock. Motorised hardware that swaps out nothing but the interior turn-piece of a single-cylinder deadbolt; the cylinder, the key and the street face of the door stay exactly as they were. On an original front door it is the least invasive choice we can offer.

Thread border router. The translator between a battery lock's low-power mesh radio and your ordinary home network; in many homes it is built into a speaker or hub you already own.

Request-to-exit. The inside half of a wired entrance: a push plate or a sensor that sees someone approaching from within and lets the strike go, so leaving the building never waits on a credential.

Usually, yes, as long as the door itself is in use and the contractor's shed does not block the reader position. Buildings taller than six stories must have their facades inspected every five years under the city's Facade Inspection Safety Program, so sheds are common on larger pre-war buildings. We coordinate with the managing agent and the facade contractor so our work and theirs do not clash.

Can you send cut sheets and marked-up photos for an LPC application or a board approval?
+

Yes. For any reader, keypad, camera or panel that will be mounted on the facade, we provide the manufacturer cut sheet, photos of the entrance with the proposed position marked, and a short description of the cable route. These match the materials the Landmarks Preservation Commission lists for equipment applications, and they are what most boards ask for too.

Can the old intercom wire to our street door be reused for a new reader or strike?
+

Sometimes. Older buzzer and intercom wiring is often light-gauge and may have been spliced many times, so we test it for continuity and voltage drop before relying on it. If it is sound it can carry a strike or reader; if not, it can often serve as a draw wire to pull a new cable along the same route without opening walls.

Does it matter which way our front door swings or which side the hinges are on?
+

It matters for the lock choice and the setup, not for whether a smart lock is possible. Most smart deadbolts are reversible and are set up for left- or right-hand doors during installation. Doors that swing outward, or pairs where the active leaf is on the unusual side, are checked at the walkthrough so the right hardware is ordered.
