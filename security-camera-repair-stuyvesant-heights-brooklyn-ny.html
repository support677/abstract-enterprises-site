The Neighborhood

Why Stuyvesant Heights Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Stuyvesant Heights is the southeastern part of Bedford-Stuyvesant that took its name, early in the twentieth century, from Stuyvesant Avenue, its main street. Most of it was built between about 1870 and 1920, much of it in a rush: as late as 1886 more than half the land in today's expanded historic district was still empty. The result is block after block of speculatively built rowhouses in Queen Anne, neo-Grec, Romanesque Revival and Renaissance Revival dress, a scattering of older wood-frame houses, and a run of four- and five-story apartment houses from the 1890s. That is the short history. For a camera repair the useful part is what those buildings are made of and how a camera was fixed to them, because that decides what a repair can and cannot do.

The landmark map comes first. The original Stuyvesant Heights Historic District, designated in 1971, is an L-shaped area north of Fulton Street with more than 430 buildings on 13 blocks, roughly between Stuyvesant and Tompkins Avenues and Macon and Chauncey Streets. On April 16, 2013, the Commission wrapped a much larger district around it, the Bedford-Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights Historic District, with about 825 buildings running from Halsey and Macon Streets down to Fulton Street and from Tompkins Avenue across to Malcolm X Boulevard. Between them they cover most of the neighborhood. So when a camera on a Stuyvesant Heights facade dies, the first thing worth knowing is not the brand but whether the repair can stay a repair: same place, same size, same anchors. If it can, the Commission's guidebook treats it as ordinary maintenance. If the honest fix is to move the camera, add one, or change its look, it becomes a different kind of job, and we say so before anything is drilled.

The buildings themselves come second. The Commission's designation report for the expanded district describes rowhouses with shared first-floor entrances through a vestibule into a hallway, round-headed double-leaf doors, areaways and raised basements, and the 1890s French flats of four to five stories. Each of those features shows up in our repair calls. The vestibule is where the door camera usually lives, boxed in between the outer doors and the inner one. The raised basement and the areaway are where the recorder, the power supply and the garden-door camera sit, closest to the street and to everything that runs off it in winter. The double-leaf doors mean visitors stand on the stoop well above the sidewalk, which changes the angle a camera needs. And the French flats are small buildings with building-sized systems: a recorder in the cellar, cameras in the stair hall and at the street door, and a landlord who is not always the person living closest to the problem.

Third is time. Many of these houses have been converted more than once, from one family to several and sometimes back again, and the cameras tend to record the history. We open cellars where an earlier owner's system still runs beside a newer one, where cameras are powered from a tenant's outlet, or where a hallway camera watches a door that no longer leads to a separate apartment. Newer buildings bring a different version of the same problem. Chauncey + Lewis, a group of new rowhouses at 39 Chauncey Street and 437–441 Lewis Avenue, and 524 Halsey Street, a former carriage house and auto repair shop turned into a 33-unit condominium with an attended lobby, came with modern network systems, and the people who set those systems up are not always the people who now need to log in to them.

Fourth is the street. The neighborhood's southern edge is Fulton Street, and in June 2026 manhole fires on Fulton Street in Bed-Stuy left around 50 Con Edison customers in the dark and cut power to what News 12 called "multiple rows of businesses," some of them still without power the next morning. After Winter Storm Fern, the Brooklyn Eagle reported more than 990 Bed-Stuy customers out at the peak and road salt damaging several manholes. Neither report lists every block affected, so we do not claim which Stuyvesant Heights streets lost power. But every one of those events sends a hard stop and a surge through recorders, PoE switches and camera supplies, and a share of what goes down does not come back right. Those systems reach us weeks later, usually the first time someone tries to play back a day that matters. Many owners pair the camera repair with a look at the door buzzer or intercom at the same doorway, since on these stoops the two systems share a wall, a cellar and often a power supply.

Security Camera Repair for Stuyvesant Heights's Architecture

The same dead camera is a different job on each kind of Stuyvesant Heights building. The surface it is fixed to changes, the cable route changes, the recorder's hiding place changes, and so does the person who has to say yes. These are the buildings we are called to most.

The single-family brownstone

A rowhouse back to one household: stoop and double-leaf doors, a vestibule, a parlor floor above a raised basement, and a recorder somewhere in the cellar. Facade cameras are almost always inside a historic district.

The two- and three-family house

An owner's duplex with rentals above or below, sharing one stoop, one vestibule and often one recorder that grew a camera at a time as the house was divided.

The 1890s French flat

Four- and five-story apartment houses with a street door, a narrow stair hall and a cellar. Building-sized systems owned by a landlord who may live elsewhere.

The wood-frame survivor

The older frame houses that outlasted the brick and brownstone rows, with cameras fixed through clapboard or siding and cable running behind wood trim.

The new rowhouse and the condo

Recent buildings such as Chauncey + Lewis and 524 Halsey Street: network cameras, video entry, a rack or an attended lobby, and logins that began with the developer.

Churches and community buildings

Congregations, schools and community groups in freestanding and rowhouse buildings, sometimes several organizations behind one door, each wanting to see different cameras.

The single-family brownstone: a long trip from the cornice to the cellar

On a brownstone that has gone back to one family, the cameras usually cover three places: the stoop and the parlor doors, the areaway and garden door below them, and the back garden. The front camera is often mounted high, under the cornice or on the lintel over the doors, because that was the only spot the original installer could reach with a straight ladder. Its cable then has a long way to go, down three stories of facade or through the house, to a recorder in the cellar. Most of the repair calls we get from houses like this are about that path rather than the camera. A connector that was wrapped in tape instead of sealed lets water in at the top; a cable pinched by a storm window has worn through; a run stapled to the facade has pulled loose and swings in the wind. When the camera itself is dead, the repair is usually simple: a same-size replacement on the same bracket. When the path is the problem, we re-terminate or replace the cable along its existing route where we can, and where it runs in plain sight we move it into a mortar joint or around through the areaway so the house looks the way the district expects.

Inside, the cellar is the hazard. Recorders end up on shelves next to the boiler, under the parlor stair, or on the floor of the raised basement where the house takes in water during heavy storms. Heat, dust and damp all shorten a recorder's life. If yours sits low or close to the boiler, part of the repair price may be moving it a few feet onto a shelf or a wall board, with its power on a small backup so the next outage does not hit it cold.

The two- and three-family house: one stoop, several households, one recorder

Many Stuyvesant Heights houses were divided into two or three apartments decades ago, and some have been combined and divided again since. The cameras reflect every stage. A typical system started with one camera at the stoop for the owner, gained a camera in the shared hallway when a tenant moved in, then a yard camera when the garden apartment was rented separately, each added by whoever was around at the time. The recorder is in the cellar, often reached through the garden apartment. Half the cameras may be powered from the owner's circuit and half from a tenant's.

That arrangement causes faults that look like broken equipment. A tenant moves out and their electric account closes, and the hallway camera goes dark. Someone flips a breaker in the wrong apartment. A tenant's Wi-Fi router is replaced and the yard camera, which was quietly using it, drops off. The repair here is part electrical bookkeeping and part camera work: we trace which outlet powers each camera, move the system's power onto the owner's side using a single supply or a PoE switch where that is possible, and leave a written map of what is where. Anything that needs a new electrical circuit is a job for an electrician, and we tell you that rather than doing it. We also sort out who can see which cameras, so that the owner and the tenants each see the doors they share and nobody sees into someone else's home.

The 1890s French flat: a building system in a house-sized building

The four- and five-story apartment houses that went up in the 1890s are small by city standards but large for a camera system run by one person. A typical one has a camera at the street door, one in the vestibule, one or two in the stair hall, perhaps one in the cellar or at the back yard, and a recorder in a locked cellar room. The landlord or managing agent owns it, the super has the key, and the tenants are the ones who notice when it stops. Brick Underground has pointed out that New York buildings are not required to have working cameras at all, and that landlords are not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process. That is the legal picture. The practical one is that a hall camera everyone assumes is recording, and is not, causes more trouble than no camera.

Stair halls bring their own repair problems. Cameras are screwed into plaster ceilings that have been patched many times, and over the years the anchors loosen and the camera droops until it watches the floor. Stair skylights and landing windows throw a strip of daylight across the frame. The cable rises through the stair hall in surface raceway that tenants knock loose with furniture on moving day. We repair these with mounts anchored into the structure behind the plaster, cameras chosen for mixed light, and raceway put back where it will survive the next move. Because the building is a rental, we work through the owner or the agent, and we leave a written note of what was found and changed that the owner can show tenants or an inspector if a question comes up.

The wood-frame survivor: the wall moves with the weather

The mid-nineteenth-century frame houses that still stand among the brick and brownstone rows are the exception on most blocks, but they generate more than their share of camera repairs. A camera on a frame house is screwed through siding or clapboard into wood, and wood moves. Over a few winters the mount loosens, the camera tilts, and the hole its cable passes through opens up enough to let water follow the cable inside. The failure often shows up first as a picture that fogs on humid mornings, then as a connector inside the wall that has turned green. When we repair one we check the wall around the hole as well as the camera, fit a weatherproof connector rather than taping the old one, add a drip loop so water falls away instead of running in, and seal what we opened. Painting and siding repair are for your painter or carpenter; we leave the opening sealed and tell you if the trim around it needs attention.

The new rowhouse and the condo: good hardware, missing paperwork

The newest buildings in the neighborhood have the newest systems: network cameras on a PoE switch, a recorder in a cellar rack or a lobby back office, a video entry station at the door, and in a building like 524 Halsey Street an attended lobby where staff watch the cameras on a monitor. The hardware is rarely the problem in the first few years. The problem is that the system was commissioned by a developer's installer, the admin password and the cloud account are in that installer's name, and the closing paperwork listed model numbers but not credentials. When something does fail, nobody on site can log in to see why. A repair in these buildings usually starts by recovering admin access with the board's or owner's authorization, setting up named accounts for the people who need them, and documenting the system so the next problem does not start from zero. In a condo with a managing agent and building rules, we follow those rules: insurance certificate, scheduled access, sign-in at the desk.

Churches and community buildings: one door, several organizations

Stuyvesant Heights has congregations and community organizations in buildings of every size, from churches on the avenues to rowhouses used as offices and classrooms. In some, several groups share one building. The Stuyvesant Mansion at Stuyvesant Avenue and Decatur Street, for example, has been used as community space by Gumbo Workshop, Freebrook Academy and GrowHouse, and BK Magazine reported in November 2025 that a court had ordered the building sold. Situations like that raise questions a camera repair has to answer before it starts: which organization owns the cameras, who can authorize work, who should be able to watch which entrance, and what happens to the equipment if a group moves out or the building changes hands. We get those answers in writing first, keep each organization's logins separate where the recorder allows it, and schedule around services, classes and programs.

Field Notes

Field Notes: We Read the Facade Before We Open the Recorder

This is the working method our technicians use on a Stuyvesant Heights repair call, written as a method rather than a story about one house. Before the ladder comes off the van, the technician stands across the street and looks at the front of the building. How many cameras are on it, and how wide is the house? Where is each one fixed: in a mortar joint, on plain brick, on the lintel, on carved stone, on a cast-iron railing? How much cable shows, and where does it go? Is there a camera in the vestibule behind the outer doors, and can it see past them? That two-minute look tells us whether the repair can be a straight swap on the same anchors or whether the honest answer involves moving something, and it lets us tell the owner which it is before any money is spent.

Then we go to the other end of the cable. In most brownstones that means the cellar or the raised basement, and the first things we look at are not the recorder's menus but its surroundings: how close it is to the boiler or the floor, whether it is plugged into a backup or a power strip, whether the PoE switch beside it feels hot, and whether there is a second, older recorder still running on the same shelf. Only after that do we log in, read the recorder's event log, and check what it has actually saved. Working from both ends inward, facade first and cellar second, is how we avoid replacing a camera that was fine while the real fault sat in a damp power supply three floors below it.

Fault by Fault

Inherited Mounts, Salted Areaways and Surges From the Street: How Stuyvesant Heights Camera Systems Go Wrong

Cameras that were never put up the way the district asks

Plenty of the cameras on Stuyvesant Heights facades went up before their current owners arrived, and some went up in ways the Landmarks guidebook would not approve today: drilled into a carved lintel or a decorative band, clamped to a cast-iron railing, three or four of them across a 20-foot front, or fed by a cable taped down the face of the house. The guidebook's limits for new equipment are specific. On a small residential or commercial building, no more than two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage. Equipment attached "at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and "not attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." Conduit concealed through plain masonry, with staff able to approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit."

Those rules describe new installations; repairing what is already there is ordinary maintenance. But a repair is the cheapest moment to put a badly placed camera right, because the ladder is already up and the old camera is already coming down. When we find one of these, we give you the choice in writing. One option is the straight swap, same place and same size, which keeps the job a repair. The other is moving the camera to a mortar joint or plain masonry nearby, dropping a camera the frontage does not need, or hiding a run of exposed cable, and we tell you plainly when a change like that is new work that may call for a staff-level Landmarks permit. The Commission says 95 percent of its approvals are issued by staff, and for an in-kind replacement it asks for the manufacturer's cut sheet rather than drawings. We supply cut sheets for anything we install.

The areaway and the garden door: where winter collects

The areaway in front of a raised basement is the lowest outdoor spot on the lot, and everything from the stoop and the sidewalk ends up there: rain, meltwater, leaves, and the rock salt shoveled off the steps all winter. The camera that watches the garden door is usually mounted low under the stoop, and its connector and power supply are often within splash distance of the ground. After Winter Storm Fern the Brooklyn Eagle reported road salt damaging several manholes in Bed-Stuy; what salt does to Con Edison's equipment underground it also does, on a small scale, to an unsealed camera connector in a front areaway. The symptoms come in spring: a picture that flickers on wet days, a camera that drops out after a thaw and comes back when it dries, and green crust inside the connector when we open it.

The repair is rarely just a new camera. We cut back to clean cable, fit sealed connectors, move the junction above the splash line, and where the camera itself sits in standing water's reach, raise it or turn it so it looks down at the garden door from higher under the stoop. A hooded camera keeps the slush off the lens. If the power supply for that camera lives in the areaway too, we move it inside.

Surges and dead stops from the street

When power on a block fails and returns, the parts of a camera system fail in a predictable order. Small plug-in supplies die outright. A PoE switch loses a port, so one camera stays dark while its neighbors work. The recorder comes back with its drive unmounted, its settings partly reset or its clock at the factory date. Battery backups, if there were any, drain in the first hour of a long outage and then let everything crash anyway. Con Edison said heat was "almost certainly the cause" of the June 2026 manhole fires on Fulton Street, which cut power to rows of businesses; the winter events were blamed on salt. Either way, the damage looks the same in the cellar or behind the counter.

Most of it does not show on the live view, which is why the first sign is so often a playback gap found weeks later. Your Questions Answered

The front of a landmarked house

A cable runs in plain view from our cornice camera down the face of our brownstone to a basement window. When the camera is replaced, can that cable be hidden?

Usually, yes, and the replacement visit is the best time to do it. The Landmarks guidebook wants conduit concealed through plain masonry, with only about ten inches visible, so we look for a route that follows a mortar joint or the edge of a pier, enters the wall near the camera and drops inside, or runs down beside the stoop into the areaway. On a three-story front the route is set by what is inside the wall behind the camera, which we check before drilling. If no clean route exists, we tell you before starting, and a wireless bridge to a nearby window is sometimes the fallback.

Our parlor-floor camera looks straight across the street into the windows of the houses opposite. Can the repair keep the sidewalk in view and black out their windows?

Yes. Most current cameras and recorders support privacy masks: blacked-out zones that are never shown or recorded, drawn over windows, doorways or a neighbor's stoop. We set the masks with you watching, then tilt or zoom the camera so the sidewalk and your own stoop fill more of the picture. On a narrow Stuyvesant Heights street a slightly lower aim often does most of the work before any mask is needed, and it puts more pixels on the faces at your door.

Vestibules, stoops and talking to visitors

Our camera sits in the vestibule between the original double doors and the inner glass door, so at night, with the outer doors closed, it records a closed door. Can one camera cover both situations?

One camera in that spot cannot see through closed wooden doors, so the honest answer is a second, small camera outside, or moving the one you have. The usual fix keeps the vestibule camera for packages and the inner door and adds a compact camera outside, under the door hood or tucked against the frame, looking down the stoop. Inside a historic district that outside camera is new equipment, so it should be small, in a plain joint or on wood, and within the two-per-25-feet limit; we check your frontage first. If you would rather not add anything outside, a doorbell station at the outer doors does the same job.

We answer visitors through the speaker on our stoop camera instead of walking down from the top floor, but by the time our voice comes out they have already turned to leave. Is the delay the camera or our internet?

Usually neither is broken: it is the route the audio takes. Many camera apps send your voice from your phone to the manufacturer's cloud and back down to the camera, and on a busy home connection that can add a few seconds. Brownstone owners have long used a camera's two-way speaker this way, and Brownstoner wrote up one couple doing exactly that during a 2019 renovation. We check the camera's firmware and your home network, move the camera onto a wired connection if it is on weak Wi-Fi, and if the delay is built into the app, we suggest a door station designed for talk, which answers far faster.

French flats and small walk-ups

The stair-hall camera on the second floor of our 1890s walk-up keeps tipping forward because its screws are in a soft plaster ceiling. Can it be fixed so it stays aimed?

Yes. Plaster over old lath does not hold small anchors for long, especially with a camera's cable tugging on it. We remount the camera on a plate fixed through the plaster into a joist or the lath behind it, or move it onto the wall where the masonry gives a solid hold, and secure the cable so it no longer pulls on the camera. If the camera has been pointing at the floor for months, we also check whether the recorder flagged anything, because a drooping camera often hides a second fault.

Couriers in our walk-up leave packages on the radiator just inside the street door, which is out of the hall camera's view. Should the camera move, or should a second one go in?

Moving the camera is usually enough if it can see the radiator and the door together; a second camera is better if the hall is long. News 12 reported a package worth about $1,500 taken from in front of an apartment door on Bainbridge Street in Bed-Stuy in February 2025, and the report does not say how the courier got in. For a walk-up, the camera worth having shows the street door opening and the spot where packages sit in one frame. A slightly wider lens or a position a few feet further along the ceiling often does it.

Storefront buildings

The cameras for the apartments above our Fulton Street store run off the store's electric meter, so when the store lost power, the residential cameras went dark too. Can the two be separated?

Yes, in most buildings. The residential cameras can be moved onto a supply or PoE switch plugged into an outlet on the landlord's own meter, usually in the cellar beside the building's recorder, with a small battery backup so short drops do not reboot them. If no landlord outlet exists where it is needed, adding one is an electrician's job, and we tell you exactly what to ask for. We then label which recorder and which meter each camera belongs to.

Delivery trucks double-park in front of our Malcolm X Boulevard shop for long stretches and block the sidewalk camera's view of our door. Can the camera be placed so a truck does not hide it?

Usually, by changing height and angle rather than adding cameras. A camera mounted at eye level behind the window sees the side of the truck. Mounted higher, under the sign band or inside at the top of the glass, and aimed down along the storefront rather than out at the street, it watches your door and the stretch of sidewalk in front of it no matter what parks at the curb. If the building is inside the historic district, an inside-the-glass position also avoids new equipment on the facade.

Newer buildings and buildings that change hands

Our new rowhouse came with network cameras wired to a rack in the cellar, and the closing binder lists the models but no passwords. How do we get the system into our name without starting over?

Ask the builder for the credentials first, in writing; sometimes they turn up. We do that with you present, set up accounts in your household's names, update the firmware, and write down what is on the rack, including the switch and any cloud link, so you are not depending on anyone else again.

In our condo with a staffed lobby, the desk monitor cycles through the cameras so quickly that the attendant cannot keep an eye on the front door. Can the display be changed without touching the cameras?

Yes. That is a recorder or display setting, not a camera fault. Varifocal domes and bullets

Cameras whose lens can be zoomed after mounting. The answer when a stoop camera on a deep brownstone sees the whole sidewalk but no faces, or when a stair-hall camera in a French flat has to read someone at the far landing.

Video door stations and doorbells

Pan-tilt and 360-degree cameras

Motorized and fisheye cameras on shop floors along Fulton Street, on church halls and in rear yards that run deep. Motors, saved positions and dome condensation are what we fix most often, along with cameras left parked facing the wrong way.

Wi-Fi, battery and cube cameras

Consumer cameras in apartments, in parlor windows and over rear yards. Signal through two or three brick bearing walls, batteries in winter, and app accounts in the name of someone who has moved out are the usual problems.

Hooded and backlight-rated door cameras

Cameras built for an entrance that faces a bright street. The usual replacement for vestibule and areaway cameras on the sunny side of the block, where the face under the stoop is in shade and the sidewalk behind it is not.

PoE switches, power boxes and battery backups

The parts that fail when Fulton Street's power misbehaves. Checked on every outage call, replaced with the right capacity, and set so the recorder shuts down cleanly instead of crashing when the backup runs out.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Stuyvesant Heights

Search summaries and listing sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give camera advice written for any house in any city. Some of it holds everywhere. Some of it leads Stuyvesant Heights owners to spend money on the wrong part, or to put off a repair because they think the landmark rules forbid it. These are the claims we hear repeated most often, and what we see on repairs in the two historic districts and along Fulton Street.

"If the cameras show live video, they are recording"

The live picture and the recording are two different jobs, and a recorder can do the first while failing the second for weeks. Repair companies list the same causes again and again: a failed hard drive, a recording schedule that was changed or never set, storage settings that stop when the drive fills, or a drive too small for the cameras on it. One New York repair firm puts it bluntly: "Running out of storage is the most common reason" cameras stop recording.

In a brownstone, the recorder is often in the cellar and nobody looks at it until something happens on the stoop. That is when the owner discovers the last saved hour was in the spring. We see this more after power events, when a recorder restarts into a state where it shows cameras but has quietly stopped writing to the drive.

So every repair ends with playback, not live view: we pick a time from the past day, play it back on each camera, and turn on the recorder's own alerts for drive errors and recording stopped, sent to a phone you actually check.

"Landmark rules mean you need a permit to touch a camera on a historic house"

Not for a repair. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says no permit is needed for "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment." A dead camera replaced with a similar one in the same position falls under that, and so does fixing a cable or a connector where it already runs.

The rules start to apply when a camera is new or moves. Then the guidebook asks for cameras that are well spaced, no more than two per 25 feet of street frontage on a small building, fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and with very little visible conduit. If a change needs approval, the LPC says 95% of its approvals are issued at staff level, not at a public hearing.

The practical result: most repairs on Macon, Decatur or MacDonough Street go ahead like any other. If what you want is a different position, we tell you before anything is drilled, and we can provide the manufacturer cut sheet that staff ask for on a replacement.

"After a power outage, just wait and the cameras will come back"

Many do. But Stuyvesant Heights sits next to a commercial corridor with a history of underground electrical trouble. In June 2026, manhole fires near 1242 Fulton Street cut power to rows of businesses, and some stores were still dark the next morning. After Winter Storm Fern, hundreds of Bed-Stuy customers were without power for days in single-digit cold, with snow and road salt damaging manholes.

When power returns hard after an event like that, the parts that fail are the small ones: a PoE switch port, a cheap power box, a recorder drive that was writing when the power died, a clock that reset to a year in the past. The live view can look normal while one of those is broken.

If your system came back after an outage, check three things: that every camera is on screen, that yesterday's video plays back, and that the time on the recorder is right. If any of the three is wrong, that is the repair, and it is usually smaller than a new system.

"The police can get your camera video whenever they want"

Not through your doorbell app anymore. Ring ended its "Request for Assistance" tool in January 2024, and as News 12 reported, "law enforcement agencies can still access videos using a search warrant." Owners of wired systems were never in such a program in the first place.

What this means for repair is simple: if your footage might help the 79th or 81st Precinct, it is your job to keep it and export it properly. That only works if the recorder was saving, the clock was right and you know how to copy a clip without overwriting it.

Our part is the first two plus a lesson in the third. Before any work, we copy the footage that matters with the recorder's own timestamps, and after the repair we show you how to do it yourself, so you are not waiting on us when a detective calls.

"A landlord has to let tenants see the camera footage"

Tenants in the walk-ups and multi-family houses here often assume so, especially after a package goes missing. Brick Underground put the question to New York attorneys: landlords are generally not obligated to show tenants footage outside a legal process, and buildings are not required to have working cameras at all. Attorney Catherine Grad's view of reviewing footage when thefts keep happening: "I don't think that it's such an arduous duty to impose on the landlord."

For an owner, that is a policy choice rather than a technical one. But the technical side decides whether the choice is even possible: a recorder with no clear owner, no working login and a full drive cannot show anybody anything.

When we repair a system in a two- or three-family house, we set it up so the owner can find and export one clip without handing out the master password, which makes it easier to help a tenant without giving away the whole system.

"Any handyman can fix a security camera"

An electrician can fix the outlet the recorder plugs into, and a handy owner can re-aim a camera. But in New York, companies that install, service or maintain security systems for others, including CCTV used to detect or watch for intrusion or theft, are licensed by the Department of State. Owners working on their own property are exempt; people working on someone else's are not. We work under NYS license #12000287431.

The tools are different too. Most camera faults in an old row house are found with a cable tester, a PoE tester, the recorder's logs and a look at the picture at the right time of day, not with a voltage meter on the house wiring.

If someone has already tried to fix your system, tell us what they changed. It saves time, and it is common to find that a well-meant fix moved the problem rather than solved it.

"Camera repair is a $100 to $300 job"

That range describes one consumer camera on a suburban house. The expensive repair is the one done twice: a camera replaced when the drive was the fault, or a drive replaced when a failing power box was killing it. Testing in order, from the outlet to the phone, is how we avoid both.

FAQ

Stuyvesant Heights Security Camera Repair FAQ

We inherited a family brownstone after a relative passed away, and the camera app, the recorder password and the email on the account were all theirs. How do we take the system over?
+

It can almost always be done without replacing anything. First, decide whether anything on the recorder needs to be kept, such as years of family video or a recent incident, because the usual way back in is a factory reset that clears settings and sometimes footage. If it matters, we copy it off first. Then we reset the recorder and cameras, create new logins in the name of whoever now owns or manages the house, connect the phone app to that person's account, and check every camera and the drive while we are there. If the estate is still being settled, tell us who has authority over the house, and we take instructions from that person only.

During our renovation the contractors, and later our cleaner, were given the recorder's main password. The work is done. How do we shut that access without losing our settings?
+

Change the administrator password and remove every user and phone you do not recognize, which on most recorders keeps the cameras, schedules and motion zones exactly as they are. We also check the remote-access side: many systems were added to an installer's or contractor's own app account, and that link survives a password change unless it is removed separately. Then we make one owner login and, if someone else needs to watch, a separate view-only login for them, so the next time a crew or a helper needs to see the stoop camera, nobody has to be given the keys to the whole system.

Our battery video doorbell is stuck to the original wood frame of our double doors with adhesive and keeps falling off. Can it be fixed to the frame properly, or does that need Landmarks approval?
+

Ask before you drill. The Landmarks guidebook treats repair of existing equipment as work that needs no permit, but it names doorbells and intercom panels installed within or next to a door opening as items that need one. If the doorbell was never there with approval, screwing it into the original frame is the kind of change staff want to see. Often the better answer is a small plate or mount on plain masonry or the inner vestibule door, or a wired door camera in a spot the rules allow, and we can lay out those options before anything is fixed in place.

Two dead cameras and their brackets from an old system are still bolted to our facade beside the one that works. Can you take them down, and what happens to the holes?
+

Yes. We remove the dead cameras, brackets and loose cable, confirm on the recorder that nothing we take down was still in use, and cap and seal the openings so water cannot get behind them. We do not patch or repoint masonry ourselves; if the old anchors leave holes in the stone or mortar that should be filled to match, that is a mason's job, and we will tell you exactly where they are. Taking old equipment down also helps if you later want to add a camera, because the Landmarks guidebook counts the cameras on a front.

The hallway lights in our walk-up are on a timer, and when they shut off at night the stair-hall camera turns grainy and floods us with alerts. Is the camera failing?
+

Probably not. When the lights go off, the camera switches to infrared, refocuses and sees the whole change as motion, and in a narrow plaster stair hall its own infrared can bounce off the walls and wash the picture out. The usual repair is to set the camera's day and night switch to a schedule or a slower response, tune its infrared for a short distance, and use person detection instead of plain motion for alerts. If the picture is still poor in the dark, a camera that sees well in low light without infrared, or a small light on the landing, solves it for good.

The date and the camera name printed on our stoop camera's picture sit right where a visitor's face appears. Can that text be moved?
+

Yes, and it takes minutes. The on-screen date, time and camera name are an overlay set in the camera or the recorder, and each piece can be moved to a corner, made smaller or turned off for that camera. We keep the date and time visible somewhere in the frame on cameras whose clips might go to the police or an insurer, because a clip with a readable time is easier to use. While we are at it, we check that the clock behind that text is actually right.

Can the recorder start recording and send us an alert when someone opens the cellar door under our stoop, even at a moment the camera does not see motion?
+

On many recorders, yes. Most have alarm inputs on the back that accept a simple contact on a door. Wired to the under-stoop door, the contact tells the recorder the door opened, and the recorder can mark that moment, record the cameras nearby at full quality and push an alert to your phone. It works even when a person slips through at the edge of a camera's view. We check whether your recorder has free inputs and quote the contact and wiring before installing anything.

We had our stoop steps and the sidewalk in front of the house redone, and the camera on the areaway gate has been dead ever since. Was the cable cut under the new concrete?
+

Very possibly, but we test before anyone thinks about breaking concrete. A cable tester tells us whether the run is open or shorted and roughly how far along it the break is. If the break is inside the new work, the usual repair is a new run along a route that avoids the sidewalk, such as along the areaway wall or up from the cellar, rather than digging. If the cable is fine, the fault is at the gate camera or its connector, which masons and concrete crews often knock or soak during the job.

We do not want our cameras reachable from the internet at all. Can the repaired system record and play back only inside the house?
+

Yes. A wired system with a recorder works without the internet: it records to its own drive and plays back on a monitor or on a phone connected to your home network. We can turn off the maker's cloud connection and remote-access features, block the recorder from reaching the internet at the router, and still leave you able to watch on your own Wi-Fi. The trade-off is that you will not see the cameras or get alerts when you are away, and you will not get automatic firmware updates, so we note the firmware version and check it on future visits.

People keep dumping bags and furniture by our trash bins in the areaway at night. Can our camera be set up to show who is doing it?
+

Usually, with a change of angle and settings rather than a new camera. A camera aimed down into the areaway shows the bins and the tops of heads; one aimed across the gate toward the sidewalk at about head height shows faces and whatever is being carried. At night, the areaway is often darker than the street, so we check the camera's low-light picture at the hour the dumping happens and add a motion alert limited to the bin area. Clips of someone dumping can help if the Sanitation Department tickets you for the mess, but that is between you and the city.

Our French flat's building cameras and the apartment intercom are two different systems. Can one recorder save the intercom's camera picture too?
+

It depends on the intercom. Many newer video door stations can send their picture to a recorder as a camera stream, so the street door is saved with the rest of the building's video. Older intercoms, and some sold as closed systems, cannot. Then the answer is a small camera of its own near the door, placed where the Landmarks rules allow on a historic building. We check the intercom's model and settings before promising anything, and test that the saved picture is actually usable, because a door station's camera is built for a face at arm's length, not for the sidewalk.

Our church shares its hall with other groups during the week. Who should hold the camera system's logins so it does not get lost again when a volunteer moves on?
+

The organization, not a person. We set the owner login on an email address the church controls, such as an office or trustees account, and give named people their own logins with only the access they need, view-only for most of them. Groups that use the hall get no login unless the church decides otherwise. We leave a short written record of the recorder's location, model and how to reach the owner account, kept with the building's other papers, so the next volunteer does not inherit a locked system.
