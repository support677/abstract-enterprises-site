Approvals

Who Signs Off Before the Drill Comes Out in Flushing

A soundbar is a small job, but in much of Flushing somebody other than the household has a say in where screws go. Knowing who that is, and what they usually want, is the difference between an installation on the first visit and a $150 return trip. This is how it tends to work in each kind of building we see here.

Condominium towers and mid-rises

In a condo the unit owner owns the interior, but the building's declaration and house rules decide how contractors get in. Expect the management office to ask for a certificate of insurance naming the condominium and its managing agent, a contact name for the technician, and a booked elevator or loading-dock time. Many offices also set quiet hours for drilling. Partition walls inside the apartment are generally the owner's to fix into; walls shared with the corridor or the next unit, and anything touching the building's structure, may need the board's approval. If you rent a condo unit from its owner, the owner's written consent comes first, and the building's rules still apply.

Co-operatives

In a co-op you own shares and hold a proprietary lease, and the lease and house rules give the board control over alterations. A soundbar on a bracket is often treated as a minor alteration or no alteration at all, but that is the board's call, not ours. The managing agent normally wants the insurance certificate, sometimes a short description of the work and the anchors, and confirmation that nothing electrical or structural is being changed. Ask the agent first; we draft the description and send the certificate free, and the bar can live on the console until the answer arrives.

Houses in Murray Hill and Broadway–Flushing

An owner-occupied house needs nobody's permission for a soundbar inside the living room. The historic-district listings in Broadway–Flushing are state and national register designations, and the neighborhood's deed covenants and low-density zoning concern what the house looks like from the street — fences, garages, roofs, density — not what is screwed to an interior wall. In a two-family house the owner decides, but it is worth telling a tenant on the other floor before the hammer drill starts.

Rentals

If you rent, the lease decides whether holes are allowed and whether they must be filled when you leave. Where the landlord won't agree to a wall mount, the console placement or a bar fixed to the TV's own stand gives most of the benefit with no holes at all. If permission comes later, moving the bar to the wall is priced as an ordinary wall mount.

Shops, food courts and office condos

Commercial tenants answer to the lease and, in the 39th Avenue developments and indoor malls, to a management office or a commercial condominium board. Expect insurance certificates naming the owner and managing company, set working hours that avoid trading time, and in some centers a written description or sketch. Electrical changes always need a licensed electrician, and in public-facing spaces the bracket position has to respect the accessibility rules described in the commercial section below.

Whichever applies, send us the name and email of whoever approves the work. We send them the certificate and the description directly, which saves you a round of forwarding and usually gets the answer back faster.

Compliance notes for Flushing businesses

  • Music limits are measured in the apartment upstairs. As the city's Department of Environmental Protection summarizes the rules, sound from a business's music may reach no more than 42 decibels inside a neighboring home, and overnight — 10 p.m. to 7 a.m. — no more than 7 decibels over the background level 15 feet out on the sidewalk, with bass judged by its own test. On Main Street and Roosevelt Avenue the floors above most shops are homes, which is why a counter bar often goes in with no subwoofer at all.
  • Keep aisles clear for people who cannot see the bar. The 2010 ADA Standards treat anything fixed to a wall at a height from 27 to 80 inches as a protruding object if it reaches more than 4 inches into a route people walk along. In a narrow Flushing shop that usually means the TV and bar go higher than 80 inches, or sit over a counter rather than over the aisle.
  • Mall and condominium rules come first. Units in the 39th Avenue developments and the indoor malls answer to a management office or a commercial condo board that may require a certificate of insurance, approved hours and sometimes a sketch of the work. We supply the certificate free and draft the description.
  • Electrical work is separate. If a new outlet is needed behind a counter TV, that is for a licensed electrician. We plan the bar so it uses an existing receptacle wherever possible.
Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Flushing: Questions Answered

These are the questions Flushing households and business owners put to us that are not already answered on our other soundbar pages. They are grouped the way the decisions arrive: the people, the sources, the building, then the shop.

Families who share one screen

My parents speak Mandarin and very little English — can they be the ones at home while the installer works?

Yes, and plenty of Flushing installations happen that way. The useful thing is to settle every decision before the day, so nothing has to be negotiated across a language gap at the wall. Send us the photo, the models and where you want the bar; we send back a written plan and price, and you agree it on your parents' behalf. On the day the technician can call or video-call you from the living room before drilling, so you see the marks on the wall and approve the height yourself.

We cannot promise a technician who speaks Mandarin, Cantonese or Korean on a given day, and we would rather say so than leave your parents guessing. What we do instead is leave the result easy to use without explanation: one remote, the inputs named in plain words, the speech and night settings saved, and photos of the finished cabling sent to you so you can talk your parents through anything later. If you would like to be there for the last ten minutes, tell us your arrival time and we will save the handover for you.

Our living room fills with relatives at Lunar New Year and other holidays — how should the soundbar be set for a crowded room?

A room full of people swallows sound. Bodies and heavy coats absorb high frequencies, the conversation raises the background level, and the people standing at the back are twice as far from the bar as the sofa is. The temptation is to turn the volume and the bass up together, which makes the room boomy and the dialogue no clearer — and the family downstairs hears all of it.

What helps is the bar's dialogue or voice mode, which lifts the center channel without lifting everything else, plus a modest rise in overall volume and the subwoofer left where it is. If the bar has an automatic volume leveller, switch it off for the evening so it doesn't keep pulling the level back down as the room gets louder. We can save a “gathering” setting on bars that support presets, so on the night someone only has to press one button, and press it again when the guests have gone.

Will family karaoke nights work through the soundbar, or do we need the karaoke machine's own speakers?

The music side usually works well: a karaoke player or app on the TV sends the backing track and lyrics over HDMI, and the bar plays the track like any other program. The difficulty is the microphones. Most soundbars have no microphone input, and if the karaoke system mixes the voices and sends them through the TV to the bar, the singer hears their own voice slightly late, because the TV and the bar each add processing delay. Many people find that delay hard to sing over.

The arrangements that work best keep the voices away from the TV's audio chain: a karaoke machine with its own powered speaker for the microphones, with the bar playing only the backing track, or a bar with a low-latency game or direct mode switched on for the evening. Tell us which system you own and we will connect it the way its maker intends and show you how to switch between karaoke and normal viewing without unplugging anything.

Overseas boxes and imported equipment

Most of what we watch comes from an overseas IPTV box that only sends stereo — will the soundbar's surround and height speakers do anything?

Many of the set-top boxes sold for Chinese, Korean and other overseas channels output two-channel stereo, and most live news and drama arrives that way whatever the box. A soundbar with extra channels plays stereo through its left and right drivers unless an upmixing mode is turned on — names vary by maker, but the feature spreads a stereo signal across the center, side and height drivers.

For news and talk shows, the part of upmixing that matters most is the one that pulls voices into the center channel, which a good bar does well even from stereo. For music programs some listeners prefer plain stereo. We set the box's audio output to the format the bar handles best, then try a news program and a drama with you and save whichever mode sounds clearest. If the box offers a passthrough or bitstream setting, we test that too, because a few boxes send surround on certain channels and stereo on others.

New towers

A ductless mini-split head sits high on our TV wall and its lines run inside the wall — where is it safe to drill for the soundbar?

Mini-split indoor units turn up in many newer apartments and conversions, and their refrigerant lines, condensate drain and control cable usually leave the back of the unit and run down or sideways inside the wall to an exterior penetration. A bit through a refrigerant line is an expensive repair, so the area below and to the side of the unit is treated as occupied until proven otherwise.

We look first at where the line set exits outside or into a soffit, which shows the direction it travels, then scan the wall and keep the bracket's anchors clear of that path. The bar's position also matters for comfort: an indoor unit blowing air straight down across the TV and bar carries dust into the grille, so we keep the bar out of the direct airstream where possible. If the only sensible spot is in the line set's path, the bar goes on the TV mount or the console instead, and nothing is drilled near the lines.

Restaurants, malls and banquet halls

Our Korean barbecue restaurant on Union Street has grills at every table — will smoke and grease ruin a soundbar on the wall?

Over time, yes, if nothing is done about it. Cooking smoke carries a fine film of grease that settles on everything in the room and gets pulled into any electronics that draw air through them. A soundbar's fabric or perforated grille collects it, the drivers behind it slowly lose clarity, and buttons on top become sticky. Bars with built-in fans or warm amplifiers draw in more.

The practical plan is to mount the TV and bar on the wall farthest from the grills and outside the path the exhaust hoods pull the smoke along, choose a bar with a metal grille that can be wiped, and keep the remote in a sealed bag or a wall holder at the counter. Clean the grille on the same schedule as the dining-room surfaces, with the power off and a barely damp cloth. If the room's layout leaves no wall away from the smoke, a pair of sealed commercial speakers is the more durable choice, and we will tell you so before the bar is bought.

Our counter is a stall in an indoor food court — who has to approve a soundbar on the wall behind it?

Usually the mall's management office, not just your landlord in the everyday sense. In a food court or an indoor mall the wall behind your counter is often a partition the center owns, finished with tile or panels it maintains, and the lease normally says any fixing into it needs written approval. Some centers also set the hours when drilling is allowed and require a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing company.

Ask the management office for its contractor requirements first. We supply the certificate free, and we can write the short description of the work most offices want: the bar's size and weight, the bracket, the wall type and the anchors, and confirmation that nothing electrical is being changed. If approval for drilling is slow, the bar can go on the TV mount or a counter shelf in the meantime, and moving it to the wall later is priced as an ordinary wall mount.

Our banquet hall already has a DJ and microphone system — can the TV's soundbar feed into it for slideshows and videos?

A soundbar is built to receive sound, not pass it on, so it is the wrong end of the chain to connect to a hall's mixing desk. What works is to take the audio from the source before it reaches the bar — the TV's optical or headphone output, an HDMI audio extractor, or the laptop playing the slideshow — and run that into a spare input on the hall's mixer. The bar then serves the TV area during ordinary service and the hall system takes over for speeches and videos.

The two shouldn't play the same video at the same time from different sides of the room, because the slight delay between them makes speech echo. We label the switch-over clearly and, if the hall's sound company maintains the mixer, we agree the connection with them before the day so their settings stay untouched.

FAQ

Flushing Soundbar Installation FAQ

What do the numbers like 3.1 or 5.1.2 on a soundbar box mean?
+

The first number counts the main channels across the front and sides, the second is the subwoofer — 1 if there is one, 0 if not — and a third, where shown, counts height channels that fire upward or are simulated. So 3.1 means left, center and right with a sub, and 5.1.2 adds two surround and two height channels. For dialogue, the center channel matters most; height channels need a suitable ceiling.

Can the same soundbar play sound from a desktop computer as well as the TV?
+

Often, if the bar has a second input. Some bars accept a computer over USB, optical or a spare HDMI input, others only through the TV or Bluetooth. Switching between the TV and the computer then means changing the bar's input, which is fine for one person but confusing in a shared living room. Send us the bar's model and the computer's outputs and we will tell you before the visit whether a cable, an adapter or simply Bluetooth is the right answer.

If the soundbar freezes or stops responding to the remote, what should we try before calling?
+

Unplug the bar from the wall for a full minute, plug it back in and wait for it to finish starting before pressing anything. If the TV remote still won't control it, switch the TV off and on as well, because the HDMI link is renegotiated at start-up. Check the remote's batteries and that nothing blocks the bar's sensor. Avoid a factory reset unless the maker's support page tells you to, because it erases pairing and settings. If none of that helps, call us.

Our TV's menu never mentions HDMI-CEC — does the TV lack it?
+

Probably not; most makers rename it. Samsung calls it Anynet+, LG calls it SimpLink, Sony uses BRAVIA Sync and Panasonic VIERA Link, and other brands use names of their own or file it under external-device settings. It has to be on in the TV's menu and in the soundbar, and the bar has to be on the TV's ARC or eARC port. We switch it on during setup and note where the setting lives in case an update turns it off.

Can the soundbar's built-in voice assistant understand Mandarin, Cantonese or Korean?
+

That depends on the assistant and the region the bar is set to, and the list of supported languages changes over time, so check the assistant maker's current language list before relying on it. Where a household's main language isn't supported, we usually leave the assistant's microphone off and set the bar up to be run from the TV remote, which works the same in every language. Subtitles and audio-track choices are made on the TV or the box, not on the bar.

We bought our soundbar in China or Korea — will it work in a New York apartment?
+

Check the label by the power socket or on the power adapter first. Homes in the United States run at 120 volts and 60 hertz, while mainland China uses 220 volts at 50 hertz and South Korea 220 volts at 60 hertz. Many adapters are marked 100–240 volts and need only a plug that fits American outlets; one marked for 220 volts only should not be used here without a proper converter. Apps, streaming services, voice assistants and warranty cover can also be tied to the country of sale, so we check those before mounting.

Our family uses both iPhones and Android phones — does that change which soundbar to buy?
+

It can. Bars with Apple AirPlay make it easy to send music and video sound from iPhones and iPads; bars with Chromecast built-in do the same for many Android phones and apps; some support both, and most also offer Bluetooth, which works with any phone at lower quality. In a household where everyone plays music from their own phone, choose a bar that supports what most of the family uses, and we put each person's phone on the same Wi-Fi network as the bar.
