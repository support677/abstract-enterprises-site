Walk-ups over storefronts

Three- to five-story brick buildings with a shop or bar at street level and apartments above, common on Bedford, Grand, Metropolitan and the numbered streets. Two systems often share one facade: the shop's cameras and the building's entrance cameras.

Converted factories and warehouses

Brick-and-timber or concrete industrial buildings turned into lofts, offices and studios, many near Kent, Wythe and Berry. Long corridors, freight doors, high ceilings and cable runs that have been extended by several owners.

Waterfront towers

The glass and concrete buildings that rose along Kent Avenue after the 2005 rezoning, and the newer Domino blocks. Large systems with dozens of cameras, garages, amenity floors and recorders in locked rooms run by a managing agent.

Mid-size new rentals and condos

Six- to twelve-story infill buildings on side streets, often with a video intercom at the door, a few common-area cameras and a small recorder in a closet that nobody has opened since the building was turned over.

Row houses and small houses

Brick and frame row houses and two-families, mostly on quieter blocks. One owner, a handful of cameras over the door, the yard and the side passage, and a recorder in the cellar or a closet.

Landmarked and older commercial buildings

Walk-ups over storefronts: two systems sharing one doorway

The classic Williamsburg mixed-use building has a business at street level and three or four floors of apartments above, reached through a narrow street door beside the shopfront. Very often two separate camera systems are fixed to the same facade. The shop owns the cameras over its own door and inside its space, recorded on a box under the counter or in the back. The landlord owns the camera over the residents' door and maybe one in the stair, recorded somewhere else, sometimes in the cellar the shop also uses. When something goes wrong, the first job is working out which camera belongs to which system and who can authorize a repair, because a tenant of the shop cannot approve work on the landlord's recorder and the landlord cannot sign for the shop's.

The failures in these buildings follow their use. Shop cameras near a roll-down gate get knocked when the gate is raised and lowered, and their cables often run through the gate box, where they chafe. Cameras over the residents' door are frequently mounted on the brick beside the buzzer panel and fed through the same hole, so a buzzer or intercom repair can disturb the camera cable without anyone noticing until the picture fails. In the stair, cameras are usually on the ceiling at the top of the first flight, which is where a ceiling camera is least useful against someone with a hood up. On a repair we test each camera's cable separately, label which recorder it belongs to on both ends, and where a stair camera only sees hats, we suggest a lower angle on the landing rather than a new system.

Converted factories and warehouses: long runs, extended many times

Williamsburg's industrial past survives in its loft buildings: brick-and-timber factories and poured-concrete warehouses whose floors now hold apartments, offices, studios and event spaces. Their camera systems tend to have grown in stages. A first owner put cameras at the main door and the freight entrance; a later one added corridor cameras on each floor; tenants added their own inside their units; and somebody extended a run with a small joiner box or a cheap switch above a drop ceiling to reach the new camera at the far end of a long hall. When one of these buildings calls about cameras that drop out at random, the cause is often one of those mid-run additions, a connector that has been pulled one time too many, or a switch in a ceiling space that cooks every summer.

The repair method is to map the system before replacing anything. We trace each camera back to its recorder, find every joint, box and switch along the way, and test the run in sections so that a fault in a ceiling halfway down the hall is found where it is, not guessed at. Concrete floors and slabs mean that most runs cannot simply be rerouted through a new hole, so we reuse the pathways that exist and replace damaged lengths inside them. Where corridors are long, a camera at each end often shows faces at the far end too small to recognize; the fix is usually a narrower lens on one camera and a second camera halfway along, not a new recorder. Freight entrances and loading doors get the most attention, because the 2025 Stagg Street burglary that News 12 reported began at a rear door of a commercial building, and that is the pattern we see repeated in these buildings.

Waterfront towers: big systems, nobody watching all of it

The towers along Kent Avenue and on the Domino site carry the largest camera systems in the neighborhood: lobby and package-room cameras, elevator cabs, stairs, garage ramps and parking levels, amenity floors, roof decks and the river esplanade side. The system is usually managed by a property manager or managing agent, with a front desk or doorman who can see the live cameras but may not know how to save a clip, and a recorder that sits in a locked room on the cellar level. The common failure is not dramatic. It is a single camera that went dark months ago, a recorder that has been warning about a failing drive with nobody reading the warnings, or an alert email still going to someone from the original developer's team.

On a tower repair we start with a written list: every camera by name and location, what it shows now, which are dark, which are recording at reduced quality and how many days the recorder actually keeps. That list is what a board or manager needs before deciding what to fix first. We then repair in order of risk, which in these buildings usually means the package room and the lobby doors first, because those are the places the CBS New York and Gothamist reports describe thieves walking into, then garage entrances, then stairs and amenity spaces. Health alerts for camera loss and drive errors are sent to the people who are actually on duty, and the desk staff are shown how to mark and export a clip so that a resident's request does not wait until the manager is in.

Mid-size new rentals and condos: the closet recorder nobody opened

Between the walk-ups and the towers sit the six- to twelve-story buildings that filled Williamsburg's side streets over the last two decades. They usually came with a modest system: a video intercom at the street door, cameras in the lobby, the mail area, the elevator and perhaps the rear yard or bike room, and a recorder in a closet near the electrical room. The developer's installer handed over a login, often to a sales office that no longer exists. Years later the building has a new managing agent, the internet provider has replaced the router twice, and the phone app shows nothing.

Most of these repairs are a recovery job rather than a hardware job. We regain administrator access to the recorder through the manufacturer's reset process with the owner's or board's written authorization, reconnect it to the building's current network, update its firmware where that fixes a known fault, set a correct time source so clips carry the right time, and give the building its own logins. Then we look at the hardware: drives that have been running for years, a small switch on top of the recorder that runs hot in an unventilated closet, and the intercom camera, whose picture may never have been recorded because it was wired only to the apartments' monitors.

Row houses and small houses: a cellar recorder and a side passage

Williamsburg still has brick and frame row houses and small two-families on its quieter blocks. Their systems are small: a camera over the front door, one at the yard, sometimes one in the side passage or alley, and a recorder in the cellar or a hall closet. The repairs we see here are about water, wiring and light. Water gets into connectors where a cable leaves the facade without a drip loop. Cables run through old window frames and soffits that have been replaced by contractors who did not know they were there. And night pictures depend on whatever light the block provides, which changes when a neighbor's floodlight goes up or a street lamp goes out.

Because there is usually only one owner, the approvals are simple and the visit is short. We replace the failed part, seal the entry, and check the night picture in the dark rather than guessing. On houses where packages are the main worry, we check whether the door camera actually shows the step where parcels are left, not just the face of whoever rings the bell; and we make sure the owner knows how to export a clip from the app or the recorder.

Landmarked and older commercial buildings: back in the same joint

Only designated landmarks fall under the Landmarks Preservation Commission's camera rules, and in Williamsburg those are individual buildings rather than whole blocks: the Domino refinery and the former Hecla Iron Works building on Wythe Avenue are two examples. The Commission's permit guidebook says cameras on these buildings "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," that equipment must be attached "at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and that it must not be attached "through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." It also says no permit is needed for "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment."

For a repair, that means we put the replacement camera back where the approved one was, into the same anchors or the same mortar joints, at the same size and color, and we do not add new holes in carved stone, terracotta or iron. If the owner wants to move a camera to a new spot or add one, that is a change rather than a repair, and we say so before work starts. Older commercial buildings that are not designated are not bound by those rules, but we treat their decorative facades the same way, because a hole in carved stone is permanent.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Williamsburg Shop the Way a Burglar Would

This is how our technicians handle the most common Williamsburg commercial call, written as our working method rather than a story about any one business. The owner says the cameras "didn't get anything" during a break-in. Before touching the recorder, the technician copies whatever was saved for that night, then goes outside and walks the building the way the 2026 reports describe people getting in: the front door, the glass beside it, the side or rear door, the cellar entrance from the sidewalk or the yard, and any window or skylight that can be reached. At each one the question is the same: which camera sees this opening, from how far away, at what height, and with what light at four in the morning?

That walk usually explains the night better than the recorder does. A typical result is a front camera that is fine, a back-door camera mounted so high that it records only the top of a hood, a cellar door no camera covers at all, and a recording schedule that saves only motion clips with no lead-in, so every clip starts after the door is already open. The repair that follows is often modest: lower one camera or add a second at chest height facing the door, re-aim another to take in the cellar stair, turn on the recorder's pre-event buffer so clips begin a few seconds before motion, and raise the saved quality on the door cameras. The owner gets a list of what each camera now covers and a price for each change before any of it is done.

How Systems Fail Here

Hooded Faces, Late Clips, Voltage Dips, Silent Drives and Unwatched Lobbies: How Williamsburg Camera Systems Go Wrong

Hooded faces: the camera is too high to matter

Installers like to mount cameras high, because a high camera is hard to reach and sees a wide area. Against someone who knows the camera is there and keeps a hood up and head down, a high camera is close to useless for identification. That is not a new observation in Williamsburg. When the 90th Precinct recorded nearly 170 burglaries in the first half of 2015, up 31 percent, officers told local businesses, as DNAinfo reported, that ceiling cameras are easily beaten by a hooded sweatshirt and that a covert camera mounted low and angled upward does better. In September 2026 Hoodline reported people breaking into Brooklyn businesses by throwing an object through a window, which is fast and leaves little time for anything but a quick look at whoever is coming through.

The repair is rarely to replace the high camera. It still shows how many people came in, how they moved and what they took, which matters to a detective and an insurer. What it lacks is a second view at face height. On a shop door that usually means a small camera at about chest height inside the door frame or beside the register, looking toward the entrance, with a lens narrow enough that a face fills a useful share of the picture. The recorder is then set to save that camera at its highest quality around the clock, because a few seconds of a clear face is worth more than hours of wide views.

Two more things help. First, light: the same DNAinfo report quotes the advice to leave lights on, because infrared night vision distorts faces. A low, steady light inside the door gives the camera color and detail that infrared cannot. Second, honesty with yourself about where the camera looks. Many Williamsburg shop owners find, when they look at the face-height view for the first time, that their old system never showed a single face clearly, even of their own staff.

Late clips: the recording begins after the door is open

Many recorders are set to save video only when they detect motion, to stretch the drive. That is reasonable for a quiet yard. On a door that is forced at speed it fails in a predictable way: the camera notices motion, the recorder starts writing, and by the time the clip begins the door is already open and the person is halfway inside. The first two or three seconds, which are the seconds when the person is closest to the camera and facing it, are simply not there.

Most modern recorders have a pre-event setting that keeps a few seconds of video in memory and attaches it to the start of every clip. It is often turned off, or set to zero, because the default was never changed. We turn it on for every entrance camera, and on the door cameras of a business that has been hit we usually recommend continuous recording at full quality instead, with motion recording reserved for less important views. The drive is sized for that, so the change does not quietly cut the number of days the recorder keeps.

We also check the detection zones. On busy Williamsburg blocks a camera that faces the sidewalk sees motion all night from people walking home from the bars and the Bedford Avenue station, so somebody often shrinks the motion zone to stop the constant alerts. If the zone was shrunk too far, the camera ignores the doorway it was meant to watch. A good zone covers the door and its approach, not the whole street.

Voltage dips: the lights stay on, the system does not

A blackout is obvious. A voltage reduction is not. On July 3, 2026, Con Edison lowered the voltage by 8 percent across part of northern Brooklyn that included Williamsburg, "to protect equipment and maintain service during repairs." In June 2025 News 12 described the same kind of event in Brooklyn: "the power isn't fully out—but it's running lower than usual." Recorders, PoE switches and the small multi-channel power supplies that feed older coax cameras are built to run on steady power. When the supply sags, some keep going, some restart again and again, and some shut down a few camera ports to stay within their power budget.

After an event like that, the symptoms can be confusing. The live picture may come back while the recorder has stopped writing to the drive. A switch may power the cameras nearest it and not the far ones. The recorder's clock may reset, so clips are stamped with the wrong date. A technical guide from Security Wholesalers lists the usual suspects after an outage: equipment that restarted "in the wrong order," a hard drive that "reports an abnormal state," and a clock that was lost, and it warns that "repeated power cycling can worsen storage and power faults." We check each of those in turn rather than restarting everything and hoping.

The lasting fix is a battery backup sized for the recorder, the switch and the camera supplies together, with its own battery actually tested, since many backups we find in Williamsburg cellars and closets have batteries that died years ago. A backup that conditions the power also rides through a dip without the equipment noticing. We size it so the system can run through a short outage and shut down cleanly in a long one.

Silent drives: everything looks fine until you press playback

The hard drive in a camera recorder writes every minute of every day. Desktop drives are not designed for that, and CCTV Camera World's guide makes the point directly that desktop drives aren't built for 24/7 recording. Even surveillance drives wear out. The danger is that the live view keeps working when the drive does not, so the system looks healthy on the monitor while it has stopped saving. Swann's support pages list the usual causes of a recorder that does not record: recording turned off, wrong motion settings, no storage installed, or a faulty drive.

The simplest test is the one nobody does: pick a random time from yesterday and play it back on each camera. CCTV Camera World recommends the same, along with confirming that the recorder sees the drive with a normal status. We do that on every visit, and when a drive is failing we replace it with a surveillance-rated drive sized to keep the number of days the owner needs. One warning from the same guide is worth repeating: formatting a drive wipes all recordings, so a drive is never formatted until any footage that matters has been copied off.

Unwatched lobbies: a camera nobody has checked since it went up

In residential buildings the most common camera failure is simply that nobody knows the camera stopped. A Reddit user in a package-theft roundup described a building whose lobby cameras "may not even be operational." The lobby camera may have been knocked out of line by a delivery cart, the mail-area camera may have lost its connection when a contractor worked in the ceiling, or the recorder may have dropped one channel after a power event. Nobody looks at the recorder until a tenant asks for a clip, and then the answer is that there is nothing.

The fix is a routine more than a repair. Each camera is named on the recorder by what it shows, so that "Cam 7" becomes "Mail area" and a dark tile means something to whoever looks at it. Camera-loss and drive-error alerts go by email or app notification to the people who are actually responsible now, not the developer's sales office. And somebody, a super, a manager or a board member, plays back one clip from each camera once a month. We set that up on every residential repair and leave a one-page list on the recorder closet door.

Break-ins while the business is closed

The summer burglar in North Brooklyn got into businesses mostly through basement-level doors. Our cameras watch the shop floor and the front, but the sidewalk stair down to our cellar door is out of frame. Can an existing camera take it in, or does the stair need its own?

It depends on where the stair sits relative to your front camera. If the cellar stair opens right beside the shop entrance, the front camera can sometimes be re-aimed or fitted with a wider lens to take in the top of the stair as well as the door, though that usually costs some detail at the door. In most Williamsburg storefronts the cellar entrance is either in a sidewalk hatch, under the stoop of the residents' door, or at the back of the building, and no front camera can see down into it. A small camera mounted on the wall above the stair, looking down at the cellar door, is normally the right answer.

On a repair visit we check whether your recorder has a free channel and whether there is a usable path for a cable into the stair. If both are there, adding the camera is a small job priced on its own line. We also check that the cellar camera is set to record continuously, not only on motion, because a basement-level door is exactly where someone can work for several minutes out of sight of the street.

Officers told Williamsburg businesses years ago to leave lights on overnight because infrared night vision distorts faces. Is that still good advice for the cameras we have, and which lights should stay on?

Yes, and it is one of the cheapest improvements a Williamsburg shop can make. When a camera has no light to work with, it switches to infrared, which turns the picture black and white and tends to flatten or wash out faces close to the lens, especially on light skin, glasses or anything reflective. A low, steady light inside the shop near the entrance and the register lets the camera stay in color and keeps detail in faces and clothing, which is what a detective needs.

The lights that help most are the ones that light the faces of people coming toward the camera: a fixture inside the door, one over the counter, and one at the back door if there is a camera there. Lights behind the person, such as a bright sign in the window behind a door camera, make things worse by turning them into a silhouette. On a repair we look at the camera's night picture with your normal overnight lighting, and if a single light left on would fix it, we tell you that rather than selling you a new camera.

After a break-in at our Williamsburg store, the clip on the phone app is too blurry to show a face, but the detective says the person looked straight at the camera. Is there a sharper copy somewhere on the recorder?

Very possibly. Most cameras send two video streams: a full-quality main stream and a small, low-resolution second stream used for phone viewing and multi-camera screens. Phone apps usually show the small stream, especially on a weak connection, so a face that looks like a smudge on your phone can be perfectly clear in the recording itself. The question is which stream the recorder was saving. If it was set to record the main stream, the sharp version is on the drive.

Do not try to export from the phone. Leave the recorder running and let us, or the detective, export the clip directly from the recorder at full resolution. If it turns out the recorder was saving only the small stream, which we do find on systems set up to stretch a small drive, the sharp version does not exist, and the repair is to change the recording settings and size the drive so the main stream is kept from now on. Either way, we can tell you within a few minutes of looking at the recorder's settings.

Our bar's cameras share the router with the point-of-sale terminals and the Wi-Fi our customers use, and on busy nights the cameras freeze on our phones. Is something wrong with the cameras?

Usually not. When cameras, payment terminals and guest Wi-Fi all share one small router, the cameras compete for bandwidth with everyone in the room, and the remote view from the phone is the first thing to stutter. The recording itself is often fine, because the cameras talk to the recorder over the local network, but if the cameras are wireless or the switch is overloaded, gaps can appear in the saved video too.

The repair is to separate the cameras from everything else. On most bar and restaurant systems that means wiring the cameras into their own PoE switch connected to the recorder, putting the recorder on a separate network segment from the guest Wi-Fi, and limiting how much upload the remote viewing uses. Your payment system and your internet provider's equipment stay as they are. We check the saved recordings from a busy night first to see whether anything was actually lost, and then fix the network so that the phone view keeps working when the room is full.

Lobbies, mail areas and who owns the footage

The person who took packages from our Bedford Avenue building came in through the main entrance and went straight to the mail area, the way the 2023 reports describe. Our lobby camera covers the street door. Is one camera enough, or does the mail area need its own?

One camera is rarely enough for both jobs. A camera that watches the street door is placed to catch faces as people come in, and from that position it usually sees the mail area at an angle and at a distance, too far to show what someone takes or to show their face as they turn away. A second camera inside the mail area, aimed at the shelves or lockers and the doorway, closes that gap, and it is the camera that answers the question tenants actually ask: who took my package, and when.

If your building already has a camera in the mail area that is not working, that is a repair. If there is none, adding one on the existing recorder is usually a modest job when there is a free channel and a reachable cable path. Either way, we set both cameras to record continuously at full quality, label them clearly on the recorder, and make sure whoever handles tenant requests can find and export a clip by date and time.

Brick Underground put a similar question from a Williamsburg renter to two lawyers. There is at least one state ruling that says yes in the right circumstances. Our waterfront building's recorder is in a locked room on the cellar level, and only the managing agent has the code. When a resident asks about something that happened, the desk can see the live cameras but cannot pull a clip. Can the desk be given the ability to save a clip without full control?

Yes, and in a building with a front desk it is usually the best setup. Most recorders let an administrator create user accounts with limited rights: live viewing only, live viewing and playback, or playback and export without the ability to change settings, delete video or add users. The desk gets a login that lets it find a time, mark a clip and export it to a USB drive or a shared folder, while the managing agent keeps the administrator account.

We set that up as part of a repair or on its own visit, with the managing agent's authorization. Power, recorders and the July voltage cut

After the February manhole fire on Montrose Avenue knocked our block out, our cameras came back but three of them now restart every few minutes. The rest are fine. What would cause that?

Three cameras restarting while the rest are fine usually points to something they share. Often it is a single power supply or a group of ports on a PoE switch that was damaged when the power went out or came back, and now cannot deliver steady power to the cameras it feeds. Another common cause is a supply that has been working near its limit for years and failed under the stress of the outage. Less often, the three cameras share a cable route, and something along it, a connector in a damp cellar or a junction above a ceiling, was affected by the same water or salt that caused the manhole problems.

We test the power at each of the three cameras while they are running, then at the supply or switch ports that feed them, and compare with a camera that is working. That usually finds the fault in a few minutes. The repair is normally a supply or a switch, not new cameras. We then add or test the battery backup so the next event does not do the same damage.

Our recorder in a Grand Street shop beeps every few minutes and shows a "disk error" message, but the cameras still appear on the screen. Are the recordings safe, and should we pull the drive out?

Do not pull the drive. A disk error usually means the recorder is having trouble writing to or reading from the drive, which can mean the drive is failing, has come loose inside the case, or is full and not set to overwrite. The live picture on the screen does not tell you anything about whether video is being saved, because the live view goes straight from the cameras to the screen. Recordings from before the error may well be fine, and the safest way to keep them is to leave the recorder running until they are copied.

On the visit we check the drive's status in the recorder, copy any footage you need, then test the drive's health. If it is failing, we replace it with a surveillance-rated drive and set the recorder to overwrite the oldest video when full. If the drive is healthy and the cause was a loose connection or a setting, we fix that instead. In either case we play back video from every camera before we leave to confirm that saving has resumed.

Lofts, venues and landmarked buildings

Our loft building's freight entrance camera was knocked crooked by a moving truck and now points at the sidewalk. Can it be put back where it was, and can it be made harder to knock out again?

Usually yes on both counts. If the camera itself still works, the repair is to remount it at its original angle and check that the mount, the anchors in the brick or concrete and the cable entry were not damaged. Moving trucks and box trucks backing into Williamsburg loading doors tend to catch cameras mounted low on the frame or on a short arm that sticks out into the truck's path.

To make it harder to hit, we usually move the camera up and slightly in, under the lintel or onto the wall beside the opening, and use a heavier mount or a vandal-resistant housing. The view still needs to show faces at the door, so moving it too high defeats the purpose; we check the picture with someone standing in the doorway before we finish. If the camera itself was damaged, replacing it is priced on its own line.

Our venue on North 6th Street uses its cameras to check the queue and the door during shows, but the recorder holds only three days, and incidents are often reported a week later. How do we keep more without losing quality where it matters?

Start by deciding which cameras need the most days. For a venue, the entrance, the box office, the bar tills and the stage door usually matter most, and the queue camera and interior wide shots matter less after a few days. Most recorders let each camera have its own quality and recording schedule, so the important cameras can be saved at full quality all the time while the others record at a lower bitrate or only on motion outside show hours.

Then size the drive. A larger surveillance-rated drive, or a second drive in a recorder that has room for one, often doubles or triples how many days are kept for a modest cost. We calculate the actual space each camera uses from the recorder's own figures, set the schedules, fit the right drive and confirm how many days the system will now keep. Two weeks at full quality on the cameras that matter is a realistic target for most Williamsburg venues.

Our office suite is inside a converted industrial building that is a designated landmark. A camera on the facade over our entrance has died. Do we need the Landmarks Preservation Commission's permission to replace it?

Usually not, if it is a like-for-like replacement. The Commission's permit guidebook says no permit is required for "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment." A dead camera replaced with a similar one in the same spot, using the existing anchors or the same mortar joints, generally falls within that. The building's owner or management may still have its own approval rules for work on the facade, and in a converted building with many tenants the facade almost always belongs to the owner, not to you.

If you want the new camera somewhere different, bigger, a different color, or want to add one, that is a change rather than a repair, and the guidebook's rules apply: cameras well spaced, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, and not through cast iron or decorative surfaces. What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Williamsburg

Search summaries, chat assistants and home-service directories such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give general camera advice. Much of it is sensible anywhere. Some of it misses how cameras fail in Williamsburg's walk-ups, lofts, towers and late-night businesses. These are claims we hear repeated, and what we find on Williamsburg repairs.

"Go wireless and most camera problems disappear"

Wireless cameras skip the cable, and a cable is one less thing to fail. That part is true. What the advice leaves out is that the radio link becomes the weak point, and Reolink lists "Weak WiFi Connection or Low Bandwidth" among the main causes of video loss. In a Williamsburg warehouse conversion with concrete floors, a brick walk-up with a shop's router on the ground floor, or a tower where every apartment runs its own network on the same channels, wireless cameras drop out, record late and miss clips.

The cameras most likely to be wireless are often the ones that matter most: a back door, a cellar stair or a yard, added later because no one wanted to run a cable there. Those are exactly the openings recent burglary reports describe being used. A camera that drops its link for a few seconds every hour can miss the one minute that counts.

Our approach is practical. If a wireless camera works reliably where it is, we leave it. If it does not, the lasting repair is to run a network cable to it, which also powers it, rather than buying a stronger router or a range extender that adds another device to fail in the next power dip.

"Buy a higher-resolution camera and you will see faces"

Resolution helps, but it does not solve the problem that defeats most Williamsburg shop cameras: angle. A 4K camera on the ceiling still sees the top of a hood. The 90th Precinct's advice, reported by DNAinfo in 2015, was about placement, not pixels: ceiling cameras are easily beaten by a hooded sweatshirt, and a camera mounted low and angled upward does better.

Resolution can also work against you. Higher-resolution cameras produce bigger files, and if the recorder's drive was sized for older cameras, the system either keeps fewer days or someone turns the quality down to compensate, which throws away the detail that was the point of the upgrade. We see both on Williamsburg systems where cameras were upgraded one at a time.

What actually produces usable faces is a camera at roughly face height looking at the door, a lens that makes a face fill a reasonable share of the picture, steady light, and a recorder saving the main stream at full quality. Often that is a re-aimed or added camera on the existing system, not a new set.

"Set the cameras to record on motion to save space"

Motion recording does save space, and on a quiet yard camera it is fine. On a busy Williamsburg block it causes two opposite problems. A camera facing Bedford Avenue or North 6th Street sees motion all night, so it records constantly anyway and floods phones with alerts until someone turns the alerts off or shrinks the motion zone. Shrink the zone too far and the camera stops watching the door it was meant to cover.

On the cameras that face entrances, motion recording without a pre-event buffer also means every clip starts late. By the time the motion triggers recording, the door is already open and the person is past the camera. For a business that has been hit, those first seconds are the only seconds with a face in them.

The better approach is to record entrance and register cameras continuously at full quality, use motion recording with a buffer for everything else, and size the drive so the recorder keeps the number of days the business actually needs. The space saved by motion recording is rarely worth the footage it loses on the cameras that matter.

"Plug the system into a surge protector and power problems are handled"

A surge protector guards against sudden spikes. It does nothing when the voltage sags, and in Williamsburg sags are part of the local power picture. On July 3, 2026, Con Edison lowered the voltage by 8 percent across part of northern Brooklyn that included Williamsburg. Low voltage is hard on recorders, PoE switches and the power supplies that feed coax cameras. Some restart repeatedly, some drop camera ports to stay within their power budget, and some keep showing live video while they stop saving. A surge strip under the counter will not prevent any of that.

The protection that works is a battery backup that regulates the power, sized for the recorder, the switch and the camera supplies together, with a tested battery. In a long outage it lets the system shut down cleanly, and when power returns the equipment can come back in the right order. We check the backup's battery on every outage repair, because a backup with a dead battery is just an expensive power strip.
