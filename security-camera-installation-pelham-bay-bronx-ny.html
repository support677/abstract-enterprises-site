The Neighborhood

Why Pelham Bay Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Pelham Bay is a neighborhood of houses with cars parked beside them, and that single fact shapes most camera jobs here. When City Planning studied the area before its 2006 rezoning, it found that detached houses made up about 72 percent of the housing stock and semi-detached houses about 17 percent, with attached houses and multifamily buildings making up the small remainder, mostly along the wider avenues. The rezoning, adopted by the City Council on March 22, 2006, mapped R5A across the inner blocks, which allows only detached one- and two-family houses on lots at least 30 feet wide, and cut back how far commercial uses can reach off Westchester, Crosby and Buhre avenues. The result is a street pattern of driveways, side passages, front yards and rear garages, the kind of layout where a camera has real ground to cover. Wikipedia draws the neighborhood between Pelham Parkway, the New England Thruway, the Bruckner Expressway and the Hutchinson River Parkway, in ZIP code 10461 and Community District 10, patrolled by the 45th Precinct from its station house on Barkley Avenue in Throggs Neck; other sources set the lines a little differently, so we go by your address. The history is long: this was Pell's Point in colonial times, named for the landowner Thomas Pell, the site of a Revolutionary War battle on October 18, 1776, and part of New York City from 1895. The houses came later, after the subway reached Pelham Bay Park on December 20, 1920, and most of them went up between the 1920s and the 1960s.

The reason owners call us now is usually a story from the curb. In May 2026, News 12 reported shattered windows, stolen airbags and thousands of dollars in potential damage after an overnight string of car break-ins near Pilgrim and St. Theresa avenues and Edison Avenue, with Hondas the cars targeted, and neighbors told the station that break-ins had become a recurring problem. The Bronx Times reported that the 45th Precinct recorded 774 grand larcenies and 547 auto thefts in 2025, both up significantly, while burglary and robbery went down. In plain terms, the risk here has shifted from the front door toward the driveway and the street. A doorbell camera aimed at the stoop sees very little of a car parked thirty feet away, and almost nothing of one parked across the street. Planning cameras for Pelham Bay therefore starts with a question many installers never ask: where does the car sleep at night, and what does that spot look like at three in the morning?

The avenues carry their own record. News 12 reported a robbery at the A&A Deli on Westchester Avenue in 2025, in which the robbers left with $1,100 in cash, and the station's story ran on the store's own camera video. Police released surveillance video of two men in an armed robbery at a store at Crosby and Buhre avenues. CBS New York aired camera footage of a man throwing a rock through the glass door of the Pelham Bay Discount and Hardware store on Westchester Avenue and breaking in. In May 2026, two men were robbed at gunpoint near Roberts and Jarvis avenues, with about $71,000 in property taken. Just north, News 12 reported a Pelham Gardens resident whose home camera caught a person taking packages off his porch. None of this makes Pelham Bay unusual; Wikipedia still describes it as one of the safest and most desirable neighborhoods in the Bronx, and we do not rank blocks. What these cases show is where a camera plan has to earn its keep: the curb, the driveway, the glass door and the counter, and the few seconds in which each event happened.

Four limits decide where a lens may go. The owner of the wall decides first: on a rented floor of a two-family or in an avenue walk-up, the outside of the building and its shared spaces belong to the landlord, and a tenant needs permission before mounting anything there. A co-op or condo board decides for its grounds, garage and lobby. The law draws the next line: New York Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people where they undress or expect privacy, and Civil Rights Law §52-a gives neighbors a way to challenge a camera aimed into their backyard or home, which matters a great deal on 30-foot lots where the next house is a driveway's width away. Wiring rules come third, covered in detail further down. And the street itself is the fourth: nothing goes on a lamppost, a street tree or a utility pole, so every curb view starts from your own house.

Where the Car Sleeps

The Curb at Three in the Morning: Planning Pelham Bay Cameras Around the Parked Car

Most camera plans start at the front door and work outward. In Pelham Bay we start at the car and work back toward the house, because that is where the recent trouble has been and because the car is where cameras most often fail. A car is low, shiny and far from the wall. A camera mounted high under the eaves sees its roof and hood, and anyone leaning into it from the street side is hidden behind the body. Headlights from passing traffic flood the picture. Infrared night light bounces off glass and paint. A thief working on an airbag or a window needs very little time, so a camera that waits for motion, wakes up and then starts recording can miss most of it. And the car is not always where you expect: some nights it is in the driveway, some nights at the curb out front, some nights across the street or two houses down. So on every Pelham Bay walk-through we ask where the car usually spends the night, where it ends up on a bad parking night, and what the light is like in each of those spots after dark. The side driveway: the most common Pelham Bay layout

A detached house on an inner block usually has a driveway running down one side, sometimes to a garage at the back, sometimes ending at a gate or fence. The best place for the driveway camera is almost never the highest point. We mount it on the front corner of the house at a height where it can see faces under hats and hoods, about where a second-floor windowsill or a porch ceiling sits on many of these houses, and aim it back along the driveway so that anyone walking in from the sidewalk walks toward the lens. That angle records the face first and the car second, which is the order you want. Where the driveway is long, a second camera at the back corner or on the garage looks the other way, toward the street, so that a person who slips past the first camera is still recorded face-on as they come up the drive. Both record continuously. We check each one after dark, because a camera that looks perfect at noon can turn into a sheet of white glare when the motion light it sits next to switches on.

The curb out front: closer framing, not wider

When the car spends the night at the curb, the instinct is to buy the widest lens available and point it at the whole street. That picture is impressive and nearly useless, because every person in it is too small to identify. A curb camera in Pelham Bay works better framed tight on your own frontage: the stretch of curb where your car sits, the sidewalk beside it and the front of your walk. On many of these houses the porch roof or the head of a front window puts the camera at a good height and close enough to the curb. We set the picture so the car fills a large part of the frame and a person standing at its door is clearly recognizable, and we keep the houses across the street out of the frame, or masked, so the view stays on what is yours. Where a streetlight sits between the house and the car, we test both infrared and color night modes and pick the one that actually shows the car, not the light.

Inside the car: what a house camera can and cannot see

The thefts News 12 reported in May 2026 were airbag thefts, which happen inside the car rather than beside it. A house camera can record the approach, the moment a window is broken or a door is opened, the person leaning in, how long they stay and which way they leave. It usually cannot show the dashboard through the windshield at night, because glass reflects light back toward the camera. That is still a great deal of information, and in most cases the approach and the departure are what matter most to the owner, the insurer and the precinct. We set expectations plainly on the walk-through: a well-placed camera gives you who, when and which way; it will rarely give you a close-up of hands on a steering wheel.

The garage and the back of the lot

Many Pelham Bay houses keep the car, the bikes, the mower and the tools in a garage at the end of the driveway, and the back of the lot is usually the darkest place on the property. A camera on the garage itself, aimed at its door and the end of the driveway, closes the gap. Its cable usually runs from the house basement out through the foundation and along the fence or under the driveway in conduit, which we plan on the walk-through so the yard is opened once. Where the garage has power, a small switch or a single long cable can feed a second camera inside it, aimed at the door from within. Where it does not, we say so, and any new outlet becomes a licensed electrician's line on the quote.

Detached houses: the basement is the highway

Most Pelham Bay houses have a basement, and on a detached house it usually runs the full depth of the building with an open or partly open ceiling. That makes it the easiest path for camera cable: from the recorder, along the joists to the front wall, up inside a closet or a corner chase to the porch ceiling or the front corner camera, and out through the foundation toward the garage. Where the basement has been finished, we look for a soffit, a utility room or a removable ceiling panel before suggesting any cut. In houses with an attic, cameras at the eaves are often reached from above instead, with the cable dropped down to the basement through an existing chase. On brick, we drill into the mortar joints, not the face of the brick, and seal every entry. On siding, we use a mounting block so the camera sits flat and water runs around it, not behind it. On the stucco and half-timber fronts of some older houses, we keep mounts off the decorative timbers and on plain wall or under the porch roof.

Semi-detached pairs: one wall that belongs to two owners

The shared wall of a semi-detached pair belongs to both houses, so we do not mount on it, drill through it or run cable inside it. Your half usually has an open side with a driveway or a yard, and that is where the side and rear cameras go. The front camera sits on your half of the porch or front wall, framed so that the other half's stoop and front door stay out of the picture or are masked. Neighbors on these pairs often decide to install at the same time; when they do, each house gets its own recorder and its own logins, and the views are planned so that together they cover the shared front walk and both driveways without either house recording the other's windows.

Two- and three-family houses: several households, one exterior

Many Pelham Bay houses hold more than one household: an owner downstairs and a tenant above, three apartments in a detached brick house, or a cellar unit with its own rear entrance. The outside of the house belongs to the owner, who decides where cameras go and who sees what. In practice, we place the recorder in the owner's apartment or a locked part of the basement, cover the front door, the driveway, the rear entrance and the yard, and then give each household a view of only the doors it uses. A tenant on the second floor might see the front door and the walk but not the yard or the owner's side door. A cellar tenant might see the rear entry they use and nothing else. Nothing is ever aimed into a window or a private entry, and the owner gets a written list of who can see which camera.

Elevator co-ops and condos: board policy first, then cameras

The co-op and condo buildings on Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road were built in the 1960s and 1970s with concrete and block, lobbies with dropped or plaster ceilings, and in many cases a garage or parking area. Camera cable usually runs above the lobby and corridor ceilings to a riser closet and down to a recorder room, with one cored and sealed opening wherever a block wall must be crossed. The work that takes thought is not the cable but the policy: who on the board or in management may view live, who may export a clip, how long recordings are kept and how a shareholder asks for footage. We help the board write that down before the cameras go live. Garages get the most attention, because a garage door opened by an outsider and a row of cars nobody is watching are exactly the combination that has led to break-ins in other Bronx co-op garages nearby.

Delis and corner stores: what the Westchester Avenue robbery points to

The A&A Deli robbery that News 12 reported in 2025 was told through the store's own video, and that is the normal pattern on these avenues. A small store needs four views before anything else. The first is a camera just inside the door, at head height, so that every person coming in is recorded face-on, the single most useful picture in any robbery. The second looks at the counter from above and slightly in front, showing the register, the drawer, the hands and the customer. The third takes in the aisles from a back corner. The fourth covers the back door or stock room from inside. The recorder goes where a robber cannot make the clerk open it quickly, in a locked box or above the ceiling in a back room, on battery backup, and a copy of any important clip is taken off the premises the same day. Where one person works the counter alone, a small screen at the register showing the door and the back of the store lets them keep watch without stepping away.

Glass doors and the hardware store break-in

CBS New York's footage from the Westchester Avenue hardware store showed a rock going through a glass door. Glass doors are the weak point of many storefronts on these streets, and the camera plan has to account for the moment the glass breaks. A camera inside, set back from the door and aimed at it, records the person on the far side of the glass before and after it breaks, and keeps recording once they are inside. A second camera outside, high on your own facade with the landlord's agreement, records the approach along the sidewalk and the direction the person came from and left in. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Pelham Bay: Questions Answered

The parked car

The thieves reported in May 2026 took airbags out of Hondas from the inside. Can a camera on the house see what someone is doing inside a parked car?

Partly. A camera on the house records the approach, the window being broken or the door being opened, the person leaning in, how long they stay and which way they go. At night it rarely records the inside of the car clearly, because the windshield and side glass reflect light back at the lens and the cabin is dark. For an owner, an insurer or the 45th Precinct, the approach and the departure usually matter more than a close view of hands at the dashboard. To get the most from the camera, mount it so the person beside the car is seen side-on or front-on, close enough to fill a good part of the frame, and record continuously so the few seconds of the break-in are not lost while a motion trigger wakes up.

Would a dash camera left in parking mode do a better job than a camera on the house?

They do different jobs. A dash camera in parking mode sits inside the car, so it can record the cabin and the person at the window from close range, but it runs from the car's battery or a separate pack, usually records only when it senses a bump or motion, and can be pulled off the windshield by the same person breaking in. A camera on the house records from outside, continuously, to a recorder indoors that a thief cannot reach, and it sees the person coming up the street before they touch the car. If the car is the main worry, the house camera is the one you can rely on every night. A dash camera can add a close view on top of it, as long as it is not the only record.

Can the driveway camera alert us when someone stays beside the car, instead of every time a person walks past it?

Many current cameras and recorders can. Instead of alerting on any motion, they can be set to alert only when a person enters a zone drawn around the car and stays there for a set number of seconds. A neighbor walking past on the sidewalk crosses the edge of the zone and keeps going, so no alert. Someone stopping at the driver's door, crouching by a wheel or leaning into a window stays in the zone, and the alert arrives with a short clip. We draw the zone tight around where the car usually sits, keep the sidewalk out of it where possible, and test it with a person walking past and a person standing still. The camera still records all night either way; the zone only decides when your phone wakes you.

The block

After a night of break-ins, neighbors on our block want to compare clips from four different camera brands. How do we make sure the times line up?

The clocks are the problem. Each recorder or cloud camera keeps its own time, and a recorder that has never been set to sync can drift by minutes over a year, which makes it hard to follow one person from one house's camera to the next. Before comparing, each owner should check their recorder's time against a phone and note the difference. Going forward, every system should be set to sync its clock automatically with an internet time server and to the right time zone with daylight saving on. When clips are shared, each owner should send the original export with its time stamp, plus a note of the camera's location and any clock difference. On systems we install, the clock sync is set and checked before we leave.

Dumpers pull off, unload and get back on the Hutch or I-95 within minutes. Can a camera on our corner show which way the car went?

Assembly Member Benedetto described exactly that pattern when he funded the area's dumping cameras: people pull off to the side, dump and quickly escape onto the Hutchinson River Parkway or I-95. A camera on a corner house can help if it is aimed along the street toward the likely exit, not across it. Set that way, it records the car leaving in the direction of the highway, its shape, color and rear, and often its plate if the car passes close enough under good light. A view across the street records a blur passing left to right. We usually pair one camera framed on your own frontage, which records the dumping itself, with a second aimed down the block toward the nearest route out, so the two together show what was left and where the car went.

For co-op and condo boards on Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road

Boards in the elevator buildings along Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road tend to bring us the same short list: a garage door that opens too easily, a mailroom where packages vanish, a lobby camera that records the backs of heads, and an old recorder nobody on the current board knows how to search. The garage door gets a camera inside and out, each row gets an overview from its end, and the recorder is set to keep the garage cameras at full quality. Wiring Rules, Licenses and Where a Lens May Point: The Rules Behind a Pelham Bay Camera Job

Most Pelham Bay camera jobs need no paperwork at all, but a few rules decide how the work is done and where cameras may point. Sorting them out before the first hole is drilled saves owners trouble later.

Low-voltage wiring and electrical permits. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, which took effect on December 21, 2025, allows wiring under 50 volts for data, communication, signaling and alarm circuits to be done without an electrical permit, provided the work is performed by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person. Camera cable that carries both power and picture over one network cable falls in that low-voltage range. Not every source reads the rules the same way, and one industry site says low-voltage work needs permits through the Department of Buildings, so we treat the exemption as applying to the low-voltage part only. A new outdoor outlet, a new circuit for a recorder rack or any change to the house wiring is line-voltage work for a licensed electrician, and it is quoted and filed that way.

The state license. Separately from city permits, General Business Law Article 6-D requires a license from the New York Department of State to be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431. Owners and boards are entitled to see it, along with proof of insurance, before work starts.

No law requires cameras. Seneca Security, describing New York City building rules, notes that there is no general NYC law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. The decision to install them, and where, belongs to the owner or the board. That also means the owner sets the rules for who may see the footage.

Renters and shared spaces. Brick Underground's guidance for New York tenants is that you need your landlord's permission to install equipment in the common areas of your building, including the hallway outside your door. In Pelham Bay, where many renters live on one floor of a two- or three-family house, the same logic applies to the outside of the house: the walls, porch, driveway and yard belong to the owner. A tenant can install an indoor camera that stays inside their own apartment. Anything outside it needs the owner's written agreement, which we ask to see.

Privacy lines that do not move. New York Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places where they undress or have a reasonable expectation of privacy, such as bathrooms and bedrooms. No camera we install looks into one. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives residents a civil claim against a neighbor whose camera is aimed into their backyard or home. On Pelham Bay's 30-foot lots, where the next house can be a driveway's width away, that is a practical concern on every side camera, so we frame each view on your own property, mask any part of the picture that takes in a neighbor's windows or yard, and show the owner the final framing before we leave.

Co-op and condo boards. In the elevator buildings on Pelham Parkway South and Middletown Road, building-wide camera work usually needs board approval, and exterior cameras on a shareholder's own windows or terrace are generally governed by the proprietary lease or house rules. We work from the board's written approval and the managing agent's requirements, including a certificate of insurance where asked.

Landmarks. The only designated landmarks we found in the area, such as the Bartow-Pell Mansion and the Orchard Beach bathhouse and promenade, are inside Pelham Bay Park. We found no historic district covering the residential blocks, so a typical house camera here does not need a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit. If your building is an exception, we check before any work is done.

Audio. We set house and business cameras to record video only. Recording conversations raises separate legal questions, and the pictures are what owners need.
