The Neighborhood

Why Boerum Hill Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Boerum Hill is a small neighborhood with a lot of cameras, and most of them were put up by someone other than the person who owns them now. The housing is mostly three-story rowhouses built between about 1840 and 1870, many of them divided over the decades into an owner's duplex with one or two rental floors, with small apartment buildings and storefronts along Atlantic Avenue, Smith Street and the avenues to the east. Houses here change hands, get gut-renovated, and get re-rented, and each of those events tends to leave the camera system a little worse documented than before. The recorder ends up in a cellar closet or a vault under the stoop, the app sits on the phone of a former owner or a contractor, and nobody notices that the drive stopped writing until the morning after something happens on the block. That quiet failure is the most common reason anyone here calls us.

The winter of 2026 made the problem visible. Starting late on a Friday at the end of January, lights across Boerum Hill and Park Slope began to flicker, and by the weekend many households had lost power entirely. ABC7 New York reported that nearly 2,000 Con Edison customers were affected, that Con Edison blamed melting snow and salt for damaging underground electrical equipment and setting off a chain of other failures, and that manholes were buried under about five feet of ice. Residents told the station that four restoration deadlines came and went. News 12 Brooklyn reported 1,632 customers still without power on the Monday night, and business owners in the two neighborhoods said they had gone four days without power or heat. Council Member Shahana Hanif, who wrote to Con Edison about the outage, put the frustration simply: "Families are unable to make arrangements because the estimated time of restoration keeps moving."

For a camera system, the outage was not one event but several. First came the flickering, the kind of brownouts that make a recorder reboot over and over and that are hard on a hard drive in the middle of writing. Then the hard stop, often with no battery backup and no clean shutdown. Then days of cold in unheated cellars and under-stoop vaults, and finally the return of power, sometimes in stages, to a recorder that may have lost its clock, its network settings or its drive. A wine shop owner told News 12 that his lights began flickering on the Friday and the power went down on the Sunday evening, and that he rented a generator to keep going. Many of the systems we have been asked to look at since then came back with some cameras showing and others not, a recorder stamping the wrong date, a phone app that could no longer find the house, or a playback calendar with nothing in it from the week of the outage onward. Each of those has a different cause, and the fix for one does nothing for another.

The second reason Boerum Hill systems need attention is that people here use their footage. Police and business owners on these blocks reach for camera video routinely, and the reports show how often the answer depends on whose system was working. When thieves smashed the front glass doors of Blank Street Coffee on Bergen Street and Piccola Cucina Casa on the same block one September night, taking $100 from one register and $1,000 from the other, Brooklyn Paper reported that "Authorities are reviewing nearby security footage in an effort to identify the suspects." When about $3,500 in packages, including a Ring doorbell camera, was taken from behind a front gate on Bond Street, the paper reported that "Cops said they are checking nearby cameras to identify the perp." In the same 84th Precinct roundup, a phone theft on Fulton Street in Downtown Brooklyn ended with the line "there was no surveillance footage available to identify the perp." The difference between those sentences is a recorder that was saving, a camera that was aimed at the door, and a clock that was right.

Footage has been part of Boerum Hill's crime stories for years. In December 2016 DNAinfo reported that a thief pried open a ground-level window at the Mile End Delicatessen at 97 Hoyt Street at about 4:15 in the morning and left with roughly $2,100 in tablets and other electronics, including the iPad that ran the restaurant's delivery orders; "Surveillance video shows the thief crawling into the restaurant through a ground-level window," the report said. In December 2023 News 12 Brooklyn reported that four people took nearly $17,000 of merchandise from a business at 166 Smith Street and drove off in a gray Chevy Malibu. A neighborhood where a ground-level window, a glass door and a front gate are the usual ways in is a neighborhood where a camera that sees the door clearly at night is worth keeping in working order.

The third reason is the buildings themselves, and the rules that cover their fronts. The Boerum Hill Historic District was designated in 1973 with about 250 buildings, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission extended it in June 2018 with about 288 more, covering Greek Revival and Italianate rowhouses from the 1850s to the 1870s and Second Empire and neo-Grec buildings from the 1880s. On a landmarked front, the commission's permit guidebook says "Security cameras must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," limits small residential and commercial buildings to "no more than two security cameras" for "every 25 feet of street frontage," and wants conduit "concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding behind the installation." The same guidebook exempts "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment" from needing a permit. In practice that means repairing what is already there is usually the simplest path on a landmarked house, and it is one reason we work hard to fix a camera in place rather than move it.

Boerum Hill also shares its blocks with the Gowanus Houses public housing campus, with Wyckoff Gardens a short walk away, and the city's own experience with keeping cameras alive is a useful warning for any building owner. At an April 2025 City Council hearing on public housing, Council Member Chris Banks cited a Department of Investigation finding that "70% of the CCTV cameras at senior buildings were inoperable or inaccessible." A NYCHA official told the committee that when a camera is found down, "We go out within twenty four to seventy two hours to repair the camera as quickly as we can." A private owner on Wyckoff Street or Bergen Street does not have a maintenance department watching a dashboard. If nobody checks, nobody knows.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Boerum Hill System That Has Been Through a Power Failure

These notes describe how our technicians work through a camera system that went down in an outage like the one Boerum Hill had this winter. They set out our standard order of work, not the story of any particular house.

Check the clock before the cameras. The first screen the technician opens is the recorder's time and date. A recorder that lost power for days with a flat clock battery often comes back years in the past, and anything it recorded since then carries a wrong stamp. We note the difference against real time before correcting it, so any clip already saved can still be lined up with what happened.

Read the drive's own report. Brownouts and hard stops are hardest on a drive that was writing at the moment the power dipped. The recorder's storage page and, where available, the drive's health readout tell us whether it is healthy, logging errors, or no longer recognized. A drive that "works" but shows errors after an outage is a drive we plan to replace, not one we wait on.

Look at the playback calendar. Gaps in the calendar after the outage say whether the recorder resumed recording on its own when the power returned. Some recorders come back to a setup screen and wait for someone to press a button, and nothing is saved until they do.

Measure before replacing. Voltage at the camera, PoE power at the port and a cable test settle most questions in minutes. No part is swapped to "see if it helps," because that is how a power supply problem turns into a bill for new cameras.

Leave it ready for the next outage. Before leaving we confirm the recorder resumes recording on its own after a restart, and where the owner agrees we put the recorder and any switch on a battery backup so the next flicker does not become a drive failure.

Fault by Fault

Power, Recorder, Cable or Camera: How Boerum Hill Systems Fail, Especially After an Outage

Every wired camera system is a chain of four links: the power that feeds it, the cable that carries the picture, the recorder or switch that collects it, and the camera at the end. When any link breaks, the screen tends to show the same black tile, which is why owners so often buy a new camera for a fault that was never in the camera. A multi-day outage stresses all four at once. Here is how each fails in Boerum Hill's houses and shops, and what the repair involves.

Power: brownouts, hard stops and the restart nobody watched

Older coax systems, the ones with round BNC plugs on the back of the recorder, usually power their cameras from a central box with a row of small fuses or from a separate plug-in adapter for each camera. Network systems power cameras over the data cable from a PoE switch or from ports on the back of the recorder. In normal times a central box that is ageing can carry the cameras by day and then sag at dusk, when every camera switches on its infrared lights at once; the result is cameras that reboot or drop out only at night. In an outage the risk is different. The flickering that began on the Friday night in January, before power failed entirely, is the kind of repeated dip that can blow a fuse in a power box, damage an adapter, or leave a PoE switch in a state where some ports stop delivering power. When power returned, a camera on a blown fuse simply stayed dark.

Power repairs are usually quick and cost far less than cameras: a new supply sized with headroom for the night load, a fuse and an output checked one by one, or a port or switch replaced. On our Brooklyn list power supply repair typically runs $150 to $400, and a reterminate or PoE port swap $150 to $350. The other half of a power repair is preventing the next one. A small battery backup on the recorder, and on the PoE switch for a network system, rides through brief flickers and lets the equipment shut down cleanly in a long outage instead of crashing mid-write. It will not keep a system running for four days, and nobody should promise that it will, but it protects the drive, which is the most fragile part of the chain.

Recorder: the drive, the clock and settings that did not survive

The recorder is where outages do their quietest damage. A hard drive that was writing when the power dipped can develop bad sectors and keep recording in fits and starts, leaving gaps an owner finds only when they need a particular night. A recorder whose clock battery has gone flat comes back with a date in the past and stamps every new clip with it. Some recorders return to their setup wizard after a hard stop and sit there, not recording, until someone completes it. Others keep recording locally but lose the network settings that let the phone app find them, especially if the household's internet router also lost power and came back with different settings.

The repairs range from a settings rebuild and a clock set to keep time from the internet, which often fits inside the service call, to a drive swap at $200 to $450 depending on capacity, to a replacement recorder. A DVR that will not work averages $300 to $700 on our Brooklyn list including the drive and labor, an NVR that is not recording averages $400 to $900 depending on the model, and a full DVR or NVR replacement with a drive and setup runs $700 to $1,500. Before we replace a recorder we confirm the existing cameras will work with the new one, because in a rowhouse the cable inside the walls is the most expensive thing to replace, and a hybrid recorder can often keep every old coax camera running beside new network cameras.

Cable: renovations, rear yards and the connector that turned green

Boerum Hill's cable faults follow its renovations. A house that has been gut-renovated since its cameras went in may have had a run cut behind new plaster, stapled through by a trim carpenter, or buried in a ceiling without a junction anyone can reach. In rear yards, cable that crosses open brick or follows a fence takes years of sun and rain, and water finds its way along a cracked jacket to the connector. In cellars, rodents chew what they can reach. Still, the most common cable fault is not the cable at all but the connector at the end of it: a twist-on BNC plug that corroded at a rear camera, or a network plug that let water in. We test the connector first and the run second.

Camera: water, cold, impact and age

Cameras themselves are the failed part less often than people assume, but they do fail. Water gets in through a cracked housing or an unsealed cable entry, and the picture fogs or spots on humid days. A week of hard cold can stick the small mechanical filter that switches a camera between day and night modes, leaving a pink or purple daytime picture or a night picture with no infrared. Infrared lights burn out with age. A dome cover gets scratched or cracked. Replacing a single camera on a working system runs $250 to $600 on our Brooklyn list with the camera, and if only the dome or front cover is damaged we can often replace just the cover for a $30 to $80 part plus labor.

Rules that touch a commercial camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install, service or maintain security and fire alarm systems, and state rules extend that license to closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in, theft or movement. An owner working on their own property is exempt; a company you pay to fix your shop's cameras is not. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras that watch employees. New York Labor Law Section 203-c requires employers to give notice of electronic monitoring and obtain written acknowledgment, and it restricts recording in places such as restrooms and changing areas. If a repair adds a camera or re-aims one over a staff area, check that your notices are current. This is general information, not legal advice; ask your own adviser how the rules apply to your business.

Landmarks on the storefront. Inside the historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook applies to exterior cameras on shopfronts as it does on houses: well spaced, attached "at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," never "through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces," and painted to blend where possible. Repairing a camera in place is ordinary maintenance; moving one to a new spot on the front is where the commission's criteria come in, and its staff can be reached at 347-934-8335.

Sharing with the NYPD. In 2024 the Mayor's Office announced a pilot aimed at retail theft that lets businesses register their cameras with the NYPD and, if they choose, share live feeds, at no cost to the business. A shop that wants to take part needs a recorder that is current, online and keeping correct time. We can bring a system to that standard; whether to register is your decision and your application.

Security Camera Repair in Boerum Hill: Questions Answered

These are the situations Boerum Hill owners, tenants, condo boards and shopkeepers describe when they call about cameras: a generator during the outage, a recorder that forgot everything, a cable stuck to a landmarked front, a face that will not resolve on a recording of a smashed door. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down the page.

After the February outage

We kept our Smith Street shop open on a rented generator during the outage. Could the generator have damaged the recorder or the cameras?

It can, though it often does not. Small portable generators can produce power that wanders in voltage and frequency as the load changes, and a recorder, a PoE switch or a camera power box plugged straight into one can be stressed by it, particularly when a refrigerator compressor or a heater kicks on from the same unit. The things to look for afterward are a power supply that runs hot or hums, cameras that reboot or flicker on and off, a recorder that restarts on its own, and drive errors that were not there before. We test the supplies under load, read the recorder's logs and check the drive's health; if a supply was damaged, power supply repair typically runs $150 to $400 on our Brooklyn list. For the next long outage, a battery backup between the generator and the recorder smooths what the generator delivers and is cheap insurance for the drive.

When the power came back, our recorder started up on its setup screen as if it were brand new. Are our settings gone for good?

The settings on the box may be, but the system usually is not. When a recorder comes back to its first-run wizard after a hard power loss, the configuration it held, which cameras sit on which channels, the recording schedule, the motion zones, the user accounts and the network details, may have been reset, while the video already on the drive is often still there. The first step is to stop anyone clicking through the wizard with default answers, because some recorders offer to initialize the drive along the way. We then rebuild the configuration channel by channel, reconnect the cameras, restore remote viewing, and check whether older recordings are still readable. Before we leave, we save a copy of the finished configuration where the recorder allows it, so a future failure takes minutes to recover rather than an afternoon.

Since the cold snap, our front camera's daytime picture has a pink or purple tint. What failed?

Almost always the small filter inside the camera that switches between day and night modes. By day it slides in front of the sensor to block infrared light; at night it slides out so the camera can see by its infrared lights. If it sticks in the night position, the daytime picture picks up infrared and turns pink, purple or washed-out. Cold, moisture and age are the usual causes, and a camera that has been switching twice a day for years is a likely candidate. Sometimes a restart or a mode change from the recorder frees it, which we try first. If the filter stays stuck, the fix is replacing the camera, which runs $250 to $600 on our Brooklyn list with the new camera, and on a landmarked front we fit the replacement to the existing mounting points so the facade is not touched.

Landmark fronts and old walls

The cable to our stoop camera runs down the front of our Bergen Street house in a plastic raceway. When you fix the camera, can the cable be hidden?

Often, yes, and on a landmarked house it is worth trying. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook wants conduit "concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding behind the installation," so a cable that crosses the face of a rowhouse is exactly what the commission would rather not see. The usual approach is to bring the cable through the wall directly behind the camera, into the parlor-floor vestibule or the space above the door, and down inside the house to the cellar, then remove the old raceway and patch its fixing holes. Whether that works depends on what is behind the camera: a vestibule ceiling with access is easy, a finished plaster wall with a mantel below is harder. We look at both sides on the service call and give you a written price for the concealed route as its own item. Where the change affects the facade beyond ordinary repair, we will tell you to check with the commission first.

Lines roll slowly across our old cameras' picture whenever the boiler fires in the cellar. Is that the cameras, the wiring or the boiler?

Usually the wiring and the power, not the cameras. Slow-moving horizontal bars on an analog picture are a classic sign of electrical interference, often a ground loop, where the camera and the recorder see slightly different electrical ground and a small current flows along the cable's shield. A boiler's motor and igniter starting nearby, or a camera cable running alongside the boiler's power feed, can make it visible. In Boerum Hill cellars, where camera cables, boiler wiring and old electrical runs often share the same joists, it is a common pattern. The fix may be re-routing the camera cable away from the power wiring, isolating the ground at one end with a small inline device, or replacing a cheap power supply that is passing noise through. We prove which it is with the boiler running before changing anything.

Our recorder sits in the vault under the front stoop, and the drive has failed twice in three years. Is the location the problem?

Quite possibly. The vault under a Boerum Hill stoop is cool in summer, cold in winter and often damp, with condensation on the stone and little air movement. Hard drives in recorders are designed for ordinary indoor conditions, and a drive that sits in damp cold, or that heats and cools sharply through the year, tends to wear out sooner. Two drives in three years is a pattern worth acting on. We check the drive and the recorder's logs, and if the location is the cause, the lasting fix is moving the recorder into a dry, heated room, such as a cellar room on the house side of the foundation wall or a closet on the garden floor, and extending the camera cables to reach it. We price the move as its own item; a replacement drive runs $200 to $450 on our Brooklyn list by capacity.

Tenants, boards and who holds the password

Packages keep disappearing from the vestibule of our walk-up, and the camera there has been dead since last year. What should we send the landlord?

Put it in writing and keep it specific. Brick Underground, answering a tenant in exactly this position, quoted the attorney Jeffrey Reich: "A landlord that is on notice of criminal activity at a property may be obligated to take reasonable safety precautions," and "Fixing the security camera would certainly be a reasonable action for the landlord to take in this instance." The same article noted that no city law requires rental buildings to have working cameras, so the case rests on notice. A short letter or email listing each missing package with its date, saying the vestibule camera has not worked since a given month, and asking for it to be repaired, does that. Keep a copy. You are welcome to send us the building's address and the landlord's or managing agent's contact; we can arrange the visit with them, since the owner has to approve and pay for work on the building's system.

Six of us own the condo units in a converted rowhouse, and nobody is formally in charge of the camera recorder in the cellar. Who should hold the admin password?

The board, or one person the board names in writing, and nobody else. Small condos often inherit a single admin login from the developer's installer, shared informally among owners or lost entirely. That causes two problems: nobody can approve changes with authority, and anyone who ever had the password can delete footage or change settings. The cleaner setup is one admin account held by the board's designee, with a second sealed copy kept with the board's records, and separate viewing accounts for each owner that can watch and play back but not change settings or delete. We can set that up during a repair visit, recover access if the old password is lost and the brand allows it, and leave a short written record of the system for the board's files.

Storefronts and footage that has to be useful

Our restaurant's glass door was smashed overnight. The camera recorded it, but the face is a blur of blocks. Is that a repair or a new camera?

Often it is neither; it is a settings problem. Many recorders save video at a reduced quality to fit more days on the drive, and at night, when the picture is grainy, heavy compression turns a moving face into blocks. Others record the camera's low-resolution second stream instead of the main one. Distance matters too: a camera that covers the whole dining room may give the door only a few dozen pixels of width. On the visit we check which stream is being recorded, the resolution, the bit rate and the frame rate, and how far the camera is from the door. Raising the recording quality on the door camera is usually free within the service call, and it costs drive space, which we balance against how many days you keep. If the camera physically cannot resolve a face at that distance, re-aiming it tighter on the door, or a dedicated door camera, is the answer, priced in writing before anything is bought.

Our Atlantic Avenue shop's recorder only keeps about five days of video. How do we get more without replacing everything?

There are three levers, and most systems can use at least two. The first is the drive: a larger surveillance drive in the same recorder is the most direct way to add days, and a drive swap runs $200 to $450 on our Brooklyn list depending on capacity, as long as the recorder supports the size. The second is how the recording is set up: recording all cameras around the clock at maximum quality fills a drive fast, while recording at full quality on motion and lower quality the rest of the time can stretch the same drive much further. The third is matching quality to purpose, high on the door and the register, lower on a stockroom. Five days is short for a business, because a theft or a dispute is often noticed a week or more later. We check the recorder's drive limit and give you the days each option would realistically give before you choose.

Selling up and the system you leave behind

We are selling our Boerum Hill rowhouse. What should we do with the camera system before the closing?

Decide early whether the system stays with the house, then either hand it over cleanly or take your accounts out of it. If it stays, the buyer will want the admin password, a list of cameras and where their cables run, the recorder's model, and an idea of how old the drive is. Remove your own phones and any cloud accounts in your name, export any footage you want to keep, and delete personal recordings if you would rather not leave them. If you take some equipment with you, agree in writing which pieces stay. A short visit before closing can confirm every camera is recording, set a temporary admin password for the buyer and label the cables in the cellar.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Boerum Hill

The claims below turn up, paraphrased, in generic online answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is followed by what Boerum Hill's houses, shops and this winter's outage actually show.

"Expect to pay about $1,400 to repair a security camera system in Brooklyn"

One of the first numbers a search turns up is HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn figure: an average of $1,416, a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and a low-to-high spread of $160 to $3,500. AI summaries tend to repeat it as the price of a repair.

That page blends installation and repair, so new systems pull its average up. A repair is usually one fault, a power supply, a connector, a drive or a single camera, and on our Brooklyn list most single-issue repairs come in between $150 and $700. Only a new recorder, a partial rewire or a rebuild of several cameras reaches the $700 to $1,500 band.

For a Boerum Hill rowhouse with one problem, the useful planning figure is the $150 service call plus the cost of the one part that failed. You get that number in writing on the visit, before anything is replaced, so your job is never measured against an average that includes other people's installations.

"After a power outage, the cameras will come back on their own"

Many generic answers treat an outage as a pause: the power returns, the recorder boots, and everything carries on as before.

That is true for many systems after a short blink, and it is often not true after the kind of outage Boerum Hill had, with flickering on the Friday, a hard loss over the weekend and days before power returned. Brownouts can blow fuses in a camera power box, a hard stop can damage a drive mid-write, a flat clock battery can send the recorder back years, and some recorders come back to a setup screen and record nothing until someone finishes it.

The only way to know is to look: check the clock, the drive's health, the playback calendar and every channel. If you have not opened playback since the outage, do it now, and if the calendar is blank from that week onward, call before you need a clip.

"Just unplug the recorder and plug it back in"

The most common first step in online troubleshooting is a restart, and plenty of AI answers end there.

A restart can clear a frozen camera. It can also do harm the answers leave out. If something just happened and you need footage, power-cycling a recorder with a failing drive can make the recording harder to recover, and on a recorder that lost its settings, the restart may drop you into a setup wizard that offers to initialize the drive. When the cause is a power box that sags every evening, a restart fixes nothing, and the cameras will drop again at the next dusk.

On a repair visit, the footage you need is saved first and the cause is found before anything is restarted or reset. If you are not sure whether you need a clip, say so when you book and we will treat it as if you do.

"Replace the old camera with a new one in a better spot"

Generic advice often suggests that a failing camera is a chance to move it wherever it would see best, and to add a couple more while you are at it.

On a Boerum Hill house inside the historic district, that advice runs into the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Its guidebook limits small buildings to two cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage, wants them "well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and bars attachment "through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." The same guidebook treats ordinary maintenance and repair of existing equipment as needing no permit.

So the cheapest and simplest repair on a landmarked front is usually a compatible camera on the existing mounting points, with the cable on its existing path. If a camera truly needs to move, we tell you before work starts, and the commission's staff can be reached at 347-934-8335 for the rules that apply to your building.

"Wi-Fi cameras do away with wiring problems"

A common suggestion is that switching a troublesome wired system to wireless cameras ends cable faults for good.

A Wi-Fi camera still needs power, which means an outlet or a battery, and it still needs to reach the router. In a rowhouse, the router is usually on the parlor or garden floor, the rear-yard camera is outside a brick back wall, and the front camera is on the far side of a stone or brick facade. Battery cameras add charging, and every wireless camera stops recording when the router does, which is exactly what happened to many homes in the February outage.

For a wired system with one bad cable, repairing that run is usually the cheapest and most reliable fix. Wireless makes sense in a few spots, a garden shed at the back of a long yard with no cable path for instance, and we say so when it does.

"Hikvision and Dahua cameras are banned, so the whole system has to go"

After the FCC's 2022 order, AI answers sometimes say that owners of these brands must replace their systems or that the equipment can no longer be serviced.

Security Sales & Integration reported that the FCC adopted rules "prohibiting communications equipment deemed to pose an unacceptable risk to national security" from new authorization, naming Hikvision and Dahua, and that Dahua's position was that "the FCC's Order does not affect products that are already authorized." The FCC also sought comment on what to do about existing authorizations, so the question is not entirely settled.

What it means for a repair is practical: getting exact replacement models of some equipment can be harder, and when a part has to change we may use a compatible camera or recorder from another maker. A working system does not have to come off the wall because of its badge, and we will tell you plainly what your options are.

"Police and city cameras cover the street, so a private system adds little"

Some generic answers suggest that in a busy part of the city, public cameras make a private system redundant.

The reporting from Boerum Hill points the other way. When the glass doors on Bergen Street were smashed, police went to "nearby security footage." When parcels vanished on Bond Street, they were "checking nearby cameras." Both lines describe private cameras on private buildings, the ones that see a particular door, gate or stoop. A camera at an intersection sees the intersection.

The Fulton Street case in the same roundup, where "there was no surveillance footage available," shows the other outcome. A private camera that is recording, aimed at the door and keeping the right time is often the only one that matters to a case on your block.

FAQ

Boerum Hill Security Camera Repair FAQ

How can I tell whether my Boerum Hill system is analog or network before I call?
+

Look at the back of the recorder. Round twist-on BNC plugs mean coax cameras on a DVR; square network plugs like an internet cable, one per camera or running to a separate switch, mean network cameras on an NVR. A mix of both usually means a hybrid. A photo of the back panel settles it.

Can you repair cameras that are still under another company's warranty?
+

We can, but check that warranty first, because work by someone else may end it. If the installer still exists and the warranty covers the fault, that is usually the cheaper route. If the installer has closed, the warranty has lapsed, or they will not come, we repair it and price it in writing before starting.

Should the default password on our recorder be changed during the repair?
+

Yes. A recorder still on its factory password, or on one shared by a former owner or contractor, can be logged into by anyone who knows it. We set a strong admin password held by the person who pays for the system and give other users their own logins with limited rights.

Can our cameras save to the cloud as well as to the recorder?
+

Some can, depending on the brand and model, usually through the manufacturer's own subscription service. Most wired systems in Boerum Hill rowhouses and shops record only to the local drive. We can tell you what your equipment supports; local recording still matters most, because the cloud stops when the internet does.

Can a family member or a caregiver see our cameras on their own phone?
+

Yes. Most recorders allow extra user accounts with limited rights, for example live view of the stoop and hallway but no playback, no settings and no deleting. We set those up on a visit and you can remove any of them later without affecting your own login.

Can you turn off the microphone on our cameras?
