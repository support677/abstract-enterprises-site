The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Gowanus grew up around a canal dug in the 1860s, and for more than a century its blocks were given over to the trades that a working waterway attracts: foundries, can makers, stone and lumber yards, warehouses and garages. Very few of those buildings were designed for anything more than a telephone line per business, and the ones that survive today were built with heavy masonry walls, timber or steel framing and open floors meant for machines, not cubicles. That inheritance is the first reason a Gowanus network has to be planned rather than improvised. In a building like that there is no hollow wall to fish a cable through and no hung ceiling to hide it in. Every cable either rides on supports across an open ceiling, climbs inside a shaft that someone has to find or create, or runs in surface raceway that needs to look like it belongs there.

The second reason is the speed at which the neighborhood is changing. The City Council approved the Gowanus rezoning in November 2021, covering 82 blocks and expected to bring around 8,500 new apartments, about 3,000 of them affordable, with towers allowed up to 30 stories near the canal. The first large rentals came even earlier: 363 and 365 Bond Street, two 12-story buildings with roughly 700 apartments between them, were designed without a cellar because the site floods. Newer projects such as Union Channel at 240 Third Avenue, a nine-story building with 224 apartments, and the 15-story Douglass Port at 251 Douglass Street arrived with app-based entry, package rooms and building-wide internet built in. Brooklyn Fiber lists 340 Nevins Street and 251 Douglass Street as fully wired with managed, building-wide Wi-Fi. For residents of those buildings the internet is already there on move-in day. The cabling questions that remain are about what happens inside each apartment, in the ground-floor shops below and in the small offices that want more than the building's shared network.

The third reason is that Gowanus still works for a living. When the Department of City Planning released its vision for the Gowanus Industrial Business Zone in May 2021, it counted nearly 300 businesses and about 4,000 private-sector jobs there, spanning manufacturing, construction, warehousing, arts, retail and offices. Hyperallergic's coverage of the 2022 Gowanus Open Studios, the 26th edition of the event, described artists working in former factories along Second, Third and Fourth Avenues. The Old American Can Factory on Third Street alone houses more than 300 creative tenants, from publishers and architects to screen printers and documentary makers. Each of those tenants needs a connection that works, is separate from the neighbors' and can be handed to the next tenant when a lease ends. The new commercial buildings add a different kind of customer: 300 Huntington, completed in January 2024 and described as the neighborhood's only new ground-up office and commercial building, offers 49,000 square feet of column-free office and creative space, and Roulston House converted three warehouses on Ninth and Tenth Streets into about 200,000 square feet with 15-foot ceilings.

The fourth reason is the one that surprises people most. In parts of Gowanus, getting a modern line to the front wall of the building has been its own project. In July 2018 Gothamist reported that Spectrum Business, the only high-speed provider willing to consider a building on Union Street, had estimated the cost of extending service to a one-person audio business there at $138,963.96, citing "extensive and costly street cutting otherwise needed to extend our network to this business address." Susan Lerner of Common Cause New York explained the problem plainly: providers "don't have the obligation to provide the final 100 yards of infrastructure." That story ended well; Spectrum crews laid the cable a few weeks later at no charge and the owner's download speed went from 10 Mbps to 481 Mbps. But the lesson stands for any older Gowanus building. Before anyone talks about Cat6 versus Cat6A, somebody has to know how the provider's cable reaches the building, where it lands and how it gets from there to the room where the network lives. That is cabling work too, and it is where we start.

Put those four things together and a Gowanus cabling job tends to look different from the same job in a brownstone neighborhood a few blocks away. It is more often commercial than residential. It more often involves a landlord who is not the customer. It more often crosses open ceilings, brick piers and steel, and more often needs a backbone between floors or between buildings on the same lot. And because so many of the spaces turn over, from studios on month-to-month leases to shops that change hands with the rezoning, the labeling and the paperwork matter as much as the cable. A network that only the original installer understands is a liability the day the tenant who paid for it moves out.

  • Some new buildings arrive pre-wired.   • The working blocks are big. The Department of City Planning counted nearly 300 businesses and about 4,000 jobs in the Industrial Business Zone in 2021, so long runs across warehouses, shops and yards are a normal part of the work.
  • Studio buildings hold hundreds of tenants. The Old American Can Factory at 232 Third Street has more than 300 creative tenants, according to the Brooklyn Eagle. One closet, many networks, constant turnover.
  • The city has promised more broadband here. The published Gowanus rezoning commitments include supporting "the expansion of broadband internet access in the Gowanus IBZ" and accelerating free or low-cost broadband at Gowanus Houses and Wyckoff Gardens. New provider builds are worth planning for.
  • Low ground still matters. The canal overflowed into streets and basements during Hurricane Sandy, and the Bond Street rentals were built without cellars for that reason. Racks, switches and battery backups belong above the water line, not on a cellar floor.

Same-Day Repair in Gowanus

A Studio Floor Went Dark, a Contractor Cut a Run, or Nobody Knows Which Cable Feeds Which Space: Same-Day Estimates in Gowanus

Network trouble in Gowanus usually follows some other piece of work. A sprinkler contractor reroutes a branch line and lifts a bundle of data cable off its hooks to get past it. A landlord's handyman closes a hole in a fire wall and crushes the cable passing through it. A tenant who is moving out unplugs every patch cord in the shared closet to take their switch, and the three studios that were plugged into it lose their connection on a Saturday. A shop renovating its storefront has the electrician move the register, and the cable to the register ends up stretched across the floor in a tape-covered loop. None of those are exotic failures, and none of them need a full rebuild. They need someone who can find the fault quickly, explain what happened and put the run back the way it should have been.

When the problem is a single run that stopped working, the fix is usually a re-termination or a short splice-free replacement of the damaged length, then a certification test so you know the repaired run is as good as the rest. When the problem is a closet nobody can read, we tone out every cable from the panel to the outlet, label both ends and give you a port map so the next move-out cannot cause the same confusion. When a whole floor is down, we start at the equipment: power, the switch, the uplink to the provider's equipment, then the patch panel, and work outward. Most of the time the cause is found in the first hour, and the estimate for the repair is given on the spot.

Tell us what changed just before the trouble started. A contractor's name, a move-out date or a photo of the closet as it looks now saves more time than any test equipment. If the connection is down for a business that cannot wait, say so when you call and we will tell you honestly how quickly we can be there.

The factory building split into studios: open ceilings, one closet, constant turnover

A former factory is the most common Gowanus cabling job and the one with the most ways to go wrong. The ceilings are usually open, which looks like an advantage until you notice that everything on them is visible: the sprinkler mains, the electrical conduit, the ducts some tenants have added and the cable left by every tenant before you. A new run cannot simply be zip-tied to whatever is nearest. Sprinkler pipe and electrical conduit are not supports for data cable, and a landlord who has seen a dozen tenants hang cable from his sprinkler branches will not thank you for a thirteenth. We carry cable on its own supports, usually J-hooks or a short run of tray fixed to the structure at intervals that keep the bundle from sagging, and we keep the route along the corridor side of the floor where it can be reached without entering anyone's studio.

The closet is the second problem. In most studio buildings the provider's line, the landlord's equipment and the tenants' own switches all end up in one small room, often under a stair or beside the freight elevator. Our goal there is a panel that the landlord controls and each tenant can rely on. Every studio gets one or more cables that land on a numbered port, the port carries the studio number the building already uses, and the landlord gets a port map. When a studio changes hands, the building manager moves one patch cord instead of calling an installer. Where tenants buy their own internet service, the same panel lets each provider's equipment reach the right studio without any tenant touching another's connection.

Brick piers and thick walls make the third problem. A cable that has to pass from the corridor into a studio usually goes over the top of the studio partition, which in many buildings stops short of the ceiling, rather than through the masonry. Where it does have to pass through brick, we core a small hole, sleeve it and seal it, and if the wall is rated we use a firestop product listed for that wall. We never cut a chase into a bearing wall to hide a cable.

The one- and two-story industrial building: height, distance and the working floor

The low industrial buildings along Second Avenue, Third Avenue and the blocks toward Hamilton Avenue are wide and tall inside. A run from the office at the front to a workstation at the back of a warehouse can approach the distance limit for a single copper link, and cable crossing a ceiling 18 or 20 feet up has to be installed from a lift, not a ladder. We plan those buildings around three questions. Where can the cable climb to the ceiling without being hit by a forklift or a truck backing in? Where does it come down to each workstation, and what protects it there? And is the farthest point close enough for copper, or does it need a small switch or a fiber link part of the way?

The working floor also decides the hardware. A jack on a workbench in a fabrication shop sits in dust, vibration and sometimes metal shavings, so it goes in a surface box with a cover, fed from above in a short length of conduit rather than hanging loose. Cable passing over welding bays, large motors or a compressor is kept at a distance from them, and where that is not possible, shielded cable or fiber takes the place of ordinary twisted pair. Roll-up doors get their own attention: a camera or access point near the door is wired so the cable is not in the path of the door or its chain.

Many of these buildings have a small office in a mezzanine or a built-out corner. That is usually the right place for the rack, because it is dry, away from the dust of the floor and reachable without a ladder, and it keeps the network equipment out of the cellar where there is one.

The avenue building with a store below: two tenants, one cellar

On Third and Fourth Avenues the classic building is three or four stories with a shop or restaurant at street level and apartments or offices upstairs. The provider's lines usually enter through the cellar, which is used by the store, and the apartments' service climbs from there. That arrangement works until the store needs more than a router behind the counter. A restaurant wants registers at the bar and the host stand, a printer in the kitchen, a computer in the office, cameras and guest Wi-Fi, and each of those should have its own cable back to a switch in a cabinet the staff cannot unplug by accident.

We keep the store's network and the upstairs network physically separate wherever we can, with separate panels, labels and, if the owner wants it, separate cabinets, so a tenant upstairs never ends up on the shop's equipment and the shop's cameras are never one patch cord away from a stranger's laptop. In the cellar we mount the equipment on the wall or in a cabinet off the floor, away from the sump, the water meter and the walk-in cooler's condensate line, and where the cellar has flooded before we recommend moving the store's equipment to the ground floor office instead.

Kitchens need their own care. Cable passing a hood, a fryer or a dishwasher area is routed away from heat and steam, and a jack in the kitchen goes high on the wall in a box that can be wiped down. If the building's ceiling is a drop ceiling that the kitchen's air handling uses as a return, the cable in it has to be plenum rated, and we confirm that before ordering cable rather than after the inspector looks up.

The new canal-side rental or condo: the developer's room and your apartment

In the rezoning-era buildings along the canal, the provider's fiber, the building's managed Wi-Fi equipment, the entry system and the package room network usually live in a telecom room the developer or management company controls. Residents rarely need cable outside their own apartment. What they ask for is a wired connection at a desk, a television or a home office corner, because the building's Wi-Fi is shared with hundreds of neighbors and a video call drops at the wrong moment. Inside the apartment, the walls are usually metal studs and drywall, which is easy to fish through, but the floors and ceilings are concrete and the building's rules decide what can be opened.

We start with the management office. Most buildings of this kind want a certificate of insurance, a short description of the work and a set of work hours, and some have a preferred contractor list. Then we look at what the developer already installed inside the unit. Many new apartments have a small media panel or a structured wiring box in a closet with a cable or two to the living room and bedrooms. If those cables are there and test well, the job may be as simple as terminating them properly and adding a small switch. If they are not, we fish new cable inside the walls of the apartment, keeping the work inside your unit.

Ground-floor tenants in these buildings are a separate case. A café or a fitness studio at the base of a tower is a commercial customer of the building and usually needs its own service and its own equipment, separate from the residential network. We plan that with the building's property team so the shop's line reaches its space by an approved route.

The new or converted commercial floor: open space and the fit-out schedule

Commercial floors such as those at 300 Huntington, delivered as column-free office and creative space, and the converted warehouses of Roulston House, with 15-foot ceilings and exposed brick and timber kept from the original buildings, arrive mostly empty. The landlord has built the base building: the provider's entry, the risers, the telecom room on each floor or in the cellar, and sometimes a backbone to each floor. Everything after that, from the telecom room to the tenant's desks, is the tenant's to build, and the timing is set by the fit-out.

The single most useful thing a tenant on a floor like that can do is bring us in when the layout is still on paper. Cable routes across an exposed ceiling need to be designed with the lighting, the ductwork and the sprinkler heads so they look intentional. Outlets at desks in an open plan need power and data from the same place, often from the ceiling in poles or from the floor, and that decision belongs at the drawing stage. And a rack in a tenant's space needs power, cooling and a lock, which an architect can provide easily before the walls go up and expensively after.

Where the landlord requires it, we coordinate with the building's engineer about which shafts and sleeves tenants may use, how penetrations through rated floors are sealed and who signs off. If the building is pursuing a connectivity rating, as Roulston House said it was in 2016 when it applied for WiredScore Platinum, the building's own risers and entry points are usually well documented, which shortens our survey.

The side-street row house: the cellar, the stair and a shared wall

The quieter streets of Gowanus toward Park Slope and Carroll Gardens still have brick and brownstone row houses, some still single family, many split into apartments, a few with a shop or office on the ground floor. Cabling them is closer to the work we do across brownstone Brooklyn. The provider's line comes into the cellar through the front wall, and the job is to get it up two or three floors past party walls that cannot be opened. The usual routes are a closet stack, the stair wall or an old chase, and we look for them before suggesting anything visible.

Gowanus row houses have one extra question that most of their neighbors in Park Slope do not: how wet the cellar gets. On the lower streets closer to the canal, we avoid putting the router, switch or battery backup in the cellar at all, and plan instead for a small cabinet in a ground-floor closet with the provider's line brought up to it. That way the network survives the kind of storm that puts water on the cellar floor.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Looks for First on a Gowanus Studio Floor

On a shared studio floor in a converted factory, the first ten minutes of a survey are spent not on the studio that called us but on the corridor ceiling and the closet. We look up to see what is already hanging there and how it is held: whether earlier tenants used J-hooks or simply tied cable to the sprinkler branch lines, whether there is dead cable from businesses long gone, and whether there is a clear lane along the corridor that a new bundle can follow without crossing the electrical conduit. Then we look at the partitions. If the studio walls stop below the ceiling, cable can drop into each studio from the corridor lane without a single hole in masonry.

In the closet we read whatever labels exist, count the free ports, check whether the landlord's equipment has battery backup and look for signs of water on the floor or the lower wall. We ask the building manager which studio numbers are official, because tenants often use their own. And we ask one question that saves more trouble than any other: who in the building is allowed to move a patch cord? The answer decides how the panel is laid out and who gets the port map.

The Last 100 Yards

The Street, the Front Wall and the Equipment Room: Getting a Gowanus Building Connected Before Wiring It

Most cabling pages start inside the building. In Gowanus that can be a mistake, because a surprising share of the trouble sits outside it. The provider has to reach the property line, the cable has to cross the front or side wall at a place that makes sense, and it has to land somewhere dry, powered and secure. If any of those three is wrong, the best-built network inside will still be waiting on someone else. The 2018 Gothamist story about the Union Street audio business is the sharpest example: Spectrum's estimate of $138,963.96 was driven not by the inside of the building but by the street cutting needed to reach it. The business owner had only been offered 10 Mbps before that; once Spectrum found what its spokesperson called "a cost-effective means to provide service to this business," his speed rose to 481 Mbps.

We cannot dig the street, and we do not speak for any provider. What we can do is make the building side of that work as small and as clear as possible, which often matters to how a provider prices and schedules it. That means three things. First, we find out which providers already serve the block and how: overhead on the poles, in conduit under the sidewalk, or from a building next door. Second, we pick a single, sensible entry point on the building, usually at the front or side wall closest to the provider's existing plant, and prepare a pathway from there to the equipment room. Third, we put that plan on paper with photos so the owner can send it to every provider they ask for service.

One entrance, planned once

Older Gowanus buildings collect entrances. Every provider that has served the building drilled its own hole, ran its own line down the front, and left its own box on the cellar wall. A new provider looking at a building like that often adds another. We prefer to set one entrance pathway with spare capacity, a sleeve or conduit through the wall sized for more than one provider, with a pull string left in it, and a backboard inside where each provider's equipment can be mounted in its own place. That keeps the facade cleaner, gives the owner a single place to point a provider's technician to, and means the next provider is a short job rather than a new hole.

Where the building has a landmark designation, or sits on a block where exterior changes need approval, the entrance plan is made with that in mind before any drilling, and we tell the owner what has to be filed and by whom. The Coignet Building at Third Avenue and Third Street, designated in 2006, is the best-known example in the neighborhood, but most Gowanus buildings are not landmarked, and for them the question is simply where the line will be least visible and most protected.

Buildings that come pre-wired

The opposite case is just as common now. Brooklyn Fiber lists 340 Nevins Street and 251 Douglass Street as fully wired, with managed building-wide Wi-Fi and activation without an installer visit, and its page for landlords says it installs "the backbone and in-building distribution" itself after surveying the building's pathways. In a building like that, the building-wide network belongs to the provider and the landlord, and nobody should be adding cable to it without their consent. Our work in those buildings stays on the resident's or tenant's side of the line: wiring from the outlet the building provides to the rooms and devices that need it, or building a separate network for a ground-floor business that has its own service.

Where the network lives

The equipment room is the third piece, and in Gowanus it carries a weight it does not carry in most of Brooklyn. Hurricane Sandy pushed the canal into the streets and basements of homes and businesses, the Brooklyn Eagle reported, and the developers of 363 and 365 Bond Street built without a cellar at all because of the flood risk. The city's Building Code, in Appendix G, requires electrical systems in flood-prone construction to sit at or above the design flood elevation or be built to resist flooding, and the 2025 Electrical Code places panels, disconnects and breakers at or above that elevation as well. Those rules are aimed at power equipment rather than data cabling, but the reasoning applies to a rack just as much. If the switch, the router and the battery backup are on the cellar floor, a few inches of water takes the network down long after the water has gone.

We therefore recommend, wherever the building allows it, that the main network equipment sits on the ground floor or higher, with the provider's line brought up to it from the entrance. When the cellar is the only practical place, the equipment goes high on the wall or in a raised cabinet, away from sumps, floor drains and the water meter, and the battery backup is chosen to carry the equipment through a short outage. That is a placement decision, not a flood design, and for new construction near the canal it should be coordinated with the architect's design flood elevation from the start.

Rules and Approvals

Permits, Ratings, Landlords and Landmarks: What Governs a Gowanus Cabling Job

The rules around data cabling in New York City changed recently, and much of what is written online has not caught up. Local Law 128 of 2024 added a provision to the city's Administrative Code, section 28-105.4.7, stating that an electrical permit is not required for the installation, alteration, maintenance or repair of wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. It took effect with the 2025 New York City Electrical Code on December 21, 2025. For an ordinary Gowanus job, cable to desks, studios, registers and access points, there is no DOB electrical permit to file.

No permit does not mean no rules. Cable in a ceiling space that carries return air for the heating and cooling system has to be plenum rated. Cable that runs between floors in a riser or shaft needs at least a riser rating. Every hole made through a fire-rated wall or floor has to be sealed again with a listed firestop system, and that matters a great deal in the converted factories, where the stair walls and floors between tenants are often rated. Fire alarm wiring is a separate trade with its own licensing and filings and is not part of a data cabling job. And any new electrical outlet or circuit for a rack is electrical work for a licensed electrician.

Landlords, leases and the people who say yes

In Gowanus the customer and the building owner are often different people. A studio tenant, a shop owner or an office tenant needs the landlord's permission for any work outside their own space, and usually for some inside it. Commercial leases commonly say who may work in the building, what insurance they must carry, where cable may run and whether it must be removed at the end of the lease. We ask to see those clauses before we quote, because they change the route and sometimes the cable choice. A lease that requires removal at the end of the term, for example, argues for surface raceway and accessible routes rather than cable buried behind new drywall.

The new residential buildings add a management office with its own requirements, usually a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent, a description of the work and defined work hours. We send those before the visit. In buildings with a co-op or condo board, the board's alteration rules apply on top of the management company's, and we put together a short scope letter that explains exactly what will be drilled, where and how it will be sealed.

Landmarks and outside work

The Landmarks Preservation Commission has a say over changes to the outside of designated buildings, including conduit and equipment on a facade. Most Gowanus properties are not designated, but a few prominent ones are, and some adjacent blocks fall within historic districts that extend from Park Slope and Carroll Gardens. Where a job involves the outside of a building that may be covered, we check the designation first and plan the entrance so that conduit is concealed or placed where it can be approved, and we tell the owner what has to be filed before we promise a date.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Gowanus: Questions Answered

These are the situations Gowanus owners, tenants and businesses describe when they call: getting a line to the building at all, working inside factory buildings and studios, new buildings and commercial floors, and the construction going on around them. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Getting a line to the building

A provider told us it would have to open the street to reach our Gowanus building and sent a large construction estimate. Is there anything on our side that can shrink that job?

Sometimes, yes. A provider's estimate covers its plant outside the building, which we cannot change, but it also reflects how hard the building is to enter, and that part is yours. We find out how the provider reaches the neighbors, overhead, under the sidewalk or through an adjoining building, then prepare a single entrance on the side of your building nearest that plant, with a sleeve, a pull string and a backboard inside, and document it with photos for the provider. It will not always bring the number down, but it removes the building from the list of reasons the job is difficult, and it gives the owner a clean plan to send to every other provider as well.

Our side of the block has only old copper phone lines coming in. Should we put an empty conduit through the front wall before any fiber provider has agreed to serve us?

If you are already opening the wall or the sidewalk for another reason, it is worth it, because the conduit costs little and the next provider's technician will look for exactly that. If nothing is being opened, it is better to wait until a provider tells you where its plant will come from, since the right wall depends on that. The city's rezoning commitments include supporting broadband expansion in the Gowanus industrial area, so new providers may reach blocks that have had little choice so far. When one does, a prepared entrance and a dry spot inside for its equipment make the installation a short visit instead of a project.

The building's managed Wi-Fi is wired into every apartment of our new canal-side rental. Can a resident who works from home still get a wired connection at the desk?

Usually, yes, inside your own apartment. Most managed-Wi-Fi buildings give each unit a wall unit or an outlet that also has a wired port, and a single cable from that port to the desk, or a small switch feeding the desk and the television, solves most problems with video calls. What we will not do is add cable to the building's own network or its telecom room without the management company's approval. We check the lease and the building's rules first, keep the work inside the apartment and use surface raceway where opening the walls is not allowed.

Factory buildings and studios

The only route from the closet to our studio crosses three other tenants' studios. Do we need their permission, or only the landlord's?

Both, in practice. The landlord controls the building and must approve the route, but each tenant controls access to their own studio under their lease, and nobody wants a crew arriving unannounced over their workbench. We look first for a route that avoids other studios entirely, usually along the corridor ceiling and over the top of partitions that stop short of the ceiling. When there is no such route, we ask the landlord to arrange access with the other tenants, schedule the work around their hours, and keep everything we install in their space on the ceiling, out of reach and labeled so it is clearly not theirs to unplug.

Our print shop washes screens at a sink right where we want the computer. How close can a jack sit to a wet area?

Close enough to be useful, as long as it is above and away from the splash. We put the outlet high on the wall, in a box with a cover, fed from above so water cannot run down the cable into it, and route the cable away from the sink drain and the floor. If the computer has to sit near the sink, a short patch cord from a higher outlet is better than an outlet low on the wall. The switch and any power for it belong somewhere dry entirely, which in most shops means the office or a cabinet on a different wall.

Our recording studio has isolated walls. How does a network cable reach the live room without spoiling the isolation we paid for?

Through a planned penetration, not a new hole in the isolation. Many studios were built with a conduit or a cable pass for audio lines, and data cable can often share it with care, or it can go through a sealed acoustic pass-through sized for future cables. Where nothing was provided, we make one small sleeved opening at the point the studio's builder recommends, seal it with an acoustic sealant and leave spare capacity so the wall never has to be opened again. Inside the room the outlet goes where it will not rattle against anything and away from the microphone positions.

We edit video and move huge files between workstations and a storage server every day. What should the cabling between them look like?

Short, fast and direct. For editing over the network, each workstation should have a cable rated for 10-gigabit speeds back to a switch that supports them, and the storage server should sit close to that switch, ideally on the same rack. Cat6A handles 10-gigabit speeds over a full-length run; ordinary Cat6 only does so over shorter distances, so on a long Gowanus floor we specify Cat6A or fiber for those links. Everything else in the studio, from the printer to the guest Wi-Fi, can share ordinary connections without slowing the edit bays down.

Shops and light industry

Our workshop has a showroom at the front and benches and machines at the back. Should the two halves of the business be on separate networks?

Yes, at least logically, and ideally with separate cable runs as well. The showroom has a payment terminal, guest Wi-Fi and perhaps a display, all of which customers touch or connect to. The workshop has design computers, files and machine controllers that should never be visible from a customer's phone. We cable each area to its own ports on the switch and set them up as separate networks, so a problem or a visitor in one half cannot reach the other. The cable route also keeps the showroom tidy, usually entering from the ceiling at the wall rather than across the floor.

We run a café in the ground floor of a new rental tower, and the building's telecom room belongs to the developer. Can our shop have its own provider line?

In most new buildings, yes, but the route is the developer's decision. A commercial tenant usually has its own service from its own provider, and the building's property team decides how that line reaches your space: through the main telecom room, through a sleeve already provided to the retail unit, or through a new entrance. We meet the property team, find out what was built for retail tenants, and run your line from the approved handoff point into a cabinet inside your space, keeping your equipment and the residential network completely apart.

Our Fourth Avenue office building has old floor ducts full of dead telephone wire. Can new data cable use those ducts?

Often it can, once the dead wire comes out. Underfloor ducts were built to bring phone lines to desks across open floors, and they are a good path for data cable if they are dry, clear and intact. We test-pull a string through each section, remove abandoned cable where it can be pulled cleanly, and check the junction boxes for water or debris. If a section is crushed or blocked, we bring that part of the floor in from the ceiling instead, and the quote says which desks are fed which way.

Construction and change around you

The city is replacing sewers on our street and a tower is going up next door. Should we time a new service entrance to that work?

If you can, yes. When the rezoning was approved, the city committed about $174 million to sewer upgrades in the area, and new buildings mean sidewalks and curbs are being rebuilt across the neighborhood. If your sidewalk is going to be opened anyway, it is the cheapest moment a provider or your contractor will ever have to lay conduit to your building. Ask the contractor working on the street or the site who to talk to, and let us know the dates; we can have the inside of the entrance ready so that only the outside piece remains.

Our old industrial building is being converted to apartments under the rezoning. What cabling should go in while the floors are stripped?

The backbone, the risers and the empty pathways that will be hard to add later. While the floors and walls are open, we install a sleeve or conduit from the provider's entrance to a telecom room above the flood line, riser pathways with spare capacity to every floor, and a conduit or cable to each apartment's media panel. Inside each unit, a cable to the living room, each bedroom and any likely office corner costs little during construction and a great deal after. Ground-floor commercial spaces get their own sleeve to the entrance so a future tenant can bring its own provider in without touching the residential network.

Our bar's back room hosts shows on weekends with a DJ, a projector and a merch table. Can network connections be added there without cords getting kicked loose?

Yes, by keeping the cables off the floor and the outlets where the action is not. We bring cable down from the ceiling to outlets at the DJ booth, the projector position and the merch table, placed high enough to be out of the way, and use a protected floor box only where nothing else will do. Each outlet goes back to its own switch port, so a payment terminal at the merch table keeps working even if the DJ's gear misbehaves, and the projector can stay connected all week without a cable taped across the dance floor.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Gowanus

Ask an AI assistant or a cost-guide site about network cabling in Brooklyn and you will get confident, general answers. Some of them are right in general and wrong in Gowanus. These are the seven we hear most from customers, and what the buildings here actually show.

"Any building in Brooklyn can get fast internet; you just pick a provider"

This is the assumption behind most advice about home and office networks, and in much of Brooklyn it holds. Gowanus as a whole tests well: Untapped Cities placed it seventh in the borough for speed, and lists several providers serving the area.

But averages hide individual buildings. In 2018 a business on Union Street had been offered 10 Mbps and was told by the only high-speed provider willing to look that extending service would cost $138,963.96 because of street cutting. As Common Cause New York's Susan Lerner told Gothamist, providers "don't have the obligation to provide the final 100 yards of infrastructure."

The practical correction is to check the building before planning the network. Which providers reach the block, how they reach it, and where they would enter decide whether a cabling job starts with an entrance or goes straight to the inside work. We look at that on the first visit.

"Low-voltage cabling in New York City needs a permit"

Many cabling guides still say this, and some list skipping low-voltage permits as a common mistake. That advice is out of date for data cabling. Local Law 128 of 2024 states that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, effective December 21, 2025.

The opposite error is just as common: reading that as "no rules." Plenum ratings in air-handling ceilings, riser ratings between floors and firestop at every rated wall and floor still apply, and the converted factories of Gowanus are full of rated stairs and tenant separations. Fire alarm wiring remains a separate licensed trade.

So the honest answer is that a Gowanus data job usually involves no DOB electrical filing, but it still has to be built to code, and the landlord, managing agent or board will usually have its own approvals.

"Open ceilings make cabling easy"

It is true that an exposed ceiling saves fishing cable through walls. AI answers tend to stop there, as if a loft or factory ceiling were a blank shelf.

In a Gowanus factory building, that ceiling carries sprinkler mains, electrical conduit, ducts and the abandoned cable of every earlier tenant. Data cable cannot be tied to sprinkler pipe or conduit, it has to stay clear of power and machinery, and on a 15-foot or higher ceiling it is installed from a lift. Every inch of it is also visible, so it needs to look like it was meant to be there.

An open ceiling makes cabling faster when there is a planned lane and proper supports. Without them it produces the tangle that the next tenant pays to remove.

"A national price range tells you what your network will cost"

Cost guides publish broad ranges. HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn figures for network and router services, for example, give an average of $462 and a range of $90 to $1,500, and AI answers often repeat numbers like these as if they described a cabling job.

Those figures mix router setups, small repairs and wiring of every kind. They say nothing about a Gowanus job, where the variables are whether the building needs an entrance, whether the ceiling is open or finished, how long the floor is, how many studios or devices need a cable and whether a lift is needed.

The only number worth planning around is a written quote from someone who has walked your route. Ours lists every outlet and the route floor by floor, so you can compare it line by line with anyone else's.

"In a new building, the developer has already done the wiring"

Partly true. Many rezoning-era buildings come with a provider's fiber and managed Wi-Fi throughout; Brooklyn Fiber lists 340 Nevins Street and 251 Douglass Street that way, and Union Channel advertised high-speed internet throughout the building.

What is built in is the building's network, sized for residents sharing it. A resident who works from home, a ground-floor café, a fitness studio or an office tenant may need wiring that the building does not provide: a wired desk, separate business service, cameras and registers on their own network.

In those buildings, the work is about connecting your space properly to what the developer built, with the management office's approval, not about replacing it.

"Wi-Fi is good enough now, so cable is obsolete"

Modern Wi-Fi is excellent, and for phones and laptops in a small space it is the right answer. AI answers often extend that to every building and every device.

Wi-Fi access points themselves need a cable to work well, and on a long Gowanus warehouse floor or a studio building with thick brick walls, a wireless signal does not travel far. Registers, editing workstations, storage servers, cameras and door stations all do better on cable, and a shared studio building full of competing networks is the hardest environment for wireless there is.

The right design in most Gowanus commercial spaces is cable to every fixed device and to ceiling access points, with Wi-Fi for everything that moves.

"Put the network equipment in the basement, out of the way"

For a typical Brooklyn row house or apartment building, AI answers usually suggest the cellar for the router, switch and backup, because that is where the provider's line arrives.

Gowanus is low ground beside a canal that overflowed into streets and basements during Hurricane Sandy, and the Bond Street rentals were designed with no cellar for that reason. A switch and battery backup on a cellar floor are the first things to fail when water comes in.

We bring the provider's line up from the entrance to a cabinet on the ground floor or higher wherever the building allows, and when the cellar is the only option, we mount the equipment high and away from drains and sumps.

FAQ

Gowanus Structured Cabling FAQ

Who owns the cabling we paid for in our leased Gowanus studio once the lease ends?
+

Your lease decides. Many commercial leases say that anything fixed to the building becomes the landlord's at the end of the term, and some require the tenant to remove it instead. Read the alterations and surrender clauses before the work starts, and tell us what they say, so the route and the cable can be planned either to stay behind cleanly or to come out without damage.

Can you cable an occupied studio floor one section at a time so the other tenants keep working?
+

Yes. On a shared floor we usually install the corridor supports and the closet panel first, then drop cable into studios a few at a time on days the landlord has arranged with each tenant. Each studio is connected and tested before we move on, so nobody is left without a working connection while the rest of the floor is finished.

Can you run a cable up to our roof for a NYC Mesh node or another rooftop antenna?
+

Yes, with the owner's approval for the roof. We bring an outdoor-rated cable from the equipment room up an existing shaft or along a protected route to the antenna position, seal the roof or wall penetration, and ground the run properly. The antenna installation and the service itself belong to the network or provider you are joining; our part is the cable and the pathway.

Can the same cable you install also power our cameras, access points and door station?
+

Yes. Most network cameras, ceiling access points and many door stations take their power over the network cable from a switch that supplies it, so no separate outlet is needed at the device. We size the switch for the total power those devices draw and choose Cat6A for the higher-power ones, and the switch goes on battery backup so cameras keep recording through a short outage.

Our warehouse floor is longer than one copper cable can reach. How is the far end connected?
+

A copper link is limited to 100 meters including patch cords. Beyond that we either place a small switch in a protected cabinet partway along the floor, fed by a backbone cable, or run fiber from the main rack to the far end and convert to copper there. Fiber is also the better choice when the run passes large motors or welding bays.

Will the jack labels match the studio or suite numbers our building already uses?
+

Yes, and we ask the building manager which numbers are official before we print anything, because tenants often use their own. Each outlet and its patch panel port carry the same label, the port map ties every label to a studio, suite or device, and the landlord gets a copy, so a new tenant's connection can be found without calling us.

What should we look for when we read the certification report you hand over?
+

A result for every link, matching the labels on your port map, with a pass against the standard for the cable category installed. The report also shows each link's length, which tells you how much distance remains for future changes. If any link was retested after a repair, the final result is the one that counts, and we note why it was retested.

Can one rack hold both our office network and the building's camera recorder?
+

It can, if the people who manage each agree on who has access. Many small Gowanus buildings share one rack between the landlord's cameras and a tenant's network to save space. We put each on separate panels and switches, label them clearly, and recommend a lockable rack with keys held by both parties, so neither has to touch the other's equipment.

Is Gowanus billed at a different rate from the rest of Brooklyn?
+

No. Gowanus is billed at our Brooklyn base rate, the same as every other Brooklyn neighborhood. What changes the total is the building, such as ceiling height, distance, whether an entrance is needed and how many outlets are pulled at once, and the written quote shows each of those so you can see where the cost comes from.

Can a new tenant reuse the cable a previous tenant left in our studio?
+

Sometimes. We tone out each abandoned cable to find where it ends, test it, and reuse it if it passes and runs where you need it. Cable that fails, has been cut short or follows a route that blocks the new layout is better removed, so the ceiling does not keep collecting dead wire for the next tenant to puzzle over.

How much notice do you need before an Open Studios weekend or a show opening?
+

As much as you can give. A few outlets in a single studio can often be scheduled within days, but a whole floor, a new entrance or anything that needs landlord approval and other tenants' access takes longer to arrange. Tell us the date you need to be finished when you first call, and the quote will say whether it can be met.

Do you also install cameras, door entry or access control over the same cabling?
+

Yes. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems installs cameras, intercoms and access control as well as cabling, so the same survey can plan all of them together. Cable for a camera or door station can follow the routes we build for the data network, and the rack and battery backup can be sized for everything from the start rather than added piece by piece.
