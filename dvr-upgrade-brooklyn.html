The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Properties Need a DVR Upgrade

Brooklyn's housing is old, small-scale and mostly rented, and that combination decides how a camera system ages. The Census Bureau's 2023 five-year estimates count 1,090,187 homes in Kings County with a median year built of 1943; 513,629 of them — almost half — are in buildings put up in 1939 or earlier. Another 175,805 are in two-unit buildings and 170,655 in three- and four-unit buildings, and renters occupy roughly seven homes in ten. Translate that into what we see on a service call: a two- or three-family house or a rowhouse with a landlord on one floor and tenants on the others, a camera system put in ten or fifteen years ago, and a recorder that was tucked wherever there was an outlet and a shelf — usually the cellar.

That cellar recorder is the weak point. Hard drives in these boxes run around the clock, in heat and damp, often next to a boiler. Many were set up once and never updated, with the default password still on them and remote access opened through the router so the owner could watch from a phone. Security researchers keep finding cheap recorders like these on the open internet: FortiGuard Labs issued an outbreak alert in June 2025 because botnets including Mirai were exploiting a command-injection flaw in one family of DVRs for which, it said, no vendor patch was available, and its advice was to isolate or replace the devices. A recorder can keep showing a live picture on the monitor long after its drive has stopped saving anything — owners usually find out the day they need footage and discover there is none.

The other half of the Brooklyn picture is what the footage is for. Storefronts on Fulton, Flatbush, Nostrand, Pitkin and Fifth Avenue get asked for clips by precinct detectives after robberies; in May 2026 NY1 carried NYPD surveillance footage of a Brooklyn bodega robbery in which, police said, a second man had helped, and a widely shared clip from a Bed-Stuy deli showed four armed men in jackets printed “NYPD” zip-tying three people before leaving with a bag of lottery tickets. Homeowners face a quieter problem: News 12 Brooklyn, citing the city's Department of Transportation, reported in December 2025 that about 90,000 packages are stolen in New York City every day, and the stoop is where most Brooklyn deliveries are left. In both cases the question is the same: is the recorder actually keeping a picture sharp enough to use, and can you get it out quickly?

An upgrade answers that without tearing into a house. Most analog systems in Brooklyn run on coaxial cable, and a modern hybrid recorder can push high-definition video over the same coax that carried the old blurry signal. So we replace the recorder, test each cable run, re-terminate the bad ends, swap only the cameras that are past it, and leave the plaster, the brownstone face and the tin ceilings alone. Landlords who upgrade the recorder often ask us next about the front door, and a recorder swap is the right moment to add a video intercom for the building entrance on the same cabling plan.

Cellar Recorder Beeping, Frozen or Dark?

A DVR that beeps every few minutes, a screen of “video loss” tiles, a store recorder that froze the night of a break-in — call (347) 934-8335. We book the next two-hour window, and most Brooklyn systems are recording again within one to two hours of arrival, even if the permanent fix is a recorder swap scheduled for later. If the problem is only the phone app, start with camera repair in Brooklyn or the app support pages listed further down.

Five checks to run on an old recorder tonight

  1. Play back last night, not live view. Pick the camera you care about most, go to playback and choose 2 AM yesterday. If the timeline is empty or the clip stutters, the recorder is not saving properly, whatever the live screen shows.
  2. Look at the date and time on the screen. A clock that is hours or years out usually means the recorder lost power and its settings at some point; footage stamped with the wrong time is hard to use and easy to challenge.
  3. Open the storage or HDD page. Most recorders show the drive status as normal, error, sleeping or not found, and how full it is. “Error” or “not found” means nothing is being kept; a drive that never fills may not be writing at all.
  4. Count the tiles that say video loss. One dark tile is usually a camera, its power or its connector. Several dark tiles on the same floor or the same side of the building point to a cut bundle, often from renovation work.
  5. Check how your phone connects. If an installer once told you to open a port on the router, or your phone app connects by an IP address and port number, the recorder is reachable from the internet. Unplug its network cable until it can be updated or replaced; it keeps recording locally while unplugged.

Write down what you find, or take photos of each screen, and send them with your request. Those five answers tell us most of what we need to price the upgrade, and they often show whether the recorder can be rescued with a new drive or needs to be replaced.

Five- and six-story tenement and later walk-ups in Williamsburg, Greenpoint, Sunset Park and Crown Heights, with cameras in the vestibule, the stairwells and the rear yard, and coax running up the stair hall in surface raceway.

How each building type changes the upgrade

Brownstones and rowhouses. Twenty-foot lots mean party walls on both sides and almost no way to run a new cable outside without it showing. The coax that is already there is valuable, so we test it before touching anything. Runs typically drop from a front camera at the parlor-floor lintel through the vestibule closet to the cellar, and from a rear-yard camera down the back wall. If a run is dead, the first choice is to pull a new cable using the old one as a pull string; the second is a network camera fed by a coax-to-Ethernet adapter on a different surviving run; opening plaster is last.

Two- and three-family houses. The practical question here is access. If the recorder sits in a tenant's closet or a basement apartment, every service visit needs that tenant's cooperation. During an upgrade we usually move the recorder to space the owner controls — the owner's own floor or a locked utility closet — and extend the coax with proper couplers or bring the runs to the new spot through the cellar ceiling. We also set up separate user accounts so an upstairs tenant can be given the front-door camera alone.

Mixed-use corner buildings. These systems grew one camera at a time, so the recorder often has a mix of channels: two good HD-over-coax cameras at the register, three tired analog ones on the stockroom and the residential hall. A hybrid recorder takes all of them. We separate the store's cameras from the apartment hallway in the user permissions, so a store manager does not see residents coming and going, and the owner sees everything.

Walk-ups. Coax in walk-ups usually runs up the stair hall in surface raceway, which makes it easy to inspect and easy to damage. Painters, plumbers and tenants moving furniture crush it; mice chew the jacket where it passes through the cellar. We walk each run floor by floor, replace damaged sections in new raceway, and label both ends so the next technician does not have to guess which tile is the third-floor landing.

Lofts and converted industrial space. Long open floors push analog cable to its limits. Dahua's own data sheets for its HD-over-coax recorders quote transmission up to 700 metres for 4K or 4MP and 800 metres for 1080p over coax, so length is rarely the problem in a loft; splices, cheap connectors and power drop are. We check voltage at the far camera and replace undersized power runs, which cure a surprising number of “night picture goes black” complaints.

Southern Brooklyn attached homes. The city Health Department's flood vulnerability index singles out southern Brooklyn for storm-surge risk, and Sandy's toll fell heavily on the coast: 23 percent of the city's Sandy injury deaths were in Brooklyn. Near the water in Canarsie, Sheepshead Bay, Gerritsen Beach or Coney Island we plan as if the cellar will flood. On these jobs the recorder goes up into the living space, the coax is brought up through a sealed sleeve, and anything electrical that must stay below grade goes high on the wall rather than on the floor.

Rules, credits and paperwork for Brooklyn camera work

  • New York's commercial security tax credit. The state program, created in the FY2025 budget, offers up to $3,000 per retail location for businesses with 50 or fewer employees that spend over a threshold on theft prevention — $4,000 per location for the smallest businesses and $6,000 for those with 26 to 50 employees, according to the city's MyCity Business guide. Surveillance cameras and alarm systems are listed as covered expenses, and the guide says applications for the certificate are due by October 31 each year. We itemise invoices so your accountant can see what was cameras, recording and labor; whether you qualify is for you and your accountant to confirm.
  • Landmarked facades. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook lets staff approve security cameras on designated buildings when they are well spaced — no more than two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on small residential or commercial buildings, three on large ones — attached at mortar joints or plain masonry rather than cast iron or decorative surfaces, with conduit concealed or limited to about ten inches of painted, visible run.
  • Video only, by default. New York's eavesdropping law makes recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party, by someone not present, a felony. We install video-only and leave microphones off unless a business has a clear reason, posts notice and decides with its own counsel.
  • No cameras where people undress. State law on unlawful surveillance covers places where a person would reasonably expect to be able to fully disrobe in privacy. Restrooms, changing areas and anything aimed into them are off the table on every job.
  • Neighbors' yards. Section 52-a of the Civil Rights Law gives an owner or tenant a right to sue over a video device installed on adjoining property for the purpose of recording their backyard without written consent. On rowhouse blocks, where yards touch on three sides, we aim and mask rear cameras to your own lot.
  • Licensing and insurance. AESS works under New York State low-voltage license #12000287431 and carries liability insurance on every Brooklyn job; we issue certificates in the wording a landlord, managing agent or co-op board asks for.
Your Questions Answered

DVR Upgrades in Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the questions Brooklyn owners, landlords and store operators put to us about their recorders, grouped by the problem behind them: where the box lives, the cable already in the walls, storefront footage, shared buildings, landmarked fronts and the internet side. Each answer is the one we give on the phone.

Cellars, Closets and Where the Recorder Lives

Our cellar took water during Ida but the old recorder still powers on — can we trust its drive?

Not without checking it, and we would not trust it for evidence until it has been checked. When Ida came through in September 2021, rain fell at up to 3.5 inches an hour against a sewer design capacity of 1.75, by the city Health Department's account, and the flooding hit low-lying areas and basements hardest. A recorder that sat in standing water, or even in a cellar that stayed damp for days afterwards, can power on and show live video while its drive is quietly failing and the contacts on its board are corroding. We open the recorder's storage page and read the drive's health status, play back footage from the weeks after the flood to see whether it was actually saving, and look inside for corrosion on the board and the power connector. At the very least the drive is replaced. If the board shows corrosion, the recorder is replaced, and in either case the new one goes up on a high shelf or a floor above, with the coax extended to reach it.

The recorder in our Flatbush two-family is in the downstairs tenant's apartment — how do we move it without losing the cameras?

By bringing the cable to a new home rather than replacing it. In most two-family houses the coax from each camera ends at the recorder in one bundle, so we trace the bundle back to where it enters the apartment, usually through the cellar ceiling or a closet wall. From there we either extend each run with proper coax couplers to a locked closet or the owner's floor, or, where the bundle passes through the cellar, cut in a small junction point and run fresh coax up to the new location. We schedule that part of the work with the tenant once, label every run, and test each camera on the new recorder before we leave. Afterwards nobody needs to enter the tenant's apartment to reboot the recorder or pull footage, which is better for the tenant's privacy as much as for yours.

Can the new recorder go in a top-floor closet when every coax run ends in the cellar?

Usually yes, though it is more work than a swap in place. Analog HD video travels a long way over coax — Dahua's HD-over-coax data sheets quote 700 metres for 4K and 4MP — so the extra three or four storeys of a rowhouse are not a picture-quality problem. The work is in the route: we look for an existing chase from cellar to roof, such as an old dumbwaiter shaft, a disused chimney flue, a plumbing chase or the closet stack, and pull a bundle of new coax up it. Where there is no clean route, a different approach is to leave a small, inexpensive recorder in the cellar and add a second recorder upstairs, or to convert the most important runs to network cameras with coax-to-Ethernet adapters and bring a single network cable upstairs. We price both options after we have looked at the building.

Coax That Was Fished Through Plaster and Brick

The coax in our Park Slope rowhouse was fished down a chimney chase twenty years ago — can a new recorder use it?

If it tests clean, yes. Age by itself does not ruin coaxial cable inside a wall; what ruins it is water, heat, kinks and bad connectors. A run fished through a dry chimney chase may be in better shape than one that crosses a damp cellar. We test each run before deciding anything: continuity on the centre conductor and the shield, a check for shorts, and then a live picture from a modern HD-over-coax camera on a new recorder. If the HD picture is clean at night as well as by day, the cable stays. If it shows rolling bars, ghosting or drops out, we cut back and re-terminate first, because most failures are within a foot of either end. Only a run that still fails after that is replaced, and in a rowhouse the old cable becomes the pull string for the new one.

A contractor's gut renovation on our parlor floor cut some camera runs — how do you find which ones are dead?

We find them from both ends and then from the middle. At the recorder, each channel that shows “video loss” or a snowy picture gets a label. At each camera, we check whether the camera has power and is putting out a signal by plugging a test monitor straight into it on a ladder. If the camera is fine and the recorder sees nothing, the break is in the wall between them. A tone generator on the cable at the recorder end and a probe moved along the walls and ceilings of the renovated floor narrows down where the signal stops — often a run that was cut where new framing went in, or buried behind fresh drywall with a nail through it. Then we decide, with you, whether to open a small patch at that spot, re-route around the renovated rooms, or replace that camera with a network or wireless one. Renovation damage is the single most common reason a Brooklyn system loses cameras.

Is the thin all-in-one cable the last installer stapled along our party wall good enough for HD?

Sometimes, and it is worth knowing which kind you have. Many 2000s and 2010s kits came with premade cables that carry video and power together in one thin jacket, and many installers used “Siamese” cable, which is RG59 coax and a pair of power wires moulded side by side. Siamese cable with a solid copper centre conductor handles HD-over-coax well. The thin premade cables and coax with a copper-clad aluminium centre are where trouble starts on longer runs, and camera-retailer guides typically rate premade cable for much shorter distances than coax from a roll. On a 20-foot-wide rowhouse most runs are short enough that even the cheap cable may work, so we test rather than assume. Where staples have pinched the cable against a party wall, we replace the crushed section; a pinched coax shows up as ghosting that no recorder can fix.

Bodegas, Delis and Storefront Recorders

The recorder behind our bodega counter keeps rebooting — is it the hard drive or the power?

It is usually one of three things, and they can be told apart. A failing hard drive tends to show up first as a recorder that takes ages to start, a drive error or “HDD abnormal” warning, gaps in the playback timeline and, eventually, reboots when the recorder tries to write to a bad sector. A power problem looks different: reboots when the cooler, the slicer or the coffee machine kicks on, a power brick that is hot to the touch, or a recorder on the same power strip as a space heater. Heat is the third: recorders crammed into a cubby above a register with no airflow shut down to protect themselves. We check the drive's health log, measure the supply, and look at where the box sits. Often the fix is a new drive and a small battery backup; if the recorder itself is past it, we swap it on the spot so the store is never left without recording overnight.

Our register camera faces the glass storefront and every afternoon clip is a dark silhouette — can the upgrade fix the backlight?

Partly, and the camera matters more than the recorder for this one. A camera looking from inside a store toward a sunny window or a glass door sees a bright background and a dark person in front of it, and older analog cameras simply cannot handle that range of light. Newer HD-over-coax cameras with true wide dynamic range balance the bright and dark parts of the scene far better, and they plug into the same cable, so swapping that one camera during the recorder upgrade usually turns a silhouette into a face. Placement helps as much: we move the door camera so it looks across or back toward the counter rather than straight out at the street, and add a second camera under the awning outside, looking at the entrance, where the light falls on faces instead of behind them. The recorder's part is to store that camera at its full resolution and a high enough bit rate that the detail survives.

Can one recorder cover our ground-floor store and the apartments upstairs in a mixed-use building on Nostrand Avenue?

Yes, and in most mixed-use buildings it already does — the question is who gets to see what. A single 16-channel hybrid recorder can take the store's register, door and stockroom cameras plus the residential entrance, hallways and rear yard. What changes with an upgrade is the user setup. We create a store account that sees only the store cameras, a building account for the residential entrance and halls, and an owner account that sees everything. If a store tenant, not the owner, runs the shop, that matters: it keeps a store manager from watching residents come and go, and it keeps tenants out of the store's footage. Splitting the system across two recorders is also possible when the store and the building have different owners or different leases, and we will recommend that where the paperwork calls for it.

Does New York's commercial security tax credit cover a recorder upgrade for a small Brooklyn store?

It may, and it is worth checking with your accountant before the deadline. The state's commercial security tax credit, created in the 2024–25 budget, offers up to $3,000 per retail location for businesses with 50 or fewer employees, and the city's MyCity Business guide lists surveillance cameras and alarm systems among covered expenses. The same guide sets spending thresholds — more than $4,000 at one location for the smallest businesses and $6,000 for businesses with 26 to 50 employees — and says applications for the certificate are due by October 31 each year, with the credit then claimed on the state tax return. A recorder swap on its own may fall short of the threshold, so owners often combine it with new door and register cameras or an alarm. We itemise every invoice by equipment and labor; eligibility is between you, your accountant and the state.

Landlords, Tenants and Shared Brooklyn Buildings

As the landlord of a three-family in Bushwick, can I let tenants see the front-door camera without giving them the whole recorder?

Yes. Every recorder we install supports multiple user accounts with different permissions, and that is the right way to share. We create a tenant account that can see live video from the front door, and if you want, the mailbox area, but not the yard, the cellar or your own entrance, and that cannot play back or export recordings or change settings. Each tenant gets the account on their own phone through the recorder maker's app. When someone moves out, you delete or change that one account; nobody else is affected and the administrator password never leaves your hands. A common Brooklyn arrangement is to give tenants the entrance live view so they can see who is ringing and whether a package has arrived, and to keep recorded footage in the owner's account for when something actually happens.

Does New York's backyard-camera law apply to a camera on our own rowhouse's rear wall?

It can, depending on what the camera is aimed at and why. Section 52-a of the state Civil Rights Law gives an owner or tenant of a home a right to sue anyone who installs a video device on adjoining property for the purpose of recording what happens in their backyard without written consent, and it defines the backyard as the area beyond the rear footprint of the house. On a Brooklyn rowhouse block the yards behind the houses touch on three sides, so a wide camera mounted high on your rear wall will see over the fences unless it is set up not to. We aim rear cameras down and along your own yard, use the recorder's privacy masking to black out any part of the frame that falls on a neighbor's garden or windows, and keep a screenshot of the masked view so you can show a neighbor exactly what is recorded. If a dispute has already started, talk to a lawyer; we can provide the layout and the settings.

Can we record sound on the hallway cameras of our walk-up?

We recommend against it, and by default we don't. New York's eavesdropping law treats the recording of a conversation, without the consent of at least one party, by someone who is not part of it, as a felony. A microphone in a shared hallway records tenants and visitors talking to each other, and the landlord who owns the recorder is not part of those conversations. Video of a common hallway is a different matter; it is standard in Brooklyn walk-ups and it is what most insurers and managing agents expect. When we upgrade an older system, we check whether any camera has a built-in microphone switched on and turn the audio channel off in the recorder. If you have a specific reason to want audio somewhere, such as a store counter where you are a party to the conversation, decide that with a lawyer first and post clear notice.

Who should hold the admin password on a brownstone owned by two siblings or an LLC?

One named person, with a sealed backup. The administrator account on a recorder can delete footage, change who can see what and reset everything, so it should not be shared casually. For a brownstone held by family members or a small LLC, we set up the recorder with one administrator, normally the person who manages the building day to day, and give each other owner a full-viewing account of their own. The admin password is written down once and kept somewhere all the owners agree on, such as with the building's papers or the family's lawyer. We also record the recorder's model and serial number and the maker's password-reset route, because if the only person who knows the password becomes unavailable, recovering it can mean contacting the manufacturer with proof of ownership. When ownership changes, the admin password changes the same day.

Landmarked Blocks and What Shows From the Street

We're in a Brooklyn historic district — do we need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval to put a new camera on the front?

For new equipment on a visible facade, plan on it. The Landmarks Preservation Commission regulates work on designated buildings and in historic districts, and its permit guidebook has a section on security cameras that LPC staff can approve by permit when an installation meets set criteria. A pure recorder swap inside the cellar does not touch the facade at all. Where the upgrade includes a new or relocated front camera, we design it to the staff-level criteria from the start — small, discreet, fixed into mortar joints, wiring concealed — and give you photos, a mark-up and the product data sheet for the application. Rear facades that cannot be seen from a public street are generally treated more leniently than the front, but the guidebook is the authority, and we check it for each address.

How many cameras can go on a 20-foot-wide landmarked rowhouse front?

Under the Landmarks Preservation Commission's staff-level criteria, two. The guidebook says security cameras must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered look, and allows no more than two cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage on small residential or commercial buildings, and no more than three per 25 feet on large ones. A typical Brooklyn rowhouse is narrower than 25 feet, so two is the working limit for the front: in practice one camera covering the stoop and the parlor-floor door, and one covering the garden-level door under the stoop, which the guidebook names as one of the discreet places equipment can go. If the old system has three or four cameras bolted across the front, an upgrade is a chance to bring it within the rules, often by replacing two fixed cameras with one wider-angle HD camera that sees more than both did.

Where do the new camera cables go so they don't run down the brownstone face?

Through the wall right behind the camera, if at all possible. The LPC guidebook asks that conduit be concealed by passing through plain masonry or cladding behind the installation; where that isn't possible, staff may approve about ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match and secured into mortar joints rather than across decorative surfaces. That fits how we like to work anyway. On a brownstone or brick front we drill through a mortar joint directly behind the camera base, never through carved stone, terracotta or cast iron, sleeve the hole and seal it, and run the coax inside to the nearest closet or the vestibule. When the old installer ran cable down the outside of the facade, an upgrade is the moment to pull it off, patch the anchor holes in the joints and reroute the cable inside, which both looks better and protects it from weather.

Old Boxes, Old Apps and the Internet Side

The previous owner left a no-name recorder in the cellar — is it one of the models botnets go after?

It may well be, and you cannot tell by looking at it. The recorders most often hijacked are inexpensive, white-label boxes sold under many brand names, running old firmware, with remote access opened through the router. FortiGuard Labs' June 2025 outbreak alert described Mirai and other botnets exploiting a command-injection flaw in one family of DVRs, said no vendor patch was available, and advised owners to isolate or replace the devices. Big-name recorders are not immune either: a 2021 Hikvision flaw, CVE-2021-36260, is on the federal Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency's list of known exploited vulnerabilities. With an inherited recorder whose password, maker and update history are unknown, the safe course is to disconnect it from the internet today, and to replace it rather than try to patch it. Its cameras and coax can almost always be reused.

Should the upgraded recorder be reachable from the internet at all, or only through the app?

Only through the app or a private connection, never through an open port. Many older Brooklyn systems were set up for phone viewing by forwarding a port on the router straight to the recorder, which lets anyone on the internet knock on the recorder's login page. After an upgrade, phone viewing goes through the recorder maker's cloud relay, which the recorder connects out to, or through a VPN on your router if you prefer to avoid the cloud entirely. We close any forwarded ports and switch off UPnP so the recorder cannot open them again by itself, change every default password, put the recorder on the latest firmware and turn on automatic update checks where the model supports them. If you manage several buildings, a VPN gives one secure way into all of them, and we can set that up on a business router.

The router is on the top floor of our brownstone and the recorder is in the cellar — how does the recorder get online?

With a cable if there is any route at all, and with something better than Wi-Fi if there isn't. A recorder needs a steady connection to send video to your phone, and Wi-Fi from a top-floor router through three floors of brick and plaster to a cellar rarely provides one. Our first look is for an existing network cable or a spare coax run between the floors: a single unused coax can carry a network connection with a pair of coax-to-Ethernet adapters, and many brownstones have an old cable-TV coax that does the job. If there is nothing, we pull one network cable up the same chase the camera coax uses. Powerline adapters are a last resort in old wiring. Recording itself never depends on the internet, so even while we sort the connection out, the cameras keep recording to the drive.

Hybrid recorder or a full move to network cameras: how we decide in a Brooklyn building

The cable decides first. If most coax runs test clean and the walls are plaster, brick or finished woodwork, a hybrid recorder on the existing cable is almost always the better value: the picture improves dramatically with HD-over-coax cameras, nothing is opened, and the recorder still accepts network cameras later. If most runs fail, or the cable is the thin premade kind and the building is being renovated anyway, new network cable and an NVR make more sense, because the walls are open and the labor of pulling cable is already being paid for.

The cameras decide second. Analog cameras from the late 2000s that give a soft, grainy picture can be swapped one by one for HD-over-coax models on the same cable. If you want features that live in network cameras — built-in people and vehicle detection, very high resolutions at the door, or a camera that also works as an intercom — those cameras can be added to a hybrid recorder on a network run or over a coax-to-Ethernet adapter, without moving the whole system at once.

The building decides third. A brownstone with four to eight cameras and a family who wants sharp footage and a working app is a hybrid job. A walk-up where the landlord wants every hallway, a camera at each stair and a separate view for each tenant often outgrows a 16-channel coax recorder, and a planned move to network cameras floor by floor is worth pricing. A store usually sits in between: a hybrid recorder now, with one or two network cameras at the door and the register where detail matters most. We put both options on paper when they are close, so the decision is yours and not the installer's.

ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe compared with an upgraded recorder

Brooklyn owners with a tired DVR often ask whether to scrap it and switch to a consumer brand instead. Ring and SimpliSafe cameras are mostly Wi-Fi or battery devices that store their history in the company's cloud on a monthly plan, and they need Wi-Fi to reach every camera — which, in a brick rowhouse with a cellar and a rear yard, is often the hard part. ADT-style systems are built around a monitoring contract and sensors, with cameras added on. An upgraded wired recorder keeps recording to a drive in your building whatever the internet is doing, costs nothing per month, and reuses the coax already in your walls. Plenty of our customers keep a monitored alarm or a video doorbell and upgrade the recorder alongside it; the two do different jobs well.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About DVR Upgrades in Brooklyn

We could not capture a live Google AI Overview from our build environment, so each claim below is one that cost guides, retailers and forum threads publish for the same searches — the material AI summaries are assembled from. Each is followed by what we find in Brooklyn cellars, storefronts and walk-ups.

“Replacing a DVR is a $100 job — you just plug the new one in”

Cost threads and installer forums repeat a version of this. In one low-voltage installers' group, a do-it-yourself homeowner wrote that he would pay about $500 to swap eight cameras and a DVR — $50 a camera and $100 for the recorder. Security.org, quoting HomeAdvisor, puts professional installation of two cameras at $100 to $200 on average.

Those figures describe moving boxes, not making a system work. A recorder swap in a Brooklyn rowhouse means testing every run, re-terminating corroded BNC plugs, finding the run a renovation cut, setting the right input format per channel, sizing the drive, closing the router ports the old installer opened and setting up each user on each phone.

Our published Brooklyn range for a four-channel swap on existing coax is $450 to $700 and for an eight-channel swap $650 to $1,000, with the price written down after we have seen the recorder and tested the cable. If a quote is far below that, ask which of those steps it leaves out.

“Old coax can't carry HD — you have to rewire for IP cameras”

Plenty of articles and sales pages say an upgrade means new network cable everywhere. In a house with open walls that is a reasonable choice. In a brownstone with plaster, tin ceilings and finished woodwork, it is the most expensive choice and often an unnecessary one.

HD-over-coax formats were designed for exactly this situation. Manufacturer data sheets for current hybrid recorders quote 4K and 4MP transmission over coax for hundreds of metres, and one camera retailer's upgrade guide says plainly that with good coax runs from solid copper cable “there's no reason to upgrade to an Ethernet IP camera system.”

The honest answer is in the testing. We keep every run that carries a clean HD picture, replace the ones that don't, and use coax-to-Ethernet adapters where a network camera is worth having. A Brooklyn system can end up mostly coax and partly IP, on one hybrid recorder.

“If the picture is on the screen, the DVR is recording”

This is the assumption behind most of the “we had no footage” calls we get from Brooklyn stores. A recorder sends live video to the monitor whether or not it is writing anything to its drive.

In a forum thread about an old DVR that had stopped working properly, the top advice was blunt: the drive inside was most likely dead, and a new one would probably fix it. Drives in recorders that run around the clock in hot cubbies and damp cellars do fail, and an old recorder may only show a small icon or a beep when they do.

Every upgrade we do ends with a playback test from the previous night, and we turn on the recorder's disk-error and recording-failure alerts and send them to the owner's phone. A recorder that stops saving should say so the same day, not the day of the robbery.

“Any DVR from a big-box store is secure enough”

Shopping guides rarely mention security beyond “change the default password.” Security researchers see it differently. FortiGuard Labs' June 2025 outbreak alert described botnets, Mirai among them, exploiting a command-injection flaw in one family of DVRs with no vendor patch available, and advised isolating or replacing the devices.

Well-known brands have had serious flaws too: CISA's catalogue of known exploited vulnerabilities includes CVE-2021-36260, a Hikvision flaw. The common factor in compromised recorders is not price alone, but a box reachable from the internet with old firmware.

That is why an upgrade with us includes closing forwarded ports and UPnP, current firmware and phone access through the maker's relay or a VPN. A cheap recorder kept off the open internet is safer than an expensive one left exposed.

“Cloud cameras are always better for apartment buildings”

Consumer reviews often steer small landlords toward cloud camera kits because they are simple to set up. In a single-family house with good Wi-Fi, they can be.

In a Brooklyn walk-up or a two-family house, the walls are brick and plaster, the cameras are in the cellar, the vestibule and the rear yard, and Wi-Fi rarely reaches all three. Each camera usually needs its own subscription for recorded history, and access is tied to one account holder rather than to separate tenant and owner roles. The Guardian's 2019 reporting on tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brooklyn fighting their landlord's facial-recognition plans is a reminder that who controls building cameras matters to tenants.

An upgraded recorder on the existing coax records locally, keeps working without Wi-Fi, and gives each person exactly the view they should have. For many Brooklyn buildings that is both cheaper and fairer.

“Put a camera wherever you like on your own house”

Generic advice treats a camera as purely your own business. In New York City, two kinds of rules can apply to where it goes and what it sees.

On a landmarked building, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's staff-level criteria limit front cameras to two per 25 feet of frontage on small buildings, require fixing into mortar joints or plain masonry, and ask for concealed wiring. And state law gives neighbors a right to sue over a video device installed on adjoining property to record their backyard without written consent, which matters on rowhouse blocks where every yard touches three others.

We plan camera positions with both in mind: discreet, correctly fixed front cameras on designated buildings, and rear cameras aimed and masked to your own lot. It costs nothing extra to do it right the first time.

“More megapixels will catch the thief”

Product listings sell resolution above everything. It helps, but on a store recording it is rarely what decides whether a face is identifiable.

What decides it is where the camera is, how far it is from the face, the light at the door, and the settings on the recorder. Many older Brooklyn store systems recorded at reduced resolution or frame rate to stretch the drive, so even good cameras produced poor clips. A ceiling-corner camera with a wide lens sees the top of a hat however many pixels it has.

When we upgrade a store recorder we record every channel at its native resolution, size the drive to match, and add a tight camera at head height facing the door. That one camera, not the megapixel count on the box, is what turns a robbery clip into something a detective can use.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Start at the Back of the Box

When I walk down into a Brooklyn cellar to look at an old recorder, I don't start with the screen. I start with the back panel and the floor under it. The back tells me how many BNC plugs are still pushed on properly, whether anyone used twist-on connectors, and how the power is fed — one supply or a dozen plug-in bricks on a strip. The floor tells me whether this room has ever had water in it. If there is a tide line on the wall or rust on the bottom of the boiler jacket, the recorder is not staying on that shelf, whatever else we decide.

The second thing I do is play back yesterday. Not live view — yesterday, at two in the morning, on the camera the owner cares about most. If that clip isn't there, or it is there and you can't tell a face from a hood, the owner has the answer to whether the upgrade is worth it.

The last thing, before I pack up, is to export a clip to a stick with the owner or the store manager doing the clicking. If they can't do it alone, the job isn't finished. — Anwar Timothy, NYS low-voltage license #12000287431

FAQ

Brooklyn DVR Upgrade FAQ

How long does a recorder swap take in a four-storey walk-up, and does someone need to be on every floor?
+

A straight recorder swap on tested coax is usually a half-day job; a building where runs need tracing or the recorder is moving can take a full day. We don't need a person on every floor, but we do need access to every camera location at some point, because each camera is checked at the camera end as well as at the recorder. For hallway and stairwell cameras that just means the common areas. If a camera or a cable run passes through an apartment, the owner or managing agent arranges a time with that tenant, and we schedule that part of the work first so the rest of the day isn't held up.

Can a bodega's recorder be swapped before opening or after closing so the store never goes unrecorded?
+

Yes. We regularly do store swaps early in the morning or late in the evening, and we plan them so the cameras are only off the recorder for minutes. The new recorder is set up and its drive formatted before we arrive, the channels are moved across one at a time, and the old recorder stays connected and recording until the new one is working. If you want to keep the old recorder's footage for a while, we leave it on a shelf, powered and disconnected from the cameras, so you can still play it back.

We own two adjoining rowhouses on the same Bed-Stuy block — can one recorder cover both?
+

Yes, as long as there is a way to get the video from one house to the other. Where the cellars share a party wall and you own both buildings, the simplest route is a sleeved hole through the cellar wall for a bundle of coax or one network cable. Where that isn't possible, a pair of small wireless bridge radios between the rear windows, or a network cable along a rear fence line, links the two. The other option is a recorder in each house, both added to the same phone app, which keeps each building's footage on its own drive. If the houses have different tenants, we set up permissions so each building's tenants see only their own entrance.

Someone keeps tampering with our store's roll-down gate at night — can a camera outside the gate record clearly?
+

Yes, if it is mounted where the gate can't block it and the night light doesn't bounce back at it. A camera fixed inside the store sees nothing once the gate is down, so the gate camera goes outside: high under the awning or the sign box, angled down along the face of the shutter, with its cable run through the wall behind it. Gates are shiny and close, so a camera's own infrared light can flare off the metal; we choose a model with adjustable infrared or rely on a nearby light, and set the frame so the lock and the bottom rail are in view. On a landmarked building the Landmarks Preservation Commission's criteria for size, spacing and fixing apply to that camera too.

Can the new recorder show the cameras on the TV in our parlor-floor living room as well as on our phones?
+

Yes. Recorders have an HDMI output, so if the TV is near the recorder it is a single cable. When the recorder is in the cellar and the TV is two floors up, there are three options: an HDMI extender over a network cable or a spare coax, a small streaming device or smart-TV app from the recorder's maker that views the cameras over your home network, or moving the recorder up to the living room floor as part of the upgrade. Most Brooklyn owners choose the app on the TV, because it needs no new cable and works on any TV in the house.

What do you need from a tenant before you go into their apartment to reach a camera run?
+

Only access at an agreed time, arranged by the owner or the managing agent. We don't contact tenants directly to schedule entry unless the owner asks us to. What helps is telling the tenant which room we need (usually a closet or a corner of the ceiling where the cable passes), how long it will take, and that we will leave the space as we found it. We work with the tenant present if they prefer. If a camera inside a tenant's apartment is part of the old system, we will raise it with the owner, because a camera inside someone's home is not something we reconnect without that tenant's clear agreement.

Our cellar recorder sits beside the water heater — does the heat shorten its life?
+

It can, mostly by shortening the life of the hard drive. Recorders write video around the clock, and a drive running hot for years fails sooner than one kept cool. Many recorders show the drive temperature on their storage or health screen, so it is easy to check. During an upgrade we move the recorder to a ventilated shelf at least a few feet from the water heater and the boiler, never inside a closed cabinet without airflow, and keep it off the floor. We also put it on a small battery backup, because a recorder that loses power mid-write can corrupt the drive or come back with its clock reset, and summer is when breakers trip.

Can we add a backyard camera to a rowhouse without opening the parlor-floor ceiling?
+

Usually. The rear-yard camera on a rowhouse is most often mounted on the back wall at the garden or parlor level, and its cable can run down the back wall a short way and in through the cellar or garden-floor wall, which is close to where the recorder already is. If there is a spare coax run to the back of the house from an old camera, we reuse it. If the camera needs to be higher, to see over a fence or an extension, a wireless bridge from the back of the house or a network camera on an existing run are alternatives. We aim and mask it to your own yard, for your neighbors' sake and for yours.

We rent our Fulton Street store — can we take the upgraded recorder with us when the lease ends?
+

Usually the recorder and the cameras you paid for can go with you, but read the lease first, because some leases treat anything fixed to the building as the landlord's at the end of the term. Cable inside walls and ceilings normally stays. If you plan to move, keep the invoice that shows you bought the recorder and cameras, and tell us at the start: we can mount cameras so they come down cleanly and leave the cable labelled for the next tenant. Taking a system down and putting it up in a new store is billed as service work at our hourly rate, and the recorder's settings and users can be carried over.

The coax in our walk-up runs up the stair hall in plastic raceway that tenants keep cracking — should that be redone during the upgrade?
+

Usually, yes, at least on the floors where it is damaged. Surface raceway in a walk-up stair hall takes knocks from furniture, bicycles, strollers and painters, and once a cover cracks the coax inside gets kinked or pulled loose, which shows up as a camera on one landing that flickers or drops out. During the upgrade we walk the run floor by floor, replace cracked covers and any crushed cable inside them, and in the spots that get hit most, such as the turn of each landing and the first few feet above the floor, use a sturdier raceway or move the run higher up the wall. Each run is labelled at both ends and at every floor so the next service call does not start with guesswork. Painting the new raceway to match the walls is easy to arrange and makes it less tempting to pick at.
