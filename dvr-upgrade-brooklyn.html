What A DVR Upgrade Is

Replace the Recorder, Keep Your Cameras

In a Brooklyn brownstone or a Nostrand Avenue storefront, a DVR upgrade means one thing: the recorder in the basement gets replaced, and the cameras and coax stay put. The video recorder is the part that ages out and fails; the cameras bolted to the cornice and the coax fished through the parlor floor are usually fine. Swapping just the box restores the whole system — phone viewing, weeks of storage, full-resolution recording — without anyone chopping into original plaster or a tin ceiling to chase new wire.

Brooklyn's camera systems tend to cluster around two setups. There's the owner-occupied row house — a four-camera DVR in the cellar covering the stoop, the alley, the backyard, and the parlor door — and there's the corner deli or bodega, an eight- or sixteen-channel box behind the counter watching the registers, the coolers, and the sidewalk. Both were usually wired eight to twelve years ago by whoever was cheapest, and both share the same problem now: the recorder stopped getting firmware years back, dropped its phone app, and overwrites itself in a day or two. The cameras still work. The box behind the counter or under the stairs is what quit.

Two things make the swap the obvious move in Brooklyn. First, the wiring is worth keeping: most of these runs are solid-copper RG59, and it carries a modern HD analog signal cleanly, so re-cabling a three-story row house is effort nobody needs to spend. Second, the demand on these systems keeps rising — a bodega owner needs register footage for a chargeback, a landlord needs the vestibule clip after a package theft, an insurer wants the break-in on file. A recorder that holds two days can't meet any of that. One box, one afternoon, and the coverage the property already paid for comes back to life.

There's a Brooklyn-specific way these systems die, too: renovation. A row house gets a gut reno, a mixed-use building gets a new storefront, a cellar gets finished — and somewhere in the work a camera run gets cut, a recorder gets unplugged and never reconnected, or the whole box gets buried behind new sheetrock still "recording" to a dead drive. The owner assumes they're covered until the day they aren't. Add the ordinary failures — a seized fan in a hot cellar, a drive worn out from years of writing — and the pattern is constant: someone calls after an incident to find the DVR stopped saving weeks or months earlier.

The cost logic seals it. Re-running cable through a finished brownstone or an occupied two-family means opening walls, fishing floors, and disrupting tenants — real money and real mess for wiring that already works. A recorder swap avoids all of it: the coax stays in the joists, the cameras stay on their brackets, and the only thing that changes is the box they feed. For the overwhelming majority of Brooklyn systems put in before the IP era, replacing the recorder rather than the whole system is simply the right-sized fix.

Which Recorder Is Right For Your System

The right recorder for a Brooklyn building is set by what's already run through the walls, not by what's newest on the shelf. Choose wrong one way and you've paid to re-cable a row house that didn't need it; choose wrong the other way and you've locked a shop into dead-end analog when a few dollars would have kept the path open. Three options, plainly.

🔌

DVR (Analog HD)

Records analog cameras over coax and converts the signal internally. The direct like-for-like replacement when your cameras and RG59 are healthy and you want the cheapest reliable fix. Modern DVRs add H.265, mobile apps, and larger drives your old unit never had.

🔁

Hybrid / XVR — Most Popular

Accepts your existing analog cameras on BNC and new IP cameras over the network on the same box. The smart middle path: keep every camera today, add a 4K IP camera at the front door tomorrow — no second recorder needed.

🌐

NVR (IP)

Records IP cameras over Cat6 with PoE. The right move when you want 4K on every channel, color night vision, and license-plate detail, and you're ready to run Ethernet. We migrate you here in stages when it fits.

Our Recommendation

Why We Default to Hybrid/XVR for Brooklyn Upgrades

We put a hybrid XVR in most Brooklyn buildings for a simple reason: it lets a row-house owner or a shop keeper upgrade one camera at a time instead of all at once. The hybrid takes the existing analog cameras on its BNC ports and also accepts IP cameras over the network, all in one box with one app. It runs maybe $20 to $80 more than a plain DVR — the price of a couple of takeout orders — and that small difference is what turns a dead-end swap into an upgrade path. When the aging stoop camera finally dies, you replace just that one with a 4K IP camera and leave the rest alone.

When Brooklyn actually needs the full IP jump: the place analog struggles is the register and the front door of a shop — where you need to read a face or a plate, not just see that someone was there. A deli owner who wants to identify the person palming items at the counter, or a landlord who needs a clear image of who followed a tenant into the vestibule, is buying a low-light 4K IP camera and the NVR to run it. If the old cameras are failing anyway — fogged domes, dead IR from years of weather on an exposed brick wall — then IP is the honest call, and we'll say so rather than sell a swap that leaves the real gap open.

The staged path, Brooklyn-style: nobody wants to shut a bodega's cameras for a weekend or gut a brownstone's wiring in one hit. So we start with the recorder: swap the dead DVR for a hybrid and every existing camera is back online the same afternoon. From there the upgrades happen on the owner's timeline — the register camera this month, the backyard camera when the old one fogs over — each new IP camera dropping onto the same box. Once most channels are IP, a dedicated NVR can take the reins. No coverage gap, no single big bill.

That sequence dodges the two things Brooklyn owners hate: a giant one-shot bill, and being trapped on obsolete analog with nowhere to go. A landlord modernizes a camera a year out of rent income; a shop owner upgrades when a slow week allows. The hybrid recorder is the hinge that keeps every option open — which is why we fit one even when a building shows up with nothing but analog on the day we arrive.

How the upgrade actually goes

The job follows the same sequence almost every time. First we assess: confirm your camera signal type per channel (TVI, CVI, AHD, or older CVBS), check the condition of the coax and BNC ends, and inspect the health of the existing power supply and drive. This step is what separates a clean swap from a call-back — a tired power supply that isn't caught can damage the new recorder the same way it stressed the old one. Then we match a recorder to what we found, almost always a hybrid so you keep an IP upgrade path. Next we swap: pull the dead unit, install a surveillance-rated drive sized to your retention goal, and re-terminate any BNC runs that need it. Finally we configure: motion recording where it saves storage, continuous where risk is highest, secure view-only user accounts instead of a single shared admin, audio disabled where it shouldn't record, and the mobile app set up and tested on your phone before we leave.

On a straightforward system with healthy cabling, that's a same-day job and your cameras are back online in an afternoon. Where there's BNC repair, added channels, or the start of an IP migration, the timeline stretches — but you know that up front, because we tested before we quoted.

Why we start with the recorder, not the whole system: when a Brooklyn owner calls with "my cameras aren't working," our first move is almost never to quote a new system. It's to figure out whether the cameras actually failed or whether the recorder took the whole system down with it. In the great majority of cases it's the latter — the cameras are fine, the coax is fine, and the box is dead or dying. Replacing just the recorder is faster, far cheaper, and less disruptive than pulling wire through an occupied building, and it keeps the equipment you already paid for doing its job. We only recommend going further — new cameras, an IP migration, fresh cabling — when the assessment actually shows you need it. That test-first, quote-second approach is the whole reason owners call us back for the next building.

When To Replace

Signs Your DVR Needs Replacing

A cellar recorder gives you weeks of warning before it goes dark — you just have to be in the basement to notice. Since most owners aren't, these are the signs worth watching for, before the stoop or register footage you need turns out to be the footage that already stopped saving.

Reboot loops

The recorder restarts on its own, often overnight, and you find gaps in the timeline right when incidents happen. Classic failing-drive or dying-power-supply symptom.

"No HDD" or missing footage

The unit boots but reports no hard drive, or footage only goes back a day or two when it used to hold weeks. The 24/7 write load has worn the drive out.

The app stopped connecting

Remote viewing that worked years ago now fails. Manufacturers retire the cloud servers old DVRs depend on — there's no fix on the old box, only a current recorder.

Black or "no signal" channels

Cameras that clearly still have power show nothing on the recorder. Sometimes a cable or BNC fault, sometimes the decoder in an aging box giving up channel by channel.

Dead fan / overheating

A recorder that runs hot or whose cooling fan has stopped is on borrowed time. Heat is what kills the drive and the board.

Discontinued & unsupported

The model is no longer made, firmware updates stopped, and you can't find compatible cameras. Even if it still runs, you're one failure from a scramble.

Decoded

DVR Upgrade Terminology

BNC

The twist-lock connector on the end of your coax cameras. If your cameras use BNC, you're on a DVR/coax system.

TVI / CVI / AHD

Three HD-over-coax signal standards. A pentabrid recorder auto-detects all of them, so mixed-generation cameras record on one box.

CVBS

Old standard-definition analog (480p). Still supported by modern recorders, so ancient cameras keep working during a phased upgrade.

H.265 / H.265+

Newer compression. Cuts storage use ~40–50% vs. H.264, turning days of retention into weeks on the same drive.

XVR / Pentabrid

A hybrid recorder that takes analog on coax and IP over the network at the same time.

Surveillance drive

WD Purple / Seagate SkyHawk — built for 24/7 writing. Desktop drives fail fast in a recorder.

DIY vs Professional

Swapping It Yourself vs. Calling a Pro

Honest take: if your cameras are one clean signal type and your coax is solid, a careful DIY recorder swap can work. The moment there's a mismatch, a multi-unit building, or a recorder that already fried once, a pro saves you the second purchase. Here's where the two paths actually diverge.

Signal-type match

DIY: you guess whether your cameras are TVI, CVI, or AHD and hope the box matches. Pro: verified per channel on site, recorder set to auto-detect so every camera shows video.

Coax & BNC health

DIY: usually skipped — until half the channels read "no signal." Pro: runs tested, connectors re-terminated where they fault, before the new box goes in.

Power-supply risk

DIY: a tired supply can fry the brand-new recorder the same way it stressed the old one. Pro: supply checked and replaced if needed before anything new connects to it.

Drive sizing

DIY: whatever drive shipped in the box, if any. Pro: surveillance-rated drive sized to your camera count, resolution, and retention goal.

Remote access

DIY: port-forwarding headaches and exposed endpoints. Pro: secure app setup with view-only user accounts, tested on your phone before we leave.

Best for

DIY: a simple, matched, healthy single-signal system. Pro: anything mixed-generation, aging, multi-tenant, or already showing failure symptoms.

Retention & Compliance

What a Recorder Upgrade Affects Legally

For a Brooklyn landlord or shop owner, the recorder is quietly the part that decides whether you can actually produce footage when it counts — how many days back it goes, who can pull it, and whether the clip holds up. Here's the plain-terms version of what a recorder upgrade touches. General guidance, not legal advice; check specifics with your attorney or insurer.

How long you keep footage is set by your recorder

No statute sets a fixed retention period for most private residential buildings in New York, so how far back you keep footage is your call — but the recorder is what enforces it. Most owners want 30 days or more, and a worn basement DVR that overwrites in two simply can't deliver it no matter what the intention was. A current recorder with a right-sized surveillance drive and H.265+ is the difference between "we thought we had it" and a full month of coverage sitting on the drive when a tenant or an insurer asks.

Insurance and commercial requirements

For the storefront side — the deli, the bodega, the Fulton Street shop — the recorder often sits inside an insurance requirement. Many commercial policies make working cameras and a set retention window a condition of theft and vandalism coverage, and some regulated operators like licensed cannabis retailers under the state Office of Cannabis Management carry explicit footage rules. If the policy expects 30 days on the entrance and registers and the box holds two, the gap shows up at the worst moment: a denied claim after a loss.

Audio, privacy & access control

Audio is its own consideration: New York is a one-party-consent state, and recording conversation in a shared hallway or a store aisle raises questions video doesn't. A current recorder lets you turn audio off per channel where it shouldn't run. It also supports layered accounts — a super or manager gets view-only playback of specific cameras while you keep the admin rights — which keeps a clean chain of custody when a clip goes to the NYPD or an insurer.

Chain of custody & footage that holds up

The moment a clip becomes evidence — a tenant dispute, a slip-and-fall, a break-in the police are working — it matters how it was stored and who could have touched it. A DVR where everyone shares one login can't show that cleanly. A recorder with separate accounts and a straight MP4 export to USB hands over a clip with a clear access trail behind it. For a landlord or shop owner who might end up in front of an insurer or a courtroom, that's the line between footage that helps and footage that gets dismissed.

Board and management approval

If your building is a co-op or a larger multi-family, common-area security work may still need board or management sign-off and a certificate of insurance from a licensed contractor before it starts. A recorder swap is the easy version of that approval — a contained cellar job, not a building-wide re-wire. As a licensed NY low-voltage contractor (NYS Lic #12000287431) carrying insurance, we hand over the COI and paperwork up front and work around tenant and super access so the job finishes in one visit instead of stalling.

Cost

Contractor wants to rewire my whole brownstone. Is that necessary?

Usually not. If the cameras still image and the RG59 tests good, the fix is a recorder and maybe a drive — not three floors of new cable. The full-rewire quote is what you get when someone skips testing the coax and sells the biggest ticket instead.

Cost

Bodega DVR died — cheaper to fix or replace the system?

Replace the recorder, keep the cameras. On a storefront system the registers and cooler cameras are almost always fine; it's the box behind the counter that quit. A recorder-and-drive swap is a fraction of a full new system.

Cost

Will a new DVR add a monthly bill?

No. It records to a drive on site and the app is free — the opposite of the cloud-camera brands that bill per camera every month. For a shop watching six or eight cameras, that difference adds up fast.

Quality / Trust

A customer disputed a charge — can I even pull that day's register footage?

If the recorder's still saving, maybe, but old boxes often overwrite in a day and export a file no one can open. A current recorder holds weeks and drops a clean clip to USB — exactly what you need for a chargeback or a police report.

Quality / Trust

How do I know the installer isn't overselling a small shop?

They test before they quote — signal type, coax, drive health — and tell you if a $150 drive solves it. An honest shop does the small job and earns the callback. Ask them to put in writing what they ruled out.

DIY / Pro

Can I swap the DVR in my two-family myself?

If the new box matches your camera signal type and the coax is intact, sometimes. Where it breaks in Brooklyn: a run a past renovation nicked, a tired power supply in a hot cellar, or mismatched TVI/CVI/AHD showing black channels.

DIY / Pro

New recorder's in and the backyard camera reads "no signal."

Either a signal-type mismatch on that channel or a coax fault — and in a renovated house it's often a run that got crushed or spliced during the work. Auto-detect on a pentabrid clears the mismatch; the rest is a cable repair on that line.

Technical

I found a coax-and-power cable in one jacket. What is that?

A Siamese run — video coax and a power lead bundled together, standard on older Brooklyn installs. If your cameras have a BNC plug and a separate barrel power plug, you've got them, and they're ideal to reuse in a recorder swap.

Technical

My cameras were added over several years. Can one box run them all?

Yes. A pentabrid recorder auto-detects each channel, so the old alley camera and the newer stoop camera record together on one drive — common in row houses that added coverage a camera at a time.

Technical

How much drive do I need to keep a month on a storefront?

Depends on camera count and resolution, but a typical bodega with eight cameras on H.265+ motion recording lands around 4TB for 30 days. We size the drive to your channels and how far back you want to look.

Landlord / Tenant

Package theft in the vestibule and my DVR saved nothing. Now what?

Classic case — the recorder quit weeks ago and nobody knew. We restore reliable retention on your existing entrance and hallway cameras so the next incident is actually on file. This is the single most common reason Brooklyn landlords call.

Landlord / Tenant

Can I give my tenant or super access without handing over control?

Yes. New recorders support view-only accounts, so a tenant or super can watch and play back specific cameras while admin rights — deleting footage, changing settings — stay with you.

Complaints

DVR reboots every night and the overnight footage is gone.

Failing drive or dying power supply — and overnight is exactly when stoop and alley incidents happen. Sometimes a drive fixes it; often the box is done. Either way you're losing the window you most need.

Complaints

The app from the original installer stopped connecting.

The manufacturer retired the server that old recorder relied on — no patch exists. A current recorder with a supported app restores phone viewing and keeps every camera you have.

AnswerThePublic Questions About DVR Upgrades

Natural-language searches we see around Brooklyn recorder upgrades — short answers to the phrasing owners and shopkeepers actually type.

Do I have a DVR or an NVR in my brownstone?

Check the recorder's camera plugs. Round BNC coax connectors mean a DVR; blue RJ45 network jacks mean an NVR. Most pre-2016 Brooklyn houses are DVR on coax.

What happens to my old bodega footage when I upgrade?

It sits on the old drive. Reuse the drive and it carries over; install a fresh one and we pull any critical clips — a disputed sale, an incident — off the old drive first.

Can I keep my old cameras and add a 4K one at the register?

Yes — a hybrid runs the old analog cameras and a new IP camera together. That's the usual first upgrade for a shop: keep coverage, add face-level detail at the counter.

Is a recorder swap worth it in an old row house, or start over?

If the coax is the original solid copper and the cameras still image, the swap wins by a mile. Starting over only makes sense when you're opening walls anyway or the cameras are failing.

Will a new DVR fix my grainy nighttime alley footage?

Only partly. The recorder stops down-recording, but low light is the camera. A dark backyard or alley usually needs a low-light IP camera, not just a new box.

How do I know if a renovation damaged my coax?

We meter each run. Solid RG59 usually passes; a line that got stapled, crushed, or spliced during past work shows up on the test and gets re-terminated.

Can I add cameras later without another big job?

On a hybrid, yes — new IP cameras land on the network side up to the channel count. No re-cabling means the next camera is a quick add, not a project.

Why does my storefront DVR only hold two days now?

Worn drive plus old H.264 compression. A fresh surveillance drive with H.265+ restores weeks on the same cameras — enough to actually cover a chargeback window.

What drive size covers a bodega for 30 days?

Roughly 4TB on H.265+ motion recording for eight cameras, depending on resolution. We size to the real channel count, not a round guess.

Can two recorders share my cameras across a two-family?

Not on the same coax runs — one recorder owns its cameras. For a bigger building we fit a single higher-channel recorder rather than two boxes and two apps.

Does upgrading change how I watch cameras from my phone?

Yes, for the better — you move to the new recorder's current app with reliable live view and playback, replacing the dead app the original installer set up.

Is a hybrid harder to use than my old DVR?

No. The app shows analog and IP cameras in one list, so daily use is identical — there's just room to add IP cameras down the line.

The cameras only capture; the recorder stores every channel, serves playback, and feeds the phone app. When the vestibule footage is missing, the recorder is why.

Heat and damp in an unventilated basement, plus 24/7 write load. The drive or power supply usually goes first, and nobody notices until footage is needed.

DVR Upgrade: What the AI Summaries Get Wrong

Google's AI Overview for "DVR upgrade cost" is stitched from national lead-gen sites — Angi, HomeAdvisor, Fixr. It's aimed at a suburban house with a garage. Here's how that generic answer holds up, and where it falls apart, once you're standing in a brownstone cellar or behind a bodega counter.

The definitions travel; the price doesn't

The concepts are right: DVR for analog over coax, NVR for IP over Ethernet, hybrid for both, reuse coax to save money, H.265 stretches storage. Nothing there is wrong. Its implied pricing assumes a driveway to park in and an attic to run cable through. On a Bed-Stuy row house the work starts with double-parking on a narrow street and carrying gear down cellar stairs. That reality never enters a national average.

So the vocabulary survives the trip to Brooklyn; the numbers don't. That's where the summary quietly stops being useful.

The "$200 DVR" ignores the actual repair

The cost ranges blend rural DIY drop-ins with licensed work in a Brooklyn building. They leave out the drive that's worn out, the two BNC ends that need re-terminating where a renovation nicked the run, and the service-call labor to actually diagnose a decade-old install. A $200 box online is an honest $500–$900 by the time the system is genuinely recording again.

A dead Brooklyn recorder is almost never only a dead recorder. On the row-house and storefront systems we see, there's usually a tired drive and a run or two disturbed by past work behind it. The summary prices the box; the job is the box plus what age and renovation did to the rest.

And the aggregators take their cut — your lead gets sold, sometimes to several bidders at once. That layer is baked into whatever you eventually pay. Calling a licensed installer directly removes it.

"Reuse the coax" is a test here, not a promise

The most important question — will the new recorder actually show my cameras? — gets a shrug. In Brooklyn the answer hinges on two things the summary can't check: each camera's signal type, and whether a renovation somewhere along the way crushed, cut, or spliced the coax. A run that was fine in 2015 may have taken a staple through it during a kitchen redo.

Get the signal type wrong and the affected channels don't degrade — they go black. That's the exact "new DVR, half my cameras say no signal" complaint that fills the forums, and it's doubly common where old work left mixed camera generations on one box.

The real fix is unglamorous: read the signal per channel, let a pentabrid auto-detect, and re-terminate the runs a renovation disturbed. That's hands-on judgment on your specific wiring, which no national article can offer.

"Just get an NVR" wastes a row house's money

Summaries love to say "buy an NVR." For an empty new build, fine. For a brownstone with healthy analog cameras on good coax, that advice means re-wiring three finished floors to solve what a hybrid handles in an afternoon in the cellar. It's the most expensive possible answer to a cheap problem.

The right framing is almost always hybrid: keep the analog cameras now, add IP later, never get forced to choose between cheap and future-proof. The DVR-or-NVR binary erases the exact option most Brooklyn owners should take.

Your specifics decide it — camera count, signal type, whether the coax survived past renovations, whether you need register-level detail. None of that lives in a Fixr table; all of it lives in your cellar.

The DIY traps that bite in a hot cellar

You won't read that a failing power supply can drag a brand-new recorder down with it, or that a marginal cable faults the new box just like it faulted the old one. Nor will it mention that big-box kit cable is often aluminum strand, not solid copper, and may not carry higher resolutions without re-termination. The summary treats reusing coax as automatic; on a real job it's something we meter before trusting a single run.

These blind spots are how a confident DIYer ends up calling a pro anyway — after paying for hardware twice. We check the supply and the cable before connecting anything new to them.

All three national brands want you on their cameras and their monthly cloud. Every one of them ignores the wired cameras already on your walls. A recorder upgrade is the only option that treats your existing equipment as an asset instead of something to replace.

vs. ADT

ADT is a monitoring company first — a contract and a monthly bill, usually paired with their own equipment. vs. Ring

Ring is built around Wi-Fi cameras and cloud video, billed monthly. Good for a renter or a single doorway; a poor fit for a wired multi-camera building. For a Brooklyn landlord with eight hallway and entrance cameras on coax, that's a downgrade in reliability and a new recurring bill.

vs. SimpliSafe

SimpliSafe is a DIY alarm and camera kit, again subscription-driven for meaningful camera features. Like Ring, it doesn't record your existing analog cameras. If you're starting from nothing in a small apartment, it's reasonable. The recorder swap is the proportionate fix.

What drives the price up or down

Three variables move the number more than anything else. Channel count: a 4-channel box is cheaper than a 16- or 32-channel unit, and you want to size for your current cameras plus a little headroom. Sizing your storage, roughly

A useful rule of thumb: about 2TB holds roughly 30 days of motion-only recording for four 4MP cameras under H.265+, or closer to a week on continuous. Double the cameras or the resolution and you roughly double the storage needed for the same retention. We size the drive to how many cameras you run, at what resolution, and how far back you need to see — then round up. FAQ

DVR Upgrade Questions

Can I upgrade my DVR without replacing my cameras?
+

Yes. Most analog HD cameras (TVI, CVI, AHD) work with a new hybrid recorder over your existing coax. We confirm the signal type first, then match a recorder that keeps every camera you already have.

How much does a DVR upgrade cost in Brooklyn?
+

Most residential DVR upgrades in Brooklyn run roughly $450 to $1,200 installed, depending on channel count, hard drive size, and how much BNC re-termination is needed. Commercial 16 and 32-channel jobs run higher.

Should I replace my DVR or switch to an NVR?
+

If your coax and analog cameras are healthy, a hybrid DVR/XVR is the cheaper path and reuses your wiring. If you want 4K on every channel, color night vision, and license-plate detail, moving to an NVR with IP cameras is the better long-term choice.

Will a new DVR improve my camera image quality?
+

A new recorder lets older cameras record at their full native resolution and frame rate, and adds H.265 compression for longer storage. It can't turn a 1080p camera into 4K, but many aging DVRs down-record cameras below their real capability.

Can a DVR upgrade add remote viewing to my phone?
+

Yes. Every recorder we install supports a free mobile app for live view and playback with no monthly fee. Older DVRs often lost app support years ago; a new unit restores reliable remote access.

Will my old cameras work with a new DVR?
+

Almost always, as long as we match the signal type. Modern pentabrid recorders auto-detect TVI, CVI, AHD, and CVBS per channel, so a mix of camera generations records on one box.

How long do DVRs last?
+

A DVR typically lasts 5 to 8 years. The hard drive is usually the first part to fail because it writes 24/7. Rebooting loops, missing footage, and app dropouts are the common end-of-life signs.

What is the difference between a DVR and an NVR?
+

A DVR records analog cameras over coax and processes video at the recorder. An NVR records IP cameras over Ethernet, where each camera encodes its own video. DVRs reuse coax; NVRs need Cat6 and PoE.

Can a DVR upgrade increase my recording storage?
+

Yes. Newer recorders support larger surveillance drives (up to 10TB+ per bay) and H.265+ compression, which can multiply your retention from days to weeks or months without adding cameras.

My DVR keeps rebooting and losing footage. Is that fixable?
+

Reboot loops and gaps in footage usually mean a failing hard drive or a dying power supply inside the DVR. Sometimes a drive swap fixes it; often the recorder itself is at end of life and a replacement is more reliable.

Can you keep my existing coax cable?
+

In most Brooklyn buildings, yes. Solid-copper RG59 coax carries HD analog fine. We test the runs first. Thin pre-made cable from big-box kits sometimes needs re-termination or replacement.

Do you install the hard drive too?
+

Yes. We supply and install a surveillance-rated drive (WD Purple or Seagate SkyHawk) sized to your retention goal. Desktop drives are never used because they fail under 24/7 recording.
