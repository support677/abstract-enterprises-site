The Neighborhood

Why Starrett City Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Starrett City sits on the Jamaica Bay shore at the southeastern edge of Brooklyn, between Fresh Creek and Hendrix Creek, with Flatlands Avenue to the north and the Belt Parkway to the south. Most of it is one property: Spring Creek Towers, 46 buildings of 11 to 20 stories holding 5,881 rental apartments, grouped into eight sections that each have their own five-story garage. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in July 2021 that 3,569 of those apartments are under a HUD Section 8 contract and 2,312 are not, and that the owners agreed with the state to keep the complex in Mitchell-Lama through 2069, with about $140 million more in repairs planned by 2030. For anyone thinking about a door buzzer, that structure is the first fact that matters: almost every front door in the neighborhood answers to the same owner.

That owner has said publicly that it wants new entrances. In an October 2021 letter to residents, published in the Spring Creek Sun, management wrote that it had designs for new hallways and new lobbies and had "begun to 'mock-up' each of these spaces," testing each "so we can see what works and what can be improved before we roll out the upgrades across the entire community." The same letter said it was interviewing architects to replace the building frontages, and that "obtaining inventory has been challenging." A lobby door system is exactly the kind of equipment that gets decided at a mock-up and then repeated dozens of times, so the choices made at the first entrance follow every household in the complex for years.

The towers already have more building technology behind their doors than most Brooklyn apartment houses. Honeywell announced in 2013 a $2 million retrofit at Spring Creek Towers that added a central building management system, a local network and "new sensors in critical areas that alert operators to doors that should not be open," along with a passive optical network that carries video and phone traffic. Upgrading access control was listed as a possible next step. A new buzzer at a lobby door therefore cannot be treated as a box on the wall. It meets door sensors, cameras and a control room that existed before it, and it has to be planned to work with them.

The city's complaint record shows why the entrances keep coming up. Since January 2024, residents of ZIP 11239 have filed 104 complaints with 311 under the descriptor for doors and 99 under the one for bells, buzzers and intercoms, plus 15 more about door frames. A new resident who moved in during 2025 asked on r/Brooklyn, "Should I trust my packages being left downstairs?" A Yelp review of the management company describes an intercom that "stopped working for nearly two months." Installation done well is how those complaints stop: a door that latches, a panel that reaches every apartment, and a record of how it was wired so the next repair is quick.

Six Starrett City facts that decide how a door buzzer gets installed

  • One owner decides for the towers. Spring Creek Towers' 46 buildings and 5,881 apartments are a single rental property, so lobby, garage and service-door systems are ordered by the owner, not by residents or individual buildings.
  • The owner tests before it rolls out. Management's 2021 letter described mocking up new lobbies and hallways and testing each before rolling the upgrade out across the community, which makes the first installed entrance a pilot for all the others.
  • The doors already report somewhere. Honeywell's 2013 retrofit added door sensors that alert operators to doors left open and a network that carries video, so new entry equipment should connect to that system rather than ignore it.
  • Doors draw as many complaints as buzzers. Since 2024, 311 has logged 104 door complaints and 99 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints in ZIP 11239; at addresses on the tower loops and Twin Pines Drive alone, 19 were about buzzers and 15 about doors and frames.
  • The towers sit on the post-1968 side of the intercom law. The complex was dedicated in 1974, and Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom with remote release at the self-locking entrance of any class A multiple dwelling of eight or more apartments erected after January 1, 1968.
  • Thousands of new apartments have opened next door. The Fountains finished 1,163 apartments across six buildings east of Hendrix Creek in 2021, and Alafia's first phase added 576 more in December 2025, each with entrances of its own.

Repair Before Replacing

Panel Dead for Weeks or a Store Door That Won't Hold? Find Out Whether You Need a Repair Visit Before Anyone Orders New Equipment

A fair number of the calls we get about "installing a buzzer" in Starrett City are really about something already on the wall. A store in the shopping center has a release button that stopped working after a renovation. A program's suite door has a strike that buzzes and does nothing. A management office wants to know whether a lobby that has been patched for years is worth another repair or should be first in line for replacement. Before any new equipment is chosen, we sort the job into one of three paths, and we say plainly which one it is.

The first path is a targeted repair, when one part is the whole problem: a power supply that has failed, a strike whose keeper has drifted out of line with the latch, a closer that no longer pulls the door the last inch. The second is a partial replacement, with a new door station and supply on cable that still tests sound. The third is a new installation, when the cable, the panel and the door hardware have all reached the end of their useful life, or when the door never had a release in the first place. If a repair is all you need, our Brooklyn door buzzer team prices it as a repair, and the $250 online service call is applied toward that work.

In the towers there is one more question we ask before anything else: whether the lobby is already on the owner's list for the new-lobby program. A repair that keeps a door working for the next year is worth doing even if a new panel is coming. A large replacement six months before the owner's own contractor rebuilds the entrance usually is not, and we would rather tell the managing agent that than sell equipment that comes off the wall with the old frontage.

Tower lobbies: one entrance, more than a hundred households and a door that never rests

Each tower's lobby door is the only way most residents and every visitor reach the elevators, and 5,881 apartments across 46 buildings means the average lobby serves about 128 households. A door like that opens hundreds of times a day. The hardware that matters most is not the panel but the strike and the closer, because they move every time. When we install at a tower lobby, the strike is sized for continuous duty, the closer is adjusted so the door shuts and latches against the plaza wind without slamming, and the frame is checked for the wear that years of heavy use leave around the keeper.

The panel itself is chosen for scale. A tower of this size needs a directory or code panel, not a row of buttons, and it needs to reach apartments over the cable already running up the building or over new cable or a network connection where the old pairs fail. We test the existing risers line by line before quoting, because a riser that passes in one tower can fail in its identical neighbor, and the owner needs to know which lobbies can reuse cable before it budgets the rollout.

On our list a tower lobby sits at or beyond the top of the $2,500 to $10,000-plus multi-unit range, and we quote each lobby on its own sheet. That matters for an owner with 46 of them: it shows exactly what one entrance costs, what a riser repair adds, and what drops out when cable can be reused.

Section garages and service doors: entry for staff and residents, not for visitors

The eight section garages are five stories tall and connect the parking decks to the grounds and, through service rooms, to the buildings' mechanical spaces. McGowan Builders' account of the boiler decentralization project describes modular boiler plants installed outside the garage mechanical spaces, one set for each section, which tells you how much equipment and staff traffic now passes through those buildings. The pedestrian and service doors there need a different system from a lobby. Nobody is visiting a boiler room, so a directory panel makes no sense; what the doors need is entry for the right people and a record of who went in.

For those doors we install keypads or card readers with an electric strike, set so that the door stays locked from outside and opens freely from inside. Where a call is needed, such as a garage stair door residents use after parking, a small call station can ring the Public Safety desk or the management office rather than any apartment. Each door's codes or cards are managed separately from the lobbies, so a contractor's code for a garage service room never opens a tower.

Garage and service doors usually land in the $400 to $900 audio range per door, or the $700 to $1,800 range where video or a networked reader is wanted, priced door by door on the same quote as the lobbies they serve.

Apartments inside the towers: the part of the entrance a resident can ask to change

A resident of a tower does not own the lobby panel, the riser or the station on the apartment wall that answers the lobby. Those belong to the building. What a resident lives with every day, though, is the apartment's own hallway door, and many of those doors have a bell button that was painted over years ago, a chime that rings too softly to hear from a back bedroom, or nothing at all, so neighbors and visitors knock. That is a small installation, and it is one a resident can arrange, with one condition: the management office must approve it in writing before anything is fixed to the door or the hallway wall.

The equipment is simple. A wired button at the door with a chime inside the apartment, or a wireless button and a plug-in chime that needs no cable through the wall, or a second chime for the far end of a large apartment. A camera at the hallway door is a separate question. Habitat magazine reported in 2023 that New York City has no law banning doorbell cameras in apartment hallways, but that building rules control the question, and a lawyer who represents boards told it that a resident "can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways." In a rental complex with one owner, that means the office decides, and many owners limit where a camera can point and what it can record.

This is priced from photos as a small job, and we tell you the figure before you book. If the apartment's lobby station itself is dead, that is the building's repair, and the right first call is the management office.

The shopping center and the offices above it: buzz-in doors that never trap anyone

The shopping center on the grounds faces Pennsylvania Avenue, with a supermarket, a CVS, food counters and services, and offices and suites above. Building records show how often those storefronts change: a 2014 filing at 1340 Pennsylvania Avenue proposed a new storefront entrance and three fire-rated doors, and a 2018 filing at 1380 Pennsylvania Avenue covered a supermarket renovation that included the main entrance. Each change of tenant or layout is a chance to put in a proper buzz-in door instead of a key and a bell.

For a store, the usual installation is a release button behind the counter, a strike in the door frame and, if the owner wants to see who is outside after dark, a small video station at the door. For an upstairs office or clinic, it is a door station at the street-level stair door or the suite door, ringing the front desk. In every case the door must open from inside with one motion, without a key, and fire-rated doors take fail-secure strikes.

Store and office doors usually land in the $400 to $900 audio range or the $700 to $1,800 video range, and the landlord's written consent comes first, because the frame and the storefront belong to the center.

Schools, the recreation center and programs: a staffed desk that buzzes all day

The school buildings on Pennsylvania Avenue, the recreation center that opened in 1978 and now houses the Brooklyn Sports Club, and the programs and senior services that rent space on the grounds all have one thing in common: someone at a desk lets people in. Building records show the entrances at 1400 Pennsylvania Avenue have been reworked more than once, with all of the exterior entry doors replaced in 2001 and, in 2018, accessibility ramps added at two entrances along with "ADA compliant hardware at exterior door." An entry system at a building like that has to work with whatever hardware is already approved on the door.

The installation here is a video call station at the entrance, a master station or desk console at the front desk with a release button, and a strike or electrified lever that suits the existing door. Reach ranges matter: the 2010 ADA Standards set 48 inches as the maximum high forward reach, so the call button goes no higher. Where the desk is not staffed all day, calls can roll over to a second station or a phone. Public schools are run by the city, and their entrances are the city's decision, so we work at schools only when the school's facility staff or the city's contractor engages us.

East of Hendrix Creek, the neighborhood has changed more in five years than in the forty before. For a later phase, we install, test and document the system before the first residents arrive, so the property's own staff are not discovering on move-in day which apartments the panel cannot reach. These are multi-unit jobs, priced from the drawings and a site visit within the $2,500 to $10,000-plus range per entrance or above it for a large building, and broken out line by line so the general contractor can carry them in a bid.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Watch the Door for Ten Minutes, Find the Wire That Already Reports It, Then Ring the Top Floor

This is how our technician spends the first part of a visit to a Starrett City entrance, before anything is priced.

First, the door at work. Before opening any panel, the technician stands at the lobby or store door for ten minutes and watches it. How often it opens, whether it closes and latches every time, whether anyone props it, how hard the wind off the bay pushes it, whether deliveries come through it or through another door. That ten minutes decides the strike, the closer setting and whether the door hardware is the real job.

Second, what already watches the door. The technician looks for a door-position switch on the frame, a camera aimed at the entrance and any wiring that leaves the door for somewhere other than the panel. In a building with a monitoring network, that wire usually goes to a control panel that reports the door to operators. We note what it reports and where, because the new entry system should send its signals to the same place.

Third, the farthest apartment or room. With the owner's staff, the technician calls the highest floor or the back office from the door and listens for clear speech both ways. In a tower, a test call to the top floor tells us more about the riser than any drawing does.

The Pilot Lobby: What One Mock-Up Entrance Has to Prove Before 45 Other Towers Get the Same Door System

Management's own approach to the new lobbies is the right one for an entry system, and it is worth spelling out what the test should cover. The 2021 letter said each space would be mocked up and tested "so we can see what works and what can be improved before we roll out the upgrades across the entire community." For finishes and lighting, a mock-up answers questions of taste. For a door entry system, it answers questions that are expensive to get wrong 46 times: whether the panel reaches every apartment over the cable in that tower, whether the strike survives the traffic, whether residents and visitors can actually use the directory, and whether the door reports correctly to the people watching it.

We plan a pilot lobby as a full installation with a written test plan attached. Before the panel goes live, every apartment line is called from the lobby and logged as clear, weak or dead. After it goes live, the strike, closer and panel are checked again at set intervals during the first months, because a lobby door at that traffic level shows its weak points quickly. Residents' questions to the management office during those weeks are worth collecting too, since the same questions will come from every other tower.

What the pilot should measure

  • Reach. The share of apartment lines that work on the existing riser, the share that need repair and the share that need new cable or a network path. That one figure decides most of the rollout budget.
  • Door behavior. How many times the door fails to latch on its own in a week, how often it is found propped, and whether the closer setting needs to change with the season.
  • Visitor use. Whether first-time visitors find their apartment on the directory without help, whether the code format is clear, and whether couriers can reach the right household.
  • Reporting. Whether door-held and door-forced signals reach the control room, and whether a released door is shown as released rather than forced.
  • Parts on hand. Which parts fail first and how long a replacement takes to arrive, so spares can be ordered before the next lobby goes in.

The last point comes straight from the 2021 letter, which said inventory had been hard to get. A rollout across dozens of lobbies is only as good as the parts supply behind it. If the pilot shows that a particular strike or power supply takes weeks to arrive, the owner should stock it before the second tower, not discover it with a lobby door standing open.

Turning the pilot into a standard

Once the pilot has run long enough to show its problems, we write it up as a standard for the remaining lobbies: the panel model and settings, the directory format, the strike and closer, the mounting heights, the cable and labeling scheme, and the connections to the monitoring network. Each new tower is then a known job with a known price, quoted on its own sheet, with only the riser test left to discover. That is how an owner keeps 46 entrances consistent enough that a technician who has worked in one tower can work in any of them.

A Buzzer in a Complex That Already Watches Its Doors: Fitting New Entry Equipment to the Towers' Building Network

Most apartment buildings in Brooklyn have an entry system and nothing else at the door. Spring Creek Towers is different. Honeywell's 2013 announcement described a centralized building management system linked to a local area network, new sensors "that alert operators to doors that should not be open" and to elevator problems, a replacement for an aging alarm system, and a passive optical network supporting video surveillance and phone traffic. Management's 2021 letter added more than 30 cameras. A door buzzer installed at a tower lobby lands in the middle of all of that.

That changes how the installation is wired. A door-position sensor on its own reports only that the door is open. It cannot tell whether the door is open because a resident buzzed in a guest, because someone has propped it, or because someone forced it. The entry system knows the first of those, and a properly wired installation passes that knowledge on. The usual way is to connect the entry controller's release output, or its door-held and door-forced outputs, to the same monitoring panel the existing sensor reports to, so operators see "released" when a resident presses the button and "forced" only when nobody did.

What we connect, and what we leave alone

We connect the entry system's outputs to inputs on the building's monitoring equipment, label them and test them with the operator on the line. We do not reprogram the building management system itself; that belongs to whoever maintains it for the owner, and we coordinate with them on the day. Where the owner wants the new panel to run over the building's existing network rather than new cable, we ask the network's administrator for a port, an address and the security rules, because an entry panel that shares a network with cameras and building controls should be kept apart from them logically even when it shares the fiber.

Two practical rules follow. First, the entry system must keep working when the building network does not. A lobby panel that cannot call apartments because a switch upstairs has failed is a lobby door that gets propped, so the call path to apartments should not depend on the same network as the cameras. Second, the door must still open from inside with one motion when everything is off. Those two rules shape the equipment we recommend for the towers far more than any brochure does.

Your Apartment Door, Not the Lobby: What a Tower Resident Can Have Installed, and What Needs the Office's Yes First

Residents call us because the lobby is where the frustration is: a panel that does not ring, a door that does not latch, a courier who cannot get in. We understand that, and we also have to be straight about it. In a rental complex with one owner, the lobby system is the owner's equipment, and we work on it only when the owner or its managing agent engages us. A resident who hires a contractor to open the lobby panel is asking for trouble with the lease and with the next repair, and we will not do it. Brick Underground's intercom guide makes the same point from the installer's side: building authorization comes before any repair, though, as one Brooklyn locksmith put it, "a tenant can handle the communication."

That communication is usually the most useful thing a resident can do. A written note to the management office that names the building, the lobby, the apartment and exactly what fails ("the lobby calls ring but the release does not open the door," or "visitors cannot hear us from the plaza") gets a repair moving faster than a general complaint, and a 311 complaint creates a city record if the office does not act.

Two questions come up whenever a large lobby project is discussed here: who ends up paying, and whether the work is optional. How a capital project reaches the budget

New York State Homes and Community Renewal describes the Mitchell-Lama rent process this way: every two years the housing company prepares a two-year, line-item budget that includes "capital expenditures and reserve provisions." For a resident, the practical point is that a large entry-system program would show up as a line in a budget that tenants have a formal chance to review. That is the place to ask what the lobby program costs, how many entrances it covers and over how many years. What the intercom law asks of these towers

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling erected after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets residents talk to a visitor at that door and release it from their apartments. The towers were dedicated in October 1974 and each holds far more than eight apartments, so they sit on the post-1968 side of that rule. The intercom is not an amenity the owner can decide to retire. It is required equipment, which is why installation and repair at the lobbies is continuing work rather than a one-time project.

What a HUD inspector looks at

With more than 3,500 apartments under a Section 8 contract, the complex is also inspected under HUD's NSPIRE standards. Those standards treat an apartment entry door that "cannot be secured" by at least one installed lock as a deficiency, flag a self-closing mechanism that "does not pull the door closed and engage the latch," give a fire-labeled door that does not close and latch a 24-hour correction time, and list a damaged, inoperable or missing exterior door component with a 60-day correction time. None of that names an intercom, but every item on that list sits on the same doors an entry installation touches, which is why we check the closer and the latch on every door we work on and write down what we find.

None of this is legal advice. It is the framework we keep in mind when we quote an entrance here, and it is why our quotes list the door hardware as plainly as the electronics.

Planning a lobby program across the towers

Management tested a mock-up lobby before rolling out new finishes. How long should a pilot door system run before the same equipment goes into the next tower?

Long enough to see the door fail the ways it is going to fail. For a tower lobby, we suggest at least a full season of use, with checks of the strike, closer and panel at set points and a log of every service call. A door that behaves in October can stick in January, and a panel that works with a few test calls can show weak lines once every household is using it.

The pilot does not have to hold up planning. Riser tests in the next towers can start while the pilot runs, so that when the standard is settled, each lobby's price is already known.

The 2021 letter said inventory was hard to get. If a part runs out halfway through a rollout, what keeps a tower's door working?

Planning, mostly. The parts that wear or fail first at a busy lobby are the strike, the power supply and the panel's front, so we recommend the owner buy a small stock of each with the first order and keep it on the property. If a part is back-ordered, the lobby that is waiting keeps its old system running until the new one is ready; we do not take a working door apart before the replacement is in hand.

Where a standard part has gone out of production, the written standard names an approved substitute, so the next lobby does not stall waiting for a decision.

The towers' monitoring already flags doors left open. How does a new lobby system tell the operator that a door was buzzed open rather than forced?

By sending its own signal to the same monitoring panel. The entry controller has an output that changes when a resident or staff member releases the door, and most controllers also have door-held and door-forced outputs that work with a position switch on the frame. We wire those outputs to inputs on the building's monitoring equipment and test them with the operator on the phone.

The result is that the operator sees a released door as released, a propped door as held, and only a door opened with no release as forced. That cuts false alarms without hiding the real ones.

Can new lobby panels use the fiber network the complex installed in 2013, instead of new cable up every tower?

Sometimes, for the panel's own connection to management software, and only with the network administrator's agreement. Honeywell's announcement described a passive optical network carrying video and phone traffic, and a networked panel can use a port on a network like that if it is given its own segment and security rules.

What the network does not replace is the path from the lobby to each apartment. That still runs over the riser, a new cable or a separate system, and we design it so that apartments can still be called if the building network is down.
