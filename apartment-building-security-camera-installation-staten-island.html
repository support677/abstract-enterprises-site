The Neighborhood

Why Staten Island Properties Need Apartment Building Security Cameras

Staten Island's rental housing is mostly hidden inside houses. A local brokerage's analysis of the borough's housing puts it plainly: eighty-three percent of Staten Island's housing units sit inside one- or two-family buildings. The two-family house is the island's signature rental — an owner on one floor, a tenant on the other, or two units side by side behind a shared driveway. The same analysis maps where the variations live: up-and-down high-ranches in Great Kills, side-by-side semi-attached pairs mid-block in New Dorp and Dongan Hills, newer semi-attached construction in Charleston and Rossville, larger detached two-families in Tottenville and Prince's Bay, and older detached and converted Victorians on the North Shore around St. George and Stapleton, where it describes the market as investor-heavy.

Bigger apartment buildings are the exception rather than the rule. Commercial Observer reported in January 2026 that rezonings between 1987 and 2003 sharply reduced the parts of the island where townhouses and apartments can be built, and quoted a Holland & Knight attorney saying the zoning makes multifamily construction “virtually impossible”. The same report counted 1,238 units in Staten Island's housing pipeline at the end of 2024 against 40,247 in Brooklyn. What does get built clusters on the North Shore: the Bay Street Corridor rezoning, approved by the City Council on June 26, 2019, covers twenty blocks of Stapleton and Tompkinsville and was expected to bring about 1,800 new homes; its first project, The Pearl at 475 Bay Street, opened in November 2025, and Lighthouse Point in St. George added 115 units.

The third kind of multi-unit property is the one most outsiders miss. A Staten Island real-estate law firm notes that the borough holds more low-rise condominium units than any other borough, almost all of them in planned attached-townhouse communities on the South Shore — Annadale, Huguenot, Eltingville — where one declaration covers a whole community and the association, not the city, owns the roads, the parking areas and the drainage. Those communities have something a Manhattan co-op never has to think about: hundreds of feet of private road and open parking with nobody watching it after dark.

That spread-out stock is exactly why camera systems here behave differently. A system for a Staten Island rental is rarely a lobby dome and a few hallway turrets. It is a driveway, a side gate, a detached garage, a rear yard and a parking row, each at a distance, each outdoors, and all of them expected to land on one recorder that a small landlord or an HOA treasurer can actually use. Package theft is the most common trigger for the call: in August 2025 the Staten Island Advance reported an Elm Park homeowner who had the theft of a delivered package on video and only found it two days later because of a notification problem — and noted that more and more Staten Islanders are turning to locked options for deliveries. Car crime is the second. Police on the island told CBS New York in July 2022 that there had already been more than 250 catalytic converter thefts on Staten Island that year, at an etching event in Graniteville that drew about two hundred people.

Then there is the water. Staten Island's East Shore and South Shore took the worst of Hurricane Sandy in the city: of the 23 storm-related deaths on the island, the city's rebuilding report counts all but one on those two shores, and more than 11,700 buildings there sat in areas that flooded. Every camera plan we write for a property in Midland Beach, Ocean Breeze, New Dorp Beach, Oakwood Beach or the low streets near the shore starts with one question — where does the recorder go so the next storm doesn't take the footage with it.

Two habits matter more on Staten Island than in the denser boroughs. The first is measuring the walk, not the building. A two-family on a deep South Shore lot can need more cable than a six-storey walk-up in another borough, because the cameras that matter are at the edges — the end of the driveway, the gate to the rear yard, the door of the garage forty or eighty feet back. We pace every run on the first visit and mark which ones fall inside a single network-cable length and which need a switch, a fibre link or a wireless bridge in the middle.

The second is deciding who the system belongs to before deciding where the cameras go. In a two-family the owner and the tenant have different rights to the same yard; in a semi-attached pair two owners share a party wall and sometimes a driveway; in a condo community the board owns the common areas and the unit owners own their front doors. Getting that settled first is what keeps a well-built system from becoming a neighbour dispute.

Separate Buildings, One Recorder

Bringing a Garage, a Back Row and a Far Lot Onto One System

On Staten Island the hard part of a building camera system is rarely the camera. It is the gap between structures: the driveway between a house and its detached garage, the courtyard between two rows of townhouses, the alley between a walk-up and the lot behind it. Every camera has to reach the recorder with power and a clean signal across that gap, outdoors, for years. There are four honest ways to do it, and we choose between them per gap, not per job.

Network cable in conduit, when the run is short enough

A single run of network cable carrying power and video is limited to roughly 100 metres (328 feet) end to end, including the patch cords at each end. Most two-family garages, side gates and rear fences fall well inside that. Outdoors, the cable has to be rated for wet locations and run in conduit where it crosses open ground or climbs a wall, with drip loops and sealed entries at each box. Where a driveway has to be crossed, we use an existing sleeve if the property has one, follow the line of a fence or a building wall, or go overhead on a messenger wire between two structures where that is neater than cutting concrete.

A switch in the far building

When the far structure has several cameras — a garage with a camera on each door and one on the lot behind it — it is usually better to run one link to a small outdoor-rated or indoor-mounted switch in that building and fan the cameras out from there. One cable crosses the gap instead of four, and each camera's run starts fresh from the switch. The switch needs local power and, ideally, a small battery backup of its own.

Fibre for the long and the electrically noisy

Beyond the copper limit, or where a run passes near heavy electrical equipment, a pair of fibre strands between buildings carries the video with no distance worry and no path for a lightning surge to ride from one building to the other. It costs more per foot and needs a media converter or fibre-capable switch at each end, so we reserve it for the community entrance three hundred feet from the clubhouse, or the second building across a big lot.

Point-to-point wireless where trenching isn't allowed

Sometimes nothing can be buried and nothing can be strung overhead — the association won't let anyone cut its road, or the gap crosses a neighbour's property. A pair of directional radio bridges, each fixed high on a building with a clear line of sight to the other, can carry a whole cluster of cameras across. Trees are the enemy here: the Greenbelt-edge streets of Mid-Island and the mature planting in older townhouse communities can block a path that was clear in March by June, so we check the line in leaf, not just in winter.

What we avoid

We do not hang battery Wi-Fi cameras on a detached garage and hope the house router reaches it. It may work at first, but a garage door, a parked van or a wet night can drop the link, and battery cameras usually record clips rather than continuous footage. On a rental property, where the footage may one day matter to a police report or an insurer, every camera that covers a common area should be wired, powered and recording continuously.

Shore, Storm and Salt

Camera Hardware That Survives Staten Island Weather

Much of Staten Island lives close to open water: the Kill Van Kull along the North Shore, New York Bay along the East Shore and its two-and-a-half-mile FDR Boardwalk, the open bay off the South Shore beaches, and the Arthur Kill to the west. Equipment that lasts for years on a sheltered inland wall can fail in two or three seasons on a beach-block porch. Most of the failures we are called to fix outdoors are not the camera's electronics at all — they are the bracket, the screws, the connector or the junction box.

Salt air

A widely used outdoor CCTV weatherproofing guide says salt air within about five miles of a coast speeds up corrosion, and recommends 316-grade stainless brackets over the more common 304, dielectric grease on electrical connections and self-amalgamating tape over connectors. By that measure, most of the island qualifies. On properties near the water we specify stainless or coated brackets and fixings, cameras with a sealed cable tail rather than an exposed connector block, and junction boxes that keep every joint out of the weather.

Ratings that mean something

The same guide treats IP66 as the minimum for outdoor cameras and IP67 as preferable for any camera exposed to rain without shelter — and makes the point that matters most in practice: a camera with an IP67 housing but an unsealed cable entry has effectively no weather protection at all. That is why, on an exposed Staten Island wall, we spend more time on the entry point and the box than on the camera body.

Condensation in the shoulder seasons

Cold nights followed by mild days push moisture inside domes, which then fog from the inside and blur the picture. Desiccant packs in junction boxes, breathable vent plugs and domes with anti-fog treatment all help; on the windiest walls we prefer a bullet or turret with a hood over a dome, because there is less glass to fog and less surface for spray to dry on.

The flood line

In a square mile bounded by Midland Avenue, Father Capodanno Boulevard, Seaview Avenue and Hylan Boulevard — Midland Beach, Ocean Breeze and part of Dongan Hills — WNYC reported that streets sit several feet below Father Capodanno Boulevard, so when the surge topped the road on the night of Sandy the water poured into what researchers called a bowl. Elsewhere on the South Shore, the city's rebuilding report describes the surge travelling inland up creeks and tributaries after eroding the bluffs. For a camera system the lesson is simple: the recorder, the PoE switch and the battery backup do not belong in a ground-floor garage or cellar anywhere near those areas.

New York City's Building Code, Appendix G, requires mechanical equipment and outside appliances on regulated work in flood hazard areas to be elevated to or above the design flood elevation — generally the base flood elevation plus two feet — while allowing electrical wiring below it when it conforms to the Electrical Code. A camera recorder is not a boiler, and a small low-voltage installation is not a substantial improvement, but the logic is the same and we follow it: equipment that stores the footage goes up, on an upper-floor shelf or in an upper closet; only cable and outdoor cameras live near grade. Many East Shore homes rebuilt or raised through the Build It Back programme already have a higher utility space, and that is usually where the rack goes.

Power cuts and surges

Storms that reach the island often bring short outages. A camera system with no backup stops recording at exactly the moment you are most likely to want footage. We fit a battery backup to the recorder and the PoE switch sized for a realistic outage window, surge protection on the incoming power and, on long outdoor runs, on the data line where it enters the building.

Compliance callouts for Staten Island building owners

  • Licensed installation. New York regulations (19 NYCRR §195.2) require a licence from the NYS Department of State for work on a closed-circuit television or video surveillance system used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in or theft. AESS holds NYS licence #12000287431. There is no separate city installer licence; the city's business portal routes this licence to the Department of State.
  • Common areas, not homes. Cameras in hallways, lobbies, lots and entrances are generally permitted; cameras must never look where tenants have a reasonable expectation of privacy, including inside apartments and bathrooms, or through a unit's window.
  • No audio without consent. New York is a one-party-consent state for audio recording, and a building camera that records conversations among people who are not party to them can raise eavesdropping concerns. We disable audio on common-area cameras by default.
  • Board and association rules. In condo and HOA communities, the declaration and house rules usually bar owners from altering common areas without approval; the camera system goes through the board.
  • Flood zones. On East Shore and South Shore properties in mapped flood hazard areas, keep recorders, switches and batteries above the design flood elevation that NYC Building Code Appendix G uses for equipment.
  • Electrical work. New outlets and circuits for outdoor poles or a new rack are for a licensed electrician; we coordinate with yours or with one you choose.

This is general information about rules we work under, not legal advice. A building owner with a specific dispute should speak to an attorney.

Your Questions Answered

Apartment Building Security Cameras in Staten Island: Questions Answered

These are the questions Staten Island owners, tenants and boards actually raise, grouped by the decision they belong to. Each answer is written for this borough's buildings; none of them is repeated in the FAQ further down.

Owners, tenants and two households under one roof

Our two-family in Great Kills has the tenant upstairs — whose phone gets the camera app, and who sees the rental unit's door?

The owner holds the administrator account; that is not negotiable, because the owner is responsible for the recorder and for anything handed to police. What the tenant sees is a choice, and modern recorders make it easy: we create a second user with live view of the shared cameras — the front walk, the driveway, the mailbox — and no access to the owner's own door or the rear yard if that belongs to the owner's unit. The camera on the tenant's private entrance is the delicate one. We recommend it covers the approach and the step, not the inside of the door when it opens, and that the lease or a short written note tells the tenant it exists and who can view it. Done that way, the tenant gets package alerts from the camera that watches their deliveries, and nobody records the inside of anyone's home.

Can one recorder cover a front house and a detached rear garage across the driveway, or does each building need its own?

One recorder is almost always better. Two separate systems mean two apps, two sets of passwords, two hard drives to check and two places footage can be lost. The garage needs only a link back to the house: a single outdoor-rated cable in conduit if the run is under the copper limit, or a small switch in the garage fed by one cable if there are several cameras out there. The only time we suggest a second recorder is when the far building has a different owner — a garage sold off with the neighbouring lot, for example — or when the link would have to cross land nobody will give permission for.

Does a Staten Island landlord need a licensed installer, or can the handyman mount the cameras?

State regulation, 19 NYCRR §195.2, requires a Department of State licence for anyone who installs, services or maintains a closed-circuit or video surveillance system used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in or theft — which describes nearly every camera a landlord puts on a rental property. An owner can mount a camera on their own home, but paying an unlicensed handyman to do it for a rental is the situation the rule is aimed at. Beyond the legal point, an unlicensed job is usually where we find the problems later: outdoor runs with no conduit, connectors left open to the weather, and a recorder still on its factory password.

Getting signal and power across the property

What do you use to cross a shared driveway or parking court between two townhouse rows when trenching isn't allowed?

In order of preference: an existing sleeve or conduit under the paving, which some communities have from the original construction; an overhead run on a messenger wire between two buildings, where the span is short and the board accepts the look; or a pair of point-to-point wireless bridges mounted high on each row with clear line of sight. The radio option carries several cameras across a gap with no digging at all, and it is the usual answer in South Shore communities where the association owns the paving and won't have it cut. We test the path with the trees in leaf, because a link that is clean in winter can fade when the canopy fills in.

Will the cameras keep recording when a nor'easter knocks out power along Hylan Boulevard?

Only if they are on a battery backup, and only for as long as that battery lasts. We put the recorder and the PoE switch that powers the cameras on one uninterruptible power supply and size it for the outage you realistically expect — the short flickers and outages of an hour or two that follow most storms. Outdoor cameras fed from the switch stay up with it. Cameras on their own plug-in adapters do not, which is one more reason we power everything from the switch. If an outage outlasts the battery, the recorder shuts down cleanly and restarts on its own when power returns, so you lose the outage period rather than the whole drive.

Shore, storm and flood

How do you keep cameras working within sight of the water in Midland Beach, South Beach or Tottenville, where salt spray eats metal?

By treating the mounting hardware and the connections as the weak point, because they are. Stainless or coated brackets and fixings instead of plain steel; cameras with a sealed cable tail; every joint inside a gasketed junction box with dielectric grease and tape on the connector; drip loops so water runs off the cable before it reaches the box. We prefer hooded bullets or turrets to domes on the most exposed walls, and we mount under eaves or on the lee side of a building wherever the view allows. A yearly check — wiping housings, looking at fixings, re-sealing anything that has started to weep — costs far less than replacing a camera every other summer.

Where should the recorder sit in an East Shore building that took water in Sandy?

On an upper floor, as far above the flood line as the building allows. In the low streets behind Father Capodanno Boulevard and around New Dorp Beach and Oakwood Beach, the ground-floor garage or cellar is the worst place for the box that holds every recording. We run the camera cables up to a closet or shelf on the first habitable floor or above, put the battery backup and switch with it, and leave only the cameras and their sealed boxes near grade. If a raised home already has an elevated utility space from the rebuilding programmes, that is usually the right spot. And because a drive can fail however well it is placed, we can also send copies of key cameras to off-site storage.

Lots, lighting and vehicles

How do we light and cover a surface lot behind a Victory Boulevard walk-up so night footage shows faces, not glare?

Start at night, not in daylight. We walk the lot after dark with the owner and note which areas sit in shadow, which lights point into a camera's path, and where headlights sweep across. A camera aimed past a bright pole light or into oncoming headlights records a white flare; a camera that relies only on its own infrared in a big dark lot records grey shapes. The fix is usually a mix: cameras mounted on the building looking out with the existing lights behind them, a narrower camera on the entrance where cars slow down, and, where the lot is very dark, extra lighting that the owner's electrician adds before we aim anything. Colour-at-night cameras help, but only where there is some ambient light for them to use.

Is a plate-reading camera worth it at the entrance of a South Shore townhouse community with its own private road?

If the community has one or two entrances and cars must slow to enter, yes — it is the single most useful camera such a community can add. A plate camera is a separate, narrow-view camera mounted to catch the rear plate as a car passes a fixed point, with a fast shutter and its own illumination; the wide overview cameras cannot do that job, however many megapixels they have. In a community whose association owns the road, the board decides whether to keep a searchable log of plates or simply record the images, how long to keep them and who can search them. We set it up to match that policy and keep it modest; a plate camera at a private entrance is about knowing which vehicle came in before a break-in, not about tracking residents.

Boards, associations and owner-installed cameras

Our condo association owns the roads and parking in Annadale — who has to approve cameras, and how do we take it to the board?

The board does, acting under the declaration and by-laws. Where the association owns the road, the parking bays and the grounds, those are common elements, and changing them is a board decision; some declarations also require a vote of owners for capital spending above a set amount. What makes approval quick is a clear package: a site drawing with each camera's position and field of view, a statement of what is not covered (no unit windows, no back gardens that belong to individual owners), who may view live and recorded footage, how long recordings are kept, and a fixed price split between equipment, cabling and labour. We prepare that package as part of the quote, and we can present it at a board meeting if that helps.

A unit owner in our Huguenot complex put a camera over their own door aimed at the walkway — can the HOA make them move it?

Often, yes, depending on the declaration and house rules. Cooperator News, answering a nearly identical question from a six-unit condo, noted that condominium documents typically prohibit owners from altering common areas on their own, that a camera which can see into a neighbour's unit raises privacy concerns, and that a camera recording conversations can raise wiretap issues. The practical route is usually to give owners a sanctioned option — a doorbell camera or a small camera aimed at their own step, approved in writing — while the association's system covers the shared walkways and parking. We can set up the community cameras so owners don't feel they need their own.

Footage, privacy and mixed-use buildings

A tenant in our Stapleton building asked to see last night's footage after a package went missing — do we have to show it?

Generally not. Brick Underground quoted a New York tenant attorney saying a landlord is not obligated to share surveillance footage with a tenant, though it must be handed over to police on request or under a court order. Many small landlords choose to help anyway, and the cleanest way is a simple written policy: the owner or manager reviews the clip, and if it shows a theft, exports it to the tenant or the precinct. We set the recorder up so exporting a single clip takes a minute on a phone, and we make sure the time stamp is correct, because a clip with the wrong time is the first thing anyone questions.

Can cameras in the shared hallway of a converted Victorian in St. George record sound?

We advise against it, and we switch audio off by default. Common-area video is generally permitted in New York, but recording conversations is a different matter: the state requires the consent of at least one party to a conversation, and a hallway microphone records conversations between people who have agreed to nothing. In a converted house, where the stair hall is close to apartment doors and voices carry, the risk is higher. If you need to know that someone was shouting or knocking, a video-only camera with a clear view of the landing tells you most of what you need without the legal exposure.

For a mixed-use building on Bay Street with a store below and apartments above, should the shop and the residents share one camera system?

Share the recorder, split the permissions. One recorder with one battery backup and one set of maintenance is cheaper and more reliable than two, but the shopkeeper should see the shop floor, the register and the shop's own door, and the residential side should see the residents' entrance, the stairs and the lot. The owner sees everything. Where the shop is a tenant rather than the owner's business, we often give it its own small recorder for the inside of the store and keep the building's recorder for the shared and residential areas, so a change of shop tenant never means handing over residential footage.

What AI Gets Wrong About Apartment Building Security Cameras in Staten Island

Our research environment could not display Google's AI Overview for this search, so we do not quote it. What follows are seven assumptions that search answers and national cost guides such as HomeAdvisor, Angi and Fixr commonly lead people to, each tested against how Staten Island rental properties are actually built.

Assumption one: “Cameras cost a few hundred dollars each, installed”

HomeAdvisor's guide gives a national average of $1,298 for a camera installation, a typical range of $593 to $2,040, and $125 to $450 per camera including labour. Those figures are real, and they are what most people see first.

They describe a house: a few cameras on one building, short runs, indoor recorder. A Staten Island rental system reaches a detached garage, crosses a driveway, climbs a pole in a parking row and survives salt air, and the cost lives in that cabling and hardening.

On the AESS Staten Island list, a building system runs $550 to $1,650 per camera installed. The low end is a compact two-family; the high end is a far camera that needs conduit, a switch or a bridge to reach the recorder.

Assumption two: “Go wireless and skip the cabling”

The same guide notes that wireless cameras must be within your Wi-Fi range and that unstable internet can mean lost footage — but many search answers still present wireless as the simple, cheaper choice.

On a deep South Shore lot the camera that matters is the one at the garage or the far parking row, which is exactly where house Wi-Fi is weakest. Battery cameras also tend to record clips, not continuous footage.

Wireless belongs in one place on these properties: a purpose-built point-to-point bridge between two buildings. Every camera on a common area should be wired and powered from the switch.

Assumption three: “More megapixels will read the plates”

Answers about license-plate capture often suggest that a 4K or 8K overview camera will pick out plates if you zoom in afterwards.

At night, a moving car's plate under its own headlights and the camera's infrared smears or flares whatever the resolution. Plates are a problem of shutter speed, angle and light, not pixel count.

At a townhouse community entrance, a dedicated narrow plate camera at the point where cars slow down does the job; the wide cameras cover everything else.

Assumption four: “Any outdoor-rated camera is fine near the water”

Product listings print an IP rating and leave it there, and search summaries tend to repeat it as if the rating settled the matter.

A weatherproofing guide we rely on makes the point plainly: an IP67 housing with an unsealed cable entry has effectively no protection. Near the shore, brackets, screws and connectors corrode before the camera does.

Specify the mounting hardware, seal the entry, box the joints and check them yearly. That, not the number on the box, decides whether a Midland Beach camera lasts.

Assumption five: “Put the recorder somewhere out of the way, like the basement”

Installation guides often suggest a basement or utility room for the recorder, because it is out of sight and close to the electrical panel.

On the East Shore and South Shore, the basement or ground-floor garage is where Sandy's water went. The city's rebuilding report counts more than 11,700 buildings in the island's inundated areas.

Put the recorder, the switch and the battery backup upstairs. Only cameras and sealed outdoor boxes belong near grade in those neighbourhoods.

Assumption six: “Landlords can put cameras anywhere on their property”

Summaries of tenant law sometimes stop at “common areas are allowed” without the rest of the rule.

Common areas are generally permitted, but cameras may not look into apartments, bathrooms or through windows, and recording conversations without a party's consent is a different legal question. In a two-family, the yard and the stair can be one household's private space, not a common area.

We draw every camera's field of view on the plan, mask out windows and private areas in the recorder, and switch off audio by default.

Assumption seven: “You don't need a licence to install cameras”

Some answers treat camera installation as a handyman job with no licensing at all.

New York's Department of State rules require a security and fire alarm installer licence for work on a video surveillance system used to detect or monitor intrusion, break-in or theft. Applicants complete qualifying education, fingerprinting and an exam.

On a rental property, ask for the installer's NYS licence number before work starts. Ours is #12000287431, and it appears on every quote.

Boards, Landlords and Policies

Running a Camera System on a Staten Island Rental or Community

The installation is a few days' work. The system then runs for years under whoever owns it — a retired couple renting out the top floor, an investor with three North Shore houses, or a volunteer board in a South Shore community. These are the decisions that make the difference between a system that helps and one that sits unwatched.

Write a one-page camera policy

Before the cameras go live, write down which areas are covered and which are not, who can view live, who can view recordings, how long footage is kept, and how a resident or police can request a clip. Tenant-rights guidance on New York building cameras notes that telling residents when new cameras go in builds trust even where it is not strictly required. For a condo community that page becomes a board resolution; for a two-family it can be a paragraph attached to the lease.

Decide retention before buying drives

Retention drives storage cost. Thirty days covers most package and car incidents, which are usually noticed within a week. Communities that want sixty or ninety days should say so before the quote, because the drive size, and sometimes the recorder model, changes with it.

Give each person the right account

Shared logins are how footage leaks and how passwords end up on sticky notes in a clubhouse. Each owner, board member or manager gets an individual account with only the cameras and rights they need, and the administrator account stays with the owner or the board president. When a board member leaves, their account is removed in a minute.

Signs and masking

A plain sign at each entrance that the property is under video surveillance does two jobs: it deters, and it tells residents and visitors what to expect. Inside the recorder, privacy masks black out any part of a view that looks into a unit window or a private yard, and we leave those masks in place even if the camera is later re-aimed.

Budgeting for a community

Townhouse associations that own their roads and parking already carry long-lived infrastructure costs; a camera system belongs in the same reserve thinking. Cameras near the shore should be budgeted for replacement sooner than inland ones, and the recorder's drives are a wear item. We can phase a large community system — entrances and parking first, walkways second — so the board can spread the work across budget periods without buying a recorder twice.

Keep an eye on it

The SILive story of the Elm Park homeowner who found a package theft two days late because notifications weren't working is the common failure in small systems: nobody notices a camera is offline or an alert has stopped. Turn on the recorder's camera-offline and drive-health alerts, send them to two people, and look at every camera once a month.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Pace the Lot, Then Look Up

This is general technician guidance — the method every AESS crew uses on Staten Island — not a story about any particular property.

Before anyone opens a box of cameras, walk the property with a measuring wheel from the spot where the recorder will live to every place a camera might go, and write the number down. Then add the climb: up the wall, along the soffit, down into the box. Any run that comes close to the copper limit gets a switch, fibre or a bridge on the plan now, not on installation day. Next, look up. Note what each camera will hang from — vinyl siding over sheathing, brick, a timber porch beam, a steel pole — and whether that surface faces the water or the prevailing weather. Note the eaves, because a camera tucked under one lasts longer than one on a bare gable. Then come back after dark, stand where a car pulls in and where a person walks to the door, and see what the existing lights really do. Finally, find the high ground for the rack. On the East Shore and South Shore that means asking the owner where the water reached in 2012 and putting the recorder well above it. A plan made that way rarely changes once the ladder comes out.

FAQ

Staten Island Apartment Building Security Camera FAQ

How long does a Staten Island installation take when the parking lot is behind a separate garage building?
+

Plan on more time for the link than for the cameras. A two-family with a detached garage and four to six cameras is usually a one-day job when the cable can follow a fence or wall to the garage. Add a day when the run needs new conduit across open ground, when a lift is needed for a pole in the lot, or when a wireless bridge has to be aligned and tested between buildings. The written quote gives you the expected number of days, so you can tell tenants or residents when to expect the crew.

Can you reuse the coax already strung between buildings at an older North Shore garden complex?
+

Sometimes. Old coaxial cable from an analogue system can carry modern video using adapters that send network signals and power over coax, which saves pulling new cable across a courtyard. Whether it works depends on the cable's condition: we test each run for breaks, water ingress and corroded connectors before relying on it. Coax that has been outdoors for decades without conduit is often too far gone, and in that case a new outdoor-rated network cable, fibre or a wireless bridge is the more reliable choice.

What happens to our cameras when the Staten Island Railway or a utility crew digs up the trench line beside the property?
+

If our cable runs in conduit along your own wall or fence, outside work next door usually doesn't touch it. The risk is a buried run near a property line or under a sidewalk strip. We record buried routes on the drawing we leave with you, so you can show any contractor where they are before digging, and we avoid burying cable near a boundary where outside crews are likely to work. If a run is cut, a single cable can be repaired or re-pulled in conduit, and the rest of the system keeps recording.

Do you cross the Verrazzano for a single small job on Staten Island, and is there a bridge or travel line on the invoice?
+

Yes, we take single-property jobs on Staten Island, including two-family houses with only a few cameras. There is no separate bridge or travel line on the invoice. The 1.10 Staten Island multiplier on the published prices already accounts for the crossing and the longer drives between properties, and it is built into every figure on this page and into the fixed quote we send after the walkthrough.

Can our HOA spread the camera cost over two budget years in a South Shore townhouse community?
+

Yes, by phasing the work rather than by financing it. We design the whole system at once, with the recorder, switches and links sized for the final number of cameras, then install it in stages: usually the entrances and parking first, and the walkways and secondary areas in the following budget year. Each phase gets its own fixed price, and nothing installed in the first phase has to be replaced to add the second.

Does wooded Greenbelt-edge property on Staten Island change where cameras can go?
+

It changes the aiming and the links more than the cameras. Trees close to a building throw moving shadows that set off motion alerts, block wireless links once the leaves come in, and drop branches on cable runs. We aim cameras along the building and the driveway rather than into the trees, set motion zones to ignore swaying branches, and keep any cable between buildings on a wall or in conduit rather than strung through branches. Wireless bridges are checked for line of sight in summer, when the canopy is fullest.

Our board wants to see footage from home — will remote viewing work on the internet service most Staten Island complexes already have?
+

Usually, yes. Remote viewing needs upload speed rather than download speed, and only for the cameras someone is actually watching; recording happens locally and uses no internet at all. We test the property's upload speed during the walkthrough and set the cameras to send a lighter stream to phones, keeping full quality on the recorder. If the service is weak, board members can still review recordings on site, or the community can add a modest internet line just for the camera system.

Can a camera system on Staten Island help with illegal dumping at the back of a lot near Fresh Kills or the West Shore industrial edge?
+

It can help identify the vehicle, which is what matters for a report. Dumping usually happens at the least-watched edge of a lot, often at night, so a camera there needs to see a vehicle's plate and shape at the point it pulls in, not just the pile left behind. That means a camera aimed at the access point with enough light for night images, and a recorder that keeps footage long enough for the dumping to be noticed. We also suggest a visible sign, which on its own often moves the problem elsewhere.

Who looks after the system if the property manager for our Staten Island complex changes?
+

The system should belong to the owner or the association, not to the manager. We set the administrator account in the owner's or board's name, give the manager their own user account, and leave the written camera list, passwords and drawing with the owner or board. When a manager leaves, their account is removed and the new manager gets a fresh one. Service visits are booked by whoever holds the administrator account.

Will snow plows clearing a private community road damage pole-mounted cameras at the entrance?
+

Not if the pole and its cable are placed with plowing in mind. We keep entrance poles and cabinets back from the road edge where plows push snow, protect any exposed conduit near the base, and mount cameras high enough to stay clear of piled snow. Plate cameras are aimed so a snowbank at the curb doesn't block the view of passing cars. If a community already has bollards or a guard island at the entrance, that is usually the safest place for the pole.
