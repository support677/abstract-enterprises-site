Between the First World War and 1932, a city tax exemption on new housing filled Ocean Avenue and the streets around it with six-story elevator apartment houses, and many of them are still standing, still rented, and still running on whatever intercom was last put in the vestibule. The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need Intercom Installation

Flatbush is a neighborhood of renters who live behind someone else's front door. The NYU Furman Center's profile of Community District 14, which takes in Flatbush and Midwood, put the homeownership rate at 24.4 percent in 2024, and an older Furman profile from 2010 counted 82.5 percent of the district's rental apartments as rent-regulated. In practice that means most households in Flatbush reach home through a street door, a vestibule and a lobby controlled by an owner or a managing agent, and they let their visitors in with whatever the building gives them. When the intercom works, nobody thinks about it. When it fails, every household in the building is affected at once: deliveries are left outside, home health aides wait on the sidewalk, elderly tenants walk down five flights to open the door, and somebody props the door open with a folded menu so the next person can get in. A broken lobby intercom in a sixty-apartment building is not one family's inconvenience; it is a building-wide hole in the front door.

The buildings themselves explain why Flatbush intercoms are their own kind of job. According to Wikipedia's history of the neighborhood, a property-tax exemption for new residential construction, which ran from the end of the First World War until 1932, produced a wave of six-story elevator apartment houses in Art Deco and Colonial Revival styles along Ocean Avenue and around the edges of the older suburban tracts. These are big buildings by Brooklyn standards. CityRealty lists 1155 Ocean Avenue as a six-story co-op of 59 apartments built in 1931, and 1111 Ocean Avenue as six floors and 103 apartments. In a building that size the intercom is a small network: a lobby panel, a power supply, a terminal board, and a riser of multi-pair cable climbing the building on each line of apartments, with a splice at every floor. Decades of repairs have been layered onto that riser, and the first task on any Flatbush lobby job is finding out what it still is.

The lobby is also where the neighborhood's security problems arrive. In November 2025, PIX11 reported that the superintendent of an apartment building on Ocean Avenue died after confronting a man he suspected of stealing packages from the lobby, and that thefts at that building had generally involved people buzzing apartments until they were let in. In January 2026, BK Reader and News 12 told the story of a Flatbush tenant who found an intruder already in her kitchen while she was home, after years of complaints that the building's front door was flimsy. And Brooklyn Paper, describing two sixteen-apartment prewar buildings on East 22nd Street, reported tenants saying that squatters walked in and lived in the basement. None of these stories is about an intercom brand. They are about who gets through the front door, and how. An intercom does not stop a determined intruder by itself, but a panel that identifies the caller, a release that every apartment can use properly, a door that latches behind each entry and a record of which apartment let someone in change the odds in the lobby considerably.

The fourth thing that sets Flatbush apart is how decisions get made. Almost nobody who calls us about a Flatbush lobby intercom owns the building alone. Rental buildings are run by managing agents who answer to owners elsewhere; co-ops on Ocean Avenue and the side streets are run by boards that budget once a year; and tenant groups, such as the Flatbush Tenant Association that News 12 reported was on a rent strike at 1111 Ocean Avenue in 2024 over leaky pipes and broken elevators, often know more about what is broken than the people who can sign a contract. So this page is written for all of them: owners who need a quote they can defend, boards who have to explain the cost to shareholders, and tenants who need to know what the law expects and what a reasonable repair looks like.

Six Flatbush facts that change an intercom plan before a panel is chosen

  • Most households rent. The Furman Center put homeownership in Community District 14 at 24.4 percent in 2024 and rental vacancy at 3.1 percent, so the intercom decision usually belongs to an owner, agent or board, not to the people pressing the buttons.
  • The big buildings came in one wave. The tax exemption that ended in 1932 produced six-story elevator buildings along Ocean Avenue and nearby streets, which is why so many lobby systems here serve fifty to a hundred-plus apartments on risers of similar age.
  • The shopping streets are lined with apartments over stores. The city's Avenue NYC assessment counted 1,662 storefronts on Church, Flatbush, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues, and many of them sit below apartments reached through a narrow street door beside the shop.

Same-Day Repair in Flatbush

Need Repair in Flatbush? A Dead Lobby Panel, One Line of Apartments That Can't Hear, or a Phone-Entry Box That Stopped Dialing

Repair calls from Flatbush apartment buildings fall into a few patterns, and the pattern usually tells us where to look before we arrive. The first is the whole building going quiet at once: no apartment can hear the lobby, and nobody can release the door. That almost always points to the power supply or the amplifier at the head end, and Brick Underground's guide to NYC intercoms makes the same point, that faults tend to sit at the lobby base, the apartment station, the strike or the power supply rather than in the core wiring inside the walls. The second pattern is one vertical line of apartments failing together, every A apartment from the second floor to the sixth, while the B and C lines work. That is a riser fault, usually a corroded splice in a junction box on one floor, or a pair cut during a plumbing repair in a chase. The third is a single apartment that cannot call or release, which is usually the handset, its wall plate, or a tenant's own renovation that buried or snapped the pair. The fourth is particular to older lobby buildings: a telephone-entry panel that used to dial each apartment's phone and now plays a recording or says nothing at all, because the copper line feeding it was disconnected, changed or stopped being paid for.

Before anyone calls, it is worth ruling out the simple things. Press the button firmly and hold it as long as the system expects; on some panels a quick tap does nothing. Look for a button that is stuck in or sits lower than its neighbors; Brick Underground notes that sticky buttons can sometimes be freed with a cotton swab and rubbing alcohol. Check whether the problem is the call or the release: if you can talk to the visitor but the door will not open, the fault is at the door, not the intercom. Then, if the street door is not latching while the intercom is down, deal with that first, because an unlocked lobby door is the real danger. Call (347) 934-8335. A $250 service call covers an on-site diagnostic visit, and you can book and pay for it online. Many single faults, such as a failed power supply, a corroded riser splice or a strike whose keeper has drifted, are found and fixed in that visit or the next; repair and upgrade work on existing systems runs $150 – $1,200 depending on what is found. If fixing the old system would cost close to replacing it, or its parts are no longer made, we put both options in writing so the owner or board can decide once.

Intercom Installation for Flatbush's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every intercom request we get from Flatbush. They differ in how many households share the door, how the wire travels, what the lobby is built from and who has authority to approve the work. The cards give the outline; the sections below explain how the job changes in each.

The six-story elevator building

Fifty to a hundred-plus apartments around an elevator and a stair, a vestibule with two doors, and a riser on every line of apartments, like 1111 Ocean Avenue.

Prewar and postwar buildings run by a shareholder board, such as the 59-apartment, 1931 co-op at 1155 Ocean Avenue, where the budget and the vote come first.

Two or three floors of apartments above shops on the commercial avenues, reached by a street door squeezed between storefronts.

The six-story elevator building: one panel, a hundred households, and a riser on every line

This is the Flatbush building we are asked about most, and the one where planning matters most. A typical one has a street door, a vestibule with mailboxes, and an inner lobby door, with the intercom panel either outside the street door or between the two doors. Behind the panel is a head end: a power supply, perhaps an amplifier or controller, and a terminal board where every apartment's pair lands. From the terminal board, multi-pair cable climbs the building in risers, usually one per vertical line of apartments, and at each floor a junction box splits off the pairs for that floor's apartments. When those buildings went up between the wars, the system was probably a simple bell or buzzer; most have since been replaced at least once, and the riser has been reused through every replacement. Our survey of a building like this tests every apartment's pair from the terminal board, finds which risers are sound and which are not, and looks at every floor's junction box on at least one line. The result is a plan that reuses the risers that pass, replaces the ones that do not, and puts a new head end in a place the super can reach and keep dry. A building of a hundred apartments cannot be changed over in an afternoon. Brick Underground quotes installers putting a hundred-unit replacement at about two days, and in our experience the in-service plan, keeping the door answerable while the change-over happens, is as important as the hardware.

The co-op apartment house: a board, a budget and a shareholder vote

Co-ops on Ocean Avenue and the side streets often date from the same 1920s and 1930s wave, and many converted to cooperative ownership later. The building is physically the same as a rental, but the decision is different. A co-op board approves the work, the managing agent usually coordinates it, and the cost comes out of reserves or an assessment that shareholders will ask about. Habitat magazine, which covers New York co-ops, published a 2024 piece titled "A Working Intercom Isn't an Amenity. It's Required by Law," answering a shareholder whose fifty-four-unit building had gone about five years without one. For a board, that framing matters. The intercom is not an optional improvement to defer until the lobby is redecorated; it is building equipment the corporation is expected to maintain. We write co-op quotes so they can be shown to the board as they are: equipment by model, risers to be reused or replaced, door hardware, the power location, the in-service plan and a list of what the corporation will own and control afterward, including the administrator login for any app-based panel.

The prewar walk-up: sixteen apartments, four or five floors and nobody on site

Between the big elevator buildings are older walk-ups, often of brick with a stoop or a few steps up to the street door, and usually without a resident super. Brooklyn Paper's description of two sixteen-apartment buildings on East 22nd Street gives a sense of the conditions these can fall into, with tenants describing apartment doors that will not close by themselves and strangers living in the basement. In buildings like these, the intercom is often the most neglected system, because nobody lives there who is responsible for it. A walk-up of this size falls squarely in the $1,200 – $4,500 band for a building-wide system with a handset per apartment. The work usually includes a new button panel sized to the apartment count, a power supply in a locked spot rather than an open cellar, a handset per apartment, and a riser rebuilt where the old pairs have been spliced and respliced into uselessness. Because nobody is on site, we also recommend that the release time be short and the street door close firmly behind every release, and that the cellar door, if it opens to the street or a yard, be secured on its own.

The building over the stores: a residential door between two shops

Along Flatbush Avenue, Church Avenue, Nostrand Avenue and Rogers Avenue, the city's Avenue NYC assessment counted 1,662 storefronts, and many of them have two or three floors of apartments overhead. The residential entrance is usually a narrow door between two shopfronts, opening onto a stair. These doors take a beating. They face a busy sidewalk, deliveries for the shops are often left in front of them, and a door that does not latch is quickly used by people who have no business upstairs. The intercom here has to belong to the residents: its own panel, its own power supply and its own release, separate from anything the store uses, so a shop's deliveries cannot buzz their way into the stair. In buildings with only a few apartments upstairs, an audio buzzer system in the $400 – $900 band is often enough; where tenants want to see who is at a door on a crowded sidewalk, video is worth the step up. We also look closely at the door itself, because a residential door on a commercial avenue is often lighter than the traffic it sees.

The new rental at the Sears site: networked entry from the first day

The former Sears department store at Beverley Road and Bedford Avenue, built in 1932, landmarked in 2012 and closed in 2021, is now the center of one of the largest residential projects in the neighborhood. Commercial Observer reported in April 2026 that the redevelopment will hold 877 rental apartments in four buildings, at 2366 and 2363 Bedford Avenue, 2201 Beverley Road and 158 Lott Street, with 354 apartments at 2366 Bedford Avenue slated to open this year. Buildings like these arrive with entry systems designed by their architects, usually networked panels that call apps and phones, and their owners have their own contractors. We do not install into a building under someone else's construction contract, but we do get calls from new residents and from businesses moving into the ground floors, and those calls are answered on this page as well. The honest advice for a resident is usually to take the question to management, who can often add a second phone number to an apartment or change how calls are routed in minutes.

The house in a protected district: a porch station people can see from the sidewalk

Flatbush also holds Prospect Park South, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission in 1979, and borders the landmarked Victorian tracts that have their own pages on our site. Freestanding houses here are usually single-family or two-family, with a porch and a front door set back from the street. The intercom is small, typically in the $600 – $1,800 range for a single or two-family video system, but the porch is part of a protected streetscape. A door station that sits flush, in a finish that suits the woodwork, and that is mounted so it can be removed without scarring original material, is the sensible choice. If a building is in a historic district and the work touches the exterior, the owner should check with the Landmarks Preservation Commission before drilling; we will describe the station, the mounting and the cable path in writing for that conversation.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Why We Start a Flatbush Lobby Survey at the Terminal Board, Not the Panel

The lobby panel is what everyone sees, so it is where owners expect the survey to begin. In a six-story Flatbush building we usually start somewhere less visible: the terminal board where every apartment's pair lands, often in the cellar near the meters, sometimes in a closet off the lobby or above a ceiling tile. That board tells us more in ten minutes than the panel does in an hour. We can see how many pairs leave for each riser, whether they are labeled, whether someone has cross-connected two apartments to fix a problem years ago, and whether the power supply is the original, a later replacement, or something wired in from a hardware store. Then we test pairs from the board to the apartments, one riser at a time, with a meter and, where needed, a tone generator. The pattern of passes and failures usually maps straight onto the building's history: a line of apartments that was gut-renovated in one decade, a floor where a leak got into a junction box, an apartment that was split in two and never given its own pair. Only after that do we go to the panel and the doors. This is our practice, not a written rule, but it is what keeps a quote from changing the first time a fourth-floor junction box is opened.

Risers and Phone Lines

Ninety-Year-Old Risers, Lobby Ceilings and Copper Phone Lines: Wiring a Flatbush Apartment House

The wiring in a Flatbush lobby building is usually better than its reputation and worse than its owner hopes. Brick Underground's interviews with New York intercom technicians make the point that the core wiring inside the walls is typically fine, even in buildings running older Lee Dan systems, and that a full rewiring is rarely needed. Our experience in prewar Brooklyn buildings matches that, with one important qualification: the cable between floors is often sound, but the joints are not. Every floor has a junction box where the riser is split to that floor's apartments, and every apartment has a wall plate where its pair ends. Those joints were made by hand, sometimes decades apart, and they are where moisture, corrosion and careless repairs collect. So the question we answer on a survey is not "is the old wire good?" but "which pairs pass from the terminal board to the apartment, and which joints have to be rebuilt to make the rest pass?"

That answer decides what system the building can have. A two-wire video system carries picture, voice, call and release on a single pair, and it was designed precisely for buildings like these, where one pair per apartment already exists in the riser. If the pairs test clean for continuity and leakage, and the runs are not too long for the conductor size, a six-story building can move from audio handsets to video monitors with new splices and new apartment stations but without pulling new cable through every apartment. If a riser fails badly, the usual fix is to replace that riser alone, not the whole building. And if the building wants a networked system, with stations that talk over data cable and can be managed from anywhere, the risers have to carry network cable, which usually means new runs, though often in the same paths the old ones used. We tell the owner which of these the building can support before anyone chooses a brand.

Many Flatbush lobbies have a dropped ceiling, added during some past renovation to hide pipes and old wiring. The space above it is often the best path from the panel to the head end and from the head end to the base of each riser, because it keeps cable out of the plaster and out of sight. Where there is no dropped ceiling, we look for existing chases, old conduit or the route the original installers used. In Art Deco lobbies with original plaster, terrazzo or metalwork, we take extra care, and we avoid cutting into decorative surfaces when a less visible path exists. The panel itself is usually mounted in the opening the old one used, with a trim plate if the new panel is a different size, so that the building's entrance does not end up with a patchwork of filled holes around the door.

The phone-entry panel and the copper line behind it

A large share of Flatbush lobby buildings use telephone-entry panels: a keypad and a scrolling directory at the door that place an ordinary phone call to the resident, who presses a number to open the door. These systems were popular because they need no wiring to each apartment. They do, however, need a phone line of their own for the panel, and in older installations that is a copper landline. ButterflyMX, which sells cloud intercoms and so has an interest in the subject, describes copper service as becoming harder for carriers to support over time, and notes that when the line fails, entry calls stop. It also advises owners to check their own carrier's notices rather than assume a single national shutdown date, which is the right advice. When we survey a phone-entry building, we find the line that feeds the panel, check who pays for it and whether the carrier has sent notices, and lay out the options. A cellular communicator can stand in for the copper line and keep the existing panel working. A network adapter can do the same where the building has reliable internet at the panel. Or the panel can be replaced with a system that calls apps and phone numbers directly, without any phone line at the door. One caution applies to all three: the fire alarm communicator and the elevator emergency phone may share the same old phone service, and those are life-safety lines that must be handled by the people responsible for them, not moved casually along with the intercom.

Who answers: handsets, monitors, phones and apps

In a Flatbush building, the same lobby panel may have to reach a retired tenant who has had the same wall handset for thirty years, a nurse who works nights and wants calls on her mobile, and a family whose relatives visit every weekend and need to be let in quickly. Most current systems can mix these. An apartment can keep a handset or monitor on the wall, have calls forwarded to a phone, or both. A Brownstoner forum thread from 2010 captured the problem with phone-only systems well: a tenant whose landlord switched the building to a panel that dialed a phone number worried that if the call went to his partner's mobile while she was out, he could not let anyone in. Good directories can list more than one number for an apartment, ringing them in order or at once, and a wall station inside the apartment means whoever is home can answer. We ask the owner or board how residents want to be reached before choosing a system, because the answer narrows the choice more than any feature list.

The Rules at the Lobby Door

Fifty Apartments, Sixteen Apartments, Two Apartments: What New York Rules Mean for Flatbush Intercoms

The rules that apply to a Flatbush intercom depend mostly on how many apartments share the door and when the building was built or converted. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a sets the main line. Apartment buildings of eight or more units erected or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking street doors and an intercommunication system. Older buildings of eight or more units, which describes most of Flatbush's six-story stock and many of its walk-ups, must provide the same when a majority of tenants ask for it in writing. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 sets the mounting heights: no push button more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet. Those figures matter when a new panel goes into an old opening, because the old opening was not always at the right height.

Once an intercom is installed, it has to work. Habitat magazine's 2024 article put it plainly: the city's housing maintenance code requires a functioning intercom, and courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service, so that a long outage can breach the warranty of habitability. Sources that track HPD enforcement describe a non-working intercom as a Class B hazardous violation. For tenants, Habitat lists the usual path: a written request to the owner or board, a complaint to 311, and, if nothing happens, an HP proceeding in Housing Court, which tenants can start without a lawyer. HPD told News 12 in 2024, speaking about the 1111 Ocean Avenue tenants, that residents should keep filing 311 complaints so the agency can inspect and issue violations. None of this is legal advice, and a tenant facing a long outage should talk to a tenant organization or a lawyer, but it does mean that owners and boards in Flatbush should treat a dead lobby panel as an obligation, not a project for next year.

Rent regulation adds one more question that comes up in Flatbush more than almost anywhere: who pays. In a rent-stabilized building, an owner who replaces a building-wide system may apply to the state housing agency, DHCR, for a Major Capital Improvement increase. DHCR's Fact Sheet 26 says such increases are capped at 2 percent a year, are spread over 12 years in buildings of 35 or fewer units and 12½ years in larger ones, must be removed from the rent after 30 years, and are not allowed where the building has outstanding hazardous or immediately hazardous violations, or where 35 percent or fewer of the apartments are regulated. The fact sheet does not list intercoms as eligible or ineligible, so whether a particular intercom replacement qualifies is a question for DHCR and the owner's advisers, not for us. What we can do is write an invoice that describes the work clearly, building-wide equipment separated from apartment-level items, so that whoever reviews it, whether the agency, the owner or a tenants' group, can see exactly what was done.

Buildings under eight apartments, and the doors in every building

Smaller buildings, the two-families and four-families and the store buildings with two or three apartments upstairs, fall below the eight-unit line in §50-a, so the intercom requirement there does not apply in the same way. The door rules still do. HPD's guidance, set out in its ABCs of Housing, is that building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors in multiple dwellings must be self-closing, and HPD's self-closing door rules require owners to keep them working. An electric strike, fitted well, does not interfere with that. It replaces the fixed strike plate in the frame, holds the latch exactly as the old plate did until someone presses release, lets it pass for a few seconds, and locks again as the closer brings the door back. Problems come from strikes set in the wrong place, closers adjusted too gently, and release times so long that somebody props the door. We set all three so the door closes and latches by itself every time.

Compliance notes for Flatbush stores and mixed-use buildings

Commercial door stations must not interfere with exits. A door that is part of a required way out of a shop or an office must still open from the inside without a key or special knowledge, whatever the intercom does, and an electric strike that releases the outside lever while the inside hardware stays free is the usual way to achieve that. Where a store occupies a building with apartments above, the residential entrance remains subject to the self-closing door rules no matter how the shop uses its own doors. Accessibility matters as well: a station at a public entrance should be reachable and usable, and the mounting heights in 1 RCNY 42-01 give a sensible range for buttons and speakers. Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Flatbush: Questions Answered

These questions come from what Flatbush owners, board members and tenants ask us, and from the questions that turned up in our research: Habitat magazine's co-op advice column, Brick Underground's intercom guide, a Brownstoner forum thread on phone-dialing buzzers, local news about Flatbush buildings and the state's rules on rent increases. A few at the end are our own, written for situations particular to Flatbush.

Lobby buildings, boards and budgets

I own shares in an Ocean Avenue co-op, and the board keeps moving the intercom repair to next year's budget. Is a working intercom something a co-op can put off?

Not comfortably. Habitat magazine answered almost exactly this question in 2024 for a shareholder whose fifty-four-unit building had gone about five years without an intercom, and its answer was that the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace it: state law requires intercoms in many apartment buildings, the city's housing code requires that they function, and courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service. The article suggested a written demand to the board, then a 311 complaint, then an HP proceeding in Housing Court if needed, and pointed out that some proprietary leases have their own terms on services. From our side, the most useful thing a shareholder can do is help the board see a real number. A survey and a written quote turn "the intercom" from an open-ended worry into a defined project, often with a cheaper repair option beside a full replacement, and boards act faster on a page with prices than on a complaint.

Our lobby panel calls residents over a copper phone line, and the phone company keeps sending notices about that line. What are our choices?

There are three, and the right one depends on how well the panel itself still works. If the panel is in good condition and residents are happy with phone calls, a cellular communicator can take the place of the copper line, so the panel keeps dialing as before without depending on the old service. If the building has reliable internet near the panel, a network adapter can do the same job. If the panel is old, its keypad is worn or its directory is hard to update, replacing it with a system that calls apps and phone numbers directly, or calls wall stations over the existing risers, removes the phone line problem altogether. ButterflyMX, an intercom maker, advises owners to read their own carrier's notices rather than assume a single deadline, which is sensible. Before anything changes, find out what else uses that phone service: in some buildings the fire alarm communicator or the elevator phone shares it, and those lines are handled separately by the people responsible for them.

We have about a hundred apartments, and most tenants are at work on weekdays. How do you get into every apartment to change the stations?

With notice, appointments and patience. Brick Underground quotes installers putting a hundred-unit replacement at about two working days, but that assumes access, and access is the hard part. We work with the managing agent or board to post notices in the lobby and on each floor at least a week ahead, listing the days we will be in each line of apartments and a phone number to book a specific time. Apartments where nobody is home can usually be entered with the super, if the lease and the building's policy allow it and the tenant has been told. Tenants who cannot be reached or refuse entry are booked again rather than skipped, and the lobby panel is programmed so their button still reaches their old station, or their phone, until their apartment is done. A large building is usually changed over line by line, so the door stays answerable for everyone throughout.

Our building has a doorman for only part of the day. How should the lobby panel behave when the desk is empty?

Openigloo lists nineteen Flatbush buildings with doormen and advises renters to confirm the hours, because some have limited coverage, and that is the situation this question describes. Most current panels can switch modes on a schedule. While the desk is staffed, a visitor's call can go to the desk station first, and the doorman can announce the visitor and release the door. When the desk closes, the same panel can ring apartments directly, so residents answer and release as they would in any lobby building. The detail that matters most is the handover between modes: if the desk closes early one night and nobody switches the panel, visitors ring an empty desk. A panel that switches automatically by time, with a manual override at the desk, avoids that.

Tenants, rent and the record

Our tenant association wants to document the dead intercom for 311 and a possible Housing Court case. What should we write down?

Write down what fails, where and when, in a way an inspector or a judge can follow. Note the date the intercom stopped working and every date it was reported, to whom and how, keeping copies of letters and emails. List which apartments are affected, because "the whole building" and "the D line from the second floor up" point to different faults. Note whether the problem is the call, the voice, the release, or the door not latching afterward. Take dated photos of the panel and of the door standing open if it does not close. HPD told News 12 in 2024, speaking about tenants at 1111 Ocean Avenue, that residents should keep filing 311 complaints so the agency can inspect and issue violations, and Habitat magazine notes that Housing Court's HP proceedings can be started without a lawyer. We do not take sides in disputes, but if the owner hires us, a clear record of symptoms helps us find the fault faster, and tenant groups are welcome to share it.

Our apartment is rent-stabilized. Can the landlord raise our rent because the building got a new intercom?

Possibly, through the state's Major Capital Improvement process, but not automatically and not without limits. DHCR's Fact Sheet 26 explains that an owner who makes a building-wide improvement may apply for an increase, which DHCR can grant in whole or in part, capped at 2 percent a year, spread over 12 years in buildings of 35 or fewer apartments or 12½ years in larger ones, and removed from the rent after 30 years. The fact sheet also says no MCI increase is allowed where the building has outstanding hazardous or immediately hazardous violations, or where 35 percent or fewer of the apartments are rent-regulated. It does not say whether intercoms qualify, so that is a question for DHCR, the owner's advisers and your tenant organization. All DHCR orders are served on every tenant in the building, so you would be told.

Half the name buttons on our lobby panel have to be pressed two or three times before anything happens. Is that worn-out hardware, or something simpler?

Sometimes simpler. Brick Underground's intercom guide notes that some "broken" intercoms are really buttons that stick or need a firmer, longer press, and that a stuck button can sometimes be freed with a cotton swab and rubbing alcohol. Try that on one button first, with the panel's power left on, and only on the button face. If several buttons respond after cleaning, the panel may simply need a proper cleaning and new button seals. If they do not, or if the buttons that fail are scattered across the panel rather than grouped, the contacts behind the buttons may be worn, which is common on panels that face Flatbush's sidewalks year round. A worn panel can often be replaced on its own, keeping the risers and apartment stations, which costs far less than a whole new system.

Cellars, doors and strangers

Strangers keep getting into our building's cellar through the side door to the yard. Should that door be on the intercom too?

Usually not on the intercom, but it should be secured as seriously as the front door. Brooklyn Paper's report on two East 22nd Street buildings quoted a tenant saying squatters walked in and lived in the basement, and cellars in prewar Flatbush buildings often have a yard door, a sidewalk hatch or an old coal door that nobody checks. A cellar door is rarely a visitor entrance, so giving it a call button invites people to ring it. What it needs is a solid door, a lock that only staff and service people can open, a closer so it never stays ajar, and ideally a switch that tells the super when it is left open. If the intercom has a door position input, that switch can report through the intercom. And if the intercom's head end lives in that cellar, the equipment should be in a locked cabinet, because a power supply in an open cellar can be switched off by anyone who walks in.

If something goes wrong in the building, can the super shut off every apartment's door release at once?

On many current systems, yes, and it is worth deciding in advance how it would be used. A building-wide release lockout, sometimes a switch at the head end and sometimes a setting in the administrator's app, stops apartment stations from opening the door while still letting them talk to the lobby. That can be useful during a police incident, a run of package thefts, or while a door is being repaired and must not be released. It also has costs: residents cannot let in visitors, aides or deliveries while it is on, so it should be used briefly and with notice. On an older system, the same effect is sometimes achieved by cutting power to the strike, which also disables keys if the door uses a fail-safe lock, so it should never be improvised. We set up the lockout, label it, and explain its effects to the super and the agent in writing.

Our street door is right on Flatbush Avenue, with buses stopping in front. Will visitors and residents be able to hear each other over the traffic?

This is our own question, because we hear the problem often on the commercial avenues. A panel at a busy curb has to overcome bus engines, brakes, horns and conversation a few feet away. Panels with noise suppression and a speaker aimed at the visitor, mounted at a height where the microphone is close to the visitor's mouth, make a large difference, and so does putting the panel in a recessed doorway or the vestibule rather than flush with the sidewalk. On the apartment side, handsets held to the ear work better than hands-free speakers for residents who have trouble hearing. We test calls during the noisiest part of the day before we finish.

Our family is moving into one of the new buildings at the old Sears site. What should we ask management about the entry system before move-in?

Also our own question. Ask how visitors reach your apartment: an app, a phone call, a wall station, or a combination. Ask whether more than one phone number can be listed for your apartment, so whoever is home can answer. Ask how deliveries are handled, whether there is a package room and how you are notified. Ask what happens if the internet or the building's network is down, and whether residents can still be called. And ask who to call when it does not work, and how fast they respond. Large new buildings like the ones Commercial Observer described at 2366 Bedford Avenue are designed with their own systems and contractors, so management is the right first stop; they can usually answer every one of these questions in a few minutes.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Flatbush

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic in our build environment. The claims below are ones that recur in generic AI answers and in cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what we find in Flatbush buildings.

"Apartment intercoms are a renter's problem to solve with a smart doorbell"

Generic answers often tell renters to add a video doorbell or a plug-in buzzer gadget to their own door.

In a Flatbush lobby building the visitor never reaches the apartment door; they stop at a panel outside the street door, and only the owner can change that panel. A doorbell on the apartment door cannot open the lobby, and a gadget clipped to an old handset does nothing about a dead riser or a door that will not latch.

The renter's real tools are a written request, a tenant association and, where needed, 311, while the system itself is the owner's or the board's responsibility.

"Old buildings need to be completely rewired for a modern intercom"

This claim is common because it sells bigger jobs.

Brick Underground's technicians say core wiring in the walls is usually fine, even in older systems, and two-wire video was built to run on existing pairs. In Flatbush the weak points are the splices at each floor.

Testing every pair from the terminal board tells a building whether one riser, several or none must be replaced, which is usually far less than a full rewire. It also keeps the job out of most apartments, which matters in an occupied building of a hundred households.

"Telephone-entry panels just keep working"

AI answers describe phone-entry panels as simple and wire-free, which they are at the apartment end.

At the door they depend on a phone line, and in many older Flatbush lobbies that is copper service the building has to keep paying for and carriers are moving away from.

A plan for that line, cellular, network or a different panel, belongs in any intercom conversation about a phone-entry building, along with a check on what else shares it. Leaving it until the line fails means the lobby goes dark first and the decision gets made in a hurry.

"Every building must have an intercom"

This is repeated without the conditions attached.

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies to buildings of eight or more apartments, automatically if built or converted after 1968 and on a written majority request if older. A two-family or a store building with three apartments upstairs is outside that line.

Most of Flatbush's six-story and walk-up stock is inside it, and once an intercom exists the housing code expects it to work, so the honest answer depends on the building. Owners should know which side of the line they are on before a violation tells them.

"An intercom costs a few hundred dollars"

National averages from cost guides describe single-family kits, not lobby buildings.

A Flatbush walk-up of sixteen apartments falls in the $1,200 – $4,500 band for a building-wide system with handsets, and a hundred-apartment building with two lobby doors and six risers is quoted after a survey.

The cost is driven by the apartment count, the riser tests, the doors and the access plan more than by the brand of the panel. A national average cannot see any of those things, which is why our quotes follow a survey.

"App intercoms suit every resident"

Many AI answers present app-based entry as the obvious upgrade.

Flatbush buildings house retired tenants with decades-old handsets, families who share one phone and residents who read Haitian Creole or Spanish more easily than English. An app-only system can leave some of them unable to let anyone in.

Mixed systems, with wall stations for some apartments and phone or app forwarding for others, usually serve a Flatbush building better than one answer for all. Where the hardware allows, menus and panel instructions can be set in more than one language.

"The intercom is separate from the door"

Generic answers treat the panel as the whole product.

The PIX11 report on the Ocean Avenue superintendent described thefts in which people were buzzed into the lobby, and the BK Reader account of a Flatbush break-in centered on a weak front door. Release, latch and closer decide who is inside afterward.

We set strikes and closers on both vestibule doors so each one locks again behind every release, which matters more than any feature on the panel. A short release time and a door that cannot be left ajar do more against tailgating than a sharper camera.

Ask for the panel, apartment stations, riser work, door hardware on each lobby door, power supply and battery, programming, the access plan for apartments and any app plan, each as its own line. Separate lines make bids comparable and show who has left something out.

Should a six-story building's panel sit outside the street door or between the street door and the lobby door?
+

Outside keeps strangers out of the vestibule and away from the mailboxes, but exposes the panel to weather and wear. Inside the vestibule protects the panel but means the street door must stay unlocked. Most Flatbush buildings we see do better with the panel outside and both doors locked.

Two apartments in our building were combined into one. What happens to the extra station and button?
+

The spare button is removed or relabeled in the directory, and the spare station is either taken out and its plate covered or kept as a second station in another room of the combined apartment. The unused pair is labeled at the terminal board so nobody wires into it later by mistake.

Our building is changing managing agents next month. Does anything about the intercom need to be handed over?
+

Yes. The new agent needs the administrator login for any app or directory, the installer's records and riser map, the location of the power supply and its breaker, and any phone or cellular account the panel uses. Without the login, simple directory changes can turn into service calls.

When is payment due on an intercom job, and how much is paid before work starts?
+

Our service agreement calls for a 50 percent deposit before equipment is ordered, with the balance due when the system is tested and handed over. The full terms, including the warranty and the late-fee schedule, are written into the agreement you sign before any work begins.

Our managing agent needs a certificate of insurance naming the building before you start. Can you provide one?
+

Yes. Send us the exact names the agent wants listed, usually the owner entity and the managing agent, and the address, and we arrange the certificate before the survey or installation date. Many Flatbush co-ops and rental owners require it, so it is worth asking early.

Can a medical office on our ground floor have a button on the lobby panel that only works during office hours?
+

On many panels, yes. The office's directory entry can be set to appear or ring only on a schedule, and outside those hours visitors see a message or nothing at all. We still recommend that the office have its own street entrance where possible, so patients do not use the residents' lobby.

Some tenants have the old audio handsets and a few bought their own video monitors. Can one system support both?
+

Often, if the monitors are from the same maker and family as the system, since stations are not interchangeable between brands. On a new system, apartments can be mixed on purpose, with handsets in some and monitors in others, all on the same panel and risers.

A tenant's contractor cut the intercom wire during a kitchen renovation. Who pays to fix it?
+

That depends on the lease or the alteration agreement, which usually make the renovating party responsible for damage to building systems. Habitat magazine makes the same point for co-ops. Technically, the repair is usually a new run from the nearest riser box to the apartment, a short job.

Can the lobby work be done early in the morning or on a weekend to avoid the busiest hours?
+

Some of it can. Panel swaps and riser work can be scheduled for quieter hours by arrangement, and apartment visits often go better on weekends when tenants are home. Drilling is limited by the building's own rules and neighbors, so we agree the hours with the agent first.

If our co-op board needs a shareholder vote first, how far ahead should we start?
+

Start with the survey well before the meeting, so the board has a written scope and price to present. Allow time for the vote, the deposit, equipment ordering and posting notices to residents. Asking early costs nothing, and a dead intercom does not wait for the annual meeting.
