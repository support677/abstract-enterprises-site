The Neighborhood

Why Bushwick Properties Need Alarm Installation

Bushwick covers about 1.83 square miles of northern Brooklyn. Wikipedia’s article puts Ridgewood, Queens, on its northeast side, Williamsburg to the northwest, Bedford-Stuyvesant to the south and southwest, and the cemeteries of Highland Park to the southeast; the community board that represents it, Brooklyn Community Board 4, runs from Flushing Avenue to Broadway and from the Queens line to the Cemetery of the Evergreens. The 2020 census counted 120,741 people across the two tabulation areas that make up the neighborhood. The same article puts the density at about 66,000 people per square mile, which in practice means many households sharing each building, each street door and each fire escape.

The buildings tell the story of how the neighborhood filled in. Community Board 4’s own history describes two- to six-family houses built throughout the area, large apartment houses appearing in the 1920s, and a street of mansions along Bushwick Avenue built between 1880 and 1913 for brewers and doctors, followed by the Irving Avenue area. Wikipedia adds that many early houses were wood-frame, quoting the New York Times of the mid-1960s calling Bushwick “a neatly maintained community of wood houses.” By 1890, a stretch nicknamed Brewer’s Row held 14 breweries in 14 blocks, and the brewing and manufacturing that followed left behind the brick factories and warehouses that, from the 1990s on, were turned into lofts, studios and offices around the Morgan Avenue L station.

For an alarm installer, that mix matters more than any single statistic. A walk-up apartment building of five or six stories usually has a fire escape on the front or rear, a roof door or hatch at the top of the stair, and a street door that every tenant and every delivery driver opens. A wood-frame two- or three-family house has light, hollow walls, a front porch or stoop, a side alley and often a rear porch or deck. A converted factory loft has tall steel-sash windows, a freight door or elevator and a shared lobby that may or may not be staffed. A storefront on Knickerbocker or Myrtle has a rolling gate in front, a back door into a yard or hallway, and a roof that connects with the roofs next door. A design that suits one of these buildings is often wrong for the others.

The local break-in record points at the same weak spots. In December 2015, DNAinfo reported that Bushwick had logged 441 burglaries so far that year, the most of any neighborhood in the city, ahead of East New York and Williamsburg. The 83rd Precinct’s commanding officer at the time, Deputy Inspector Maximo Tolentino, blamed doors and windows left unlocked, and the article described Bushwick’s row houses and small apartment buildings as linked by fire escapes and connected by rooftops, which makes it easier for an opportunistic thief to move between them. A 2013 DNAinfo report described two burglars climbing into a Bushwick apartment through its kitchen window in the middle of a weekday and leaving with a television, a printer, a computer and jewelry. In March 2017, CBS New York reported an intruder entering a third-floor bedroom near Irving Avenue through a window reached by the fire escape. And in July 2026, Hoodline, citing police reports to CBS New York, described a home invasion in a third-floor apartment near Pilling Street and Evergreen Avenue in 2025 that began at an unlocked kitchen window; the 83rd Precinct was still asking the public for information.

Businesses have their own history here. During the July 13, 1977 blackout, according to both Wikipedia and Community Board 4, the Broadway shopping district was looted and burned, and a year later 43% of its stores stood vacant, while store owners on Knickerbocker Avenue, many of whom lived nearby, protected their shops with their neighbors. The same night an arson fire at an abandoned factory at Knickerbocker and Bleecker destroyed four blocks and 45 homes. Bushwick’s commercial streets have long since come back, but the lesson stuck: a store is safest when someone knows the moment something is wrong. In January 2021, News 12 reported that at least three Bushwick businesses had been burglarized in a month, naming a shop on Knickerbocker Avenue. A modern alarm is how a shop owner who does not live upstairs knows at once.

None of this means Bushwick is unusually dangerous today; crime across all categories in the 83rd Precinct fell by 80.3% between 1990 and 2018, according to figures Wikipedia cites. It means the openings that matter are predictable, and an alarm should be designed around them rather than around a package. Most owners who call about an alarm also ask about cameras; we cover those on our Bushwick security camera installation page and can wire both on one visit.

Same-Day Alarm Repair in Bushwick: Chirping Sensors, Dead Keypads, Sirens That Keep Coming Back

Some calls are not about a new system at all. A walk-up tenant inherits a keypad from the previous renter and it beeps every few minutes. A loft’s panel has been showing a trouble light since the building changed internet providers. Most of these faults belong to a short list, and most are put right in a single visit of an hour or two. A steady chirp almost always means a weak battery in a wireless contact or in the panel’s standby battery, or a trouble condition such as lost AC power. A panel that says it cannot communicate usually has a communicator that lost its network, often a cellular radio that was never upgraded when the older networks were switched off, or an internet module that stopped talking after a router was replaced. A siren that keeps re-triggering usually comes from one sensor: a door that has dropped on its hinges so the magnet no longer lines up, or a motion detector aimed at a radiator, a window or a hanging plant.

Call (347) 934-8335, tell us the brand printed on the keypad or the panel door, read us what the screen shows, and say whether anyone else in the building uses the same system. Take the earliest arrival window offered. If the siren is sounding right now and you have no code, remember that New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be capable of shutting itself off, and to shut itself off, within fifteen minutes of being activated; a siren that runs past that needs attention the same day. If a panel is too old to be worth repairing, we say so before any billable work starts.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Bushwick

The list below is broken out the way the work is priced, but a typical Bushwick job combines several lines on one panel. A renter on the fourth floor of a walk-up might need only the first two; a family in a frame three-family on a quiet side street might need five; a bar on Wyckoff Avenue needs a different five.

Fire-escape window protection

Contacts and glass-break detectors on every window that opens onto a fire escape, chosen so nothing interferes with the window as a way out. Where an FDNY-approved gate is already fitted, the gate itself can carry a contact so the alarm knows when it is swung open.

Removable alarms for renters

All-wireless panels, contacts and detectors held on with removable mounting strips or one or two small screws, a keypad by the apartment door and an app on every roommate’s phone. When the lease ends, the system comes down in an hour and goes to the next apartment.

Roof doors, hatches and skylights

A contact on the roof door or bulkhead hatch at the top of the stair, or on the skylight of a top-floor unit, for buildings whose roofs connect along the block. In multiple dwellings this is a conversation with the landlord first, because the roof door belongs to the building.

Frame-house alarms

Wired contacts fished through hollow walls where the frame allows it, wireless where siding or plaster makes that cleaner, and coverage for the side alley door, rear porch door and cellar entrance that a front-door-only kit leaves bare.

Loft and studio systems

Large open floors divided into zones by what is in them: the entry, the sleeping area, the work area, a storage cage. Freight-door and roll-up-door contacts, beam or curtain detectors along banks of tall windows, and separate codes for the people who share the space.

Store, bar and restaurant alarms

Rolling-gate floor contacts, glass protection tuned for elevated trains on Myrtle and Broadway, rear-door and roof-hatch contacts, a hold-up button where cash is handled and opening-and-closing reports, so an owner knows the last person out armed it.

Warehouse and workshop alarms

Overhead-door contacts, outdoor-rated motion detectors aimed away from the street, perimeter coverage for loading areas and a siren where it can be heard, for the workshops and storage buildings in the industrial blocks.

Takeovers of systems you inherited

Panels left behind by a previous tenant, a former shop owner or a defunct alarm company, tested zone by zone and either revived with a new communicator and fresh codes or replaced. We never leave a stranger’s master code in a panel we take over.

Monitoring, video checks and repairs

App-only self-monitoring or central station monitoring without a long contract, a camera linked to a zone so an alarm can be checked on video, and service on other installers’ equipment when something chirps, drops offline or will not stop.

Many Bushwick buildings pair an apartment alarm with a better front door, because the alarm can only report on doors the household controls. Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Bushwick’s Architecture

The building types below come from the sources listed at the bottom of this page. What we say about each is how it behaves when you try to alarm it, from our working practice; none of it is a claim about any particular address.

Walk-up apartment buildings

Four to six stories, often six families or more, with a fire escape on the front or rear, a roof door at the top of the stair and a street door shared by everyone. Tenants want protection inside their own apartment; owners want the common doors covered. These are two separate systems with two separate owners.

Wood-frame two- and three-family houses

The houses the 1960s Times called “wood houses,” many now in vinyl or aluminum siding. Light framing and hollow stud bays make wiring quicker than in brick, but porches, side alleys and rear decks add openings, and a century of alterations hides surprises in the walls.

Brick rowhouses and townhouses

Two- and three-family brick houses in attached rows, with a cellar, a stoop or a low entry, and a rear yard that meets the yards behind it. Party walls on both sides and a cellar ceiling that is usually open make the cellar the natural route for wiring.

Bushwick Avenue and Irving Avenue mansions

Large free-standing or semi-detached houses from 1880 to 1913 in Romanesque Revival, Queen Anne and other styles, with tall parlor windows, carriage-house or garage doors at the back and many rooms. More openings than any other house type in the neighborhood, and finishes owners rightly do not want cut.

Converted factory lofts

Brick or concrete industrial buildings near the Morgan Avenue and Jefferson Street L stations, converted from the 1990s on. Open floors, steel-sash windows several feet wide, freight elevators or freight doors, and residents who may be tenants, sub-tenants or members of a building association.

Storefronts with apartments above

On Knickerbocker, Myrtle, Wyckoff, Broadway and the corners of the side streets. A gate and a glass front at street level, a back door into a yard or hallway, and one or more apartments upstairs reached by a separate street door or a shared hall.

How we wire each type

Walk-ups. Inside a rented apartment the design is wireless from end to end: a small panel or hub, a keypad inside the apartment door, contacts on that door and on every window that opens onto the fire escape or can be reached from a roof or yard, a glass-break detector in rooms with several windows, and a motion detector in the main hallway of the apartment if no pet will set it off. Nothing is drilled into the building’s common areas. If the owner wants the street door, the cellar door and the roof door alarmed, that becomes a second system on the owner’s account, wired through the cellar and up the public hall with the owner’s approval, and it never shares codes with the tenants’ systems.

Frame houses. We start with the cellar or crawl space and look for open joists. In a balloon-framed or lightly built house, a wire dropped from the attic or pushed up from the cellar often reaches a first- or second-floor window in minutes, and a recessed contact drilled into the frame edge disappears completely. Where a wall bay is blocked by fire-blocking, old plumbing or later insulation, we switch that window to a small wireless contact rather than open the wall. Porches get attention: a front porch roof under second-floor windows makes those windows reachable, and a rear deck stair is an invitation to the back door.

Brick rowhouses. The panel and battery go high on a cellar wall, clear of the boiler and above any line water has reached. Cable runs along the joists to the front and rear walls and up into the first-floor openings; upper windows over a rear extension, a porch roof or a neighbor’s flat roof get wireless contacts. In a two- or three-family the system is split into separate areas so each household arms its own floor, with the street door and cellar shared.

Mansions. The approach is the same as a rowhouse, scaled up and slowed down. We count the openings first, floor by floor, and decide window by window between a hidden wired contact and a wireless one. Glass-break detectors cover rooms with many panes, so the owner is not paying for a contact on every sash of a bay. Carriage-house or garage doors at the rear get heavy-duty contacts on their own zone.

Fire Escapes and Roofs

Fire-Escape Windows and Shared Roofs: The Openings That Define a Bushwick Alarm

Every source on Bushwick break-ins in the research for this page returns to the same two features. DNAinfo’s 2015 report described the neighborhood’s row houses and small apartment buildings as linked by fire escapes and connected by rooftops. CBS New York’s 2017 report described an intruder reaching a third-floor bedroom through a window off the fire escape. Hoodline’s 2026 report on a 2025 home invasion near Pilling Street and Evergreen Avenue quoted police saying the attacker entered through an unlocked kitchen window of a third-floor apartment, and the victim telling News 12 he climbed down a rope tied to the building’s chimney. None of these involved a front door. All of them involved a window above the street, reached from outside the building’s locked doors.

The obvious fix, a locked gate over the window, is the one the law limits. The FDNY’s residential apartment fire-safety guide tells tenants that only approved security gates, ones that open without a key and carry an FDNY approval number, may go on a window used as a fire escape. A padlocked or keyed gate over that window can trap the people inside in a fire. That leaves the fire-escape window as the one opening in the apartment that, by design, cannot be locked tight against someone standing on the landing. It is exactly the opening an alarm is best at protecting, because an alarm never blocks an exit; it only reports that the window or gate has opened.

Protecting it well takes three layers. First, a contact on the sash, positioned so it trips the moment the window moves. Where a tenant likes the window open a few inches for air in summer, a second magnet can let it sit locked at a small opening without tripping. Second, a glass-break detector in the room, because a burglar on a fire escape can break a pane and reach the latch without moving the sash at all. Third, if an approved gate is fitted, a contact on the gate itself, so the panel knows the gate was opened even if the window behind it was already up. All three are wireless in a rental and leave no holes when they come off.

Roofs need a separate decision. In a walk-up, the roof door at the top of the stair belongs to the building, and so does any hatch. If roofs connect along the row, that door is a path into the stairwell for anyone who reaches the roof from next door. A contact on that door is a five-minute job, but it should be on the owner’s system, not a tenant’s, and the building has to keep the door usable as an exit. In a top-floor apartment with its own skylight or hatch, the opening is the tenant’s, and it goes on the tenant’s system like any window. In a frame house or rowhouse, rear windows over an extension roof or a neighbor’s flat roof are treated exactly like fire-escape windows: if someone can stand outside them, they get a contact.

Renters and Roommates

An Alarm That Fits a Lease: Renters, Roommates and Shared Apartments in Bushwick

A large share of the people who call us from Bushwick rent. Wikipedia reports that median rent in the neighborhood reached $2,180 in 2022, up 81.7% since 2006, and sharing an apartment is one way people manage it. Renting changes three things about an alarm: what can be fixed to the walls, who holds the codes, and what happens when the lease ends. A system designed for an owner-occupied house gets all three wrong.

The walls. We build renters’ systems so they leave no more trace than a picture hook. Contacts and detectors go on with removable mounting strips or one small screw each; the panel or hub sits on a shelf or a small bracket near an outlet; the keypad mounts beside the apartment door the same way. Nothing is drilled into door frames, nothing is run inside walls, and nothing is installed in the building’s halls. When the lease ends, an hour’s work takes it all down, and the equipment, which the tenant owns, goes to the next apartment. If the lease requires the landlord’s consent for anything fixed to the walls, we keep to removable mounts and the question rarely arises; if it does, we provide a short description of the equipment for the landlord.

The codes. Three or four roommates on different schedules cannot share one code and one app login. If they do, nobody knows who disarmed the system at three in the morning, the code ends up written on the fridge, and when one roommate moves out the code never changes. Instead, each person gets a personal code and a personal app login, and one roommate, chosen by the household, holds administrator rights to add and remove users. When someone moves out, that person’s code is deleted in a minute from a phone. A sub-letter for the summer gets a code that expires on the date the sublet ends.

Moving out. On the last day, the household deletes its users, cancels any monitoring plan, and either takes the system down or asks us to. If the next tenant wants to buy the system as it stands, the departing administrator hands over the administrator role and then deletes their own access; we can reset the panel to factory settings at the service rate if no one is sure what codes are still in it. Monitoring plans through our partners do not carry long-term contracts, so moving out of the neighborhood does not leave anyone paying for an alarm they no longer have.

Lofts and Studios

Lofts, Studios and Workshops in Bushwick’s Former Factory Blocks

North of the Myrtle Avenue line and around the Morgan Avenue and Jefferson Street L stations, Bushwick turns industrial. Untapped Cities’ 2012 account of the area some call Morgantown places it inside the East Williamsburg Industrial Park, one of the city’s designated industrial zones, which then still held more than 8,000 manufacturing jobs; it describes factory owners converting empty buildings into lofts from the 1990s on, and names the McKibbin Lofts at 248 and 255 McKibbin Street, home to about 400 residents. In 2016 Brownstoner listed more than a dozen conversions under way in the same blocks: warehouses on Moore and Cook Streets turned into offices, a former paper mill at 456 Johnson Avenue, a former factory at 455 Jefferson Street and the former Schlitz brewery building at 95 Evergreen Avenue, among others, with the note that much of the area is zoned for nonresidential use. The streets themselves became an outdoor gallery: NY1 reported in 2016 that the Bushwick Collective, founded in 2012 by Joe Ficalora, whose family runs a steel business on Troutman Street, had persuaded owners of dozens of buildings to offer their walls for murals.

The result is a set of buildings where an apartment-style alarm does not fit. Ceilings are often far higher than in an apartment. Windows are steel sash, several feet wide, sometimes in continuous bands along a whole wall. Doors include a heavy steel entry door, a freight elevator gate or freight door, and on the ground floor a roll-up loading door onto the street. The people inside may be residential tenants, commercial tenants running a studio or small business, or both in the same unit. And what needs protecting is often not jewelry and laptops but equipment, materials and finished work that cannot be replaced.

We design loft alarms around three ideas. First, zone by use, not by room: the entry and freight door, the living area, the work area and any storage cage each get their own zone, so a sculptor can work late with the living area disarmed and the storage cage still armed. Second, protect window banks with detectors that watch the glass line rather than the floor, so someone moving around inside does not trip them and someone coming through the glass does. Third, use contacts built for big doors: heavy-duty floor-mounted contacts with armored cable for roll-up doors, and wide-gap contacts for freight doors that never close perfectly flush.

Murals deserve a word. Many Bushwick walls, including steel doors and roll-up shutters, carry painted artwork, and owners who agreed to host a wall rarely want it drilled. Contacts for a painted door can usually go on the inside face and the frame, out of sight from the street, and a siren or strobe, if one is wanted outside at all, can sit on an unpainted return or under the cornice. We ask before we drill anything that has paint on it.

Before the Visit

Three Things Bushwick Tenants, Owners and Shop Keepers Can Settle Before the Technician Arrives

None of the steps below is required. Each one saves a second trip or a change order, and together they let the technician spend the visit on the building instead of on questions.

Open every window that touches the outside of the building

Walk the apartment or house and open each window. Note which ones open onto the fire escape, which look down onto a roof, an extension or a porch, and which drop straight to the street or yard. That list, not the number of rooms, sets how many contacts the job needs. If a fire-escape window has a gate, check whether it opens without a key and has an FDNY approval label; if it does not, tell the landlord, because that gate is a fire-safety problem before it is an alarm question.

Agree on who administers the system

In a shared apartment, decide which roommate holds administrator rights and who gets notifications for what. In a two- or three-family, decide whether the owner, a relative or each household administers its own area. In a shop, decide which partner or manager adds and removes employees. Settling this in advance means codes are programmed correctly on day one.

For businesses, list the hours and the people

Write down the opening and closing times for each day, who opens and who closes, and who should be called first if the alarm goes off at night. A bar that closes in the early hours and opens at noon, a bakery that opens before dawn and a studio used at all hours each need different arming schedules and call lists, and the list is what makes opening and closing reports useful.

Common Fixes

Four Alarm Setups We Regularly Rework in North Brooklyn Walk-Ups, Lofts and Shops

These patterns come from our service calls across Bushwick and the neighborhoods around it. They are described from experience, not counted from a survey, and none of them means the first installer broke a rule; they are designs that do not match the building.

1. Every sensor on the front of the apartment, nothing at the back

The entry door has a contact, the living-room windows facing the street have contacts, and the kitchen and rear bedroom windows onto the fire escape have nothing. The fix is to move protection to where the fire escape is, add a glass-break detector in the rear rooms, and use a motion detector to cover street windows that nobody can reach anyway.

2. A keyed gate on the fire-escape window wired into the alarm

We still find padlocked or keyed gates over fire-escape windows, sometimes with an alarm contact added so the owner feels doubly protected. The alarm part is fine; the gate is not, because it can trap people in a fire. We tell the household and the landlord, recommend an FDNY-approved gate that opens without a key, and move the contact to it.

3. A loft protected like a one-bedroom

One motion detector in the middle of a two-thousand-square-foot floor, a contact on the entry door, and nothing on the freight door or the window bank. The detector cannot see the far corners, and the windows are the easiest way in from the fire escape or the roof. We split the loft into zones, add detectors aimed along the glass, and contact the freight door properly.

4. A storefront alarm that cannot tell anyone it was never armed

The panel works, the siren works, but nobody receives a message when the store is closed without arming, so a forgotten closing goes unnoticed until morning. Turning on closing reports and a late-to-close alert, sent to the owner’s phone if the system is not armed by a set time, fixes it in a few minutes of programming.

What a Bushwick store, bar or studio alarm needs

Glass protection that ignores the el. Storefronts directly under the Myrtle Avenue and Broadway structures feel each passing train in their glass. Shock sensors stuck to the window read vibration, so they are the wrong tool there. Acoustic glass-break detectors that listen for the sound of glass breaking, mounted inside and adjusted while trains are running, protect the window without false alarms, and gate and door contacts back them up so the system never depends on glass protection alone.

Gate, back door and roof. A rolling gate gets a floor contact with armored cable, so the alarm knows the gate has been lifted even if the glass behind it is intact. The back door, often onto a yard or a shared hall, gets a heavy-duty surface contact. Roof hatches, skylights and rear windows over an extension get contacts, because on a commercial row the roofs connect.

Schedules that match the business. A bar that closes in the early morning hours, a café that opens at six and a studio that is used whenever inspiration strikes each need a different arming routine. We program an expected closing time; if the system is not armed by then, the owner gets a message. Every opening and closing is logged with the code that was used.

Rules that apply to every Bushwick alarm

  • Sirens stop within fifteen minutes. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 says no owner of a building may operate an audible burglar alarm on it unless the alarm is capable of terminating, and does terminate, within fifteen minutes of activation. We program a siren timeout inside that limit on every system we install or service. In a walk-up, everyone on every floor hears a siren; in a row of storefronts, so do the apartments above all of them.
  • Fire-escape windows stay usable as exits. The FDNY’s apartment fire-safety guide limits security gates on windows used as fire escapes to approved gates that open without a key. Alarm sensors never block a window; we will not install or wire a keyed gate onto a fire-escape window.
  • Paid installers need a state license. Article 6-D of the General Business Law makes installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems for others a licensed business, regulated by the Department of State; unlicensed workers helping a licensee carry ID cards the licensee issues, and property owners working on their own premises are exempt. AESS holds license 12000287431.
  • Burglar alarms and fire alarms are not the same system. A smoke listener on an intrusion panel is a useful extra in a frame house, yet each apartment still needs its own required detectors, and a walk-up, loft building or mixed-use building that needs a fire alarm system faces separate code and filing rules. For that, see our Bushwick fire alarm installation page.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Bushwick: Questions Answered

These are the questions we hear standing in Bushwick apartments, houses, lofts and shops, grouped by subject. Shorter questions about cost, timing and logistics are in the FAQ further down.

Railroad flats, roommates and shared landings

Our railroad apartment’s rooms open one into the next, with windows only at the front and the back. Where do the detectors go in a layout like that?

In a railroad flat everyone, including an intruder, has to walk through the rooms in a line, which makes the layout easy to protect. The openings that matter are at the two ends: the street windows at the front and the kitchen or rear-room windows at the back, usually where the fire escape is. The back gets contacts and a glass-break detector; the front windows, if nothing sits below them, are covered by one motion detector placed to look down the line of rooms toward them.

A second detector near the kitchen catches anyone coming in from the fire escape before they reach the middle rooms. Two detectors and a handful of contacts usually cover the whole apartment, and all of it is wireless and removable.

Can each roommate keep their own bedroom off the system while the shared rooms stay protected?

Yes. Each bedroom becomes its own small area that only its occupant can arm or disarm, while the entry door, hallway, kitchen and living room form a shared area that arms automatically when the last person leaves. Motion detectors stay in the shared rooms only, so nobody’s movements in their own room are recorded, and a bedroom with a fire-escape window can still have its window contact armed by its occupant at night.

Notifications follow the same split: an event in one bedroom goes to the person who sleeps there, and shared-area events go to everyone.

Our fire escape runs past a neighbor’s window as well as ours. Will their comings and goings on the landing set off our alarm?

Not if the sensors are chosen for it. Contacts only react when your own window or gate moves, and an acoustic glass-break detector listens for the specific sound of breaking glass, not footsteps or voices outside. What we avoid on a shared landing is an outdoor motion detector or a camera-triggered siren aimed at the fire escape itself, because a neighbor watering plants would set it off.

If you want warning before anyone touches your window, a camera that sends a clip to your phone without sounding the siren is the better tool; we keep it pointed at your own window only.

Fire escapes, roofs and windows

The landlord fitted an FDNY-approved gate on our fire-escape window. Can the alarm tell the gate opening apart from the window opening?

Yes, by giving each its own contact on its own zone. The gate contact reports the moment the gate swings; the sash contact reports the moment the window lifts. The app and the keypad then say which one moved, which matters in summer, when the window may be up behind a closed gate. A glass-break detector in the room covers the remaining case, a broken pane with both closed.

An approved gate opens without a key, as the FDNY requires, so leaving it unlocked is correct; the alarm, not a padlock, is what protects it.

Our kitchen window and a bedroom window both open onto the same fire-escape landing. Does each need its own sensor?

Each window that opens onto the landing gets its own contact, because either one is a way in and the panel should report which. If the two rooms are next to each other, one glass-break detector placed between them can sometimes hear both windows; if they are separated by a hallway, each room gets its own. Contacts are inexpensive, so we never leave a fire-escape window uncovered to save one.

The kitchen window deserves particular care: two of the Bushwick break-ins described on this page started at kitchen windows.

Our frame house has vinyl siding and a dark alley down the side. Where should the sensors go that a front-door kit would miss?

Start with the side door off the alley, the rear porch door and the cellar entrance, which kits usually ignore. Each gets a contact. Windows along the alley that a person could reach standing on a trash can or the alley fence get contacts; windows over a front porch roof get them too, because the porch roof is a platform. An outdoor motion detector or floodlight camera aimed down the alley adds warning before anyone reaches a door.

In a frame house, hollow walls often let us hide wired contacts in the frames without opening plaster; where they do not, small wireless contacts are used instead.

Lofts, studios and industrial space

We live in a converted factory loft with steel windows running the length of one wall. How do you protect a whole bank of glass?

Not with a contact on every sash. Long banks of steel-sash windows are covered by detectors whose coverage runs along the glass, curtain-pattern motion detectors or glass-break detectors placed so each pane is within range, and contacts go only on the sections that actually open. That protects the glass line while leaving the floor free for people to move around at night.

Where the windows open onto a fire escape or a lower roof, those operable sections get contacts as well, exactly as in a walk-up.

My studio is one unit inside a Morgan Avenue warehouse full of other studios. Can it be alarmed separately from the building?

Yes. The studio’s own door, windows and any roof access form its perimeter, and its system reports only on those. The building’s lobby, freight elevator and loading dock belong to the landlord or the association and stay off your system. Because buildings like this often run their own internet, we usually fit a cellular communicator so the studio does not depend on a shared network.

If you store work or equipment in a separate cage, it can be its own zone that stays armed while you are working in the studio.

The roll-up door on our ground floor is painted with a mural. How is it alarmed without drilling through the artwork?

From the inside. A heavy-duty overhead-door contact mounts on the floor or the track behind the door, with armored cable, and its magnet goes on the bottom bar on the inside face. Nothing is attached to the painted side. The contact is set with enough tolerance that the door rattling in its guides does not trip it, but lifting it a few inches does.

If an outdoor siren or strobe is wanted, it goes on an unpainted surface such as a side return or under the cornice, and we confirm the spot with the owner before drilling.

Storefront roofs and large old houses

Our Knickerbocker Avenue shop is in a row of attached storefronts whose roofs run together. How do we stop someone coming in from above?

Contact the roof hatch, skylight or any rear window over an extension, and add a motion detector in the back room or stockroom under the roof, aimed so it would see someone dropping in. On connected commercial roofs those openings are often less guarded than the gate in front, and a contact on a hatch costs little.

If the shop has a drop ceiling, a detector covering the space below the hatch catches entry even if the hatch itself is bypassed.

Our Bushwick Avenue house is a turn-of-the-century brick mansion with tall parlor windows and a carriage-house door at the back. What changes for a house that size?

Mostly the count and the care. A house built between 1880 and 1913 for a brewer or a doctor can have far more openings than a rowhouse. We count them floor by floor, use glass-break detectors for rooms with many panes rather than a contact on every sash, hide wired contacts in frames where the cellar allows, and give the rear carriage-house or garage door its own heavy-duty zone.

Original woodwork and plaster are left alone: anything that would cut a molding or a cornice is done wirelessly instead.

“Put bars or a locked gate on any window you are worried about.”

General home-security advice often treats window bars as the strongest answer to a vulnerable ground-floor or upper window, and for a window that is not an exit route that can be reasonable.

In a Bushwick walk-up, the most vulnerable window is usually the one onto the fire escape, and the FDNY allows only approved gates that open without a key there. A locked gate on that window is a fire hazard, not a security upgrade.

The right answer is an alarm layered on an approved gate: contacts on the sash and the gate, a glass-break detector in the room, and nothing that slows anyone leaving in an emergency.

“Renters should stick to cameras, because alarms have to be wired in.”

Quick answers sometimes steer tenants to cameras or doorbells on the assumption that a real alarm means drilling and wiring the landlord will not allow.

Modern wireless panels and sensors install without cutting anything, and a renter’s system can be taken down in an hour. In a neighborhood where a large share of residents rent, that difference matters.

“Cover your doors and you have covered the way burglars get in.”

“Shock sensors protect store windows.”

Field Notes: Start at the Fire Escape, Not the Front Door

This describes how we survey a Bushwick walk-up, not a story about a particular apartment. The technician comes in, says hello, and walks straight through to the back. He opens the window onto the fire escape and leans out. Is there a gate, and does it open without a key? Does the fire escape go up to the roof as well as down to the yard? Only after that does he go to the front door, check the lock, and count the street windows. In most walk-up apartments the front half needs very little: a contact on the door and a motion detector that covers windows nobody can reach. The back half, where the fire escape is, needs most of the sensors. Writing the quote in that order is the difference between a system priced from a package and one priced from the building.

Can the alarm panel sit on a shelf instead of being screwed to the wall?
+

Yes, in most rentals. Wireless hubs and many panels are designed to stand on a shelf or a small bracket near an outlet, ideally out of sight of the windows and away from the entry door, so an intruder cannot find and smash it during the entry delay. We place it where the cellular signal is good and the power cord cannot be knocked out.

Will taking the sensors down at move-out damage the walls and cost us our security deposit?
+

Renter systems are mounted with removable strips or a single small screw per device, so taking them down leaves at most a pin-sized hole that a dab of spackle fills. We can do the removal for you, or show you how on installation day.

Can you install in a loft the week we move in, before the furniture arrives?
+

Yes, and it is the easiest time. With the floor empty we can see every window and door, place detectors where furniture will not block them and test coverage across the whole space. Tell us roughly where the bed, the work area and any storage will go, so the zones match how you will use the loft.

Do you need the building’s freight elevator booked to install in a loft?
+

It helps if the panel, ladders and any long cable runs are coming up together, but most loft installations fit in a couple of trips by stairs or passenger elevator. Tell us the building’s rules on contractor access, insurance certificates and work hours when you book, and we will send the certificate ahead.

Do you put alarm stickers on the windows of a rental, and do they help?
+

Only if you want them. A window sticker or a small sign by the door tells a would-be burglar the apartment is alarmed, and some tenants like that; others prefer not to advertise. On a storefront a discreet decal by the door is common. Either way, the stickers are removable and come off with the system.

Can the keypad go inside the apartment rather than out in the hallway where neighbors can see it?
+

Yes, and for a tenant’s system it always does. The keypad mounts just inside the apartment door, within reach during the entry delay, and the hallway stays untouched. Many people also arm and disarm from the phone app and keep a key fob on their keys, so the keypad is used only as a backup.

Is a cellular communicator included in your Brooklyn prices, or is it extra?
+

It is included in the basic residential package from $600 and in every complete system, because many Bushwick apartments and lofts share an internet connection or have none the alarm can depend on. Where a building’s internet is dependable, the panel can report over both paths.

Will an apartment alarm interfere with the building’s buzzer or intercom wiring?
+

No. A tenant’s alarm is a separate wireless system with its own power and communicator, and it does not connect to the building’s buzzer, intercom or door-release wiring at all. Integration is possible on an owner’s system, for example letting the alarm know the street door was buzzed open, but only with the owner’s agreement and as part of a building job.

We live in our loft and also make and sell work from it. Does that change what the alarm has to do?
+

Usually, yes. A loft used only as a home needs personal codes and app alerts. Once the space doubles as a workplace, it often also needs employee or assistant codes limited to working hours, opening-and-closing reports, a separate zone for stock or equipment and, if an insurer asks, central station monitoring with a certificate. We set the system up for both uses at once and tell you at the visit which list the job is priced from.
