Borough Park runs roughly from 39th Street down to the low 60s, between Fort Hamilton Parkway and McDonald Avenue, and it was built quickly. A developer laid out farmland here as a "modern residence park" in the late 1880s, the subway arrived in 1916, and most of the brick and limestone houses and small apartment buildings went up in the 1920s and 1930s; the median building in the neighborhood dates from 1938. Since then, houses have been split into more apartments, enlarged upward and backward, and joined by thousands of new apartments in infill buildings. Every one of those changes reaches the front door: a buzzer wired for two families now has to serve five, a vestibule built for one house now leads to a store and three apartments, and a door that works on electricity all week has to open by hand from Friday evening to Saturday night. This page explains how we plan an intercom for each kind of Borough Park building, which lock hardware suits a household that keeps Shabbos, what the city and state actually require for buildings above and below eight apartments, and how we price the job in writing at our Brooklyn rate before anyone drills.

The Neighborhood

Why Borough Park Properties Need Intercom Installation

The second clue is who lives behind those doors. Borough Park is home to one of the largest Orthodox Jewish communities outside Israel; Wikipedia records an estimate that about 85 percent of residents were Jewish by 1983, and brooklynjewish.org counts more than 300 religious institutions in the neighborhood. Local guides put it plainly: the rhythm of the week bends around Shabbos and the Jewish holidays, and on Thirteenth Avenue, the main shopping street, nearly every store is shuttered from Friday evening until Saturday night, chain stores included. An intercom is an electrical device that a household uses dozens of times a day, and for many Borough Park families it must also behave in a completely different way for about a day each week and for longer stretches over Yom Tov. A system that only works when someone presses a button, or a lock that only opens when electricity tells it to, does not fit that week. Choosing the right door hardware for it is the single most important decision on a Borough Park intercom job, and it is the reason this page spends so long on locks.

Six Borough Park facts we check before choosing a panel

  • The buildings are old, and the entry systems are newer than the doors. With a median construction year of 1938, most buildings predate modern intercoms. We assume the wiring has been changed at least once and trace it rather than trust it.
  • Most homes are small buildings. Yak Management describes rows of two- and three-family brick houses and older four- to six-family walk-ups, and The Real Deal calls the housing stock mostly small walk-ups. Many of these fall under the eight-apartment line in state law, which changes whether an intercom is legally required.
  • Apartment counts keep changing. Conversions of one-family houses into several units, enlargements and new infill mean the number of buttons on the panel often no longer matches the number of homes. We count apartments on the visit, floor by floor.
  • Seven in ten households rent. Point2Homes puts owner occupancy at 30 percent. The owner decides on the system, the tenants live with it, and a broken intercom is the owner's responsibility under city housing rules.
  • The avenues mix stores and homes. Thirteenth Avenue is zoned either commercial or residential with shops on the ground floor, and New Utrecht Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway add more mixed-use frontage. The apartments upstairs need a door of their own that the shop does not control.
  • Landmark review is probably rare. Our research found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district in Borough Park, unlike nearby Sunset Park and Ditmas Park. We still confirm each address, but most facades here can take a new panel without LPC review.

Same-Day Repair in Borough Park

Need Repair in Borough Park? A Buzzer That Sounds but Will Not Open, a Silent Handset, or a Door That No Longer Latches

Most urgent intercom calls from small Brooklyn buildings come down to one of four things, and the age of Borough Park's buildings makes all four common. The first is a release button that buzzes but does not open the door. Usually the strike in the door frame has worn out, has been knocked out of line by a door that sags on its hinges, or is being asked to release while the door is pressed hard against it by a closer set too tight. The second is a handset that has gone completely silent, often in one apartment only, which usually points to a broken wire somewhere between the street panel and that unit, very often at a spot where someone renovated, enlarged or converted part of the house and cut through the old run. The third is a door that no longer closes and latches on its own, so the lock never engages and the intercom is pointless. The fourth is a panel that has failed after years of rain, sun and fingers.

Before the technician arrives, two things help. Write down exactly which apartments are affected and what each one experiences: no ring, ring but no voice, voice but no door release. And if the door is not latching, tell everyone in the building, because until it is fixed the building is open. City housing guidance says the owner, the managing agent or the superintendent should repair broken locks and intercoms immediately, and a street door that no longer closes and locks itself is treated even more seriously. Call (347) 934-8335 with the address and the list. On the visit we test each piece separately, the panel, the wire to each apartment, the power supply, the strike and the closer, and we tell you which one failed and what it would take to fix only that part. If the system is near the end of its life, we say so, and we price the repair and the replacement side by side so the owner can choose.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Borough Park's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every Borough Park intercom call. They differ in three ways that matter more than any brand: how many homes the street door serves, what the facade and door frame are made of, and who decides what goes on the door. Here is what each one usually asks of us.

The brick two- or three-family house

The classic side-street building: attached brick, a street door shared by two or three apartments, often with the owner on one floor and family or tenants on the others.

The four- to six-family walk-up

Older small apartment buildings with a vestibule, a row of buttons and stairs only. Many fall just under the eight-apartment line in state law.

The converted or enlarged house

One-family houses split into several apartments, or houses that gained floors and rear additions. The button count rarely matches the homes inside.

The limestone row house

Attached limestone fronts among the brick rows. Beautiful facades that need careful drilling and a panel placed where it will not crack the stone.

The store with apartments above

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues, with a shop at street level and homes upstairs reached through their own door or a shared vestibule.

The new infill building

Multifamily buildings completed since 2010 and new row houses on the way, where the intercom is planned from scratch and often runs over the building's network.

The brick two- or three-family house: a short wire, a big household and a door everyone uses

The two- and three-family brick house is the backbone of Borough Park's side streets. The street door usually opens into a small hall or vestibule with a stair to the upper apartment, and the original bell or buzzer, if one survives, often runs on thin wire stapled along the baseboard or hidden in the stair wall. The distances are short, which is good news: a two- or three-apartment system can often be wired cleanly along the stair without opening walls, and a modern two-wire system can sometimes reuse an old pair if it tests sound. What makes these houses different is the traffic. Borough Park families are large and growing, and a house with three apartments can easily have a dozen or more people coming and going, plus visitors for meals and simchas. A door station that only one person in the household knows how to work will not survive that. We choose simple panels with large, clearly marked buttons, place the indoor station where the person most likely to answer actually spends the day, and, where the family wants it, add a second station on another floor of the same apartment.

The door itself deserves as much attention as the electronics. In many of these houses the street door is the original wooden or an older metal door, heavy, often slightly out of square after nearly a century, and fitted with a lock that was never meant to work with a strike. The strike, the part in the frame that lets the latch go when someone presses the release, has to line up with the latch exactly, and if the door has dropped on its hinges a few millimeters the release buzzes and nothing happens. We adjust or reset the door before fitting the strike, check that the closer pulls it fully shut every time, and only then connect the intercom. For households that keep Shabbos, this is also where we fit the manual hold-open or latch, because it has to be designed into the strike and the frame, not added as an afterthought.

The four- to six-family walk-up: one button per home, and the eight-apartment line

Older four- to six-family walk-ups sit all over Borough Park, especially on the side streets between Thirteenth and Eighteenth Avenues and closer to Fort Hamilton Parkway. Most have a vestibule with an outer door, an inner door and a button panel in between, and a riser of intercom wire that climbs the stairwell to each apartment. In buildings of this age, that riser has often been patched many times. A typical survey finds some apartments on the original wire, one or two on a newer pair run along the stair, and at least one unit whose wire was cut during a renovation and never reconnected. We test every run with the panel disconnected, so we know before quoting which wires can carry a new system and which need replacing, and we label each one at both ends.

These buildings also sit right on the line in state law. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom in multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and allows tenants in older buildings of that size to require one by majority request; buildings under eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement, although their entrance doors must still be self-closing and self-locking. A six-family walk-up is therefore not legally required to have an intercom at all, but nearly every owner we meet wants one anyway, because six households sharing a door without one means keys handed to visitors, doors propped open and packages left in the hall. We explain plainly which parts of the job are required by law and which are simply good sense, so an owner can decide with the facts in front of them.

The converted or enlarged house: counting the homes before counting the buttons

Borough Park's housing shortage has reshaped a great many houses from the inside. Cityneighborhoods.nyc records older one-family houses being converted into multi-unit dwellings, and the city's special zoning for the neighborhood in the 1990s allowed larger lot coverage and smaller yards, which encouraged enlargements upward and toward the back. For an intercom, the result is predictable: the panel at the door was installed for the house as it was, and the house is no longer that house. We regularly find a two-button panel serving a building with four or five apartments, with the extra families relying on a phone call, a separate doorbell, or a neighbor who lets visitors in.

The first job on a converted house is therefore to count, floor by floor, with the owner, and to agree on what each apartment will be called on the panel. Then we look for the path. New floors and rear additions usually have fresh walls with space to run cable, while the original part of the house has plaster on lath or masonry. Where we can, we use the stair as the spine of the system and branch off at each landing. Where a new apartment is reached by its own entrance, such as a separate side or rear door, we plan a second door station rather than sending visitors to the front. And because the number of homes may rise above eight after a conversion, we mention the state rule for that size of building, so the owner knows if the requirement now applies.

The limestone row house: a beautiful front that is easy to crack

Among the brick rows, BHS describes attached brick and limestone row houses, and limestone needs its own care. Limestone is softer than granite but brittle at the edges, and a large hole drilled close to a corner or a joint can spall the face. We place surface panels on the masonry beside the door rather than on carved trim, drill with the hammer action off where the stone allows, and keep anchor holes back from edges and joints. Where an owner prefers no new holes in the stone at all, a panel can often be set into the door frame or the vestibule wall instead, with the visitor ringing from just inside the outer door.

The store with apartments above: two doors, two schedules

On Thirteenth Avenue and the other shopping streets, Wikipedia describes the zoning as either commercial or residential with shops on the ground floor, and that mix is exactly what an intercom has to sort out. The store opens and closes on its own schedule, closes completely for Shabbos and the holidays, and may change hands; the families upstairs need to come and go, and receive visitors, every day of the week. When the apartment entrance shares a vestibule or a wall with the shop, the two can easily end up on one system, and then a store owner's decision about locks or power can leave the tenants without a working door. We keep the residential door on its own panel, its own power supply and its own strike, wired so the store can be closed, rewired or renovated without touching it.

The new infill building: planning the system before the walls close

Between 2010 and 2025, the Furman Center counts 2,340 new apartments in Borough Park buildings of four or more units, and Yak Management notes a steady layer of new multifamily construction and infill between Fort Hamilton Parkway and New Utrecht Avenue. New buildings are the easiest intercom jobs if we are called early and the hardest if we are called after the drywall is finished. The ideal is a conduit or cable path from the street door to a small equipment closet and from there to each apartment, planned with the electrician, and a decision made in advance about whether the system will be a conventional wired intercom or a networked video system with app answering.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Half Hour at a Borough Park Street Door

Open and close the door ten times. Before touching the intercom, the technician opens the street door and lets it close on its own, again and again. Does it close fully every time? Does the latch catch, or does it bounce? Does it drag on the saddle? An intercom cannot fix a door that does not latch, and in a building of this age the door is the most common reason a "broken intercom" is not broken at all.

Find the power and the old wires. Next comes the transformer or power supply, often in the cellar, sometimes in a closet under the stair, and the bundle of wires that leaves it. The technician measures what the supply is putting out and notes how many conductors climb toward the apartments.

Test every run with the panel disconnected. Each wire to each apartment is tested on its own. A run that tests open is traced with a tone generator to find where it was cut.

Ask about the week, not only the door. The technician asks how the household or the tenants use the door on Shabbos and Yom Tov, and whether a rav has advised them on what is acceptable. We do not decide those questions. We write down the answer and choose hardware that can do what the household needs.

Count the homes out loud. Finally, the technician walks the building with the owner and counts apartments floor by floor, front and back, including the basement and anything added on top. The number on that list, not the number of buttons on the old panel, is the number we plan for.

Shabbos-Ready Entry

When Nobody Presses the Button: Setting Up a Borough Park Street Door for Shabbos and Yom Tov

This section is about hardware, not halacha. We are installers. What a household may or may not do with an electric door on Shabbos and Yom Tov is a question for the household's rav, and different families and different buildings follow different practices. What we can do is make sure the door is capable of whatever the household has been told is right: opening by hand without electricity, standing unlatched for the day, staying locked with a mechanical way in, or simply not lighting up, beeping or recording when someone walks past. All of that has to be designed into the door and the intercom from the beginning.

Why the door hardware matters most. An intercom is two things joined together: a way to talk to the person at the door, and a way to unlock it from a distance. The talking part can simply be left alone on Shabbos. The unlocking part is where households run into trouble, because an electric lock that only opens when it receives power will not open at all if nobody presses the release. Din Online's June 2026 Q&A on electric doors on Shabbos notes that such doors often have a latch that can be moved before Shabbos to bypass the electric operation and let the door be opened by hand. That latch, or an equivalent mechanical way to open the door, is the heart of a Shabbos-ready installation.

Strikes that hold, strikes that release, and the latch in between

Most Borough Park street doors use an electric strike: the part in the door frame that holds the latch until the intercom tells it to let go. Strikes come in two basic types. A fail-secure strike stays locked when it has no power and opens only while power is applied; a fail-safe strike is the opposite and opens when power is removed. Many strikes can be set either way. On top of that, many strikes and many door locks have a mechanical feature that keeps the latch free, or keeps the lock's own handle working, regardless of power. For a household that wants the door to stand unlatched for Shabbos, that feature, set before candle lighting and reset after, is often the simplest answer. For a household that wants the door locked but openable with a key or a mechanical code, the strike stays fail-secure and the lock carries the mechanical way in.

The choice is not only about Shabbos. A fail-safe strike on a street door means the door opens whenever the power fails, for any reason, which in a small building with no one watching the door may not be what the owner wants. A fail-secure strike keeps the building locked during an outage but needs a reliable key or mechanical entry for everyone who lives there. We talk this through with the owner and the household, write the choice into the plan, and label the strike setting inside the frame cover so the next technician knows how it was configured.

Magnetic locks: what they do when the current stops

Some buildings, especially newer ones and some institutional buildings, use a magnetic lock: a strong electromagnet on the frame that holds a steel plate on the door. As Din Online explains, an electromagnetic lock holds the door locked by electric current, and when the current is interrupted, the door can be opened. That makes a magnetic lock fail-safe by nature. It also means that a magnetic lock is only as secure as its power supply, and that anyone who can cut the power can open the door. Because people inside must always be able to get out, magnetic locks on exit doors come with release devices and rules about how they are wired. We install them where they suit the building, for example on an inner door of a new building with a monitored power supply, and generally prefer a strike on the street door of a small house or walk-up.

Lights, screens, cameras and sensors

A modern video panel does more than ring. Its screen may light up when someone approaches, its camera may begin recording when it senses movement, its buttons may glow, and its indoor monitor may chime or show the picture without anyone touching it. Driscoll Lock and Key points out that motion-sensor lights, automatic door closers and alarms can activate unintentionally, and that smart lock makers often include a Shabbat mode. Some households are not concerned with any of this; others want the street door to be completely passive for the day. Before we choose a panel, we ask which features the household needs to be able to switch off, and we check that the panel we propose can do it, whether through a setting, a schedule or a switch on the power supply. If a feature cannot be turned off on a particular model, we say so and suggest a different one.

Mechanical code locks and keys for the day

For households that want the door locked on Shabbos, a fully mechanical keypad deadbolt, sold widely as a "Shabbos lock," is one option: the code is entered by pressing mechanical buttons, with no battery and no electronics. It can be fitted on the street door alongside the intercom's strike, so the building has electric release all week and a mechanical code on Shabbos. The other option is a plain key, which works for the family but leaves guests waiting on the step. We fit whichever the household chooses and make sure it does not interfere with the strike, the closer or the latch.

The weekday test, done with you

Din Online suggests a very simple check: during the week, turn off the power to the intercom and the lock system, then see whether the door can be opened by hand without electricity. We do exactly that at the end of every installation where the household needs the door to work on Shabbos. With the household watching, the technician switches off the power, sets the latch or the lock the way it will be set before Shabbos, and someone in the family opens the door from outside and from inside. Then we reset it and switch the power back on. If anything does not work as expected, it is fixed then and there, not discovered on a Friday night with guests at the door. We also write the steps on a small card for the household, in plain words, so whoever sets the door before Shabbos does it the same way each week.

City and State Rules

What New York Law Says About Intercoms in Borough Park's Houses and Walk-Ups

Buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 that contains eight or more apartments to have self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system at the main entrance. A Borough Park house converted after that date into eight or more apartments falls under this rule, even though the house itself is older.

Older buildings of eight or more apartments. The Department of Buildings' tenants' rights guide says buildings from before 1968 must also have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of all the apartments request it, and that landlords may recover the cost of providing this equipment from the tenants. The DOB rule 1 RCNY §42-01 repeats the requirement for buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted before 1968.

Buildings under eight apartments. Parker Custom Security's summary of the law notes that buildings with fewer than eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement, although the self-closing and self-locking door requirement still applies. That describes a very large share of Borough Park's two-, three- and six-family buildings. One installer's glossary claims the law covers buildings of three or more units; that conflicts with the eight-apartment threshold in §50-a, and we rely on the city's own documents.

When an intercom exists, it must work. The same summary describes a non-working intercom or buzzer as a Class B hazardous violation under the Housing Maintenance Code, enforced on tenant complaint, and HPD's own guidance says the owner, manager or superintendent should repair broken locks or intercoms immediately. City rules also require building entrance doors in multiple dwellings to be self-closing, and HPD treats a failure to maintain self-closing doors as a Class C immediately hazardous violation.

What the minimum system must do. Rule 1 RCNY §42-01 sets the minimum as a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device, that lets the occupant of each apartment communicate with a person outside the main entrance door, plus a return buzzer mechanism to release the door. It also sets heights: no push button more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet from the floor. We use those heights on every panel we mount, whether or not the building is required to have an intercom, because they suit almost everyone who will use it.

Intercom Installation in Borough Park: Questions Answered

Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Shabbos, Yom Tov and the street door

Guests walk over from shul for the Friday night meal, and nobody in our house will press the door release. How do they get in?

The usual answer is to decide, before Shabbos, how the street door will stand, and to fit hardware that makes that easy. Many households set a manual latch on the strike before candle lighting so the door can be pushed open from outside without any electricity, then reset it after Havdalah; others keep the door locked and fit a mechanical code lock so guests can be given a code, or simply have someone come down to open it by hand. Which of those is right for your home is a question for your rav. What we do is make sure the door can do it: a strike with a mechanical hold-open, a closer that still pulls the door shut, and a card explaining how to set and reset it. We test it with you, with the power off, before we leave.

Is an electric strike or a magnetic lock the better choice for a Borough Park building where the door must open by hand on Shabbos?

For most houses and walk-ups, an electric strike. A strike leaves the door's own lock and handle in place, can be set fail-secure so the building stays locked during a power cut, and often has a mechanical feature that frees the latch when the household wants it. A magnetic lock is fail-safe by its nature: as Din Online describes, it holds only while current flows and releases when the current is interrupted. That can suit an inner door in a newer building with a monitored power supply, but on the street door of a small building it means the door is open whenever the power fails, and it needs a proper exit release. We explain both, write the choice into the plan and label how the hardware is set.

Our video panel's screen lights up and the camera starts recording whenever someone walks past. Can it stay dark on Shabbos?

On many panels, yes, but not on all of them, and it is worth knowing before you buy. Some models let you schedule the screen, the button lights and motion-triggered recording off for set hours; some only allow it from the app; on some, the only way is to switch off the panel's power supply, which also stops the intercom from ringing. Before we recommend a panel, we ask which features the household needs to be able to quiet and check the model can do it. If you already own a panel that cannot, we can sometimes fit a labeled switch on its power supply so the household can turn it off before Shabbos, and we test that the door still opens by hand with it off.

Houses that keep changing

We converted our three-family into five apartments. Does each one need its own button and its own handset?

In practice, yes. Every household should be able to see or hear who is at the street door and let them in, without relying on a neighbor or a phone call. We count the apartments floor by floor, agree with you what each will be called on the panel, and plan one button and one indoor station per apartment. The old two- or three-button panel is usually replaced rather than extended, because adding buttons to an old panel rarely works well. Where a new apartment has its own entrance, we plan a separate door station there. If the conversion brings the building to eight or more apartments, it may now fall under the state intercom rule, and we mention that so you can check it.

We are a big family on three floors of our own house. Can the door ring on every floor, and can the little ones be stopped from buzzing in strangers?

Both are easy to arrange. Most systems allow several indoor stations for one apartment, so the door can ring in the kitchen, on the bedroom floor and in the basement, and whoever is nearest answers. To keep children from releasing the door for anyone who rings, we place stations where adults are, mount them higher on the wall, or choose stations where the release needs a deliberate second press after answering. On video systems, the picture lets an adult see who is there before deciding. We set this up with you on the day and show older children how the system works, so the door is not a mystery to anyone in the house.

If Hatzolah is called at night for someone on the top floor, how do they get through the street door quickly?

The fastest way is a door release close to where the call is made. In a house where the only indoor station is in the kitchen downstairs, a family member upstairs may have to run down two flights to open the door. A second station on the bedroom floor, or a release button by the apartment door, cuts that time. We also make sure the door releases reliably, because a strike that buzzes without opening is the worst thing to discover in an emergency. Questions about Shabbos and Yom Tov in an emergency are for your rav; our job is to make sure the hardware can open the door fast whenever someone needs it to.

What the law asks of smaller buildings

Our walk-up on 47th Street has six apartments. Is the owner legally required to have an intercom at all?

Under the state rule as published, probably not. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a ties the intercom requirement to buildings of eight or more apartments, and summaries of the law say buildings with fewer than eight are not covered by it. The entrance door must still be self-closing and self-locking, and if the building does have an intercom, it has to work, because a non-working intercom or buzzer is treated as a Class B violation enforced on tenant complaint. So a six-family owner can choose not to have one, but almost every owner we meet keeps one, because without it visitors are given keys or the door gets propped open. We are not lawyers; check with HPD or an attorney if it matters for your building.

Our building has more than eight apartments, was built before 1968 and has never had an intercom. Can the tenants ask for one?

According to the Department of Buildings' tenants' rights guide, yes. Buildings from before 1968 with eight or more apartments must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of all the apartments request it, and the guide adds that the landlord may recover the cost of providing the equipment from the tenants. In practice, that means the tenants organize a written request signed by a majority, and the owner then has to provide a system that meets the minimum in 1 RCNY §42-01. We can survey the building for the owner and price a system that meets that minimum or goes beyond it with video, so both sides know what the work involves.

Panels, names and cameras

How high on the wall should the buttons and the speaker go?

The city rule for apartment building intercoms, 1 RCNY §42-01, says no push button should be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device should be between four and five feet. We use those heights on every panel, including on houses that are not required to follow the rule, because they work for adults, older visitors and most children. On a video panel, we also check that the camera sees faces at the step, not just the tops of heads or chests. On a Borough Park stoop or a step above the sidewalk, that means measuring from where the visitor will actually stand, and sometimes angling the panel slightly.

Do we have to put family names on the outside panel?

No. Many households prefer not to show family names to the street, and many owners find names hard to keep current when families move. Panels can show apartment numbers, floor and position such as "2 Rear," or a short code, and digital directory panels can list names only on a screen that visitors scroll through. In a building with many apartments, a clear numbering system that matches the doors upstairs is often more useful to visitors than names. We agree the labels with the owner and the households before printing anything, and we keep a copy of the directory so it is easy to update later.

Can the camera in our video door panel be registered with Shomrim's camera network?

BoroPark24 reported that Shomrim asks residents to register their outdoor cameras, so that when a crime happens nearby the volunteers know how to reach the camera's owner quickly. A video intercom panel records the street door, so it is the kind of camera that can be useful. Registration is between you and Shomrim; we do not register cameras for customers. What we can do is make the panel worth registering: mounted at face height, with the step and the sidewalk in view, set to record when someone rings or approaches if the model allows, and with the recordings kept long enough to be retrieved. We show you how to find and save a clip on the day.

We want to replace our wired buzzer with an app-based "virtual doorman." Could that get the owner a violation?

It can, depending on the building. In a case summarized by the ALB law firm, a condominium board replaced its hard-wired intercom with a virtual doorman, an HPD violation followed, and it was dismissed only because the building had four units, below the threshold that requires an intercom. In a building of eight or more apartments, the replacement must still let every apartment talk to the person at the door and release it, including households without a smartphone or that do not use one on Shabbos. We usually recommend keeping an indoor station in every apartment, with the app as an extra rather than the only way to answer.

Stores and institutions

Our Thirteenth Avenue building has a store downstairs and four apartments above, sharing one vestibule. Can the tenants' door be kept apart from the shop?

Yes, and it is worth doing. We give the apartment entrance its own panel, its own power supply and its own strike, and route its wiring so it does not pass through anything the store controls. If the vestibule is shared, the outer door can stay on the residential system while the store's door off the vestibule has its own lock. That way the store can close for Shabbos and Yom Tov, renovate or change tenants without affecting the families upstairs, and the tenants' door can be set for Shabbos as they need. We check with the owner who controls each door and write it into the plan, so nobody is surprised later.

Our shul's office wants a door station that rings the office during the day, but the main doors stand open during davening. Can one system handle both?

Usually, yes. The main doors can simply be left on their own locks and schedule, while a door station at the side or office entrance rings a desk station in the office during working hours. When the office is empty, the station can be switched off, or a second station can ring a caretaker. If the building has several entrances, each can have its own station, all answered from the office. A video panel at the side door lets the office see who is ringing. We plan the cable route with whoever manages the building, and work on weekdays outside the times the building is busiest.
