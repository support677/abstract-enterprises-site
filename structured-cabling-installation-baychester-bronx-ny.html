The Neighborhood

Why Baychester Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Baychester is mostly brick and frame houses from the middle of the last century, set close together on flat land, with one of the largest retail districts in the city on its eastern edge, and each of those facts shapes how a network cable travels from the provider's line to the place it is needed. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood as dominated by one- and two-family homes of various styles, bounded by East 222nd Street to the northeast, the New England Thruway to the east, Gun Hill Road to the southwest and Boston Road to the northwest, with Eastchester Road as the main street through the middle. The section between Boston, Eastchester and Gun Hill Roads is also known as Fish Bay. That is the area this page is about. Co-op City and the Bay Plaza shopping district, on the other side of the Thruway in ZIP 10475, are sometimes counted as part of Baychester too, and we cover the stores and offices there as well, while Co-op City's own towers have their own cabling page.

The age of the houses explains a great deal. NeighborhoodScout reports that 81 percent of Baychester's homes were built between 1940 and 1969 and that most are owner-occupied, and Point2's figures give a median construction year of 1953. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes the earlier wave, between about 1920 and 1940, when modest one- and two-family houses in Tudor and Colonial Revival styles began to line newly cut streets between Boston Road and Eastchester Road on what had been farmland and marsh. None of those houses was built with a data jack. The best they had was a telephone wire to the kitchen, and everything since then, from cable television to the fiber box in the basement, was added by whichever provider was selling service at the time and routed by whatever path was quickest that day.

The house listings we found show what that means in practice. A 1935 brick Colonial on Fish Avenue is described as semi-detached, with a fully finished walk-out basement holding a bedroom, a bathroom and the laundry, and a detached garage at the end of a shared driveway. A 1955 house on Edson Avenue is listed as a brick semi-detached single-family home of about 1,780 square feet on a lot of about 2,000. A 1960 house on Gunther Avenue is a solid brick two-family on a corner lot, a two-bedroom apartment over a one-bedroom, with a finished walk-out basement that has its own kitchenette and bath. Brick exterior walls, a party wall shared with the neighbor, a basement that someone already finished and lives in, and a second household upstairs: those four conditions decide almost every route we draw in Baychester.

The city's zoning keeps the neighborhood that way. When the City Council adopted the Village of Baychester Rezoning in 2006, City Planning counted 65 percent of the homes in that area as one- and two-family detached houses, and the new zoning permits only detached one- and two-family residences there. The 2011 Williamsbridge/Baychester Rezoning covered 181 blocks between East 233rd Street and East Gun Hill Road, and City Planning's photographs from that study show a detached single-family house and a multifamily building on East 220th Street and attached multifamily houses on De Reimer Avenue. So the houses that exist today are, for the most part, the houses that will be here in twenty years. A cable run properly once is worth the effort, because nobody is going to knock the house down and wire it fresh.

The provider side is in better shape than the inside of most houses. BestNeighborhood.org reports Verizon fiber and EarthLink fiber available to 99.91 percent of Baychester and Optimum fiber to 99.78 percent, and BroadbandNow puts fiber availability across the Bronx at 84.7 percent. Those are commercial comparison sites, and availability at a given address can still differ, so we never promise which service a particular house can order. The point is that the bottleneck in a Baychester house is rarely the street any more. It is the stretch between the provider's box, usually in the basement or just inside the front wall, and the bedroom on the second floor where someone works from home, separated from that box by a brick wall, a floor, a staircase and a kitchen full of pipes.

Two-family houses add a second layer. When an owner lives downstairs and rents the upstairs, or the other way around, both households usually share one entry point for service, one basement and sometimes one provider account that has been stretched between floors with a long cable up the stairwell or a Wi-Fi extender on a landing. That arrangement works until somebody's video call drops, a tenant moves out, or a printer on one floor starts showing up on the other floor's network. A structured plan gives each apartment its own cable, its own equipment and its own network from the start, with a clear label on everything in the shared basement so it is obvious which ports belong to whom.

Then there is the commercial edge. Wikipedia calls Bay Plaza the largest shopping center in New York City, about 1.3 million square feet built in 1987 and 1988 by Prestige Properties between Bartow and Baychester Avenues, expanded to about two million square feet once the enclosed Mall at Bay Plaza opened on August 14, 2014. Around it sit power-center buildings on Baychester Avenue, a Montefiore primary care practice in a suite at 2100 Bartow Avenue and a CityMD at 2146 Bartow Avenue, while Boston Road, Gun Hill Road and Eastchester Road carry the neighborhood's own strips of shops and offices. Each of those businesses runs registers, card terminals, scheduling and patient systems, cameras and guest Wi-Fi over its network, and each depends on a landlord who controls the ceiling, the roof and the route in from the street.

Where Service Enters

From the Basement Box Up: Where a Baychester House's Network Begins

Every home network has a starting point, the spot where the provider's line stops being the provider's and becomes the household's wiring. In a tall apartment building that spot is a telecom room in the cellar. In a Baychester house it is usually a small box on the basement wall near where the line comes through the foundation, or a fitting on the front of the house with a modem just inside the living room window. Where that box ended up was decided by the first installer's convenience, not by where anybody in the house needs a connection today, so the first thing we do on a walk-through is find it, read it, and decide whether the network should really start there.

The provider's equipment and ours are kept apart

The provider owns its line from the street or the pole, the fitting on the house and its terminal or modem. We do not cut, open or relocate any of that; if a fiber terminal has to move, the provider moves it. What belongs to the household starts at the port where the provider's equipment hands off the connection: the router's location, the cable to every room, the enclosure, the switch, the access points and the battery backup. A structured cabling plan covers that household side. Drawing the line clearly has a practical payoff. When the provider sends a technician for a repair, the technician can see exactly where their job ends, and has no reason to drill a fresh hole through the brick because they cannot find the old one.

When a house has had more than one provider

In a neighborhood where three fiber services are listed as available, it is common to find a house that has switched at least once. That usually leaves two or three entry points through the foundation or the front wall, some carrying wire that no longer connects to anything. Each of those holes is a place for water, cold air and insects to get in. Before we add anything, we put a tone on each wire to learn which ones are live. Dead wire comes out if the owner agrees, its holes are sealed from inside and out, and the house ends up with one documented entry. We also plan the inside wiring so it does not care which provider the house uses next. Every room's cable runs back to the same enclosure, so changing providers means changing one box, never rewiring a bedroom.

Bringing the starting point to the right place

A router on the basement shelf beside the boiler is rarely the right place for a house's network to begin. The signal has to climb through a floor and a brick or plaster wall to reach anyone, and the equipment lives in the dampest and dustiest corner of the building. We do not need the provider to move anything to fix that. One tested cable runs from the provider's box to a small enclosure mounted high on a dry wall, either in a clean corner of the basement or in a closet on the first floor near the middle of the house, and the router, switch and battery live there. From the enclosure, each room, access point and device gets its own run. The provider's box stays exactly where it is, and the household's network now starts somewhere sensible.

Brick and Basement Routing

Brick Walls, Shared Walls and Finished Basements: How Cable Moves Through a Baychester House

A typical Baychester house gives a cable installer three floors to work with: a basement, a main floor with the living room, dining room and kitchen, and a second floor of bedrooms, sometimes with a small attic or knee-wall space above. In a house built before the war that might be a Tudor or Colonial Revival with plaster walls; in a house from the 1950s or 1960s it is more often brick outside with plaster or early drywall inside. The cable does not care which style the house is. It cares about where the hollow spaces are, which walls are solid, and how many times the house has been changed.

Reading the house one floor at a time

We start in the basement because that is where the service usually arrives and where the joists under the first floor are often easiest to reach. If the basement ceiling is still open, the joists are a highway: cable travels along them on proper supports and comes up into the first-floor walls through small holes in the plate. If the basement has been finished, that highway is closed off behind drywall or a dropped ceiling, and we look for a utility room, a laundry area or a closet where the ceiling was left open or can be opened neatly. Upstairs, we look for the spaces that stack: a first-floor closet under a second-floor closet, the wall beside the stairs, a plumbing chase that leaves room alongside the pipes, or an attic hatch over the bedroom hall. The best route in most Baychester houses uses one of those stacks to climb and the basement or attic to travel sideways.

Brick on the outside, so the route stays inside

The outer walls of a brick house are not hollow in the way a frame wall is. There may be a thin space between the brick and the plaster, but it is often packed, furred with narrow strips, or interrupted at every floor. Fishing cable down an exterior brick wall usually means fighting the house, and drilling through it to run cable outside and back in again leaves holes in masonry that let in water. So in Baychester we keep almost every route on interior walls and inside closets, use the stair wall and the stacked closets to change floors, and only go through brick where a device actually has to be outside, such as a camera over the driveway or an access point for the yard. When we do go through, we use a core bit sized to a sleeve, slope the hole so water runs out, and seal it from both sides.

The wall you share with your neighbor

Semi-detached houses, like the brick houses listed on Fish and Edson Avenues, share one side wall with the house next door. That wall is the one place where a misplaced drill bit can come out in somebody else's living room, and it may also be part of the building's fire separation. We do not fish cable inside a party wall or drill into it to mount anything heavy. Jacks along that side of the house are set on a furred surface, an interior partition or a short raceway, and every hole near the shared wall is measured and checked with a detector before it is made. Where a provider's line enters on one side of a shared driveway and crosses to the other house, we treat that line as the provider's and route our own cable from the inside of your house only.

Finished basements: the ceiling everybody forgot

Listings in Baychester describe finished walk-out basements with bedrooms, bathrooms, laundries and even kitchenettes. Those rooms are lovely to live in and awkward to wire, because the open joists that used to carry every cable in the house are now hidden. There are good ways around it. A dropped ceiling with lift-out tiles can carry cable on its own supports above the grid. A drywall ceiling can usually be crossed by drilling through the joists from a utility room where the ceiling is still open and fishing along a single joist bay, with a small access hole at each end that we patch. A soffit that hides a duct or a beam often has space alongside it. When none of that works, a slim painted raceway along the top of the wall is better than cutting a long trench in a finished ceiling. We show you the options on the walk-through, with the trade-offs, and the plan records which one we chose for each run.

Reusing old phone and coax paths

Almost every house here has phone wire running to a kitchen or bedroom jack and coax running to the rooms where televisions used to sit. Neither of those cables can carry a modern wired network properly, but the holes and paths they follow are often useful. If an old wire still moves freely when tugged, it can sometimes be used to pull new cable through the same path and then removed. If it is stapled tight or painted over, it stays where it is and we find another way. We never cut an old line until we know whether anything still depends on it, because some houses still have an alarm panel or a phone line for an elderly parent running over wire that looks abandoned.

Two-Family Houses

One Basement, Two Households: Cabling a Baychester Two-Family

Wikipedia, City Planning and the listings all point to the same thing: a large share of Baychester's houses hold two households. A two-family might be an owner downstairs and a tenant upstairs, two generations of the same family, or a house where the finished basement has quietly become a third living space. Each arrangement wants something slightly different from the network, but they share one rule. Each household should have its own cable, its own equipment and its own network, and the shared parts of the house should be labeled so clearly that a tenant, a relative or the next owner can tell which piece belongs to whom.

Where each apartment's cable lives

The cleanest arrangement keeps each apartment's cable inside its own walls as far as possible. The downstairs apartment's runs come up from the basement ceiling into the first-floor walls. The upstairs apartment's runs travel through a stack that reaches the second floor without crossing the first-floor rooms, typically the stair wall, a closet stack or a chase beside the plumbing, and then spread out through the second-floor ceiling or the attic. Both sets end at the basement enclosure on separate, labeled patch panels. Nothing belonging to the upstairs apartment passes through a downstairs bedroom closet, which matters when the tenant changes and the owner does not want a stranger's technician working in their home.

Turnover without rewiring

Tenants come and go. When an upstairs apartment changes hands, the new tenant may want a different provider, a different router, or a desk in a different room. A properly built two-family plan absorbs that without anybody touching the walls. The provider connects at the basement, the tenant's router plugs into the upstairs apartment's section, and every jack upstairs is already wired and labeled. If the owner later decides to use the upstairs apartment for family, the two networks can be joined at the patch panel in a few minutes. We leave a short written note in the enclosure explaining exactly how each apartment is set up, so whoever opens it next does not have to guess.

The basement apartment question

Some finished basements are used as separate living spaces. Whether a basement unit is a legal apartment is a question for the Department of Buildings and the owner, not for a cabling contractor, and we do not advise on it. What we can do is wire the space safely and practically for whoever uses it: jacks or an access point in the rooms that need them, cable kept clear of the boiler, water heater and electrical panel, and a clear choice about which network the basement rooms join. If the basement rooms belong to the owner's household, they join the owner's network; if they are used by someone else, they get their own section of the panel like any other apartment.

Low Ground

Flat, Low Land and Basement Equipment: Keeping a Baychester Network Dry

Wikipedia describes Baychester's terrain as low-lying and flat, with parts of the East Bronx built on former marshland, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc recalls that the land was farmland and marsh before the houses went up in the 1920s and 1930s. We did not find flood maps or records that single out particular Baychester blocks, so we make no claim about any one street. But in a neighborhood where the network almost always starts in the basement, a little caution about water costs very little and saves a great deal.

Nothing on the floor, nothing on the bottom shelf

The modem, router, switch and battery backup belong high on a dry wall, not on the floor beside the washing machine or on the lowest shelf near the stairs. A wall-mounted enclosure keeps them off the ground, away from mops and buckets, and out of reach of the moisture that collects near a foundation after a heavy rain. If the only sensible spot in the basement is low or damp, we move the enclosure to a first-floor closet and run the provider's connection up to it on one cable. The battery backup sits in or beside the enclosure, sized to keep the network running through a short outage.

Cable that crosses a basement floor or wall

Cable belongs overhead in a basement, never lying on the slab or stapled along the bottom of a wall where water collects first. Where a run has to come down a basement wall to a jack or a device, it travels inside a raceway or conduit with the jack mounted well above the floor. Where cable passes through the foundation to the outside, the hole is sleeved, sloped and sealed so it never becomes the low point where water finds its way in. And if a basement has already taken water, cable that sat in it should be tested; a run that fails gets replaced rather than coaxed along.

Interwar houses: respect the plaster

A Tudor or Colonial Revival from the 1920s or 1930s usually has plaster on wood lath, and plaster does not forgive rough work. Fishing a cable down a plaster wall is possible, but a careless hole can crack a section around it or knock plaster keys loose behind the surface. We cut small openings with a fine blade rather than punching through, drill the top or bottom plate from the attic or basement where we can, and fish gently with flexible rods. Where a wall has a fire block or diagonal bracing partway down, we may come in from a closet on the other side instead. If a house of this age still has traces of very old wiring in the walls, we leave it for an electrician to assess and route our low-voltage cable well clear of it.

Semi-detached houses: one side is off limits

In a semi-detached house the shared wall is the side we treat as untouchable. All of the climbing happens on the other walls and through interior closets, and all of the sideways travel happens in the basement ceiling or the attic. Television and desk jacks on the shared side are placed on interior partitions or mounted in a surface box, never fished into the party wall itself. The enclosure goes on a basement wall that belongs only to your house. If your driveway is shared, we keep any garage or camera run on your side of the line and talk through the route with you before anything is drilled.

Two-families: two stacks, two panels

The question in a two-family is never whether cable can reach the second floor; it is whether it can get there without passing through the first-floor apartment. We look for a stack that serves only the upstairs, such as the wall beside the common stair, a chase beside the plumbing or a closet stacked under an upstairs closet. Once the upstairs runs reach the second-floor ceiling or attic, they spread out to the rooms. Both apartments end at the basement on separate labeled panels, and the port map says which jack belongs to which apartment in plain words.

Detached houses: the outside walls become an option

A detached house has the most freedom inside and the most temptation to run cable outside. Sometimes going outside is the right answer, for example to reach a garage or a camera at the back of the lot, but we never use the outside of the house as a shortcut between two inside rooms. Where a run does leave the house, it goes through a sleeved, sloped hole under an overhang, uses cable rated for outdoor exposure, stays in conduit wherever it could be hit or chewed, and comes back in the same careful way. The rest of the house is wired from the inside like any other.

Attached rows and small buildings: the owner decides first

In an attached row or a small apartment building, the cellar, the hallways and the roof are common areas, and the owner or managing agent decides what can run through them. We confirm the route with the owner before quoting, carry each apartment's cable through the cellar ceiling and a shared riser or stair path, and label everything in the common spaces by apartment. A tenant who wants a better connection in a rented unit needs the owner's written permission before anything is drilled, and we are happy to talk the owner through the plan.

Brick houses, basements and the rooms upstairs

In a brick semi-detached house whose outside walls are solid, which wall does the cable use to reach the second floor?

Almost always an inside one. In the semi-detached brick houses listed on Fish and Edson Avenues, one side wall is shared with the neighbor and the other outside walls are masonry, so the climbing has to happen in the middle of the house. The usual candidates are the wall beside the staircase, a first-floor closet that sits under a second-floor closet, and the chase that carries the bathroom plumbing up through the house, which often has room for a cable alongside the pipes. We check each one from the basement ceiling and the attic or upstairs hall, using an inspection camera through a small opening, and pick the one that reaches the most upstairs rooms with the fewest holes. Once a cable reaches the second-floor ceiling or attic, it can spread out sideways to each bedroom. If none of the inside paths is open, a painted raceway in a closet corner is the fallback, never the exterior brick.

We bought our house from an estate and found a bundle of unlabeled network cables coming out of the basement ceiling. Can we use them?

Possibly, and it is worth finding out before anything new is pulled. Someone may have wired several rooms years ago and never documented it. We put a tone on each cable to find where it ends upstairs, look at the jack or the loose end, and then test each run on a cable analyzer. A run that passes can be terminated on a new patch panel, labeled and used like a new one. A run that fails, or turns out to be phone wire or an older grade of cable that cannot carry a modern connection, can sometimes be used to pull a new cable along the same hidden path. Cables that go nowhere are cut back neatly and removed where they are reachable. You end up with a port map that finally explains what is in your walls, and often a cheaper job than wiring from scratch.

Our upstairs bedrooms each have only a couple of electrical outlets. Does every new network jack need a new outlet beside it?

No. A data jack and a power outlet are separate things, and a jack does not need power of its own. A desk computer or a television plugs into the nearest existing outlet and into the new jack, which we place close to where the device will sit. Access points and many cameras do not need an outlet at all, because they take their power over the same network cable from a switch in the enclosure. Where a room really does need another outlet, for example behind a wall-mounted television, that is a job for a licensed electrician, and our plan marks it clearly so you can arrange it or ask us to coordinate. What we will not do is run data cable inside the same box or the same hole as power wiring, which is why jacks and outlets usually sit side by side rather than in one plate.

We want a wired doorbell at the front steps and a camera over the driveway. Should they join the house network or have their own?

They can share the cabling but should not share the network freely. Both devices are cabled back to the basement enclosure like any other run, and both can usually take their power over that cable, which avoids an outdoor outlet. Inside the switch, we can put cameras and the doorbell on their own segment, so the devices that face the street cannot see your laptops and phones, while your phone app still reaches them. If you use a recorder for the cameras, it sits in the enclosure on the same segment. At a semi-detached house with a shared driveway, we also talk about aiming before anything is mounted, so the camera covers your side of the drive and your car rather than your neighbor's door. The pathway out through the brick is planned once and sized for both cables, plus a spare.

If we sell the house in a few years, does a structured network do anything for a buyer?

It does not change the listing price on its own, and we would not promise that it does. What it changes is what a buyer, an inspector and the next family find. A labeled enclosure on a dry wall, a port map, tested runs to the main rooms and access points on each floor are much easier to explain than a shelf of loose boxes and cables stapled along the baseboards. Buyers who work from home tend to ask about the internet early, and being able to say that every bedroom has a tested wired connection answers that question in one sentence. Keep the handover file with the house papers; it is the part that makes the wiring understandable to someone who was not there when it went in.

Two-family houses and tenants

Our two-family has one internet account. Can the upstairs tenant have a private network without us paying for a second provider?

Yes, technically, although it is a decision for you as the landlord as much as a wiring question. One provider connection can feed two separate networks if the router or a small firewall in the basement enclosure is set up to keep them apart, so neither household can see the other's devices. The upstairs apartment then gets its own cabling, its own access point and its own network name, all running from its own section of the patch panel. Many landlords prefer that each household orders its own service, which keeps billing and responsibility simple, and the cabling we install works either way: both apartments' runs end in the basement, so switching from one shared account to two separate ones later means changing equipment in the enclosure, not opening a wall. Whatever you choose, we document it in the enclosure so a future tenant's provider understands the setup.

Does our tenant need to be home while you wire the upstairs apartment?

Someone authorized needs to let us in, and the tenant should know exactly when we are coming and what we will do. As the owner, you arrange access with your tenant in whatever way your lease and New York law require; we do not enter an occupied apartment without the owner's go-ahead and a time agreed with the people who live there. In practice most of the upstairs work happens in a few predictable spots, such as the hall ceiling, a closet and the rooms getting jacks, and we tell the tenant in advance which furniture may need moving. We lay drop cloths, keep dust to small drilled holes, and clean up before leaving. If the tenant cannot be home, a short visit can be split into two sessions so the apartment is never left with open holes overnight.

A wired connection at each end of the stream. A livestream over Wi-Fi from the back of a busy hall can stutter just as the sermon starts, because dozens of phones in the room are competing for the same air. We run a cable to the camera position, which can often power a network camera directly, another to the sound desk or the computer that encodes the stream, and a run back to wherever the internet comes into the building. If the hall also wants a screen or projector for the congregation, that position gets a cable too. The routes stay out of sight in a hall that is often an older building, through a ceiling, along a balcony front or inside a raceway painted to match, and every cable is labeled so volunteers can find the right one on a Sunday morning.

Our office on Gun Hill Road is replacing its desk phones with internet phones. Does every desk need its own cable for the phone?

Not necessarily. Many internet desk phones have a small switch built in, so the phone plugs into the wall jack and the computer plugs into the phone, sharing one cable. "If fiber reaches the house, the wiring inside is already fast."

Fiber availability in Baychester is listed at more than 99 percent for Verizon, EarthLink and Optimum, according to BestNeighborhood.org. FAQ

Baychester Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Can the network enclosure go in the same basement corner as our boiler and water heater?
+

Near it, but not in it. The corner around a boiler and water heater is usually the warmest, dustiest and most likely place in the basement to see a leak, and it needs clear space for service. We mount the enclosure on a dry wall a sensible distance away, high enough that water on the floor cannot reach it, and keep cable clear of flue pipes, hot water lines and gas piping. If the only free wall in the basement is close to the heating equipment, we often put the enclosure in a first-floor closet instead and run the provider's connection up to it on one cable.

Does a cabling job include setting up the Wi-Fi name, passwords and devices, or is that separate?
+

Basic setup of the equipment we install is part of the job: the switch, the access points and the network names and passwords you choose, checked on your devices before we leave. Setting up everything else in the house, such as smart televisions, streaming boxes, printers, game consoles and doorbell apps, is a setup visit, which has a fixed Bronx list price of 225 dollars for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks. Many households book that time on the same day as the final test so everything is working together when the crew leaves.

The wall where our television goes is brick on the inside. Can the cable and power still be hidden?
+

Usually, with a little planning. Interior brick, such as a chimney breast or an old bearing wall, cannot be fished like a hollow wall, so we bring the network cable and the power to the television from a nearby hollow wall, from the basement ceiling below or from a closet behind, and finish with a short neat channel where that is the only way. When the screen is mounted on the brick itself, the Bronx list price adds 65 dollars for a brick or stone wall, and the code-compliant in-wall power and cable kit is 169 dollars where a hollow wall is available beside it. The drop plan shows the route before anything is drilled.

Can you remove the old coax and phone wire stapled across the outside of our brick house?
+

Yes, once we know it is dead. We trace each wire first, because some of it may still serve a phone line, an alarm or a television you forgot about. Wire that is confirmed dead is removed with your permission, the staples and clips come out, and every hole it used is cleaned and sealed so water and cold stop finding their way in. Anything the provider still uses stays in place for the provider to handle. The removal is listed as its own optional line in the drop plan, so you can decide whether to do it now or later.

What exactly is in the handover file when the job is finished?
+

A printed port map inside the enclosure that names every jack and access point and which apartment or room it serves; the cable analyzer results for every run; photos of the routes where they disappear into walls and ceilings and of each sealed penetration; the model of every piece of equipment we installed; and a short note on how the networks are arranged, especially in a two-family. You get a digital copy as well. It is the document a future electrician, provider technician, tenant or buyer will be glad to find.

Does the spot chosen for the enclosure need its own electrical outlet?
+

It needs a dependable outlet nearby, because the router, switch and battery backup all draw power. Many basements already have one close to the laundry or the provider's box. If the best wall for the enclosure has no outlet, a licensed electrician adds one, and we either coordinate with your electrician or tell you exactly what is needed and where. We do not run extension cords to network equipment as a permanent arrangement, and we keep the battery backup plugged directly into the wall.

Our Tudor has a small finished room in the attic that we use as an office. Can it get a wired connection?
+

Usually, and it is often one of the most useful runs in the house, because an attic room is the farthest point from a basement router. The cable climbs through the same interior stack used for the second floor, then continues up beside the attic stair or through the space behind a knee wall into the room. We keep the cable off hot roof sheathing, supported along the framing, and finish with a jack near the desk and, if the room is large, a small wall-plate access point. Where the attic stair is enclosed, the wall beside it is often the cleanest route.

Will the cabling you install work with whatever brand of router or access points we buy later?
+

Yes. The cable, jacks and patch panels follow open industry standards, so any router, switch, access point or device that uses a normal network port will work with them. That is a large part of the point of structured cabling: the wiring in the walls is expected to outlast several generations of equipment. When you replace a router or switch to a different brand in five years, it plugs into the same labeled patch panel. The only thing to check is that a new switch can supply power over the cable if your access points or cameras depend on it.

We want a connection in a shed at the back of the yard. Is the cable different from the indoor kind?
+

Yes. Cable that leaves the house is rated for outdoor exposure or direct burial depending on the route, and it travels in conduit wherever it could be hit by a mower or a shovel or chewed by animals. It leaves the house through a sleeved, sloped and sealed hole under an overhang and enters the shed the same way. If the run is long or the shed has its own power, a fiber link or a pair of outdoor wireless bridges may be a better choice, and we explain the trade-offs. Digging across a yard is planned with you first so nothing underground is disturbed.

Can you test cabling another company installed and give us a written report?
+

Yes. We trace and identify each run, test it end to end on a cable analyzer, and write up what we found: which runs pass, which fail and why, what is unlabeled, and what we would recommend fixing first. Many Baychester owners ask for this when they buy a house with existing wiring, and stores ask for it when they take over a unit. The report is useful whether or not we do the repairs, and it often shows that a few re-terminated jacks and a proper patch panel are all a building needs.

How do you protect our floors and finished rooms while the work is going on?
+

Drop cloths go down along every route before anything is opened, furniture is moved only with your permission, and drilling is done with dust collection wherever possible. Holes are kept small and patched neatly where we make them in finished surfaces. Ceiling tiles in a finished basement are lifted and replaced one at a time. At the end of each day the work area is vacuumed and nothing is left open where children or pets could reach it. If a job runs over two visits, we tell you exactly what will be left in place overnight.

What should we have ready before the crew arrives?
+

Clear access to the basement ceiling along the planned routes, to the closets we will use and to the spots where jacks and access points are going. Move fragile items off shelves near the work, and make sure someone can let us into every part of the house we need, including an upstairs apartment if it is part of the job. Have the provider's account details handy in case the router needs reconnecting, and tell us about any planned painting, flooring or basement work so we can fit around it. For a store, confirm the working hours and any building access with the landlord.
