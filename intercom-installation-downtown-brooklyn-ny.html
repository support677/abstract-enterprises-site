The Neighborhood

Why Downtown Brooklyn Properties Need Intercom Installation

Downtown Brooklyn is the borough's business district, and by Wikipedia's count the third-largest central business district in New York City after Midtown and Lower Manhattan. For most of its history the people arriving at its doors were shoppers, office workers, students and court visitors, and the buildings were designed around reception desks, guards and storefronts. The 2004 rezoning was meant to add more of the same. It did not. When Borough President Eric Adams's office looked back in 2016, PoliticsNY reported that the plan had projected 4.6 million square feet of new office space and about 1,000 new homes, and that by 2014 roughly 1.3 million square feet of office had been built while more than 11,000 apartments had been built or were in development. The Real Deal, citing a Downtown Brooklyn Partnership report in August 2025, put the total at 26,853 units since 2004. For an intercom installer, that turn of events is the whole story: a district of office-style lobbies became one of the densest concentrations of apartment entry systems in Brooklyn.

What that means at the door is volume. A typical Brooklyn walk-up has a panel with six or eight buttons and a handful of visitors a day. A Downtown rental tower has a directory of several hundred names, a stream of couriers and food delivery riders from late morning to late night, guests arriving for residents on every floor, movers on weekends, and in many buildings a front desk that is staffed for some hours and not others. 6sqft's April 2026 renter guide describes the range: keyless entry at 300 Ashland, a 24/7 doorman at One Boerum Place and a 24-hour attended lobby at 505 State Street. Each of those is a different answer to the same question of who decides whether a visitor gets in, and the intercom has to fit whichever answer the building chose. When it does not, the failures are public and immediate: a guest standing on the sidewalk while a call rings a phone that was logged out months ago, a delivery rider let into the elevators because the release was pressed by the wrong apartment, a desk that cannot reach a resident because the directory was never updated.

The second reason is change. Downtown is still building, and it is now converting too. 141 Willoughby Street, a 24-story, 355,000-square-foot tower that Mann Report says was delivered in 2023 as Class A office space and never occupied, is being turned into 239 rental apartments on floors 8 through 23 while floors 1 through 7 stay commercial. A building like that needs an entry plan the original architects never drew: residents and office visitors sharing a street, perhaps a lobby, and certainly a set of elevators, with different hours and different people deciding who goes up. Meanwhile buildings finished only a few years ago are reaching the age when the first round of entry hardware, apps and subscriptions starts to show its weak points, and when boards discover that the developer's installer still holds the administrator account.

The third reason is the older stock that sits between the towers. Concord Village, built in 1950 between Cathedral Place and Tillary Street, is listed by CityRealty as seven mid-rise towers with about 1,022 apartments, a full-time doorman, security guards and a live-in superintendent. The Offerman Building on Fulton Street, finished in 1893 and designated a landmark in 2005, had been converted to 121 apartments by 2017. Fulton Mall itself, the stretch from Adams Street to Flatbush Avenue with about 230 stores, carries only buses, delivery and emergency vehicles, so the apartments above its shops are reached through narrow residential doors between storefronts. And the area's colleges, from LIU Brooklyn at Flatbush and DeKalb to City Tech, NYU Tandon and Brooklyn Law School, bring student housing with its own patterns of arrivals and move-outs. None of these is a glass tower, and none of them should get a glass tower's intercom by default.

What the record says about Downtown doors and the people who use them

  • The apartments came instead of the offices. The 2004 plan projected about 1,000 homes; more than 11,000 were built or in development by the time of the Borough President's 2016 report, and The Real Deal counted 26,853 units by August 2025. Most Downtown intercom work is therefore residential lobby work inside buildings that look like office towers.
  • The pace has not slowed. The Downtown Brooklyn Partnership's 2025 year in review reported more than 3,700 units built in 2025, a record, with thousands more expected in 2026. New buildings mean new systems, and new systems mean takeovers from developers and their installers.
  • Office towers are becoming apartments. 141 Willoughby Street is being converted to 239 rentals on floors 8 through 23 above seven commercial floors, under a $208 million construction loan announced in September 2026. Split-use towers need split entry rules.
  • There is a large mid-century co-op in the middle of it. Concord Village, built in 1950, has seven towers and about 1,022 apartments with a doorman and security staff, which puts the desk at the center of the intercom design rather than at its edge.
  • The main shopping street carries no private cars. Fulton Mall from Adams Street to Flatbush Avenue is open only to buses, delivery and emergency vehicles, so ride-share passengers and many couriers arrive on foot from the corners, looking for a residential door among some 230 storefronts.
  • Two precincts and two ZIP codes. Wikipedia places Downtown in Community District 2, served by the 84th and 88th Precincts, with ZIP 11201 north of DeKalb Avenue and 11217 to the south. Couriers and delivery apps follow the ZIP and the street number on the lobby, so both have to be right on the panel and in the app.

Repair Calls in Downtown Brooklyn

Downtown Lobby Intercom Not Working? Calls That Ring Nobody, Doors That Say Open and Stay Shut, a Desk Station Gone Quiet

Repair calls from Downtown buildings tend to come from managing agents and boards rather than single tenants, and they usually describe one of five problems. The first is the panel that works for some apartments and not others: visitors can reach the fourteenth floor but not the thirty-second, or every unit added since a renovation rings nowhere. In a networked system that is usually the directory or the call routing, not the hardware. In an older wired system like a mid-century co-op's, it is more often a riser connection on one floor or a failed distributor. The second is the call that arrives on residents' phones late or not at all, which is usually an app logged out after a phone upgrade, notifications switched off, or a change to the building's internet service or firewall. The third is the release that reports success while the door stays locked. ButterflyMX's own resident help page describes this case, an app that shows the door unlocked while it stays shut, as pointing to a wiring or lock hardware fault, and that matches what we find: a strike out of adjustment, a magnetic lock whose power supply has failed, or a door that has dropped on its hinges so the latch binds. The fourth is the desk station that has stopped receiving calls, often because it was moved, unplugged or replaced during a lobby refresh. The fifth is physical damage to a street panel on a busy block.

Each has a different first check, and we start with the cheapest. Pete Carmello of the Brooklyn company Lock and Tech told Brick Underground that "a lot of times it's user error," and Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith added, "It's all in the wire connections." Both are right in Downtown: a large share of calls are solved by fixing an account, a directory entry, a schedule or a setting, and most of the rest are solved at a terminal, a splice or a power supply. Before a technician comes out, we ask what changed: a new internet provider, a lobby renovation, a new managing agent, a move-out season, a power event, construction on another floor. If the problem is a lapsed subscription or a password nobody has, we say so. If the panel is damaged beyond sensible repair or no longer supported, we tell you whether replacement makes more sense, and the repair visit is quoted before work starts.

Repairs on a system another company installed are routine. We need to know the brand and model on the panel, who holds the administrator login, and whether a service contract is still running, because some developers' systems are tied to a monthly agreement and the provider may need to be called first. Brick Underground's guide also makes a point every tenant should know: you will need authorization from the building before any repair to a building intercom takes place, so in a rental tower the request should come from, or be cleared with, the managing agent.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Downtown Brooklyn's Architecture

Downtown's buildings span from the 1890s to towers that received their certificates of occupancy last year, and an intercom is planned differently in each. What changes from one to the next is where visitors arrive, how many apartments or suites share the panel, who sits at the desk, how cable gets from the lobby to each home, and whose rules apply to the lobby finishes. The cards name the six kinds of building we meet most; the notes below explain the method for each.

Rental towers since the rezoning

Glass and concrete towers with several hundred apartments, an amenity floor, a package room and a lobby that may or may not be staffed around the clock.

Condo towers and mixed tenure

Towers like the Brooklyn Tower at 9 DeKalb Avenue, which Wikipedia lists with about 425 rentals and 150 condos under one roof, where boards and rental management share a lobby.

Office towers turning residential

Buildings like 141 Willoughby Street, where apartments start above seven commercial floors and the original risers were laid out for office telecom rooms.

The 1950 co-op complex

Concord Village's seven mid-rise towers and about 1,022 apartments, with a doorman, security staff and decades of older intercom wiring in the walls.

Apartments above Fulton Mall stores

Older commercial buildings, including converted landmarks like the Offerman Building, where the residential door sits beside shop entrances on a car-free street.

Office buildings and campuses

Suites in older Court Street and Montague Street office buildings, Brooklyn Commons at the former MetroTech, and college buildings, where the lobby belongs to the landlord.

Rental towers since the rezoning: a directory, a desk and a lot of deliveries

The towers that went up after 2004 share a basic plan. A street entrance, often a glass vestibule with an outer and an inner door, leads to a lobby with a desk, a mail and package room, and elevator banks. The intercom panel sits at the outer door or in the vestibule, the release controls the inner door, and the equipment lives in a telecom room on the ground floor or the cellar, with cable or network connections running up the building's risers to each floor. In the newest buildings the apartment side often has no wall station at all: residents answer on an app, and the building's own network or the panel's cellular link carries the calls.

The design work in these buildings is mostly about routing and volume rather than wire. A panel that searches several hundred names needs a directory that management can keep current without a service call. The desk, where there is one, needs to see and answer street calls during its hours and hand them to apartments when it closes. Couriers and food delivery riders need a path to the package room or the desk that does not give them the elevators. And because a tower lobby is the only way in for hundreds of households, the panel, the release and the network switch behind them need battery backup that keeps the door answerable through a short outage. The News 12 report from June 2026 on The Addison, a 26-story Downtown building where tenants said that even getting in and out had become a daily challenge during elevator problems, is a reminder of how much depends on the lobby working.

Where we install the apartment side as wall stations, the cable path is usually the existing low-voltage riser. We open it during the survey, because a riser that the developer's electrician filled to capacity or ran without pull strings can turn a one-day job into a three-day one. Where the apartment side is app-only, the work is in the telecom room and at the door, plus the checks on the building's network described further down.

Condo towers and mixed tenure: two kinds of owner, one lobby

Some of Downtown's tallest buildings mix rental apartments and condominiums. Wikipedia describes the Brooklyn Tower at 9 DeKalb Avenue, beside the landmarked Dime Savings Bank, as 1,035 feet tall with about 425 rentals and 150 condos and about 120,000 square feet of amenities; it received its permanent certificate of occupancy on April 25, 2025. In a building like that, the lobby system serves both groups, but the decisions about it may sit with a condominium board, a rental management company, or both, under documents the developer wrote. Before quoting a change, we ask who owns the lobby equipment, who holds the administrator account, and whether either side's governing documents say anything about entry systems.

The practical design questions are about separation without duplication. Condo owners may want features rental management does not, such as visitor video saved to the owner's own account, or the ability to give a contractor a time-limited pass. Rental management may want move-ins and move-outs handled centrally. A single panel with one directory and different permission groups usually serves both better than two panels at the door. We write the permission groups out on the design so both sides can see exactly who can do what.

Office towers turning residential: residents above, offices below

An office tower becoming apartments is new territory for most of Brooklyn, and Downtown has the clearest example. Mann Report describes 141 Willoughby Street as a 24-story Class A office tower delivered in 2023 and never occupied, now being converted by a Capstone Equities and BH3 joint venture into 239 rental apartments on floors 8 through 23, with floors 1 through 7 remaining commercial. Pincus Co. reported the original filing in December 2025 as a 233-unit conversion. We have no role in that project and no knowledge of its design. We use it here because it shows exactly the problem a split building poses at the front door.

Office entry and apartment entry run on different logic. Office visitors are expected, arrive in business hours, check in with a desk or a tenant's reception and are sent to one floor. Residents' guests arrive at any hour, are approved by one household, and should reach only that household's floor. Couriers serve both, in very different volumes. The intercom for a split building usually ends up with one of three arrangements: separate street entrances with separate panels; one entrance with one panel whose directory is divided into offices and residences, each with its own routing and schedule; or one entrance where the desk handles office visitors and the panel handles residents. Which one fits depends on the lobby layout and the elevator plan, and the elevator plan matters most, because a visitor approved by an apartment should not be able to ride to an office floor, and the reverse.

The wiring question is just as specific. Office towers are laid out with a telecom room on each floor feeding open plates through ceilings and raised floors. Apartments need a connection to each unit's door or wall, through finished corridors. In a conversion the best time to solve that is during construction, when corridors are still open and the architect is still drawing the units. A short written intercom design added to the drawings, showing where each apartment's station or network jack goes, which riser feeds it and where the lobby equipment lives, costs very little compared with fishing cable through finished corridors after the residents move in.

The 1950 co-op complex: the doorman stays at the center

Concord Village is a different world from the glass towers around it. CityRealty describes the complex at 270 Jay Street as seven mid-rise towers built in 1950 on one site, with about 1,022 apartments, a full-time doorman, security guards and a live-in superintendent, and lists other Concord Village addresses including 230 Jay Street and 215 and 225 Adams Street. In a co-op like that, the intercom has two jobs: connecting the doorman and security staff to every apartment, and letting a visitor at a lobby door reach a resident when the desk is busy or the door is unattended. Decisions go through the board and the managing agent, and the money usually comes from reserves or an assessment that shareholders feel directly.

The engineering questions in a building of that age are what wiring is in the walls and what it can still carry. Older lobby-to-apartment systems often run on multi-pair cable up a riser, with each apartment's handset on its own pair or on a shared bus. Some of that wiring can carry a modern two-wire video system; some of it cannot, and some is in such poor condition that reusing it would only move the problem. We test before recommending, floor by floor, and we tell the board which buildings and which risers can be reused, which need new cable, and what each option costs. A board can then decide whether to replace building by building over several budgets or all at once.

In a co-op the apartment side also matters more than in a rental tower. Many shareholders have lived in their apartments for decades, and a wall station they can answer without a phone is often non-negotiable. Modern systems can offer a wall station in every apartment and an app for those who want one, on the same lobby panel. We always quote both, so the board sees the cost difference rather than guessing at it.

Apartments above Fulton Mall stores: a narrow door on a crowded street

Fulton Mall's buildings were built for department stores, banks and shops, and many of their upper floors now hold apartments. The Offerman Building, finished in 1893 and designated a New York City landmark in 2005, is the best-known example; Wikipedia's article on the mall says it had been converted to 121 apartments by 2017. Smaller commercial buildings along the mall and on its side streets have the same arrangement on a smaller scale: a residential door between two shop entrances, a narrow stair or a small elevator, and a handful to a few dozen apartments.

At those doors, the intercom has to solve three things the towers do not. The station must be easy to find among storefront signs, because ride-share passengers and couriers arrive on foot from the corners of a street that carries only buses, delivery and emergency vehicles. It must be kept apart from the shop's own doorbell or delivery button, so a store delivery never rings an apartment. And it has to survive a sidewalk that is busy from morning to night, which favors a vandal-resistant metal station, set at the heights the city's rule requires, with the cable entering from behind. On a landmarked building, anything new on the facade also needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission's review, so we design the station's position, finish and fixing method to be as small and reversible as possible and provide photos and drawings for the owner's application.

Office buildings and campuses: the landlord's lobby, the tenant's door

Downtown's office stock runs from older buildings around Court Street and Montague Street near Borough Hall to Brooklyn Commons, the 16-acre superblock formed in 1992 as MetroTech Center between Flatbush Avenue Extension and Jay Street, which holds the FDNY's headquarters at 9 MetroTech and NYU's engineering school. In those buildings the landlord runs the lobby: turnstiles, a security desk, visitor passes. A tenant's intercom starts at its own suite door. The design questions are where the station mounts given the landlord's corridor finishes, how it reaches the tenant's network rather than the building's, and who answers when reception is empty. College buildings add schedules and many more users, and student residences bring the move-in and move-out rhythms covered in the questions below.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Watch the Lobby for an Hour Before Designing It

The most useful thing to do before designing a tower lobby intercom costs nothing but time: stand in the lobby for an hour at a busy part of the day and watch. Count who comes through the street door and how each person gets in. Residents with fobs. Guests calling up. Couriers with carts heading for the package room. Food delivery riders who wait for a resident to come down, or who press every button until someone opens. Movers, cleaners, dog walkers, maintenance staff. In a building with a desk, notice how many of those people the desk actually deals with and how many simply walk past it.

That hour tells you more than a floor plan. It shows where visitors look for the panel, which is often not where the developer put it. It shows whether the desk is overwhelmed, which decides whether street calls should go to the desk first or straight to apartments. It shows whether the delivery traffic needs its own door or its own pass. And it shows how loud the vestibule is when the street door opens onto Flatbush Avenue Extension or Jay Street traffic. We design around what that hour shows, then check the design against the building's busiest evening and its quietest night.

The Desk and the Panel

Attended Lobbies, Part-Time Desks and Virtual Doormen: Who Answers the Street Door in a Downtown Tower

Downtown's buildings answer their doors in three broad ways, and the intercom design follows from which one a building uses. Some, like the attended lobbies 6sqft listed at One Boerum Place and 505 State Street, keep a person at the desk around the clock. Some staff the desk for part of the day. And some, like the keyless-entry building the same guide described at 300 Ashland, rely on credentials and an app, with nobody at the door at all. Each model fails in its own way when the intercom is designed for a different one.

In a fully attended lobby, the intercom is the desk's tool. Street calls usually ring the desk first. The concierge sees the visitor, asks who they are there for, calls the apartment on an internal line or an app, and releases the inner door. The weak points are the desk station itself, which has to be fast enough for a busy evening, and the call to the apartment, which has to reach residents who are not home or not answering their wall station. The desk also needs a record of who it let in and for whom, because when a package or a guest goes missing, that record is the first thing management asks for. Cyrus Claffey, the president of ButterflyMX, put the selling point of networked entry plainly to Habitat magazine in 2015: "Every single transaction on the screen is documented." That is useful, and it is also a reason to set retention rules on purpose, as the next section explains.

In a part-time desk building, the design has to handle the handover. A schedule switches street calls from the desk to apartments when the desk closes, and back when it opens. What happens to a call that the desk does not pick up within a set time during staffed hours, whether it rolls to the apartment or waits, is a decision for management, and it should be written down. We set the schedule to the desk's real hours, including weekends and holidays, and give management a simple way to override it when the desk closes early.

In a building with no desk, every street call goes straight to residents, usually on an app, sometimes to a wall station, and the building leans on delivery passes, guest codes and a package room door that couriers can open without reaching the elevators. The risk there is the resident who cannot be reached: a phone logged out after an upgrade, notifications switched off, a resident traveling. Each of those leaves a visitor on the sidewalk, and the most common response is for someone to prop the door. A good no-desk design gives every apartment at least one way to be reached that does not depend on one phone, and gives management a weekly look at which apartments have stopped answering.

Habitat's 2015 report on phone-based entry also shows why these systems spread. It described a 114-unit Ditmas Park co-op, built in 1961, that replaced a failing hardwired intercom with a system that calls residents' phones; rewiring the building would have cost about $70,000, the article said, while the phone system cost about $7,000. The magazine later noted those prices were out of date, and a Downtown tower is not a Ditmas Park co-op. But the trade-off it describes, cheaper and faster to install against reliance on residents' phones, is the same one every Downtown board and managing agent weighs.

Data and the Law

Smart Entry Keeps Records: What New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Law Means for a Downtown Lobby

An app-based or fob-based entry system knows who came and went, when, and who let whom in. That is why managers like them, and it is why New York City regulates them. HPD's page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, sets out the rules for owners of buildings with smart access systems. Owners must obtain express consent "either in writing, or through a mobile application" before collecting the data such a system uses to identify people. They may collect "only the following minimum amount of authentication data and reference data." They must destroy authentication data "no later than 90 days after such data has been collected or generated" unless it is anonymized. They may not sell, lease or disclose the data except as the law allows. And they must "provide tenants with a data retention and privacy policy." The Real Deal's 2021 report on the law, introduced by Council Member Mark Levine in October 2019, added that owners must remove, anonymize or destroy a tenant's information no later than 90 days after the tenant moves out.

For an installer, the law turns into settings. A modern lobby panel can usually store call logs, door release logs, snapshots of every visitor, video clips, and sometimes face images, and many store far more by default than an owner needs or than the law allows them to keep. During programming we go through each of those with the owner or managing agent, set what is kept and for how long, switch off what is not needed, and record the settings in the handover so the owner's privacy policy matches what the system actually does. We do not give legal advice, and owners should have their own counsel review their policy, but we make sure the hardware is not quietly keeping records that nobody decided to keep.

The law matters most in Downtown's busiest months. In buildings near the colleges, many residents move in and out in the same few weeks; in large rental towers, turnover is continuous. Each move-out starts a clock on that resident's data. The cleanest approach is a move-out checklist that removes the resident's app account, fob and directory entry on the same day, so nothing lingers by accident. We set that up with management during handover, along with a short guide to doing it without a service call.

Face recognition deserves its own mention, because residents ask about it. Some entry panels offer it, and the Tenant Data Privacy Law's consent requirement applies to the data such features collect. A building that wants it has to obtain that consent first, and residents who decline still need a practical way in. In most Downtown buildings we recommend against making face recognition the only way in. Fobs, codes, app passes and a call to the apartment cover the same needs with far less data.

Rules at the Street Door

Self-Locking Doors, Intercom Requirements, Mounting Heights and Low Voltage: The Rules Behind a Downtown Entry System

Most of Downtown's apartment buildings were built after 1968, which puts them under the plainest part of New York State's Multiple Dwelling Law. Section 50-a requires buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 to have automatic self-closing and self-locking doors at their entrances, and requires those "containing eight or more apartments" to have an intercommunication system that lets a visitor speak with each apartment and lets the occupant release the door. The same section requires older buildings of that size to install the doors and the system when tenants in a majority of apartments ask in writing, with installation to start within ninety days, and it makes willful damage or tampering with the equipment a misdemeanor. For a Downtown tower, that means the intercom is not optional. For an office tower converted to apartments, the word "converted" in the law is the one to note.

HPD's ABCs of Housing adds the maintenance duty in plain terms: the building manager, superintendent or owner should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms. In practice, residents in rental buildings report a broken intercom to management first and to the city through 311 if it is not fixed. For owners, a design that keeps working through a power cut, that management can troubleshoot from a phone, and that does not depend on one person's login is the best protection against a door that stays broken.

The Department of Buildings rule on entrance doors and intercoms, 1 RCNY §42-01, sets heights for the systems it covers, and we follow them as the baseline everywhere. No push button "shall be more than six feet from the floor," and the speaking and listening device must be "not less than four feet and not more than five feet from the floor." The U.S. Access Board's guide to the ADA Standards adds what an accessible entry system needs. Where two-way communication systems are provided to gain entry, "they must include visual and audible signals to accommodate people with hearing, speech, or visual impairments," "lighted signals should have labels to indicate their meaning," and if handsets are provided, "cords must be at least 29″ long." We choose panels that show a call is connected and the door is released on a screen or a labeled light as well as by sound, and mount them within those heights and within reach.

The last rule is about who does the work. New York City's Administrative Code treats wiring designed to operate at under fifty volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data as low-voltage work, outside the electrical permit provisions, while leaving life-safety systems and connections to power circuits on the electrical side. Intercom panels, apartment stations, network cable and most door releases fall in the low-voltage category. If a lobby door's lock is tied to the fire alarm, that connection stays with the fire alarm contractor and its approvals; we identify such doors during the survey and coordinate rather than rewire them. And if a design needs a new outlet or circuit, that piece goes to the building's licensed electrician.

Two patterns run through all of these. The first is the business that sits inside someone else's building. A tenant on a Brooklyn Commons floor, a shop under a rental tower, a vendor in a food hall: each controls its own door and nothing else. The intercom design starts with what the lease allows, where the landlord's network ends and the tenant's begins, and who has to approve anything mounted outside the premises. We provide a short written design for the landlord's review, with a device list, mounting details, cable route and power source, so approvals do not hold up the work.

The second pattern is the office that cannot open its door to everyone. Offices near the courts, social service providers and clinics often see visitors who are anxious or upset, and staff need to speak with a visitor and see them before the door moves. A video station at the suite door, a release limited to a few staff, and a discreet way for whoever answers to alert a colleague are the usual elements. We set those up as part of the design rather than as an afterthought, and we test them with staff in the room.

What a business intercom in Downtown needs that an apartment one does not

  • Accessible entry. Where an entry system is provided, the Access Board's guide calls for visual and audible signals and labeled lights, and handset cords of at least 29 inches where handsets are used. We select stations that meet those points and mount them within reach.
  • Free exit. Any door with an electric release must still open from inside without a key or special knowledge. We match the strike or lock to the door and frame and test free exit with the power off on every job.
  • Rated doors. Many suite doors off office corridors are fire-rated. Release hardware on those doors must be listed for use on rated openings.
  • Fire alarm interfaces. Doors whose locks release on a fire alarm signal are the fire alarm side's responsibility. We identify them and coordinate with that contractor.
  • Landlord review. Corridor walls, lobby finishes and storefront facades usually belong to the landlord. Anything mounted there goes through the landlord first, and on a landmark, through the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • Recording and privacy. A camera door station records whoever stands in front of it. Decide who can view it and how long it is kept, and tell staff and regular visitors it is there.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Downtown Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the situations owners, boards, managing agents, residents and office tenants in Downtown Brooklyn are most likely to bring to us, grouped by the kind of building. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Office towers becoming apartments

Our apartments begin on the eighth floor of a tower whose lower seven floors stay commercial. Should residents' guests and office visitors arrive through the same street panel?

Only if the lobby and elevators are laid out so the two groups can be separated after the door. If there is one street entrance, one panel with a divided directory works well: residents listed under apartments with calls going to their homes around the clock, offices listed under companies with calls going to their reception during business hours and a polite message after hours. What must not happen is a resident's guest riding to an office floor or an office visitor reaching the apartment floors, so the release from each kind of call should be tied to the elevator access for that group. Where the building has a separate residential entrance, a separate panel there is simpler and clearer for visitors.

The tower was laid out with a telecom room on each floor for office tenants. How does cable get from there to an intercom station inside each new apartment?

Usually through the corridor ceiling, from the floor's telecom room to a point inside each apartment's entry, which is why the time to plan it is while the corridors are still open during the conversion. Each apartment gets either a network jack for a video wall station, a jack for the residents' own equipment if the system is app-only, or both. The telecom room on each floor then becomes a convenient place for a small switch that powers the stations on that floor, on battery backup. Fishing the same cable after the corridors are finished is possible but slower and more visible.

Offices in our building close at night, but residents come and go around the clock. Can someone visiting an office be turned away after hours while residents' guests still get through?

Yes. Each directory group gets its own schedule. Office listings can ring reception during the hours the offices set, then switch to a recorded message or simply stop appearing on the panel, while apartment listings ring residents at any hour. Office tenants who need after-hours visitors, such as a cleaning crew or a late meeting, can be given time-limited passes rather than leaving their listings open all night.

Our apartment floors will open while crews are still finishing other floors. How should the entry system handle workers and new residents at the same time?

Keep them on separate credentials and, if possible, separate doors. Construction crews usually get a contractor entrance or a set of passes that open only the construction areas and expire on a set date, issued and revoked by the general contractor or the owner's representative rather than by residents. Residents get the finished lobby, the panel and the elevators that serve occupied floors. The worst arrangement is a construction worker holding the residents' lobby door open all day because nobody planned another way in.

Rental and condo towers

Our building's entry app says the lobby door is unlocked, but it stays shut. Is the problem my phone or the building?

Almost always the building. If the app reports that the release command went through, your phone did its part. The failure is at the door: a strike or magnetic lock out of adjustment, a failed lock power supply, a door that has dropped and binds on its latch, or a wiring fault between the panel and the lock. ButterflyMX's resident help page gives the same diagnosis for its own system. Report it to management with the time it happened, which helps whoever services the system find it in the logs.

Our lobby has a desk, but the concierge often has no idea who a resident is expecting. Can residents send a guest's name to the desk ahead of time?

On many current systems, yes. Residents create a guest entry in the app with a name and a date, and it appears on the desk's screen or list, so the concierge can greet the guest by name and call up only to confirm. Some systems can also send the guest a pass that works at the street door during a set window. Whether to let guests in on a pass without the desk seeing them is a building policy decision, and we set the system to match what management decides.

Management is considering face recognition at our tower's lobby door. What should residents know before it goes in?

Under the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, an owner of a smart access building has to obtain express consent, in writing or through an app, before collecting the data such a system uses to recognize people, must limit what it collects, must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and must give tenants a data retention and privacy policy. Residents can ask for that policy and should expect another way in if they decline. From an installer's side, fobs, codes, app passes and calls to the apartment already cover every entry need without storing anyone's face.

Many apartments in our building turn over in late summer, when students from the nearby colleges move in and out. How do we handle dozens of entry accounts changing in the same week?

Plan it as a batch. Before the season, management exports the list of leases ending and starting, and the system's accounts, fobs and directory entries are scheduled to switch on the move-out and move-in dates. Most current systems let a managing agent do this in bulk from a web dashboard rather than one apartment at a time. Each removal also starts the clock on that resident's data under the Tenant Data Privacy Law, which is one more reason to do it on the day rather than whenever someone notices.

Our tower's package room runs on a locker company's own app. Should the lobby intercom connect to it?

It helps if the two can share one thing: the courier's route. Couriers should be able to call or open a delivery door that leads to the package room or lockers without reaching the elevators, and the intercom is usually what opens that door. Whether the intercom and the locker system share resident lists or notifications depends on both brands; some integrate and many do not. If they do not, we keep them separate and make sure the door between them is controlled properly.

Older buildings, walk-ups and offices

Our apartments are above shops on Fulton Mall, and the street is crowded until late. How do we stop strangers from slipping in behind residents at our street door?

Start with the door itself: a strong closer that shuts and latches quickly behind each person, and a lock that re-locks every time. Then make the intercom the easy way in for anyone who belongs there, so residents' guests and couriers are not tempted to wait for someone to open up. A video station that records each call helps residents see who is asking to come in. If the building has a vestibule, keeping the inner door on its own release, so it opens only after the outer door has closed, cuts down on people following a resident through.

Our co-op's buildings were finished around 1950. Should we expect the original wiring to be reused or replaced?

Expect a mix, and expect a test before anyone decides. Some mid-century risers carry modern two-wire systems well; others have splices, water damage or missing pairs on certain floors. We test each riser, floor by floor, and give the board a building-by-building result with the cost of each option. Reusing sound wiring saves money and disruption in occupied apartments; forcing a modern system onto failing wiring only moves the repair calls into next year's budget.

Our office near Borough Hall and the courts sometimes has visitors who arrive upset. Can staff talk to someone at the suite door and quietly alert a colleague?

Yes. A video station at the suite door lets reception see and speak to the visitor with the door still locked. The answering station or the phone app can include a discreet button that sends an alert to a manager or a group of colleagues, and some systems can trigger a separate signal in a back office. The door release can be limited to a few staff. We set the alert path with your office manager and test it with staff so everyone knows what happens when it is pressed.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Downtown Brooklyn

Search summaries and cost guides about intercoms are mostly written for houses and small apartment buildings, and they often miss what makes Downtown different. Here are claims that come up often, checked against the sources on this page and what our surveys find.

"A new luxury building's intercom is built in, so there's nothing to plan"

Quick answers tend to treat the entry system in a new tower as a finished feature, like the elevators: it came with the building, and it works.

In practice, Downtown's newest buildings are where we find the most unfinished entry systems. Directories still list the leasing office, desk schedules were never set, the administrator account sits with the developer's construction manager, and nobody has decided what the system keeps or for how long. With more than 3,700 units built in 2025 alone, according to the Downtown Brooklyn Partnership, a lot of those systems are only months old.

A new building's system is a starting point. The owner or board should confirm who holds the account, set the routing to match how the building is staffed, and set retention to match the Tenant Data Privacy Law before the first move-in season, not after the first complaint.

"Intercom rules only matter in old walk-ups"

Summaries of New York's intercom rules often frame them as a problem for prewar buildings with broken buzzers.

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies most directly to buildings erected or converted after January 1, 1968, which is almost all of Downtown's apartment stock: those with eight or more apartments need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and an intercommunication system that reaches each apartment and releases the door. An office tower converted to apartments is covered by the word "converted."

So the rules apply to the towers as much as to the walk-ups. What differs is the failure mode: in a tower, it is rarely a dead buzzer and much more often an apartment that cannot be reached because its account or directory entry is wrong.

"App-based entry is just an intercom on your phone"

Many descriptions present app-based entry as a convenience upgrade with no other consequences.

Smart access systems collect data, and New York City regulates it. HPD's page on Local Law 63 of 2021 requires express consent before collecting the data these systems use to identify people, limits what can be collected, requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, bars selling or sharing it, and requires a written data retention and privacy policy for tenants.

The app is the visible part. Behind it are logs, snapshots and sometimes face images, and the owner is responsible for what is kept. We set those options deliberately during programming and record them in the handover.

"Go wireless in a new building; it saves running cable"

Product roundups often recommend Wi-Fi door stations and apartment monitors because they avoid new wiring.

Downtown towers are concrete and steel, with long corridors, glass curtain walls and lobbies full of other wireless traffic. A Wi-Fi station at a vestibule door may sit far from any access point with concrete between them, and a building-wide managed network may not allow third-party devices at all. A failed wireless link at the only street entrance to several hundred apartments is not a small problem.

Lobby panels, desk stations and releases should be cabled, powered over the network where possible and on battery backup. Residents can still answer wirelessly on phones and tablets; it is the door end that needs a cable.

"An intercom system costs about $2,500"

HomeAdvisor's national guide says installing an intercom costs an average of $2,500, with a typical range of $1,200 to $8,500, video systems from $1,500 to $15,000 or more, and roughly one to two hours of labor per station. AI summaries often repeat the average as if it were a price.

That average blends single-family homes and small buildings from across the country. A Downtown tower lobby with a directory panel, a desk station, a release on a glass vestibule door, battery-backed network equipment and hundreds of app accounts is a different scale of job, and a converted office tower adds design work on every apartment floor.

The national figure is a sense of scale for a house, not a budget for a Downtown building. We quote each building separately, after a survey, with every item on its own line.

"A virtual doorman replaces a staffed desk"

Marketing for remote and app-based entry often presents it as a straight swap for a person at the desk.

A remote or app-based system can answer calls, issue passes and keep logs. It cannot sign for a package, notice a resident in distress, stop someone following a resident through the door, or deal with a delivery rider pressing every button. Buildings like the attended lobbies 6sqft described at One Boerum Place and 505 State Street chose people at the desk for reasons a panel does not cover.

The two work best together: the desk uses the intercom as its tool during staffed hours, and the system carries the building when the desk is closed. Where a building is deciding between them, we lay out what each covers so the decision is made on facts.

"Accessibility means putting the buttons at the right height"

Accessibility advice for intercoms often stops at mounting height.

Height matters, and 1 RCNY §42-01 sets the limits we work to: push buttons no more than six feet from the floor and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet. But the Access Board's guide to the ADA Standards also says entry systems "must include visual and audible signals," that lighted signals should be labeled, and that handset cords must be at least 29 inches long where handsets are used.

A visitor who cannot hear the panel needs a screen or a labeled light that shows the call connected and the door released. We choose panels that give both kinds of signal and check them at handover.

FAQ

Downtown Brooklyn Intercom Installation FAQ

Our tower runs on a building-wide managed internet service. Should the lobby intercom share that network or have its own connection?
+

Usually its own segment, and ideally its own connection or a cellular backup. A managed building network is run by its provider for residents' internet, and changes on its side can cut the intercom off without anyone noticing. We ask the provider whether the intercom can have a dedicated segment, and we add a cellular path where the system supports one, so street calls keep reaching apartments if the building's internet goes down.

Our lobby has a revolving door with a swing door beside it. Which one should the intercom release?
+

The swing door. Revolving doors are generally left free-turning or locked as a unit, and they are not practical to release for one visitor. The swing door beside it is normally the accessible entrance, so it gets the electric release, the panel nearby and, where the building has one, the automatic opener. We check that the revolving door's locking does not leave visitors confused about which door to use.

Our lobby entrance is a frameless glass door with no frame to hold a strike. How can it be released remotely?
+

With hardware made for glass doors, such as a lock fitted in the floor or the header above the door, or a magnetic lock on the header with a matching plate clamped to the glass. The right choice depends on the door's pivot hardware and whether it must also open freely from inside, which it must. We look at the door and its fittings during the survey before recommending one, and the lock is tested for free exit with the power off.

Can lobby calls ring a resident's smartwatch?
+

Often, indirectly. When the lobby calls a resident's phone through an app or a phone call, a paired watch usually shows the notification or the call and may let the resident answer by voice. Video and the door release may only work on the phone itself. It depends on the system and the watch, so we suggest residents test it once after setup rather than rely on it.

Does our building need to book the freight elevator or loading dock before your crew arrives?
+

In most Downtown towers, yes. Managing agents usually require the freight elevator, the dock and sometimes a lobby work window to be reserved in advance, and some ask for the crew's names. We send our equipment list and preferred dates early so the bookings can be made, and we plan lobby work outside the morning and evening rush wherever the building allows.

Our building uses a resident-management platform for packages and work orders. Can the intercom share its resident list instead of keeping a second directory?
+

Sometimes. Several entry systems can sync residents from property management or building operations software, so move-ins and move-outs update the directory automatically. Whether that works depends on both products and on the plans the building pays for. We check the integration before choosing a panel, and if no sync exists, we set up a simple routine so the two lists are updated on the same day.

We run student housing near the Downtown campuses. Can visitors call a student's phone while the front desk keeps the final say on entry?
+

Yes. The panel can call the student, and the student's answer can notify the desk rather than open the door, so staff release the door after checking the visitor. During hours the desk is closed, a different rule can apply. Many residence operators also use sign-in at the desk, and the intercom's log supports that rather than replacing it.

Can a lobby intercom panel call 911?
+

We do not set up building entry panels as emergency calling devices, and residents should call 911 from their own phones. Elevator emergency phones are a separate system with their own requirements. What the intercom can do is help responders get in: the desk or a resident can release the door when police, EMS or firefighters arrive, and the building can keep its responder access arrangements as they are.

Can residents see a picture of a visitor who rang while they could not answer?
+

On many current systems, yes. The panel saves a snapshot or short clip of each call, and the resident's app shows it in the call history. Those images are data the building keeps, so we set how long they are stored with management at programming, and the setting goes in the handover so the building's privacy policy matches it.

Our lobby panel's keypad is worn from heavy use. Are touch-free panels a better choice for a busy tower?
+

They can be. Touchscreen panels with a searchable directory have fewer moving parts to wear out, and many allow visitors to call by scanning a code with their own phone or by voice, so the panel is touched less. Screens need protection from glare and impact, though, and a building with many older visitors may still want large physical buttons. We look at the lobby's traffic and light before recommending one.

Could someone use the network cable behind a street panel to get into the building's other systems?
+

Not if it is set up properly. We put lobby panels on their own network segment, separated from residents' service, management computers and building controls, disable unused ports and services, change default passwords and keep the administrator account in the owner's name. The cable behind an outdoor panel should lead only to the intercom's own equipment, so pulling the panel off the wall gives nobody a way into anything else.
