The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Buys Cameras for Houses in the Woods

Putnam County is the most detached-house county in the Hudson Valley, and that single fact shapes almost every camera we sell here. A typical Putnam home is not one of a row; it sits on its own lot, often back from the road behind trees, with a driveway that bends, a garage that may stand on its own, a shed or two, and on a lot of streets a lake or a reservoir somewhere at the bottom of the hill. Cameras built for a small suburban yard, where the router is thirty feet from everything, run out of reach on these lots. That is why we sell security camera equipment in Putnam the way an installer would: we ask where the farthest view is, how the internet comes into the house, and what happens to the property when nobody is there.

The Census numbers say it plainly. The American Community Survey 2019–2023 five-year estimates count about 38,369 housing units in Putnam County, and 77.7 percent of them are single-family detached houses — a higher share than Ulster (68.6), Dutchess (62.6), Orange (62.4), Rockland (57.1) or Westchester (43.5). In Putnam Valley the detached share reaches 95.6 percent. Every one of those houses has its own four sides, its own driveway and its own outbuildings, and each is a separate camera location that has to be wired, powered and recorded.

Those are the owners who want to open an app in Manhattan on a Tuesday in February and see that the driveway is unplowed, the doors are shut and the boathouse is still standing.

What the crime figures do and do not say

Putnam is a low-crime county, and we would rather say so than sell fear. The New York State Division of Criminal Justice Services counted 38 burglaries in the whole county in 2024, with 400 larcenies and 18 motor vehicle thefts. Larceny rose from 341 in 2023 to 400 in 2024, and much of it is the kind of theft a camera is good at recording: items taken from cars, packages taken from porches, tools taken from a truck or a job site. The number of burglaries is small, but a burglary in Putnam tends to happen at a house set back from the road, where no neighbor sees the car come up the driveway. A camera is often the only witness.

The Putnam County Sheriff's Office has its own reasons for watching the roads. Its license plate reader network — 48 cameras at 21 locations, according to The Highlands Current — logged 808 alerts on 250 vehicles between January 1 and August 11, 2026, as reported in the Putnam County Press. Twenty-seven of those vehicles were flagged as belonging to burglary crews, and 46 percent of the burglary-crew alerts came from cameras at the county line. The Sheriff's Office put it this way: "When one of these vehicles shows up here, it is a strong indicator that a crew is canvassing our neighborhoods for target homes." Those cameras read plates on public roads. They do not see your driveway, your garage door or your back deck, and their footage is not yours. A camera system you own is what records what happens past the end of the pavement.

Unlocked cars, open porches and lake neighborhoods

The theft reports that come out of Putnam are mostly quiet, overnight ones. In June 2024 The Highlands Current reported a run of thefts from unlocked vehicles across the county, including Philipstown, where a car left unlocked with the keys inside on Lurch Lane was stolen and later found abandoned in the Town of Fishkill; residents near Jaycox Road reported items taken from unlocked cars overnight; and in Mahopac, police reported thieves going through unlocked vehicles in the South Lake and North Lake communities. In October 2025 the Carmel Police Department warned residents about porch pirates ahead of the holiday shopping season, News 12 Hudson Valley reported, telling people to bring deliveries in, track them and report anything suspicious.

Neither of those problems needs a fortress. They need a camera that sees the driveway where the cars are parked, a camera that sees the porch or the spot where the delivery driver leaves boxes, and a recorder that keeps the clip when the phone alert gets swiped away. Those are the first two cameras on most Putnam lists, and both are covered by every four-camera kit we sell.

Lakes, reservoirs and the woods behind the house

Water shapes Putnam's neighborhoods. Lake Mahopac drew summer visitors as early as the nineteenth century, the Patterson historical society notes; Lake Carmel in the Town of Kent was created by developers in the early twentieth century; and Putnam Lake in Patterson was made by damming a small branch of the Croton River. Each grew into a neighborhood of small lots, cottages that became year-round houses, private roads and beach rights. A house on one of those lakes needs cameras that handle glare off the water at sunrise, fog on humid mornings and a dock that may be pulled in every fall.

Behind many other houses lies New York City's water supply. The West Branch Reservoir sits in Kent and Carmel, Boyd's Corner Reservoir is in Kent, and Croton Falls Reservoir spans Carmel and Southeast, according to the NYC Department of Environmental Protection. Owners who back onto watershed land or state parkland like Fahnestock usually want a camera on the back of the house that sees the yard and the tree line, aimed so it records their own property rather than the land beyond it.

Dead zones, outages and snow

Three Putnam conditions decide the recorder plan before anyone talks about resolution. The first is signal. In 2015 Senator Charles Schumer released a RootMetrics ranking that put the Hudson Valley, including Putnam, last of 125 metro areas for cell performance, and said: "There are more cell phone dead zones in Northern Westchester and Putnam Counties than post-winter potholes on the West Side Highway." Coverage has improved since, but a camera that depends on a cell signal or on WiFi reaching the end of a wooded drive is still a gamble on many Putnam roads.

The second is power. After the storms of May 15, 2018, NYSEG reported 28,043 Putnam customers without power, with more than 4,000 out in each of Southeast, Putnam Valley, Patterson, Kent and Carmel, Daily Voice reported; an October 2017 storm left about 13,000 out, according to Patch. The third is snow. The National Weather Service in New York recorded 18.8 inches two miles west of Putnam Valley and 12.0 inches in Cold Spring in the February 22–23, 2026 blizzard, with a 41 mph gust measured at Brewster; a month earlier, the January 25–26 storm left 13.0 inches near Nelsonville, 11.5 near Putnam Valley and 11.0 in Mahopac. A Putnam camera system has to record through all three: a dropped connection, a dark house and a drift against the wall.

PoE, WiFi or coax: how the picture gets back to the house

PoE security cameras get power and data over one network cable, usually Cat6, from a PoE NVR or a PoE switch. A single run can go up to 100 meters, or 328 feet, under the Ethernet standard, and a switch or extender in a garage or barn adds another run from there. PoE is what we recommend on most Putnam lots because a wired camera does not care how thick the trees are or how weak the WiFi is at the end of the driveway.

WiFi security cameras and wireless cameras still have a place: a single view where cable cannot reach, a temporary camera during a renovation, a renter who cannot drill. They need power at the camera (or a battery and solar panel) and a strong WiFi signal at the mounting spot, and in Putnam's woods that signal fades fast. HD-over-coax cameras on a DVR are the third route; they make sense when a house already has coax runs from an older system that test clean, and our F-series kits are built that way.

Night vision under trees with no streetlights

Most Putnam roads are dark. Night vision security cameras use infrared LEDs to light the scene in black and white; the house-brand kits list ranges from 60 to 120 feet depending on the model. Color night vision cameras and full color night vision cameras either gather more light through a larger sensor and brighter lens (starlight security cameras and low light security cameras) or switch on a small white spotlight when something moves. On a wooded lot that spotlight does double work: it gives you a color image of the person or car, and it tells the person the house is watching.

Infrared has one weakness here. Leaves, snowflakes and spider webs close to the lens reflect the infrared back into the camera, which shows up as glowing specks and false motion. We mount cameras clear of branches and gutters for that reason, and we often choose a turret over a dome because a dome bubble can bounce infrared light inside itself.

Smart detection that ignores deer and swaying branches

Plain motion detection cameras trigger on any change in the picture. On a Putnam lot that includes deer, wild turkeys, a fox, headlights sweeping across the trees, and every gust that moves a branch. Smart motion detection cameras and AI security cameras sort what they see into people and vehicles, and only alert on what you choose. Security cameras with person detection and security cameras with vehicle detection are the two filters that make phone alerts usable here. The D4 and D5 house-brand kits list smart human and vehicle detection, and the partner-brand cameras we sell list it on their spec sheets too. AI human detection cameras, AI vehicle detection cameras, edge AI security cameras, deep learning surveillance cameras, object detection security cameras and video analytics security cameras are all names for this same idea: the camera or the recorder decides what moved before it bothers you.

Smart search security camera systems let you search a day of footage for “vehicle in driveway” instead of scrubbing through hours of trees. Facial recognition security cameras, people counting cameras and license plate recognition cameras (LPR cameras, ANPR cameras, license plate capture cameras) are specialized products; we only call a camera capable of them when its spec sheet says so, and a plate needs a narrow, well-lit view at the point where cars slow down.

Recorders & Accessories

NVR and DVR Recorders, Drives, Switches and Backup Power in Putnam

The recorder is the part of a camera system that matters most in Putnam, because it is what keeps working when the internet line goes quiet. A network video recorder or digital video recorder writes every camera's video to a surveillance hard drive inside the house. Phone viewing, alerts and remote playback ride on top of that, but the recording itself does not depend on any outside service. That is the difference between security cameras with local storage and cameras that only record when they can reach a cloud server.

Security camera laws for Putnam homes and businesses

This is general information, not legal advice. Security camera laws, CCTV regulations and security camera privacy laws in New York start with a few state statutes that apply in Putnam as everywhere else:

  • Audio. New York Penal Law §250.05 makes eavesdropping, including the mechanical overhearing of a conversation without a party's consent, a class E felony (§250.00 defines the terms). Audio recording security cameras and two way audio security cameras are sold with audio that can be switched off, and we leave it off by default at businesses.
  • Private places. Penal Law §250.45 makes unlawful surveillance a crime, including recording people in places like bathrooms or bedrooms where they expect privacy. Cameras do not belong in those rooms.
  • Employees. Labor Law §203-c bars an employer from video recording employees in a restroom, locker room or room set aside for changing clothes, unless authorized by court order.
  • The New York City sign law does not apply. The city's biometric identifier signage rule for certain businesses is part of the NYC Administrative Code and covers the five boroughs, not Putnam.
  • Local rules. In Cold Spring's historic district, exterior changes go to the Historic District Review Board. HOAs and condo boards may have their own rules on cameras outside a unit.

Are security cameras legal at a Putnam house? Recording your own property is common and generally lawful; aim cameras at your own land and away from neighbors' windows, and keep audio off unless you know the rules for the space.

Your Questions Answered

Buying Security Cameras in Putnam County: Questions Answered

Which views come first on a Mahopac colonial whose detached garage backs onto woods?

Start with the four places a person or car has to cross: the driveway where it meets the parking area, the front door, the garage doors, and the side of the garage that faces the trees. On most of these lots a fifth camera on the back of the house covers the deck and the yard. The garage cameras should run to a PoE switch inside the garage, fed by one cable from the house recorder, so you are not pulling four separate cables across the lawn. Aim the tree-side camera along the wall of the garage rather than straight into the woods; it records anyone walking up to the building and skips most of the branches and deer.

How should a camera on a Lake Mahopac or Lake Gleneida dock handle glare off the water?

Do not point it across the open water toward the morning or evening sun; mount it on the shore side looking along the dock and the shoreline, slightly down, so the water fills less of the frame. A camera with wide dynamic range, a turret housing rather than a dome, and a sun hood on bullets all help.

Where should the recorder sit in a Lake Carmel cottage with a crawl space and no basement?

Inside the heated part of the house, out of sight and off the floor: a bedroom closet shelf, a cabinet in the utility room, or a high shelf in a hall closet. Crawl spaces are damp and cold and invite mice, and an unheated porch freezes. The cable from each camera usually runs through the attic or along the crawl space in a protective sleeve and up into the closet. Put a battery backup next to the recorder and the modem, and do not leave the recorder where a burglar walking through the front door would see it first.

How do I keep deer, turkeys and blowing leaves from flooding my phone with alerts in Putnam Valley?

Use person and vehicle detection instead of plain motion, draw detection zones that cover the driveway and walkways but not the tree line, and set a minimum size so a squirrel near the lens does not count as a person. Schedule alerts so the driveway camera only pings you overnight or while you are away. Keep infrared away from close branches and spider webs, which glow at night and trigger motion. If a camera still sends nonsense alerts, re-aim it; usually it is looking at too much forest.

Should a weekend house in Garrison record around the clock or only on motion?

Both, if the recorder supports it, and most of the ones we sell do. Record a lower-resolution substream continuously so nothing is ever missing, and record full resolution on motion or on a person or vehicle event. That gives you a complete timeline at small file sizes plus sharp clips of anything that happened, and it stretches the drive across weeks away. For a house empty from January to March, size the drive for the event recordings and let the continuous stream overwrite after a few weeks.

Where should a camera go so drifting snow and roof slides don't bury it after a Putnam storm?

Under an eave or a soffit, a foot or more below the roof edge, and never directly below a valley where snow and ice slide off a metal or slate roof. Keep cameras off the low parts of walls on the windward side, where drifts pile up, and off fences that the plow buries. Bullets with a sun hood shed snow better than flat-faced cameras. After the February 2026 blizzard dropped 18.8 inches near Putnam Valley, the cameras that stayed clear were the ones mounted high under deep overhangs.

What should a Cold Spring Main Street shop in an old building buy so the cameras stay small and reversible?

Small indoor domes or turrets inside the front window and over the register, a small camera inside the back door, and a recorder in the back room. Inside cameras do not change the facade, and a camera behind the glass can see the sidewalk in daylight if it sits close to the window with its infrared turned off. If you want a camera outside, keep it small, mount it into mortar joints or wood trim rather than brick faces, and expect it to go to the Historic District Review Board if it changes the building's exterior.

How should a Brewster storefront cover the door, the register and the back lot with one 8-channel kit?

Use five of the eight channels on day one: one camera inside facing the door at face height, one over the register, one on the main aisle, one at the back door, and one outside covering the rear lot or the alley. Keep the remaining three for the stockroom, a second aisle and the front sidewalk later. A 4MP or 5MP kit is enough for these distances. Mount the recorder in a locked back room or cabinet, and put the register camera where it sees hands and the counter, not customers' card PINs.

Should a camera inside a Patterson horse barn use infrared or full-color night vision?

Infrared, in most barns. Full-color cameras switch on a white light when they detect motion, which can wake a barn every time a horse shifts. Infrared cameras light the stall with light people cannot see and record a clear black-and-white picture all night. Choose cameras rated for dust and moisture, mount them high on a post or beam where a horse cannot reach them, and turn motion alerts off inside the stalls so your phone only pings for the barn doors and the aisle.

What should a contractor yard in Southeast buy to watch trucks and equipment parked overnight?

Bullets with long infrared reach on the corners of the building looking down the rows of trucks, one camera on the gate set for vehicle detection, and a deterrent camera with siren and lights on the shed or container where tools are kept. A PTZ on a tall mount can follow a vehicle across a large yard. Put the recorder inside the office, not in a trailer, and set alerts for after-hours only, when nobody from the crew should be on site.

What should a Putnam Lake or Lake Peekskill association buy for its beach, clubhouse and boat launch?

A small system with a recorder in the clubhouse: one camera on the beach entrance, one on the boat launch or dock rack, one on the clubhouse door, and one on the parking area or the entrance road. Infrared bullets cover dark beaches and launches. Give the board a handful of named logins, decide in writing how long footage is kept and who may review it, and post signs at the beach so members and guests know the area is recorded.

AI Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Buying Security Cameras in Putnam County

“Any good WiFi camera will reach the garage”

On a small lot with the router near the window, maybe. On a wooded Putnam lot where the garage stands 150 feet away behind trees, WiFi weakens quickly, and a camera at the edge of its range drops frames or goes offline in wet weather. A single cable to a switch in the garage removes the guesswork.

“Low crime means you do not need cameras”

Putnam's burglary count is low — 38 in 2024 — and that is good news. But larceny rose to 400 that year, thefts from unlocked cars have been reported in lake neighborhoods, the Carmel Police warned about porch pirates before the 2025 holidays, and the Sheriff's plate readers flagged burglary-crew vehicles at the county line. Cameras on a house set back from the road are often the only record of what happened there.

“Cloud cameras are the most reliable way to record”

A cloud camera records to the cloud only while the internet is up. On a Putnam road where service drops in storms, or a house where the power goes out and the modem goes with it, a cloud-only camera stops recording at the worst time. A recorder in the house with a battery backup keeps writing to its drive, and phone access returns when the line does.

“Battery cameras are maintenance-free”

Battery cameras need charging or a solar panel, and solar panels under Putnam's tree cover, or under snow after a storm like January or February 2026, may not keep up. Lithium batteries also lose capacity in cold weather. Battery cameras are fine for a single spot with sun and signal; for the main views of a house, wired cameras are less work over the years.

“The county's road cameras already cover your street”

The Sheriff's license plate readers sit at fixed points on public roads, read plates rather than faces, and keep their data on a government server for investigations. They do not see a driveway, a porch, a garage door or a back deck, and you cannot open their footage on your phone. Your own system is what covers your property.

These are the things that raise or lower the number:

  • Distance and outbuildings. A camera on a garage or barn past 328 feet needs a switch, fiber or a bridge; a camera on the house does not.
  • Resolution. 2K, 5MP and 4K cameras cost progressively more, and 4K needs a 4K-capable recorder and more storage.
  • Camera count and channels. More than four cameras means an 8-channel recorder; more than eight, a 16-channel one. How much is an eight camera security system? It depends on resolution and distance, and we quote it as a 2K and a 4K option.
  • Connection type. PoE, coax reused from an old system, or WiFi each change the parts list.
  • Storage days. Two weeks, a month or a winter away need different drive sizes; see the storage table above.
  • Backup power. A battery backup sized to the recorder, switch and modem.
  • Camera features. Varifocal lenses, full color night vision, sirens and strobes, two-way talk, PTZ and person and vehicle detection.
  • Installation. For installed systems, the cable route: an open basement is quicker than a finished house on a slab or a cottage with only a crawl space.

FAQ

Putnam County Security Cameras FAQ

Will you deliver camera equipment to a lake community with a private road and a gate, like Lake Carmel or Putnam Lake?
+

Yes. Give us the gate code, the road association's directions or a landmark, and the name of whoever will receive the order. Private lake roads that are not plowed promptly after a storm can delay a delivery by a day, and we will call ahead if that happens rather than leave boxes at a gate.

Can a camera confirm the oil or propane delivery came while I'm away from my Putnam house?
+

Yes. Aim a camera at the fill pipe, the tank or the spot where the truck parks, and set a vehicle detection zone there. You get a clip each time the truck arrives and can see the driver at the fill. In a county where most homes heat with oil, owners of weekend houses find this one of the most useful views on the system.

Can I mount a camera on a tree beside my Putnam driveway?
+

It can be done, but we rarely recommend it. Trees sway in wind, which shakes the image and triggers motion, and they grow, which loosens hardware and shifts the aim over time. A pressure-treated post set near the drive, or a varifocal camera on the house zoomed toward the spot, holds its aim far better. If a tree is the only option, we use a strap mount and leave slack in the cable.

Will a camera see through the screens of a three-season porch on a Putnam house?
+

In daylight, mostly yes, with a softer image. At night, no: the camera's infrared light bounces off the screen mesh and whites out the picture. Mount the camera outside the screen, under the porch roof edge, or inside the porch looking in toward the house door rather than out through the screens.

Will the timestamps stay accurate on a Putnam system that loses internet for days?
+

Yes. Recorders keep time with an internal clock and set it from an internet time server when one is reachable. During a long outage the clock keeps running on its own, and it re-syncs when the connection returns. We set the time zone and daylight saving correctly at setup so recordings line up with real time if you ever need to hand a clip to the Sheriff or an insurer.

My dock comes out of the lake every fall. Where should its camera go?
+

On something that stays put: the boathouse, a post on the shore, or the lake side of the house. A camera on a removable dock has to be taken down, stored and re-aimed every year, and its cable ends up in the water. From the shore, a camera looking along the dock line covers the dock in summer and the empty shoreline in winter.

Which camera covers a mailbox at the end of a long Putnam driveway?
+

If the mailbox is within about 150 feet and in a clear line from the house, a varifocal or 4K camera on the house zoomed toward it usually works. Past that, or around a bend, put a camera on a post near the road, fed by buried cable or a PoE extender, or use a solar and cellular camera if there is a reliable carrier signal at that spot.

Why does a camera near a Putnam lake look foggy at dawn, and what helps?
+

Morning mist and dew on the lens or the dome cover scatter light, and infrared bounces off the droplets. A turret or a bullet with a hood collects less moisture than a dome, a camera tucked under an eave stays drier, and wide dynamic range keeps the picture usable as the sun comes up. Wiping the lens cover during a seasonal check helps too.

Can a camera tell me when the plow has cleared my Putnam driveway?
+

Yes. A camera at the top or bottom of the drive with a vehicle detection zone sends you a clip when the plow truck comes through, so you can see that the job was done and when. Owners away for the winter often give the plow company no access at all and simply watch for that alert.

Does the Sheriff's plate-reader network make a camera on my own driveway unnecessary?
+

No. The county's plate readers are fixed on public roads, read plates rather than faces, and their data stays with law enforcement. They do not see your driveway, porch or garage, and you cannot view them. A camera you own records your own property and keeps the footage on your own recorder.

Can the recorder go in an unheated detached garage over a Putnam winter?
+

We advise against it. Recorders and hard drives list a minimum operating temperature on their spec sheets, and an unheated garage in a Putnam cold snap can drop below it; damp and condensation are hard on drives too. Keep the recorder in the heated house and run a cable to a PoE switch in the garage instead. If the garage is heated, a recorder in a locked cabinet there works fine.
