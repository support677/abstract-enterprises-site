The Neighborhood

Why Borough Park Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Borough Park has a fire problem that has little to do with old wiring stereotypes and a lot to do with how its buildings are used. In a little over two years, local and city outlets have reported a shoe store fire at 13th Avenue and 39th Street that filled the apartments above with smoke at two in the morning, a basement e-bike fire on 48th Street that gutted all three floors of a house, an early-morning fire at a synagogue on 60th Street where three people were pulled from the third floor, a basement fire on 39th Street near 15th Avenue that climbed through the walls of two attached houses, and two three-alarm fires on Fort Hamilton Parkway within a week of each other in April 2026. Most of them started below or behind the rooms where people were: a cellar, a stockroom, a closed store, a warehouse next door.

That pattern matters because the alarm most families rely on is the one they can hear, and in a big house that is usually the one nearest the kitchen. A fire that starts in a cellar with the boiler, the storage and a charging bike can grow for a long time before smoke reaches a hall detector on the first floor, and longer still before anyone upstairs hears it behind a closed bedroom door. Detection in Borough Park has to start where fires here have actually started, and it has to be heard where people actually sleep.

The households are large and often several generations deep, and the houses have been changed to fit them. Two-family houses have become three-family houses; one-family houses have gained a floor and an apartment for a married child; cellars have become playrooms, guest rooms and second kitchens used at Pesach. Every one of those changes can move a property into a different set of city detector rules. A house with three homes in it is a multiple dwelling for HPD, with posted notices, records and owner duties that a two-family does not carry. A rented two-family picks up carbon monoxide duties, and from January 1, 2027, natural gas detector duties, that an owner-occupied house does not.

The community has done a lot on its own: since 2017 a BPJCC, Senator Felder and Red Cross initiative has installed free smoke detectors in tens of thousands of homes, covered further down. What that work does not cover is a landlord's duties for rented apartments, gas detection in every gas room, alarms linked across four floors, or a building system for a store or shul, and the alarms it put up in its first years are now nearing the end of their working life. That is where an installer comes in.

One- and two-family brick and frame houses that have gained bedrooms in a raised roof, a rear extension or a finished cellar, with many children on the upper floors and the parents often on a lower one.

Two- and three-family rentals

Houses with separate apartments on each floor, a shared stair to the street, a cellar holding the boiler and meters, and an owner who may live in one unit or elsewhere.

Houses that hold a family and a shtiebel

Homes where a small congregation davens on the ground floor or in an extension, and the rav's family lives on the floors above.

Walk-ups on the side streets and avenues

Storefronts with apartments above

Two- and three-story buildings on 13th, 16th and 18th Avenues and New Utrecht Avenue with a store on the street, stock in the back and households upstairs.

Warehouses and wholesale floors

Houses built out for one big family: the alarm has to reach the farthest child

The typical large-family house we survey here has more bedrooms than its footprint would suggest. Rooms have been added in a raised roof or a new top floor, the rear has been pushed out, and the cellar has been finished into a playroom or guest room. Parents often sleep on the second floor near the youngest children, while older children are a floor or two higher. That layout is where a single hall alarm fails. A fire in the cellar or kitchen sets off the alarm nearest to it, and the people most likely to sleep through it are the ones at the top of the house, behind closed doors, with a fan or an air conditioner running.

So in these houses the plan is built around linking. Every smoke alarm, and the carbon monoxide alarms near the sleeping areas, are connected so that one alarm sounding anywhere sets off all of them. We place a smoke alarm inside each bedroom and in the hall outside each group of bedrooms, one at the top of each flight, and one at the cellar stair, where smoke from below arrives first. Then we test it the way the house is actually used at night: every bedroom door closed, a second person sounding the cellar alarm, and someone listening in the top-floor room farthest from it. If that alarm cannot be heard clearly there, the design is not finished, whatever the minimum says.

Two- and three-family rentals: the third apartment changes the rulebook

Many Borough Park houses hold a family on two floors and a tenant on the third, or an apartment for a married child that later becomes a rental. HPD's detector page treats these differently by count. A building with three or more homes is a multiple dwelling: each dwelling unit needs at least one approved, working smoke detector, and in a Class A building that detector goes within fifteen feet of the primary entrance to each room used for sleeping. The owner posts a notice in the entrance area, gives a notice to an adult in each unit, and keeps the install and expiration date of every device. A one- or two-family house the owner does not live in carries HPD's carbon monoxide duties wherever fuel is burned, and natural gas duties wherever there is gas piping from January 1, 2027.

For these houses we plan two layers. Inside each apartment, the devices HPD expects, placed for that apartment's bedrooms and its kitchen. Then the parts everyone shares: the front hall, the stair and the cellar, where a fire can grow while every household is asleep behind its own door. In a building this size, linked alarms in the shared spaces that sound on every floor are usually the right answer, and we write down which devices are the owner's to maintain and which the tenants are expected to test monthly, as HPD asks of them.

Houses that hold a family and a shtiebel: two uses, one stair

A small congregation in a house is one of the most Borough Park arrangements there is, and one of the hardest to classify. The ground floor or a rear extension is set up for davening and learning, often busy before sunrise and late at night, and the family lives upstairs. On a weekday morning the people most at risk are asleep above a room full of seforim, a coffee urn and extension cords. On a Shabbos morning the same building may hold far more people than a house was ever built for.

Whether a space like that is still a house for fire alarm purposes, or a place of assembly that needs a designed system, depends on its size, its occupant load and its construction, and that is a registered design professional's call, not ours. What we do on the survey is lay out both sides honestly: the household detection the family needs upstairs, linked so a fire in the shul is heard in the bedrooms, and the questions the congregation's board should put to an engineer about the shul itself. We put both in writing, because these arrangements tend to grow quietly until someone asks.

Warehouses and wholesale floors: cartons, deliveries and the homes next door

Fort Hamilton Parkway in the 50s and 60s mixes light industry, wholesale and storage with houses and apartments on the same blocks. On April 12, 2026, a three-alarm fire there was first reported as coming from an e-bike shop at 5311 Fort Hamilton Parkway, and officials later said it started next door in a clothing warehouse where boxes caught fire and spread quickly. The Department of Buildings posted full vacate orders on 5311 and 5313, and seven adults and three children were displaced. Five days earlier, a fire that began in the basement of 6007 Fort Hamilton Parkway went to a third alarm and spread to the first floor.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Bedrooms, Then Go Down to the Cellar

The bedroom count comes first. In a Borough Park house we start the survey at the kitchen table with a sketch: every floor, every bedroom, who sleeps in each, and which doors are closed at night. The number is usually higher than the owner says at first, because a playroom has a pull-out bed, the cellar guest room is in use for every Yom Tov, and the newest top floor was finished after the last alarm was bought. Each sleeping room on that sketch gets a device, and each group of rooms gets one in the hall outside.

Then the cellar, slowly. We go down and look at everything that can start a fire or make carbon monoxide: the boiler and water heater, the gas meter, a dryer, a second range or a Pesach kitchen, a freezer on an extension cord, and the corner where the e-bikes and scooters are charged. Every gas appliance is written down by room, because from January 1, 2027 every room with one in a rented home needs a gas detector. Every charger is written down too, and we say plainly when it should move.

Follow the additions. Where a house has been enlarged, we find where the old house ends and the new one begins, because that seam decides how cable can travel between floors, and because a floor added under a building permit may have alarms shown on its drawings that nobody ever installed. We ask to see the plans if the family has them.

Batteries in the Cellar

The 48th Street Fire and What It Means for Every Borough Park House With an E-Bike

In the early hours of Thursday, February 8, 2024, a fire spread through a three-story house on 48th Street and, in Boro Park 24's words, "gutted all three floors of the home." Investigators removed two e-bikes and their batteries from the basement where the fire started, and officials said the batteries were knockoffs without safety certifications, most likely bought on the street, and that one of them started the fire. Nobody was hurt, which says a lot about how quickly the household got out. The Red Cross helped five displaced families, twelve adults and three children.

Chief Fire Marshal Daniel Flynn's advice after the fire was about the batteries themselves: check that lithium-ion products meet UL standards and, when replacing a battery, "buy it from the manufacturer that you bought the original product from." Boro Park 24 returned to the subject in July 2025, reporting that structural fires were up 26 percent compared with the same time the previous year, with many started by e-bike or scooter batteries charged or stored indoors, and that officials were asking people to keep batteries outdoors when possible and to avoid charging overnight.

What an alarm can and cannot do. A lithium-ion battery that fails does not smolder politely. It can go from a hiss to a jet of flame in moments and fill a cellar with heavy, toxic smoke. No detector prevents that. What detection can do is buy the people upstairs the minutes they need, and that depends on three things we check in every house that has a bike, a scooter or a power-tool charger in it.

  • Where the charging happens. The safest answer is not in the cellar, not in the stair and not by the only door out. If a family is going to charge indoors anyway, we want to know exactly where, because that spot drives where the nearest detector goes.
  • Whether the cellar alarm reaches the bedrooms. A smoke alarm in the cellar that sounds only in the cellar is almost useless at 3 a.m. Linked to every alarm in the house, it is the one that wakes the top floor first.
  • What kind of detector sits nearest the charger. A photoelectric smoke alarm near the charging spot and at the head of the cellar stair is the core. In a dusty, unfinished cellar where a smoke alarm would false-alarm, a heat detector can cover the boiler corner, but it is never a substitute for smoke detection on the stair.
January 1, 2027

Gas Detectors in Borough Park Rentals: Counting Every Gas Room, Including the Second Kitchen

HPD's detector page says natural gas detectors are required in multiple dwellings, and in one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy, wherever there is gas piping, starting January 1, 2027. The Mann Report's October 2026 summary of Local Law 157 says the Department of Buildings has confirmed the date will not be pushed back to 2029, that covered alarms must be listed to UL 1484, and that carbon monoxide or smoke alarms do not satisfy the law. As of this page's review, the deadline is about seven weeks away.

Where they go. According to the same summary, where a gas-burning appliance is inside a dwelling, the alarm generally goes in the same room, at least three feet and no more than ten feet from the appliance measured horizontally, on the ceiling or on a wall, with wall units typically within twelve inches of the ceiling, subject to the rule and the maker's instructions. In most of Brooklyn that means a kitchen and a boiler room. In Borough Park it often means more. A kitchen with two ranges is still one room. A second kitchen on another floor is another room. A Pesach kitchen in the basement with its own gas range is another room again, even if it is used only a few weeks a year. A gas dryer in a laundry room adds one more.

Who it reaches. On HPD's wording, the requirement covers walk-ups and any house with three or more homes, plus one- and two-family houses rented out by owners who live elsewhere. It does not reach a one- or two-family house the owner lives in, though nothing stops an owner from installing gas detection anyway, and in a house with gas appliances on three floors we usually suggest it. The day part of the house is rented, the picture changes for that unit.

How it gets installed. The Mann Report notes that hardwired gas alarms require a New York City licensed electrical contractor and permits, while battery-powered and plug-in units can be installed by others, including owners. That makes the choice between hardwired and sealed-battery models partly a scheduling question. In an occupied rental, sealed-battery or plug-in units placed correctly can usually go in during the same visit as smoke and CO work. Where an owner wants hardwired gas detection, typically during a renovation, the electrical work is coordinated with their electrician.

What to keep. HPD asks owners to give a notice to at least one adult occupant of each unit when devices go in, to post a notice in the entrance area of a multiple dwelling, and to keep the posting date, each device's install date and each device's end-of-life date. Our device list records the install and expiration date of every detector we put up, by apartment and room. Because getting into every apartment is the slowest part of the job, we price smoke, CO and gas work together, at our per-unit landlord rate of $400 to $800 per unit.

The two fires below were reported in Borough Park by News 12 Brooklyn and ABC7; the basement and warehouse fires mentioned elsewhere on this page came from News 12 Brooklyn and Boro Park 24. We describe them only as those outlets did. We do not know, and do not claim, how any of them was detected, whether any alarm worked or what caused them unless the report said so. Community Programs

Free Smoke Alarms Through the BPJCC Program, and What Comes After Them

If your household has never had its alarms looked at, the first call does not have to be to us. In November 2021, Boro Park 24 described the fire safety awareness initiative run by the Boro Park Jewish Community Council and Senator Simcha Felder with the American Red Cross: a free smoke detector for each room in the home, free installation by a qualified technician, and a visit from a safety professional to write a personalized evacuation plan, open to everyone regardless of income. The BPJCC office is at 1310 46th Street. "Smoke alarms save lives," Senator Felder said, and he urged families not to wait for the next tragedy to sign up.

By May 2022, the Senator's office reported that the initiative had reached tens of thousands of homes since 2017, and that about 50 Chaveirim volunteers had been trained by Red Cross experts to install detectors and assess homes for evacuation plans, so the long waiting list could be cleared. "Every life is precious and if we save even one, all our efforts will have been worth it," said Rabbi Kohn, Chaveirim's founder. That is a remarkable record, and for a family living in its own house it may be all the smoke detection they need for now.

Where a paid installer fits. The program, as described, puts smoke detectors in rooms and writes an escape plan. It does not take on a landlord's HPD duties for rented apartments, natural gas detection in each room with a gas appliance, carbon monoxide planning around a cellar boiler, alarms hardwired or linked across four floors of an enlarged house, or a building system for a shtiebel, store or warehouse. Those are the jobs we do. And every alarm has a working life: alarms installed by the program in its early years are approaching the point where their makers say to replace them, so a household that signed up in 2017 or 2018 should check the date printed on the back of each one now.

Business fire alarm work in Borough Park sorts into a few settings: the stores on the shopping avenues, almost always with homes above them; the shuls, study halls and schools that fill the side streets; and the wholesale, storage and light-industrial space along Fort Hamilton Parkway. In nearly every one, the people the alarm most needs to protect are not the business's own staff but the families sharing the building or the block.

Thirteenth Avenue's stores under apartments

Clothing, shoe, housewares and seforim stores with deep stockrooms and families upstairs, where a fire in a closed store is the families' fire, as the April 2025 shoe store fire at 39th Street showed.

Sixteenth and Eighteenth Avenue storefronts

Groceries, bakeries, takeout counters and offices in mixed-use buildings, where kitchen heat and steam decide which detector can live in the room without false alarms.

Shuls, shtieblach and study halls

Congregations from a room in a house to large buildings, used before dawn and late at night, with fuel-burning heat, crowds on Shabbos and Yom Tov, and sometimes a family living above.

Yeshivas and girls' schools

School buildings with designed, filed fire alarm systems maintained by their fire alarm company, where we work to approved drawings on additions, renovations and device changes.

Wholesale and storage on Fort Hamilton Parkway

Clothing, packaging and goods stacked in rooms nobody is in for hours, next to e-bike shops, delis and apartments, where the April 2026 warehouse fire led to vacate orders on two buildings.

Mikvaos, halls and community spaces

Rooms with steam and humidity, or large gatherings a few times a week, where detector type matters as much as count and a building system is often a design question.

For shuls and schools, the first question is whether the building already has a fire alarm system, and who services it. A large shul or yeshiva building will usually have a designed system with a fire alarm company of record, and any change to it, such as an addition, a new room or moved walls, goes through a design professional and the FDNY. A small shtiebel in a house or storefront may have only household alarms, and whether it needs more is a design question we raise with the board rather than answer on our own. In both, we pay close attention to the hours the building is busiest and the hours it is empty, because that decides whether a fire would be seen by people inside or has to be reported by monitoring.

Compliance notes for Borough Park stores, shuls and warehouses

  • Ask whether a system is required first. The FDNY's guide to fire alarm approval tells owners to confirm with the Department of Buildings whether a fire alarm system is required at their location, and to check the Fire Code, Building Code and Zoning Resolution for the specific requirements.
  • Design and plans come before installation. The same guide says a New York State registered design professional, a professional engineer or registered architect, prepares the plans. They are filed online through FDNY Business with a TM-1 application and either a valid Certificate of Occupancy or an approved Schedule A, or, if neither exists, a Letter of No Objection from the Department of Buildings.
  • Plan for months, not weeks. The FDNY guide says initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks and puts the whole process, from submission to final inspection and approval, at roughly 20 to 24 weeks. If plans draw objections, no new filing fee is due when they are resubmitted within 6 months of the objection date.
  • Who does the work is regulated. The guide says installation is done by a licensed New York City electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY-issued Fire Alarm Company Certificate, and that owners should use only FDNY-approved installation, inspection and testing services. We are a New York State licensed installer, license #12000287431, and on filed work we install to the approved drawings alongside the design professional and the fire alarm company of record.
  • Inspection has preconditions. Before the FDNY inspection is scheduled with form B-45, the installation must be complete, the control panel permanently installed and free of alarm, supervisory and trouble conditions, any sprinkler system working, and the central station monitoring connection active, with a copy of the monitoring agreement and an executed TB-60. A technician holding a Certificate of Fitness S97 or S98 has to attend.
  • Repairs and changes are different things. Replacing a failed device or panel part with an identical one is a repair. Adding devices, moving a panel or changing how the system works is a change to the system, which may need a design professional and an amended filing. We say which one a job is before connecting anything.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Borough Park: Questions Answered

These are the situations Borough Park families, landlords, shul boards and store owners bring to us. Each one is answered in full here and is not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Enlarged houses and growing families

We added a new top floor for the older children's bedrooms. What does that floor need that the rooms we already had did not?

Two things, usually. The first is devices: a smoke alarm inside each new bedroom and one in the hall outside them, plus carbon monoxide detection near those bedrooms if the house has a gas boiler, range or water heater anywhere. The second, and the one most families miss, is the connection back to the rest of the house. A new floor is the farthest point from the cellar and the kitchen, where fires here tend to start, so its alarms have to sound when any alarm in the house sounds. If the existing alarms are a mix of brands and ages, the new floor is often the moment to replace the whole set with one linked system, because alarms from different makers rarely talk to each other. We also look at the new stair: the top of each flight should have an alarm, since that is where rising smoke collects first. Before we finish, we close every door on the new floor and sound the cellar alarm, so the children's rooms are tested as they will be used.

Our enlargement drawings show hardwired, interconnected smoke alarms, but the contractor says battery alarms will do. Which is right?

The drawings are the document the work was approved against, so they are where to start, and the architect who signed them is the person to ask. In New York, new construction and work done under an alteration permit is where hardwired alarms with battery backup, linked to each other, typically appear in the plans, and an inspector or a future buyer's engineer will compare what is on the ceilings with what is on the drawings. If the plans call for hardwired interconnected alarms, battery alarms are a substitution the contractor is not entitled to make on their own. The practical side matters too: hardwired alarms need a circuit, and the 120-volt part of that is electrician's work, which is easiest while the walls of the new floor are open. If you are past that stage, we can show you what a hardwired run would involve in the finished rooms, and the architect can say whether a listed wireless interconnected system would be acceptable. Either way, write the decision down, so it does not come up for the first time at a sale.

When we extended the house we created an apartment for our married daughter and her family. Does that change which detector rules apply to us?

It can, and it is worth sorting out now rather than when a tenant arrives. HPD's detector rules are written around how many homes a building holds and whether the owner lives there. A house that has become two homes, with you in one of them, is a different case from one rented out entirely, and a house that has become three homes is a multiple dwelling, with posted notices, records and owner duties for each unit. Whether your daughter pays rent, and what the Certificate of Occupancy says the building is, also bear on it, and HPD or your attorney is the right place for a definite answer. What we do is plan as if the duties apply: a smoke detector placed for each sleeping room in her apartment, carbon monoxide near the bedrooms if any fuel-burning appliance serves the building, a gas detector in her kitchen if it has a gas range, and a dated record of each device.

Cellars and Pesach kitchens

Our Pesach kitchen is in the basement and has its own gas range. Does it need a natural gas detector even though we use it only a few weeks a year?

If the house is covered by the 2027 rule, almost certainly yes. As the Mann Report summarizes Local Law 157, where a gas-burning appliance is inside a dwelling, a detector generally goes in the same room, three to ten feet from it horizontally. The rule is tied to the appliance and its gas piping, not to how often the room is used, and a range that sits connected all year is a gas appliance all year. HPD applies the requirement to multiple dwellings and to one- and two-family homes the owner does not occupy. If you live in your own one- or two-family house, the rule does not require it of you, but a basement kitchen that is closed up for most of the year is exactly where a slow leak could go unnoticed, so we still recommend a UL 1484 listed detector there. Count every gas room in the house when you plan: the main kitchen, any second kitchen, the basement kitchen, the boiler room and a laundry room with a gas dryer each count separately.

Every year before Pesach, kashering the oven and the stovetop sets off all the alarms in the house. How do we get through it without taking them down?

By planning for it instead of fighting it, and never by removing an alarm, because the one taken down for kashering is often the one still sitting on the counter after Yom Tov. Start with placement: the smoke alarm nearest the kitchen should sit outside the cooking area, not directly over or beside the range, and if your kitchen alarm is too close, we can move it to where it still protects the house but sees less cooking smoke. Inside a busy kitchen, a heat alarm linked to the rest of the system can cover the room without reacting to smoke and steam. On the day itself, open windows, run the range hood, and use the hush button on the nearest alarm, which quiets it for a short time while keeping the rest of the system on. If your alarms have no hush feature, that is a sign they are older models due for replacement anyway. And when kashering involves an open flame on the grates or counters, keep a working alarm in the hall and a fire extinguisher within reach of the person doing it.

The calendar: Yom Tov and Sukkos

Can our alarm replacements be timed so nothing starts chirping in the middle of a three-day Yom Tov?

Yes, and it is one of the most practical things we do here. Most alarms announce the end of their battery or their working life with a chirp, and a chirp that starts on the first night of a long Yom Tov means days of noise for a family that will not handle the alarm. There are two ways around it. The first is to replace alarms with sealed ten-year models, which do not need battery changes at all, and to put the whole house on the same replacement date so every alarm ages together. The second is to schedule the replacement itself in a quiet stretch of the calendar, well before Rosh Hashanah or Pesach, rather than waiting for the first chirp. On every job we write each device's install date and end-of-life date on the device list, so you can see years ahead when the next round falls and plan it for a weekday in an ordinary month. If an alarm does start chirping right before Yom Tov, call us early in the day.

Our sukkah goes up on the deck right outside the kitchen, with string lights and a space heater. A smoke alarm cannot go outdoors, so where does detection help?

Inside, at the doorway the sukkah opens from. A sukkah on a deck or a porch is an outdoor structure, and household smoke alarms are not made for the weather, wind and open sides of a sukkah, so we do not mount one in it. What we can do is make sure the room the sukkah opens into has a working smoke alarm close enough to the door to catch smoke that drifts in, linked to the rest of the house, because that is how a family eating outside, or asleep inside, would learn about a fire on the deck. The bigger protection is in how the sukkah is set up: outdoor-rated lights and cords, a heater with a tip-over switch kept well clear of the walls and schach, no cords run through a window or under a door where they can be crushed, and the heater off when nobody is out there. If your sukkah sits against the house below a bedroom window, mention it on the survey, since it changes which alarm we want closest.

Shuls and avenue stores

Our shtiebel davens on the ground floor of a house and the rav's family lives upstairs. Is the building treated as a home or as a place of worship for fire alarm purposes?

Possibly as both, and the answer depends on facts we cannot settle by looking: how many people the shul room holds at its busiest, how the space is built and separated from the house, and what the building's Certificate of Occupancy says. A room used for regular public worship can be an assembly use, and once a space crosses certain thresholds the codes can call for a fire alarm system designed by a registered design professional and filed with the FDNY. That is the engineer's question, and the board should ask it before the next renovation, not after. What we can do right away is protect the family upstairs: smoke and carbon monoxide alarms in their bedrooms and halls, linked to a detector in the shul room and the stair between them, so a fire in the shul at night is heard by the people asleep above it. We put both parts in writing, the household protection we installed and the questions for the engineer, so the board has a clear record.

Our shul is full before sunrise and late at night for learning, and nearly empty in the middle of the afternoon. When should detection report to a monitoring center?

During exactly those empty hours, and whenever the building is locked. Detection that only sounds a horn works when people are there to hear it and get out. The March 2024 synagogue fire on 60th Street broke out shortly after 6 a.m., and three people were pulled from the third floor, so the early hours are not quiet in every shul. But a shul that empties out between Mincha and the evening shiurim, or stays locked from late at night until the first minyan, can have a fire grow with nobody inside to hear anything. Central station monitoring closes that gap: a signal from a detector reaches a monitoring center, which calls for help and calls the contacts you name. Through our partners, monitoring runs $30 a month with a keypad or $45 a month with the app. If the shul already has a building fire alarm system, monitoring is usually part of it already and belongs with its fire alarm company; we check that before proposing anything new.

Our store on 13th Avenue is long and narrow, with the stockroom at the back and no rear windows. Where should detection go so a fire in the back is caught while it is small?

At the back, where nobody is looking, and the farther back the better. In a long, deep store, a single detector near the front door is a long way from the stockroom, and smoke from a fire in stacked boxes has to fill the back of the store before it reaches it. So we put detection in the stockroom itself, at the rear of the selling floor, and in any closet or office back there, with spacing set by the ceiling and the stock height. The April 2025 fire at 3901 13th Avenue started at the rear of a shoe store, and the FDNY said its stock and the lack of rear windows made it hard to fight, which is the case for catching a fire early. Because the store is closed at night, the detection has to tell someone: by sounding where the families upstairs can hear it, by central station monitoring, or both. And keep a clear path from the back to the front. Stock stacked into the aisle slows a fire's discovery and everyone's way out.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Borough Park

Quick answers from AI tools and national guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr are written for an average American house with one family and one kitchen. These are the shortcuts that mislead Borough Park owners most.

"A house needs one smoke alarm per floor"

One per floor is a floor-plan answer, and Borough Park houses are not average floor plans. Many have been enlarged with extra bedrooms, a new top floor or a finished cellar, and they hold large families who sleep behind closed doors on three or four levels.

HPD's rule for multiple dwellings already goes further, placing a detector within fifteen feet of the entrance to each room used for sleeping, and in a big house the real test is whether the farthest child hears the alarm that goes off first.

That is why we count bedrooms before devices, put alarms inside and outside sleeping rooms, link them, and test from the top-floor room with the doors shut.

"If you got free alarms from a community program, you are covered"

The BPJCC, Senator Felder and Red Cross initiative has put smoke detectors into tens of thousands of homes since 2017, and for an owner-occupied house it is an excellent start.

It was not built to meet a landlord's HPD duties for rented units, gas detection in each room with a gas appliance, carbon monoxide planning around a cellar boiler, or alarms linked across an enlarged house.

And alarms age. A unit installed in the program's first years is getting close to the replacement date printed on it, so a free alarm from 2017 deserves a look now.

"A smoke alarm in the front hall covers the basement"

Local fires say otherwise. Reports of the 48th Street fire in 2024, the 39th Street fire in December 2025 and the 6007 Fort Hamilton Parkway fire in April 2026 all describe fires that started in a basement or cellar.

Smoke from below has to fill the cellar and climb the stair before a first-floor hall alarm sees it, and by then the stair may be the one route out that is no longer usable.

A cellar alarm, plus one at the top of the cellar stair, linked to every bedroom, is the cheapest meaningful improvement in most Borough Park houses we survey.

"Battery fires happen in repair shops, not homes"

Some do, but the 48th Street fire began with two e-bikes and their batteries in a family's basement, and fire marshals said the batteries were uncertified knockoffs.

Boro Park 24 reported in July 2025 that structural fires were up 26 percent on the year, with many started by batteries charged or stored indoors.

A charging spot in the cellar or the hall is a home fire risk, and it is one of the first things we ask about on a survey and write into the plan.

"Natural gas detectors only matter next to the stove"

The Local Law 157 summary in the Mann Report says the alarm generally goes in the same room as each gas-burning appliance, so the boiler room and the laundry room with a gas dryer count too.

In Borough Park homes that often adds a second kitchen and a basement Pesach kitchen with its own range, rooms a generic checklist never mentions.

HPD's requirement for rented homes and multiple dwellings starts January 1, 2027, and counting every gas room is the first step to getting the number of detectors right.

"Any building can add a fire alarm and file the paperwork afterward"

Not in New York City. The FDNY's guide says plans by a registered design professional are filed and reviewed before installation, with a TM-1 application and a Certificate of Occupancy, approved Schedule A or Letter of No Objection.

Initial review can take up to eight weeks, and the guide puts the whole process at roughly 20 to 24 weeks, with an active monitoring connection required before inspection.

A shul board, school or store planning a renovation should start the fire alarm design months before the work, not after the walls close. Bringing the design professional in at the same time as the architect means device locations, cable paths and the panel position are settled on paper, and the installation can follow the approved drawings the week the ceilings are open rather than waiting on a filing after they close.

"A store's fire alarm protects the store"

On Borough Park's avenues, the store's alarm protects the families upstairs first. The April 2025 shoe store fire on 13th Avenue started at the rear of a closed store and filled the apartments above with smoke at two in the morning.

Detection in a stockroom that sounds only inside a locked store warns nobody. It has to reach the apartments, a monitoring center, or both.

That is why our plans for a store under apartments start with the people upstairs and work back to the stockroom, not the other way round. The shared stair gets detection of its own, so smoke rising from a closed store is caught before it reaches the first apartment door.

Fire marshals blamed knockoff batteries for the 48th Street fire. How do we know the alarms we buy are not knockoffs too?
+

Buy from an established store or the maker, not from a street seller or an unknown online listing, and look for the listing mark of a recognized testing laboratory on the alarm itself, not only on the box. Gas detectors should be listed to UL 1484. When we supply the devices, we install only listed alarms from established makers, and we check any alarm a family bought before mounting it.

Can your technician show our older children what each alarm sounds like and what to do when they hear it?
+

Yes, and we encourage it. At the end of the job we sound the smoke alarm and the carbon monoxide alarm so everyone hears the difference, show the older children and adults how to test and hush an alarm, and walk through the way out from each floor. The BPJCC program's evacuation plans are a good companion to that walk-through.
