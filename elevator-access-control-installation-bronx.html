Bronx by the Numbers

How Many Elevators the Bronx Has, and Why That Shapes Elevator Security Here

The Bronx is often described as a borough of walk-ups and row houses, but the city's own records tell a different story. Most Bronx homes are reached by elevator, and the borough's elevator stock is concentrated in exactly the kinds of buildings where a locked front door is not enough: big prewar apartment houses, postwar co-op towers, very large planned developments and a fast-growing set of new rental and supportive buildings in the South Bronx. Before we talk about readers and relays, these are the numbers we work from.

The same open dataset shows where the elevators sit. Grouped by the city's neighborhood tabulation areas, the Bronx areas with the most active elevator devices are Concourse and Concourse Village (600), Mott Haven and Port Morris (592), Riverdale and Spuyten Duyvil (531), Bedford Park (371), Melrose (361), University Heights South and Morris Heights (338), Mount Hope (318), Norwood (314), Mount Eden and Claremont West (297), Parkchester (256), Co-op City (244) and Longwood (242). Those twelve areas alone hold more than 4,400 of the borough's elevators.

Two practical lessons come out of that list. First, the Bronx is not one elevator market but several. The Concourse, Mount Hope, Bedford Park and Norwood counts are dominated by five- and six-story prewar apartment houses with one car, sometimes two. Riverdale's count is weighted toward postwar co-op towers with garages and several cars. Mott Haven and Melrose mix older public and private housing with new construction. Parkchester and Co-op City are planned developments where the same design repeats dozens or hundreds of times. A good elevator access control design for one of these is often the wrong design for another.

Second, a lot of these elevators are old, and the elevator side of the job varies building by building. One car may run on a modern microprocessor controller with spare inputs ready for an access interface; the car two blocks away may still have a relay controller and a traveling cable with no free conductors. That is why we never quote floor restriction over the phone, and why the elevator contractor is part of every Bronx survey we run.

How It Works

What an Elevator Access Control System Does in a Bronx Building

An elevator access control system adds one question to every ride: is this person allowed to go where they are asking to go? The elevator still does everything an elevator does. It still levels at the floors, opens its doors, answers fire service and obeys its own safety circuits. The access control system only decides whether a floor button, or a call button at a landing, is allowed to register for the person standing there.

In practice that decision is made in one of four places, and Bronx buildings use all four:

  • A reader inside the car that enables the car. The rider presents a fob, card, code, phone or face, and the car becomes usable. Once enabled, the rider can usually press any floor the car serves. This is our Level 1 and Level 2, and it is the most common request from Bronx apartment houses where the main problem is people who are not residents riding the elevator at all.
  • Floor-by-floor restriction. Every floor button is locked by default, and a valid credential unlocks only that person's floors for a few seconds. This is what a Riverdale co-op board usually means when it asks for an elevator floor restriction system, and it is part of Level 3.
  • Hall-call control. A reader at the lobby, cellar or garage call station stops the car from being called to that landing without a credential. In Bronx buildings with a basement laundry, a garage under the building or a side entrance off the courtyard, this is often the stop that matters most.
  • Destination dispatch integration. In the few newer Bronx office and institutional buildings with lobby kiosks that assign cars, permissions are sent to the elevator system as data instead of through button relays.

Whatever the design, the hardware on our side is similar: a reader, an access control panel, an elevator interface (usually a bank of relays or an input/output board, one point per controlled floor per car), a power supply with battery backup, the cable that joins them, and software where the managing agent or super adds and cancels credentials. The elevator contractor connects our floor-enable outputs to the elevator controller or car operating panel, which in New York is licensed elevator work. That is the part we coordinate and never do ourselves.

People search for this in a lot of ways: an elevator security system, an elevator key fob system, an elevator card reader, lift access control, elevator floor control or elevator access management. They are all describing some mix of those four designs plus the software that sits behind them, and in the Bronx, the design that fits is decided far more by the building than by the brand of reader on the wall.

Grand Concourse

Elevator Access Control for Grand Concourse Art Deco and Prewar Apartment Houses

The Grand Concourse is the Bronx's great apartment-house boulevard, and it is the single densest cluster of elevators in the borough's open data. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report for the Grand Concourse Historic District, approved in 2011, describes 61 apartment houses built between 1917 and 1959, from neo-Renaissance buildings of the 1920s to the Art Deco and Moderne houses the boulevard is famous for. The report singles out buildings such as 888 Grand Concourse, an Emery Roth design with rounded bays and a mosaic and terrazzo entrance vestibule, and 1150 Grand Concourse, the building residents call the fish building for its mosaic of tropical fish. Beyond the district lines, the same generation of five- and six-story elevator apartment houses runs through Concourse Village, Mount Eden, Mount Hope, Highbridge, Fordham and Bedford Park.

These buildings share a few traits that shape elevator access control:

  • One car, sometimes two, and often a cellar stop. Many Concourse-era houses have a single passenger elevator that serves a basement level with the laundry, storage, the boiler room and sometimes the super's apartment. Securing that cellar stop is frequently more important to the building than the upper floors.
  • Courtyard and side entrances. A lot of these buildings were designed around courtyards with more than one door. A fob reader at the main lobby door does little if the courtyard door is propped open, so the elevator becomes the second line of defense.
  • Old controllers and tired traveling cables. Some cars have been modernized; some still run older relay or early solid-state controllers. Whether there is a spare conductor running to the car decides how simple Level 1 can be.
  • Finishes worth protecting. Terrazzo floors, marble wainscot, original brass and mosaic vestibules are part of why people love these buildings. Nobody wants a surface-mounted conduit across them.

Our approach in a Concourse apartment house usually starts with the car rather than the lobby wall. A Level 1 reader mounted inside the cab, on or beside the car operating panel, protects every floor without touching the lobby finishes at all. Where the board or owner also wants the lobby call button controlled, we look for a mounting spot on the elevator entrance frame or on a section of wall that was already altered, and we run cable through existing pathways, the elevator machine room and the cellar ceiling rather than across historic surfaces. In a building inside the historic district, exterior and storefront changes are what the Landmarks Preservation Commission typically reviews; interior elevator readers are generally not landmark work, but if a change touches a designated feature, the owner should check with LPC before we drill, and we will wait for that answer.

Many of these buildings are rentals, many are rent stabilized, and some are co-ops. In a rental, the decision belongs to the owner and managing agent, but the rollout lives or dies with how tenants are told about it, which we cover further down. In a co-op, the board sets the floor rules and we write them down before programming starts.

Riverdale & Spuyten Duyvil

Riverdale and Spuyten Duyvil Co-op Towers: Floor Restriction, Garages and Doormen

Riverdale and Spuyten Duyvil together rank third in the borough for active elevators, and the character of that stock is very different from the Concourse. This is the Bronx's postwar co-op country: mid-century and later towers along Independence Avenue, Henry Hudson Parkway, Johnson Avenue, Palisade Avenue, Kappock Street and Riverdale Avenue, many with a doorman or a part-time lobby attendant, a garage under or beside the building, a laundry room, a bicycle or storage room and a long list of shareholders. Published listings give a sense of the range: The Burton at 2550 Independence Avenue is described as a seven-story, 142-unit postwar co-op, and the River Point Towers co-op at 555 Kappock Street is one of the mid-century high-rises that define the Spuyten Duyvil skyline.

In buildings like these, elevator access control is usually less about strangers walking in off the street and more about control and record-keeping:

  • Garage-level call stations. When a car opens directly into the garage, anyone who walks in behind a car can ride upstairs. A reader at the garage call button, or a garage-level restriction inside the car, closes that gap without bothering residents who arrive through the lobby.
  • Floor-by-floor restriction for shareholders. Boards often want each apartment's credentials to reach the lobby, the shareholder's own floor and the shared amenity floors, with staff credentials reaching everything.
  • Doorman hours. Many Riverdale buildings have staff on the door for part of the day. The access system can run the same rules around the clock, or it can relax certain rules while staff are present; we set whichever the board decides and record it in the floor chart.
  • Contractors and renovations. Co-op alteration work brings a steady stream of workers. A temporary credential that reaches only the lobby, the service floor and the apartment under renovation, and expires on the last day of the job, replaces a borrowed shareholder fob.

Most Riverdale co-ops already have a key fob or card system at the front door and the garage. Where that platform supports elevator control, extending it into the cars keeps residents on one credential and one software, which is almost always the cheaper and calmer choice. Where it does not, we explain the options, including replacing the door system at the same time, and put the costs side by side. Boards here also tend to ask more pointed questions about data, so the Local Law 63 section of this page is worth reading before a board meeting.

Co-op City

What a Development the Size of Co-op City Teaches About Elevator Access Control

Co-op City in the northeast Bronx is the largest residential development in the United States. According to the cooperative's own published facts, it is a New York State Mitchell-Lama housing cooperative with 15,372 apartments in 35 high-rise buildings and seven townhouse clusters, completed in 1971, with its own around-the-clock private public safety department. The management company also reports that it has modernized all 160 residential elevators in the high-rise buildings and built new lobby entrances. We have no relationship with Co-op City and do not claim it as a customer; we mention it because it is the clearest example of a problem many smaller Bronx developments share.

When one organization runs dozens of buildings and hundreds of cars, the hard part of elevator access control stops being the reader and becomes the administration:

  • One credential list, not one per building. Residents move between buildings, staff work across the whole campus, and contractors come and go. A platform that holds every credential in one database, with permission groups per building and per floor, prevents the drift that happens when each building keeps its own list.
  • Modernized controllers make the interface cleaner. When elevators have recently been modernized, the controllers usually have provisions for card-reader or security inputs, and the elevator contractor can often connect floor-enable signals more simply than on an old relay controller. That is a question for the elevator contractor, and it is one of the first we ask.
  • Public safety and management need different views. A security desk wants live events and quick lookups; the management office wants credential issuing, move-ins, move-outs and reports. Good platforms give each role its own access and a log of who changed what.
  • Phasing. Large developments rarely do every building at once. A design that works for one building and repeats exactly in the next keeps training, spare parts and resident instructions simple.

The same thinking applies, at smaller scale, to Bronx developments of three, five or eleven buildings, such as the cooperative housing clusters around Van Cortlandt Park, where the Amalgamated Housing Cooperative alone counts 11 residential buildings and 1,468 apartments. If your development has more than one building, tell us at the start; the platform choice matters more than any single reader.

Parkchester

Parkchester and Other Planned Complexes: Many Lobbies, One Car Each

Parkchester is a different kind of large development. Built from the late 1930s into the early 1940s, it holds 12,271 apartments in 171 red-brick buildings grouped into 51 clusters across about 129 acres, with heights from seven to thirteen stories, according to the complex's own history and the Historic Districts Council. Today the apartments are condominiums, some owner-occupied and some rented out. The open data puts 256 active elevators in the Parkchester area.

What matters for elevator access control is the layout: lots of separate entrances, many of them with a single car, spread over a big campus. That changes the economics and the design:

  • Per-car pricing scales simply. Because our Level 1 and Level 2 prices are per car, a complex with many single-car lobbies can see a clear number per building before committing to the whole site.
  • Standardize the hardware. The same reader, the same mounting height, the same interface layout in the machine room and the same labels in every building make service faster and cheaper for years.
  • Owners and renters in one building. In a condominium where some units are rented out, credentials need an owner of record and a clear process for issuing and cancelling a tenant's fob when a lease ends.
  • Remote management is worth more here. When a management office looks after many buildings, adding and cancelling credentials without walking to each lobby saves real time. That depends on a network connection reaching each panel, which we confirm during the survey and price as its own line if it needs new cable.

Other Bronx complexes follow similar patterns at smaller scale, from Mitchell-Lama clusters to privately managed groups of mid-rise buildings that share one management office. In all of them, the first meeting is about who will run the system day to day, because that person's workflow should shape the platform.

South Bronx New Rentals

Mott Haven, Port Morris and Melrose Rentals: Extending the Front-Door App Into the Cars

The South Bronx has been the borough's fastest-changing elevator market. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2025 Housing Supply Report counts 9,842 new homes completed in the Bronx in 2023, more than any other borough that year, and the open data puts Mott Haven and Port Morris second in the borough for active elevators, with Melrose fifth. Projects such as Bronx Point, listed on NYC Housing Connect as a newly built 432-unit residential building in Mott Haven, along with market-rate towers near the Harlem River waterfront and along Third Avenue and Bruckner Boulevard, have added buildings with several cars, package rooms, gyms, roof decks, lounges and bicycle rooms.

These buildings usually arrive with a cloud-managed system at the front door: a video intercom, fob readers at the lobby and amenity doors, maybe phone credentials. The request we hear most from their managers is simple to describe and worth doing carefully: make the elevator part of the same system.

  • Amenity floors on schedules. A roof deck that closes at 10 p.m. or a gym open to residents only can be controlled at the elevator as well as at the amenity door, so the car stops carrying people to a closed floor.
  • Affordable and market-rate residents in one building. Many new South Bronx buildings combine lottery units, supportive units and market-rate units. The access rules should treat all residents the same unless the owner has a documented reason not to.
  • Leasing turnover. New buildings lease up fast. Credentials have to be issued and cancelled in step with move-ins and move-outs, and the platform's records should show it.
  • Deliveries. With a package room on the ground floor, most couriers never need to go upstairs. The elevator rules can reflect that, while the intercom still lets a resident release their own floor for a food delivery or a guest.

The good news in a new building is that the elevator controller is usually modern and the elevator contractor can tell us quickly how floor-enable signals connect. The thing to check early is whether the door platform the developer chose supports elevator control in the cars you have, and what licence fees that adds. We confirm that before quoting so the manager does not find out after the readers are on the wall.

Supportive & Senior Housing

Elevator Access Control for Bronx Supportive, Senior and Affordable Residences

A large share of the new elevator buildings opening in the Bronx are supportive, senior or deeply affordable residences run by nonprofit operators. Recent public announcements give a picture of the scale: YP Senior Residence, a 117-unit affordable senior building with supportive apartments opened in 2025; Baez Place, 154 affordable apartments including 86 supportive units for New Yorkers who have experienced homelessness and live with mental health concerns; and Starhill Phase 2 at 49 Featherbed Lane, listed in the Bronx Borough President's 2025 development report with 244 units, 75 of them supportive. We mention these as examples of the building type, not as our customers.

Elevator access control in these buildings has to balance security against dignity and safety, and the operator's program staff usually know more about that balance than any installer. What we bring is a design that does what they decide:

  • Office and program floors. Case management offices, clinical rooms, staff break rooms and records rooms often sit on a lower floor or the cellar. Floor restriction lets residents reach their own floor and the shared lounges while those staff floors stay locked to anyone without a staff credential.
  • Front-desk control. Many of these buildings have a staffed desk around the clock. The desk can release a floor for a visitor who has signed in, and the system records which floor was released and when.
  • Home care aides and visiting nurses. Aides who come every day need reliable access to one apartment's floor on a schedule, without being handed a resident's credential. Time-limited credentials do this cleanly.
  • Residents who lose credentials. Fobs go missing in every building, and the replacement process should be quick and free of embarrassment. Cancelling the old fob takes seconds in the software; issuing the new one should be just as easy for the desk.
  • Accessibility. Readers have to be reachable from a wheelchair inside the car, unlock windows may need to be longer for residents who move slowly, and a phone or face credential can help residents who struggle with a small fob.

Operators of these residences answer to funders, agencies and their own policies on resident privacy. Local Law 63 applies to residential buildings that use smart access, and many operators have stricter internal rules than the law. We keep the logs to what the operator needs, avoid biometric readers unless the operator has a written consent process, and give the operator the platform's privacy documentation for its resident handbook.

Campuses & Medical

Hospital, University and Medical Office Elevators in the Bronx

The Bronx is home to large hospital systems and university campuses, including Montefiore's medical centers and Fordham University's Rose Hill campus, alongside Bronx Community College, Lehman College, Manhattan University in Riverdale and a long list of medical office buildings and clinics along East Fordham Road, the Grand Concourse, Pelham Parkway and Westchester Avenue. We name these institutions only to describe the borough; they are not our clients, and large hospital and university security programs are usually run by in-house teams with their own standards and vendors.

Where we fit in this world is usually the smaller building: a medical office building with a few practices, a clinic that leases two floors in a mixed-use building, a dormitory or faculty residence run by a smaller school, a nonprofit headquarters, a house of worship with an elevator to its community hall. The needs are familiar across all of them:

  • Public floors by day, controlled floors at night. Patients and visitors ride freely during clinic hours; after hours, the cars carry only staff to the practice floors.
  • Service elevators. Freight and service cars used for supplies, waste and moves are often left open by default. A reader on the service car keeps it for staff and authorized vendors.
  • Records, pharmacy and equipment floors. These floors may need stricter rules than the rest of the building, sometimes with two credentials or a staffed desk release.
  • Badges the staff already carry. Most organizations want one badge to work everywhere. Multi-technology readers can read many existing card formats, which we check against a sample badge before quoting.

Local Law 63 does not apply to offices, clinics or campus buildings that are not residential multiple dwellings, but healthcare and education organizations usually have their own data and privacy policies. We work inside those policies and hand over documentation their compliance staff can review.

Upgrades

How to Upgrade Elevator Access Control in an Older Bronx Building

A lot of the calls we get from the Bronx are not about a first elevator system but about replacing one. The building put in a keypad, a key switch or an early proximity reader ten, fifteen or twenty years ago, and now it is unreliable, insecure or both. Upgrading elevator access control is a different job from a new install, because there is already a pathway, an interface and a set of habits to work with. This is how we approach it.

Start with what is actually there

Before talking about new equipment, we open the old system up. We find the reader or keypad, the controller box (often in the machine room, the cellar or behind a panel in the lobby), the power supply, the relays or key switch contacts, and the cable to the car. We note how the old system was tied to the elevator: through a spare conductor in the traveling cable, through a separate cable added later, or through contacts in the car station. That one discovery often decides most of the upgrade cost, because if the old interface is sound, the new system can often reuse the same path.

Decide what the upgrade should fix

Common reasons Bronx buildings upgrade an elevator system include:

  • A shared code everyone knows. A keypad with one building-wide code works until the code is passed around the block. Individual codes, fobs or phone credentials give each person their own access that can be cancelled alone.
  • Old proximity fobs that are easy to copy. Many older 125 kHz proximity fobs can be duplicated cheaply. Current encrypted credentials are much harder to clone.
  • No record of who rode. Older keypads and key switches keep no log at all. A modern system shows which credential was used at which reader and when.
  • No remote management. If cancelling a fob means someone has to stand in front of a box in the cellar, the building is one lost fob away from a security hole.
  • Parts that no longer exist. When the reader or controller is discontinued, every failure is a scramble.

Choose the upgrade path

Most upgrades fall into one of three paths. A like-for-like reader swap keeps the existing controller and only replaces the reader with a compatible one; it is the cheapest, but it is only possible when the controller is still supported. A controller-and-reader replacement keeps the cable and the elevator interface but replaces the brains of the system, which is the most common Bronx upgrade. A full replacement starts over, usually because the building is also changing its front-door system or moving to floor-by-floor restriction. In every case, the elevator contractor checks the connection at the controller before the old system is removed, so the elevator is never left in an unknown state.

Move residents over without a lockout

The riskiest moment of any upgrade is the switchover. We collect the resident list first, issue new credentials before the old system goes dark, run both systems in parallel where the wiring allows, and set a clear date when old codes stop working. For buildings with many older residents, we suggest a lobby table on two afternoons where the super or managing agent hands out credentials and shows people how to use them.

Repairs

Who Repairs Elevator Access Control Systems in the Bronx, and How We Diagnose a Dead Reader

When an elevator reader stops working, managers often do not know whom to call. The elevator company says the elevator is fine. The intercom company says the elevator is not theirs. The original installer may have closed, changed its phone number or stopped answering. The honest answer is that an elevator access control system has two owners. The low-voltage part, meaning the reader, the access panel, the relays, the power supply, the cable and the software, is security work, and that is what we repair. The elevator controller, the car operating panel wiring at the controller, and anything to do with fire service are elevator work, and they belong to your licensed elevator contractor.

A good repair visit separates those two halves quickly. This is what we check, in roughly this order:

  1. Power. A failed power supply or a dead backup battery is the most common cause of a reader that is completely dark. Bronx summers are hard on equipment in hot machine rooms and cellars, and batteries age out on their own.
  2. The reader itself. A reader that lights but does not read may have a damaged face, water in the housing, or a wiring fault at the back plate.
  3. Communication. If the reader reads but nothing happens, the problem may be the cable between the reader and the panel, a loose connector, or a panel that has lost its network connection to the cloud platform.
  4. The credential database. Sometimes the hardware is fine and the credential has simply expired, was never assigned to the elevator, or belongs to a permission group that does not include that floor.
  5. The relay or interface output. If the panel grants access but the floor button stays dark, we test the output at the relay. If our output is switching correctly, the fault is on the elevator side, and we call the elevator contractor with a precise description rather than a guess.

Buildings whose original installer has disappeared often have a second problem: nobody has the administrator login. Depending on the brand, the manufacturer or distributor may be able to restore access for the building owner with proof of ownership, or the controller may need to be reset and reprogrammed, which means re-enrolling credentials. Intercom Integration

Integrating Elevator Access Control With the Lobby Intercom in a Bronx Building

Once a building locks its elevator, it has to answer a practical question right away: how do guests, aides and deliveries get upstairs? In a building without a doorman, which describes most Bronx apartment houses, the answer is the lobby intercom. Delivery drivers say it plainly in online forums: in buildings where the elevator needs a fob, the resident either comes down or the driver waits. A well-designed intercom tie-in removes that friction without opening the whole building.

How intercom floor release works

The visitor calls the apartment from the lobby panel. The resident answers on an indoor station or a phone app and presses the button to unlock the door. With floor release, the same action also tells the elevator system to allow that resident's floor for a short window, usually a minute or two. The visitor steps into the car, presses the floor, and the car goes there and nowhere else. The log shows which apartment released which floor and when.

There are two ways to build it, and which one fits depends on the intercom you have:

  • Platform integration. Several current video intercoms and access platforms can talk to the elevator control system directly, so the intercom knows which floor belongs to each apartment. This is the cleanest option and is common in newer South Bronx rentals and recently upgraded co-ops.
  • Relay-based release. Where the intercom cannot talk to the access system, a dedicated output can release a floor for a timed window. This is simpler but less precise, and in a building with many apartments per floor it releases the whole floor rather than one apartment.

When an old audio intercom is the bottleneck

Many Bronx buildings still have an analog audio intercom with handsets in each apartment and a riser of old multi-conductor cable. Those systems usually cannot tell anything else which apartment answered, so they cannot release one specific floor. The choices are to accept a simpler arrangement, such as a front-desk or super-controlled release, or to replace the intercom with a system that supports floor release. Replacing the intercom at the same time as adding elevator control often costs less in total than doing them years apart, because both jobs share the same survey, the same pathways and the same resident rollout.

Rules worth writing down

  • How long a released floor stays open, and whether a second ride is allowed in that window.
  • Whether the intercom can release amenity floors such as a roof deck, or only residential floors.
  • What happens late at night: some buildings disable floor release after a set hour and require residents to come down.
  • Whether couriers are sent to the package room instead of upstairs, and how the intercom directory says so.

We set those rules with the owner or board and program them into both systems, then test every apartment's release from the lobby panel before handover.

Unauthorized Riding

How to Stop Unauthorized People From Using the Elevator in a Bronx Building

The complaint behind most Bronx elevator calls is easy to describe. Someone follows a resident through the front door, or comes in through a courtyard door that does not latch, then rides to the top floor and hangs around the stair landing or the roof bulkhead. Tenants in some buildings have also told local news about trespassing and people sleeping in stairwells. An elevator reader can help a lot with that problem, but only if the building understands what it does and what it does not do.

What elevator access control stops

  • Riding without a credential. With a reader in the car, a person who slips in cannot make the car go anywhere. That alone discourages most casual trespassing on upper floors.
  • Riding to the wrong floor. With floor-by-floor restriction, even a person with a valid credential reaches only their own floors and the shared ones.
  • Calling the car from the cellar or garage. A hall-call reader at the cellar or garage landing stops someone who enters through a service door from bringing the car down to them.
  • Unlogged access. Every ride is recorded against a credential, which helps a manager spot a shared or stolen fob.

What it does not stop, and what to do instead

  • The stairs. Stairwells are exit routes and must stay usable. A person who is determined to reach the roof can still climb. Roof doors and stair doors are governed by fire and building code rules on egress, so the answer there is usually an alarmed or monitored door and a camera at the landing, designed within those rules, not a lock that traps people.
  • Tailgating at the front door. If the lobby door is propped open or the closer is broken, the elevator becomes the only barrier. Fixing the door hardware is often the cheapest security improvement in the building.
  • Riding with a resident. If a resident lets someone ride with them, the system cannot tell. Cameras in the lobby and car, and a clear building policy, help here.

A layered plan for a typical Bronx apartment house

For a six-story building with one car and a cellar stop, a sensible plan often looks like this: repair the front and courtyard door closers and locks; add a Level 1 reader in the car so the elevator only runs for residents and staff; add a hall-call reader or a cellar-floor restriction so the basement cannot be used to bring the car down; put a camera on the lobby, the car and the top-floor landing; and make sure the roof door is alarmed in a way that meets code. The elevator part is our Level 1 or Level 3 work, the door and camera work can be added to the same written scope, and the roof door design is reviewed against code before anything is changed.

Tenant Privacy

Local Law 63, Rent-Stabilized Tenants and Elevator Fobs in Bronx Apartment Buildings

Because most Bronx elevator buildings are residential, NYC Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act, applies to many of the systems we install here. As the Department of Housing Preservation and Development summarizes it, owners of residential multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as fobs, cards, phone apps or biometrics must get express consent before collecting reference data such as biometric templates, give tenants a plain-language privacy policy, use the data only to grant access and monitor entrances, exits and common areas, protect it with real safeguards, and destroy a departed tenant's reference data within 90 days. The law reaches common areas, and an elevator is a common area.

For a Bronx owner or board, that translates into a handful of design choices we make with you:

  • Keep the log narrow. The system records which credential was used at which reader and when. We do not set up reports that follow individual tenants' habits.
  • Avoid biometrics in apartments unless there is a consent process. Our Level 2 face and fingerprint readers suit staff areas and buildings with a written consent and enrollment process. In most rental apartment buildings, a fob, card or phone is the simpler choice.
  • Plan the move-out step. Cancelling a departing tenant's credentials and clearing their data on time is a property-management routine, and we write it into the handover notes.
  • Hand over the platform's privacy documentation. The owner needs it to prepare the policy given to tenants.

Rollout matters just as much. In online tenant forums, renters ask whether a landlord can make them use a fob when the lease never mentioned one, and some are unhappy when a new system appears without explanation. Many Bronx buildings are rent stabilized, and tenants there may raise questions about changes to building services. We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice; owners should get their own counsel on the lease and service questions. What we can do is make the change easy to live with: a letter to every apartment before installation, a clear explanation of what is recorded and why, a way to get a replacement credential quickly, and options for tenants who cannot easily use a small fob.

For co-ops and condos, the same privacy principles apply, and the board usually adopts the floor chart and the privacy notice together at one meeting. Office buildings, clinics and campus buildings are not covered by this particular law, though most choose the same discipline voluntarily.

Glossary

Elevator Access Control Terms for Bronx Boards, Owners and Supers

  • Car operating panel: the button panel inside the elevator car. A Level 1 reader is usually mounted on or beside it.
  • Hall-call station: the up and down buttons at a landing such as the lobby, cellar or garage. A reader here controls who can call the car to that landing.
  • Floor-enable relay: a switch, controlled by the access panel, that allows a particular floor button to register for a few seconds after a valid credential is presented.
  • I/O board: an input/output interface that does the work of many relays on one board.
  • Traveling cable: the flexible cable that hangs from the car to the hoistway and carries its signals. A spare conductor in it is often how a reader in the car reaches the machine room.
  • Relay controller: an older type of elevator controller built from electromechanical relays, still found in some Bronx buildings.
  • Firefighters' service (Phase I and Phase II): the emergency operation that recalls cars and hands them to firefighters. Access control must drop out of the way when it starts.
  • Permission group: a set of floors and hours assigned to a group of credentials, such as residents of the fourth floor or building staff.
  • Intercom floor release: letting a resident who answers the lobby intercom allow their floor for a visitor's ride.
  • Encrypted credential: a fob or card that uses cryptography so it is far harder to copy than older proximity fobs.
  • Audit trail: the record of which credential was used at which reader and when, kept within the limits the building sets.
