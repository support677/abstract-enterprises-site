The Neighborhood

Why Ditmas Park Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Ditmas Park sits in the middle of Flatbush, roughly between Ocean Avenue, Coney Island Avenue, Newkirk Avenue and Dorchester Road, in Community District 14 and the 70th Precinct, with the B and Q trains running through it in an open cut. The Ditmas family farmed the land before developer Lewis H. Pounds bought it and began building in 1902, and the houses that followed are why a door buzzer job here so often starts on a porch.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report describes most of the houses as "freestanding, frame, two-story residential structures with attics," faced in clapboard or shingle, almost all with columnar porches, set back behind wide lawns and built as one-family homes. Over a century many have been divided; Brownstoner's 2019 report on 551 Rugby Road described a condo that is "one of three units in what was originally a two-family Edwardian home." When a house built around one front door becomes two or three homes, the old bell stops being enough: the top floor cannot hear it, nobody knows which household a visitor wants, and letting a guest in means walking down two flights.

The other half of Ditmas Park is where most people actually live. In Brick Underground's 2018 guide, a broker described blocks of Victorian houses "mixed with blocks of 6-story pre- and post-war apartment buildings," adding that "a large majority of neighborhood residents live in apartments." The historian Suzanne Spellen notes that the Cohn Brothers alone put up large six-storey buildings across Flatbush, most between 1926 and 1938. Public records give the scale: 770 Ocean Avenue has 41 apartments from 1923, 570 Westminster Road has 185 apartments from 1935, and 1250 Ocean Avenue has 106 apartments from 1953.

Those doors are where residents notice problems first. A tenant review of the Westminster Road building says simply, "Buzzers and intercoms don't work." In 2018, Bklyner reported that residents of a building near Cortelyou Road and Stratford Road counted more than 100 missing packages in six months, and a resident of a nearby building said "about 40% of the time the lock on the front door is broken." Community Board 14 reports housing code violations rising from 506 per 1,000 units in 2010 to 879 in 2024. A door that latches, a panel that calls the right apartment and a release that works from every floor are the first things residents ask for.

Ditmas Park facts that change how a buzzer is installed

  • The Ditmas Park Historic District, bounded by Marlborough Road, Dorchester Road, Ocean Avenue and Newkirk Avenue, was designated by the city on July 28, 1981; its houses were built mostly between 1902 and the First World War. Any change to a house front there goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission.
  • In November 2025 the Commission added two more districts next door: Ditmas Park West, with 127 houses built nearly all between 1902 and 1910 between Dorchester Road and Ditmas Avenue, and Beverley Square West, with 118 houses from 1894 to 1910. Owners there who never needed a permit for a doorbell now do.
  • The Commission's permit guidebook says intercom panels and doorbells must sit "within or adjacent to a door opening," allows one intercom panel per door opening, and lets its staff approve them when the design, size and finish are compatible with the house.
  • New York's Multiple Dwelling Law requires a two-way intercom at the self-locking front door of buildings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968; older buildings must install one if tenants of a majority of apartments ask in writing.
  • HPD gives owners 30 days to correct a hazardous Class B violation and 14 days for a Class C violation of the self-closing door rules, counted from the date the notice is served.
  • Community Board 14 counts over 48 percent of the district's area as one- and two-family homes, which is why "how many households live here" is our first question on a house job.

Many Ditmas Park owners pair the buzzer with a camera on the porch or lobby; the two are quoted together and installed on the same visit.

Repair or Replace in Ditmas Park

Need Repair in Ditmas Park? A Bell Nobody Upstairs Hears, a Lobby Door That Will Not Open, or Half a Building Without Talk-Back

Repair calls from Ditmas Park sort into two families. On the house blocks, the bell works but rings only in the front hall, or the old button has been painted over or swapped for a battery doorbell that rings one phone. On the apartment blocks, a lobby panel calls but does not release, a door releases but does not latch, or a whole stack of apartments goes quiet because a riser splice has failed.

The symptom usually tells us where to look. ButterflyMX's small-building guide says a door that will not release can be "a relay, power supply, electric strike, magnetic lock, or the door hardware itself," and "A building-wide outage may indicate a shared power or control problem." Brick Underground's 2019 repair guide quotes a locksmith: "It's all in the wire connections." A working system with one bad part should be repaired, and as ButterflyMX warns, "Do not replace a system based on age alone."

Replacement makes sense when parts are no longer made, when service calls keep finding new faults in the same old cable, or when the property now needs something the old system cannot do, such as a button for each household in a divided house. If repair is the honest answer, that is what we quote.

If an existing system is dead and has to be diagnosed, book the $250 service call online or call (347) 934-8335; the $250 is applied toward the repair or the new system. We book visits on weekdays, in two-hour arrival windows, and once a time is confirmed the technician arrives inside it.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Ditmas Park's Architecture

Six kinds of property make up nearly every door buzzer job in Ditmas Park. They differ in who owns the door, how many households the station has to call, where the cable can travel and what the city or the Landmarks Commission expects, and each has its own mistake that shows up when the work is rushed.

One-family frame houses

Freestanding clapboard and shingle houses with porches and attics, built from 1902 onward on Argyle, Rugby, Marlborough, Westminster, Stratford and Buckingham Roads and the blocks around them.

Houses divided into flats

The same houses turned into two-families, three-families or small condos, often with one shared porch door and stairs that once served a single household.

Pre-war six-storey buildings

Brick apartment buildings of the 1920s and 1930s on Ocean Avenue and the nearby side streets, with a vestibule, a lobby, one or two elevators and a riser running up from the cellar.

Post-war apartment buildings

Buildings from the late 1940s through the 1960s, such as the 1953 building at 1250 Ocean Avenue, with simpler lobbies, aluminum-framed doors and a mix of rental and co-op ownership.

Small walk-ups

Three- and four-storey buildings with a dozen or so apartments, such as the 1925 building at 377 Argyle Road, where the super may be the owner and the panel is often decades old.

Shops with apartments above

Mixed-use buildings along Cortelyou Road, Newkirk Avenue and Coney Island Avenue, where a narrow door beside the storefront leads to the apartments upstairs.

One-family frame houses: four levels, one bell and a porch in between

A Ditmas Park house built for one family has a cellar, two main floors and an attic that is very often a bedroom floor or an office now. The original bell was meant to be heard in the front hall and the kitchen, and families tell us they miss deliveries in the attic or the back garden. The answer is a station at the porch door, an answering station or chime on each floor that needs one, and a release if the household wants to let people in without coming down. Wood framing helps: an open cellar, closet stacks and an attic usually give a route to every floor without cutting finished plaster. The preservation architect Norma Barbacci told 6sqft about her own 1903 house here that such services "can be retrofitted pretty well within a historic structure."

The common mistake is putting the station where it was easiest for the installer rather than where a visitor stands. On a deep porch the visitor stands at the door, so the station belongs on the house wall or casing beside the latch side. On a wrap-around porch whose side entrance the family uses daily, a second small station at that door often matters more than a better camera at the front.

Houses divided into flats: one door, three households and an owner in the middle

When a house is split into two or three homes, the porch door usually stays shared and the old bell rings in a hall that now belongs to one household. Each household needs its own button, its own station inside, talk-back and a release for the shared door. If an upstairs flat has its own door at the top of the stairs, its station goes inside that door, so the ring is heard and the release is private. The cable for the upper flats travels through the cellar, a closet stack or the common stair hall, so the downstairs household does not have to open its rooms for someone else's buzzer.

The mistake to avoid is the battery video doorbell that rings one household's phone and nobody else's. It is fine for a one-family house, but in a house with three households it leaves two with no way to answer. We ask how many households actually live there before suggesting anything, because that number, not the size of the house, sets the system.

Pre-war six-storey buildings: vestibules, risers and heavy traffic

The brick buildings on Ocean Avenue and the side streets typically have an outer street door, a vestibule with mailboxes and the panel, and an inner door into the lobby, which is usually the one the panel releases. In a building of 40 to 200 apartments that inner door opens hundreds of times a day, and its closer, strike and latch take a beating no house door sees. The riser is the other question. Aiphone, whose GT Series is rated for up to 5,000 tenant stations, says existing wires in good condition can be reused, so the electronics are rarely the limit; the copper is.

The mistake to avoid is promising a board or owner that the old riser will work before it has been tested. We test every pair, write down which pass, and price the replacement of any pair that might fail before equipment is ordered.

Post-war apartment buildings: aluminum doors and mixed ownership

Post-war buildings such as the 106-apartment building at 1250 Ocean Avenue often have an aluminum and glass lobby entrance. Narrow aluminum frames can take an electric strike, but only one made for that frame, and the closer has to suit a lighter door. Some of these buildings are co-ops, and Habitat Magazine's February 2024 report quoted a lawyer stating that a co-op "has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." Boards that accept that early approve a straightforward replacement; boards that treat it as an upgrade can debate it for months while residents prop the door.

The mistake is choosing a lobby system without asking how residents want to answer. Older residents at home during the day may want wall stations; commuters may want phone answering. Many buildings need both, and the system should allow both.

Small walk-ups: a dozen apartments and a panel older than most tenants

Smaller walk-ups, such as the 16-apartment building from 1925 on Argyle Road, often have an owner who manages them directly and a panel repaired so many times that nobody knows which wire goes where. A walk-up with eight or more apartments falls under the same intercom rule as a big building. Access matters more here: Brownstoner's tenant forum has a 2009 thread about a 12-apartment building where one tenant refused entry and her whole line stayed dead for months.

We ask owners to give tenants notice and agree a two-hour access window for each apartment, and we finish the units that are open on the day while keeping the rest of the system working.

Shops with apartments above: two doors that must stay separate

On Cortelyou Road, Newkirk Avenue and Coney Island Avenue, many buildings have a shop on the ground floor and apartments above, reached by a narrow street door beside the storefront. That door's panel calls the apartments only, and the shop's own buzzer, if it has one, is a separate system. The problem we see most is the two mixed together, a shop that can release the residents' door or an apartment station ringing in a restaurant kitchen.

The mistake to avoid is mounting the apartments' panel where the shop's roll-down gate or awning covers it at night, or where sign lighting blinds the camera. We check the door at closing time as well as at noon.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Households, Watch the Door, Then Find the Route

These notes describe how our technicians approach a property in a neighborhood like Ditmas Park. They are our standard routine, not a story about one address.

Count the households first. On a house job, the first question is not about the bell, it is about who lives there. A house that looks like one home from the sidewalk may have a basement flat, a parlor-floor household and an attic apartment, each with its own visitors. The technician asks, checks the mailboxes and the inner doors, and writes the number down before suggesting a station.

Watch the door close by itself. Before the technician looks at the panel, the street or porch door is opened and let go, several times. If it stops short of the latch, drags on the sill or bounces off the strike, no new buzzer will make the house or building secure, and the owner hears that before anything else.

Find the route before promising it. In a frame house the technician goes down to the cellar and up to the attic, looking for the path from the porch wall to every floor; in an apartment building the technician goes to the cellar room where the old power supply lives and tests the riser pairs. The quote is written around the route that actually exists, with any part that could change named and priced before ordering.

Landmark Districts

Three Historic Districts, One Porch Door: Putting a Station on a Landmarked Ditmas Park House

A large share of Ditmas Park's houses now sit inside a city historic district. The original Ditmas Park Historic District was designated in 1981. In November 2025 the Landmarks Preservation Commission added Ditmas Park West, 127 houses just west of it, nearly half designed by the architect Arlington D. Isham, and Beverley Square West, 118 houses on three streets between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. A station on the front of one of these houses is an exterior change, and exterior changes in a historic district need a permit from the Commission.

What the Commission's own guidance says. Chapter 11 of the LPC Permit Guidebook, which covers health and safety installations, deals directly with intercoms. It says "Light fixtures and intercom panels, as well as doorbells, must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," that an intercom can also go on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, and that "A maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening." The design, size and finish "must be compatible with the architecture of the building," and the guidebook asks for installations that are neutral and discreet. When those criteria are met, "Staff can issue permits approving light fixtures and intercom panels," which means the application goes to staff rather than to a public hearing. The application needs the manufacturer's cut sheet for the intercom, and a like-for-like replacement of an existing intercom does not need elevation or large-scale drawings.

Cable and attachment. The same chapter sets the rules for the cable that feeds a station. Conduit is to be hidden where possible, "concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding" behind the installation; where that cannot be done, staff can approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit," painted to match and fixed into mortar joints where there are any. Equipment "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." On a frame house, the wall cladding is clapboard or shingle, and running the cable straight back through the siding into the wall cavity behind the station is usually the cleanest way to meet that rule.

Where that leaves a porch. Most porch doors here sit in a plain board casing with clapboard or shingle beside it. A slim station on the flat casing or the siding beside it, on the latch side, is plainly "adjacent to a door opening," and one station with up to three buttons is one intercom panel. We keep off carved brackets, turned columns, original leaded or beveled glass and decorative trim.

What we prepare. For a house inside one of the districts, we choose a station whose size and finish suit the house, mark the proposed spot on photos of the door, and give the owner the cut sheet, the photos and a note on the cable route for the application. The owner, or the architect or expediter already on the house, files with the Commission, and the exterior work is scheduled once the permit is issued. Indoor work, the cellar run and the stations inside, can often go first.

Houses that were just designated. If you are planning a new station, or moving an old one, on a house in Ditmas Park West or Beverley Square West, assume a permit is needed and leave time for it. If you are unsure whether something already on your porch needs attention, the Commission's staff can tell you; we do not guess on their behalf.

Frame Houses

Porch to Cellar to Attic: How Buzzer Cable Travels Through a Ditmas Park Frame House

In a Ditmas Park frame house, running thin low-voltage cable is often easy, but only when the route is planned from the cellar up rather than from the door in.

Through the wall behind the station. The station's cable leaves through a small hole behind the station itself, into the wall cavity between the siding and the interior wall. In a balloon-framed or early platform-framed house that cavity often runs down toward the cellar, and the cable can be dropped or fished down it. The hole is sealed around the cable, and the station's back plate covers it completely, so there is nothing to see from the porch and nothing for water to run into.

Under the porch or through its ceiling. Where the wall behind the door does not lead down cleanly, the cable can cross under the porch floor and enter the cellar through the foundation, or rise into the porch ceiling and enter the house at the second floor, after checking each route for water and for original woodwork that should not be opened.

Along the cellar ceiling and up. The power supply usually goes in the cellar, on a board on the wall and a circuit the owner controls. From there each floor's cable runs along the joists to a closet stack, a chase beside the chimney or the back stairs, and up to the second floor and the attic. In a divided house, the top flat's cable stays in common space or inside that flat, so neighbors do not have to give access for someone else's buzzer.

When the walls are closed. In a renovated house with drywall over every cavity, we use closets, baseboards or the back of a built-in, or phone answering for the top floor. Anything that has to be opened is named before the price is agreed.

Weather on a porch. A porch keeps off direct rain but not wind-driven rain or blown snow. Aiphone describes its GT door stations as designed to withstand rain and snow; on shingle or clapboard we also seal the top and sides of the station against the siding and leave the bottom free to drain.

Apartment Buildings

Vestibules, Mailboxes and Missing Packages: Getting the Lobby Right in Ditmas Park's Six-Storey Buildings

Bklyner's 2018 report from a building near Cortelyou Road and Stratford Road is worth reading by any owner or board on the apartment blocks. Residents kept a shared spreadsheet of lost packages, one of them told the reporter that "In the month of August, it exploded," and management installed a camera in the mail area only after residents pressed for it. Packages, the report said, were left "downstairs by the mailboxes or in the lobby." A resident of a building nearby said, "We do not have a camera and about 40% of the time the lock on the front door is broken." None of that is unusual for a big building, and most of it starts with the street door.

The door that has to lock. If the vestibule door does not latch behind every visitor, the mailboxes, packages and stairs behind it are open to anyone who walks in behind a resident. The usual causes are a closer out of adjustment, a strike bent by people forcing the door and a frame that has shifted, all mechanical repairs that come before any electronics.

Where the panel goes. With an outer street door and an inner lobby door, the panel sits in the vestibule and releases the inner door, so couriers reach the panel but not the lobby. If the mailboxes are in the vestibule too, carriers keep their postal-lock access; if they are inside the lobby, anyone buzzed in for one apartment can reach every mailbox, and the plan needs more thought.

Where packages go. A video panel lets a resident tell a courier to leave the parcel in the vestibule instead of buzzing them into the lobby; some panels can give a courier a code that opens only a locked package room; and a camera on the vestibule and mail area gives a record of who came and went. A building that wants a package room should decide before choosing the panel.

When the elevator is out. News 12 Brooklyn reported in May 2024 that residents of a Ditmas Park building were fed up with their only elevator having been out of service for a year. In a six-storey building, a broken elevator turns every visitor into a long walk down and back up for anyone who has to open the door by hand, and for older or disabled residents that walk may simply not be possible. A working release from every apartment, and answering by phone for residents who are out, are what let home aides, nurses and deliveries reach people on the upper floors while a long repair drags on.

The City's Rules

HPD, 311 and the Intercom Law: What a Ditmas Park Owner, Board or Tenant Should Know

Ditmas Park has both houses, which the intercom law does not reach, and multiple dwellings, which it does. Here is how the rules apply in plain terms; this is not legal advice.

Which buildings must have an intercom. Section 50-a of the Multiple Dwelling Law requires every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets residents talk to a visitor and release the door from their apartment. Older buildings with eight or more apartments must install the same doors and intercom if the tenants of a majority of apartments request it in writing; once they do, the work has to start within 90 days and finish within six months. Brick Underground's 2014 guide notes that in those older buildings the cost can be passed to tenants. Habitat Magazine's 2024 report adds that the city's housing maintenance code also requires a working intercom once one is installed. Most of the six-storey buildings on Ocean Avenue and the side streets are well over eight apartments, so in practice nearly all of them are expected to have a working system.

Houses divided into flats. A one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, so the intercom law does not reach it. A house with three or more independent households generally is a multiple dwelling, though the eight-apartment threshold in §50-a still means a three-family house is not required to have an intercom. A house divided without approval is a Buildings Department matter, and Community Board 14 has asked for more action against illegal conversions, so we install for the household count that legally lives in the house.

How a tenant reports a dead buzzer. NYC 311 files a broken or missing doorbell, buzzer or intercom as a maintenance complaint for an apartment or a public area, and asks tenants to try the owner, managing agent, super or board first. HPD then contacts the managing agent, warns that a violation may follow, calls the tenant back and sends an inspector if the problem remains. Brick Underground has reported waits of months for an inspector, so written, dated notice to the owner matters.

How long an owner has. HPD's Clear Violations page gives an owner 90 days to correct a Class A violation, 30 days for a Class B hazardous violation and 24 hours for most Class C immediately hazardous violations, with 14 days for Class C self-closing door violations, counted from service of the notice. The owner then certifies the correction, and HPD "may audit any certification of correction." Citywide, violations rose 24 percent in fiscal year 2024, to 895,457, according to City Limits.

Co-ops and condos. In a co-op the intercom is part of the building, and whether a new apartment station is billed to a shareholder is a question for the proprietary lease. In a small condo made from a divided house, the porch door and its station are usually common elements and the unit stations belong to each unit.

Locks on the way out. The door must always open freely from inside. An electric strike leaves the inside lever working; a magnetic lock needs an inside sensor and push button and must release on power loss or fire alarm under the building code, which is why we seldom use one on a residential street door.

Rules that apply to Ditmas Park business entry installations

  • Doors that are part of the way out of a business must open from the inside without a key or special knowledge; an electric strike keeps the inside lever working, and a magnetic lock needs a sensor, a push-to-exit button and release on fire alarm and power loss under the building code's rules for electrically locked egress doors.
  • Fire-rated doors, such as doors into a stair or a cellar, need hardware listed for fire doors; a fail-secure strike keeps the door latched for fire protection while the lever still lets people out.
  • Entry panels that the public uses are mounted with their controls within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range, no higher than 48 inches and no lower than 15 inches for an unobstructed forward reach.
  • We do low-voltage work only; a new 120-volt circuit for a power supply or a door operator is done by a licensed electrician, and the landlord's approval comes before any cable runs through common space.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Ditmas Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions Ditmas Park homeowners, owners of divided houses, landlords, boards, tenants and shop owners bring to us, along with ones raised in Bklyner's reporting, tenant reviews of local buildings, the Landmarks Commission's guidebook, HPD's and 311's guidance, and the buzzer guides published by Brick Underground, Habitat Magazine and ButterflyMX, answered for the houses and buildings here.

Houses, porches and divided homes

Our house was split into three condos, and the old bell still rings only in the ground-floor hall. What does it take to give the other two owners their own buttons?

A three-button door station at the porch door, a station inside each unit, and a release on the shared door that any of the three can trigger. The cable for the upper units runs through common space or inside their own units, usually from the cellar up a closet stack, so the ground-floor owner does not have to open their rooms for someone else's buzzer. Because the porch door and the cable in the common hall are usually common elements of the condo, and the stations inside belong to each unit, we can split the written price the same way so the owners can divide it as their declaration says.

The top-floor flat in our house is two flights above the porch. How does that household let a guest in without walking down?

The station in the top-floor flat has a release button. When a guest presses that household's button at the porch, the station rings upstairs, the household talks to the guest and presses release, and the strike in the porch door frame frees the latch for a few seconds so the guest can push the door open. If the flat has its own door at the top of the stairs, that door stays locked with its own key. If the household is often out, the same station can also ring their phones, so they can answer and release from wherever they are.

We turned our attic into a bedroom floor. Can a station go up there without running cable through the bedrooms below?

Usually, yes. In most Ditmas Park houses there is a vertical path that does not cross a finished room: a closet stack, the space beside the chimney, a laundry chute, or the wall beside the back stairs. The cable goes up that path from the cellar to the second floor and then a short distance into the attic. If the attic stairs are enclosed, the stairwell wall itself is often the route. Where no hidden path exists, a station that answers over the house's Wi-Fi, or a ringer and phone answering instead of a wired station, can cover the attic without any cable at all.

Our house is in Ditmas Park West, which was designated in November 2025. Do we now need a permit to swap our old doorbell for a video station?

Yes. Once a district is designated, a change to the front of a house in it needs a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and a new or different door station is a change to the front. The Commission's guidebook lets its staff approve an intercom panel when it sits within or beside the door opening, is the only panel at that door, and has a design, size and finish compatible with the house. We pick a station that fits those criteria, mark the spot on photos, and give you the cut sheet for the application, and the exterior work is scheduled once the permit is issued.

The wall beside our porch door is cedar shingle. Does a station there need special sealing to keep water out of the wall?

It needs the same care any fixture on shingles needs. Shingles are uneven, so the station's back plate sits on a small flat mounting block or on the flat door casing rather than straight on the shingle edges. The top and sides of the block are sealed so water running down the wall is carried around the station, the cable hole behind it is sealed, and the bottom is left open to drain. A deep porch keeps most rain off, but wind-driven rain and blown snow still reach the wall, so we do not skip any of this because the porch has a roof.

Six-storey buildings, tenants and the law

Our building's only elevator has been out for months. What should the intercom be able to do for residents on the top floors?

It should let every resident answer and release the door from their apartment, without exception, because the walk down and back up is exactly what the elevator outage has made impossible for some people. News 12 Brooklyn reported in May 2024 on a Ditmas Park building whose residents said their only elevator had been out for a year. In a building in that state, the priority is testing and repairing every apartment's station and release, adding louder ringers for residents who are hard of hearing, and giving residents who want it the option to answer on their phones, so aides, nurses and deliveries can reach the upper floors.

Couriers leave parcels on the vestibule floor by our mailboxes. Can the panel let a courier into a locked package room and nowhere else?

Yes, if the package room has its own lockable door with an electric release. Some panels can issue a code or a one-time access that opens only that door, so a courier can drop a parcel without being let into the lobby. The building decides who holds the codes and how they are changed. The package room's door, its release and its wiring have to be planned at the same time as the panel, because the panel then controls two doors instead of one. Bklyner's 2018 report from a building near Cortelyou Road and Stratford Road shows why it matters: residents logged more than 100 missing packages in six months, most of them left by the mailboxes or in the lobby.

The owner certified our intercom violation as corrected, but the panel still does not call our apartment. What can tenants do?

HPD's Clear Violations page says tenants can challenge a certification, which leads to an audit inspection, and that if the condition is still uncorrected, the violation stays open as a false certification, which can bring civil penalties. Keep dated notes and photos or video of the panel failing to reach your apartment, send them to the owner in writing, and contact HPD about the certification. From our side, a fault that affects one apartment but not its neighbors is usually that apartment's station or its pair in the riser, which is a repair, not a new system, once the owner authorizes it.

Tenant reviews of our building say the buzzers do not work, and prospective renters read them. Where should an owner start?

With a test of the whole system, apartment by apartment, so you know whether you have one fault or many. Reviews of a 185-apartment Ditmas Park building on Westminster Road say, among other things, that "Buzzers and intercoms don't work," which may mean anything from a dead lobby panel to a few silent apartments. A walkthrough tests the panel, the release, the door's closer and latch, the power supply and each riser pair, and the written report tells you exactly which apartments fail and why. Repairs can then be scheduled floor by floor, and the tested record is something you can show residents.

Our building has more than 150 apartments. How many days does a new entry system take, and does the front door stay locked the whole time?

Brick Underground's 2019 guide quoted one installer estimating about one day to replace a system in a five-apartment building and about two days for a 100-unit building, and a building larger than that takes longer, largely because of apartment access. The street door stays locked throughout: the panel is changed with the door's mechanical lock in place, residents use their keys while the release is down, and the new panel and strike are usually switched over in one working session. Apartment stations are changed floor by floor in two-hour access windows agreed with tenants in advance.

Shops and community spaces

Our shop is on Newkirk Plaza, which cars cannot enter. Where should our delivery buzzer go?

At whichever door the driver reaches first with a hand truck, which on the plaza is usually the shop's own front door on the walkway or a rear door off Marlborough Road, East 16th Street, Newkirk Avenue or Foster Avenue, depending on where the shop sits. We look at where drivers actually park and walk in, then put a weatherproof station at that door with the answering station at the counter or the back room. If the plaza door stays open during business hours, the buzzer only needs to work before opening and after closing, and it can be set to ring only then.

The apartments above our Cortelyou Road shop use a street door right next to ours, and customers keep ringing the apartment buttons. How do we make the two doors obvious?

Give the apartments' panel a clear label with the building number and "Apartments," mount it on the apartments' side of the doorway rather than between the two doors, and make the shop entrance obvious with its own sign and, if you take deliveries at the front, its own button clearly marked for the shop. A panel with a small lit nameplate helps at night. The two systems should stay separate, so the shop cannot release the apartments' door and the apartments do not ring the shop.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Ditmas Park

These claims turn up in generic online answers, in national cost guides such as HomeAdvisor and Angi, and on manufacturers' and vendors' blogs. Each is quoted or summarized, then checked against Ditmas Park's houses and buildings, the makers' own guidance and the city's rules.

"Wireless systems are the best option if you want to DIY"

HomeAdvisor's intercom cost guide says exactly this, and it prices wireless systems at $150 to $1,500 against $1,000 to $15,000 for hard-wired ones.

For a one-family Ditmas Park house, a wireless doorbell may be fine. For a house divided into three flats, a kit that rings one phone gives neither a button per household nor a release on the shared porch door. In a six-storey brick building, radio between floors is unreliable through masonry, and the law expects every apartment to talk to the visitor and release the door.

Wireless is not wire-free either. ButterflyMX's buzzer guide notes that "a wireless system isn't free of wiring," because the station still needs power and the release still needs a connection to the strike, and on a shared entrance that is not a DIY job.

"An old house means opening walls to run new cable"

Many cost guides warn that wiring an older home means opening walls and patching plaster, and they price that in.

Ditmas Park's freestanding frame houses are, for low-voltage cable, among the easier old houses in Brooklyn. Open cellar joists, attics, closet stacks and wood-framed wall cavities usually let a quarter-inch cable reach every floor without touching a finished wall.

Walls usually have to be opened only where a renovation closed every cavity and finished the cellar ceiling, and then closets, baseboards or phone answering often avoid it. Anything that must be opened is named in the written price first.

"Replacing a small building's intercom costs about $1,500 to $2,500"

ButterflyMX's guide to replacing a small building's buzzer cites "roughly $1,500 to $2,500," and Swiftlane cites "roughly $2,200 to $5,000 or more for a small single-entrance project."

Both guides call these planning references, and ButterflyMX says, "There is no reliable universal price." For a Ditmas Park walk-up or six-storey building, the number depends on the street door, the riser test, the apartment count and the choice of monitors or phone answering. Our Brooklyn list starts multi-unit work at about $2,500 and runs past $10,000 for large buildings.

Divided-house owners often make the opposite mistake and expect a building price for a house job; a two- or three-button station with stations in each flat usually falls in our $400 to $900 audio or $700 to $1,800 video range.

"Landmark rules don't cover small things like doorbells"

Generic answers often say that minor fixtures such as doorbells and intercom buttons do not need approval in historic districts.

The Commission's own guidebook regulates intercom panels and doorbells, with rules for where they go, how many one door can have and how their cable is hidden, and its staff issue permits when those criteria are met. With three districts now covering several hundred houses here, that applies to a large share of the neighborhood's porches.

The good news is that a compatible station at the door usually qualifies for a staff-level permit, without a public hearing. The mistake is mounting first and asking later, which can mean taking the station down again.

"Software subscriptions add $5 to $15 per unit per month"

Swiftlane's cost guide gives that range for cloud intercoms, or $30 to $100 per door per month.

That is true of many networked panels, and across 100 or more apartments on Ocean Avenue a per-unit fee adds up fast. It is not true of every system: a wired two-wire system with wall stations has no monthly fee, and several systems add phone answering without charging per apartment.

Boards and owners should ask, before choosing, what the monthly cost is, who pays it, what happens to the door if it stops being paid, and who holds the admin logins. We put those answers in writing with the quote.

"A magnetic lock is the simple way to secure the door"

Vendor blogs often present magnetic locks as an easy, strong option for entry doors.

Allegion's guide notes that "Electromagnetic locks are only available fail safe," meaning they unlock when power is lost, and the building code's rules for electrically locked egress doors add an inside sensor, a push-to-exit button and release on fire alarm or power loss. On a residential street door, an electric strike does the job with the inside lever working normally.

For Ditmas Park's wooden porch doors, steel and wooden lobby doors and aluminum storefront doors, a strike matched to the frame is almost always the right answer. We use magnetic locks only where a door truly cannot take a strike, and then with everything the code asks for.

"Only buildings with eight or more apartments need to worry about entry systems"

This is a fair summary of the Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom rule, and some answers stop there.

The rule is real, but a three-family Ditmas Park house is still three households sharing one porch door, and the practical reasons for a working buzzer, letting guests in from the top floor, knowing who is there, not propping the door, are the same at three households as at thirty.

And for buildings that are covered, the eight-apartment line is only the start. A working intercom also has to keep working, and the city's housing maintenance code, HPD's violation deadlines and, in a co-op, the board's own legal obligation all follow from that.

Ditmas Park Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

What should we have ready before asking for a door buzzer quote on a Ditmas Park house or building?
+

The address, how many households or apartments use the door, every entrance to cover, what is there now and what fails where, the kind of lock, whether there is internet near the panel, and whether monthly fees are acceptable. ButterflyMX's small-building guide suggests a similar list. Photos of the door, latch, old panel and power supply let us give a range before the walkthrough.

Our porch already has a security camera. Does the new door station still need a camera of its own?
+

Not necessarily. A porch camera records what happens, but it does not let the person inside see a visitor while talking to them. If your camera shows the porch door clearly and you watch it live on your phone, an audio station may be enough; if the upstairs household wants a picture on a monitor without an app, a video station is simpler.

Can the hole left by our old porch bell button in the clapboard be filled once the new station is up?
+

Yes. The old button comes out, its wire is disconnected and made safe, and the hole is plugged with wood and sealed so water cannot get behind the clapboard, ready for your painter. If the old button sat in the right spot, the new station can often cover its hole and avoid a new opening.

If a burglar gets in through our building's broken street door, does renter's insurance still pay for what is taken?
+

Brick Underground's guide to broken buzzers quoted an insurance broker saying standard renter's insurance generally covers items stolen in a burglary even when the building door was left open. Policies differ, so check yours, and report the broken door to the owner in writing either way.

Can a landlord be held responsible if a crime happens because the buzzer left the door unlocked?
+

Brick Underground, citing the New York Attorney General's tenants' rights guide, reported that a landlord could be held liable if a crime occurs because a broken buzzer left the building door unlocked. Whether that applies to a given case is a question for a lawyer; for owners, it means a door that does not latch is not a repair to put off.

Why does the buzzer in my apartment buzz without stopping?
+

ButterflyMX's guidance says continuous buzzing may point to a stuck button or another entry-panel problem, and in older buildings a short in the cable can do the same. If every apartment hears it, the fault is likely at the panel or shared equipment; if only yours does, it is likely your station or its wires. Report it to the owner or super rather than opening the station.

Do we need an architect to get a Landmarks permit for a door station on our Ditmas Park house?
+

Not always. The Commission's guidebook says staff can approve an intercom panel that meets its criteria, and the application needs the manufacturer's cut sheet, with photos of the door and the proposed spot; a like-for-like replacement does not need elevation drawings. Many owners file that themselves. If you already have an architect or expediter working on the house, we give them the station details and photos so it goes in with the rest of the work.

Can the porch door stay unlatched a few seconds longer for an older parent who takes a while to reach it?
+

The length of time the release holds the latch open can usually be set on the station or the power supply, typically from a few seconds to longer. That helps a visitor who is slow to push the door, such as someone with a walker or a stroller. The door should still close and latch on its own once they are through, so we set the release time and the closer together and test both with the person who needs them.

+

Often, yes. If the shed or studio has power and the house Wi-Fi reaches it, phone answering lets you answer and release the porch door from there with no new cable. For a fixed station, cable runs from the cellar to the outbuilding, ideally alongside an existing buried line.
