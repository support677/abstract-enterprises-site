The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need Arlo Camera Installation

New York is a city where most people do not own the walls they live behind, and Arlo is built for exactly that situation. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 Housing Supply Report puts rental units at 68.1 percent of the city's housing stock, using the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, and one housing market analysis puts the Bronx's renter share at 79.7 percent, the highest of the five boroughs. Add the co-op shareholders who own shares rather than walls and answer to a board, and you have millions of New Yorkers who cannot simply drill a hole and run a cable. Connextivity's guide to NYC apartment camera law makes the point directly: Wi-Fi cameras that need no drilling and no permanent wiring generally raise no issue with a landlord or a board, and the living room and bedroom are the resident's call. That is the space Arlo's battery cameras and wire-free doorbells were designed for, and it is why a Manhattan renovation firm, KS Renovation Group, names the Arlo Essential Wire-Free Video Doorbell as one of its two most-installed doorbells for co-op apartments, alongside a battery model from Ring, precisely because no electrical work is required.

The trouble is that a camera designed to install in minutes on an American suburban porch meets some very old, very solid buildings here. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council reports that more than 80 percent of the city's housing was built more than 50 years ago, and that over half of all units, about two million of them, went up before 1947. One analysis of city records puts the median year built at 1931 citywide, 1910 in Manhattan, 1925 in Brooklyn, 1935 in Queens and the Bronx and 1970 on Staten Island. Building the Skyline notes that New York built more than 740,000 housing units in the 1920s alone. Those buildings were made of brick, stone and plaster, and one prewar guide describes walls of 12 to 18 inches of masonry. Arlo's setup guides say that almost every Arlo camera needs a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi connection to set up, and 2.4 GHz signal does not travel well through a foot of brick. An NYC installer, Seneca Security, warns that brownstone masonry is dense enough to significantly degrade Wi-Fi between floors and between the rear yard and the front of the building, and our own Brooklyn wireless notes record backyards and basement units routinely reading around -85 dBm, which is too weak for any wireless camera to hold a connection. The camera that streamed perfectly from the kitchen counter during setup is often the one that drops offline the week after it is mounted on the back fence.

Then there is the question of who gets to say yes. Guzov LLC, a co-op and condo law firm, writes that New York City has no law prohibiting residents from installing doorbell cameras in apartment building hallways, but that a co-op's own rules may prohibit a shareholder from placing a video doorbell in a common area. Brick Underground's co-op lawyer column goes further: a board could pass a resolution banning video doorbells as a new house rule, with no shareholder vote required, and the complaint discussed in that column arose because a shareholder pointed a camera directly at a board member's apartment. KS Renovation Group adds that most co-op proprietary leases classify door-mounted devices and any wiring work as alterations requiring board approval, even battery-powered doorbells that face shared hallways. So the very feature that makes Arlo attractive, that it sticks to a door without a wire, does not by itself make it allowed. In this city, the first step of an Arlo install is often a conversation with a managing agent, not a drill.

What pushes people to install anyway is usually the lobby. Security.org's annual package-theft report found apartment residents were more than three times as likely as other households to have had a package stolen in the previous three months, 10 percent against 3 percent, and noted that apartment dwellers may not be allowed to put doorbell or security cameras outside their units. CBS New York has reported a man apparently buzzed into a Brooklyn building on Lexington Avenue and then seen taking a package and what appeared to be a suitcase from the hallway, thieves in a Park Slope lobby cutting open packages and taking things from them on the spot, and a thief caught on video strolling up the steps of a Crown Heights home near Montgomery Street and New York Avenue. A Brooklyn resident profiled by Delivery Amigo logged more than 50 porch-pirate and trespassing cases within two years. A property manager told Brick Underground that his team updated front door locks, installed cameras, and added security gates and package lockers in lobbies, and that none of it had completely stopped theft. The New York State Attorney General's 2024 package-theft alert recommends that New Yorkers use a doorbell camera or video surveillance system to deter thieves or help identify them. For a renter on the fourth floor of a walk-up, a camera is not a gadget; it is the one piece of building security they can control.

The city also has the country's busiest landmarks regulator looking at facades. Wikipedia's entry on the Landmarks Preservation Commission counts 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, and the city's 2025 Mayor's Management Report credits the commission with 157 historic districts and extensions. In 2018 the commission amended its rules to cover objects commonly used for security, including security cameras, lighting and intercom panels, and those rules prohibit installing many such objects on a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative. The LPC Permit Guidebook has a whole section, in Chapter 11, titled "Installing Security Cameras," under Rules Section 2-20. NoHo BID guidance for landmarked storefronts notes that an LPC permit comes before any Buildings Department permit, and that no LPC permit is required for fixtures that do not physically attach to any part of the building. For an Arlo owner on a landmarked block, that distinction between a mount screwed into a brownstone and a camera that sits behind the glass or on furniture is not academic. It decides whether you need a permit.

Finally, the law around recording is stricter than many quick answers suggest. Connextivity notes that a camera installed covertly inside a unit can support a charge under Penal Law 250.45, a Class E felony. Audio is murkier: RecordingLaw reads New York's Penal Law 250.00 as a one-party consent rule for cameras that record sound, Connextivity calls passive hallway audio unsettled, and other installer pages in this city describe the rule differently. In rent-stabilized buildings, Seneca Security notes that courts and the state housing agency take seriously any landlord action that could look like harassment of a stabilized tenant, and cameras pointed at a tenant's door can become part of that story. In public housing, AOL reported in 2012 that a resident of Parkside Houses in the Bronx received an eviction notice saying she had breached her lease by installing her own cameras. A participatory budgeting proposal for Gowanus Houses in Brooklyn asks for cameras in elevators and near apartment doors, which shows residents want them, but the building's rules still decide who installs what.

We want to be clear about the gaps in our research too. We could not capture Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches, Bing or DuckDuckGo results, or AnswerThePublic data for Arlo installation in New York City from our build environment, and our searches returned no Reddit threads on the subject. We found no city dataset of HPD violations, 311 complaints or Buildings Department permits tied to consumer camera installs. Two claims that circulate on vendor blogs, that recent Real Property Law amendments require landlords to let tenants install cameras and that a city Local Law 50 requires camera notices in common areas, could not be confirmed against the statute or the city's legal text, so this page does not rely on either. Where this page describes what we find inside walls, it is drawing on the age and type of the buildings in the public record and on what we see in buildings of that age generally, not on a survey of your building.

New York City facts that shape an Arlo install

  • Mostly renters: rental units are 68.1 percent of the city's housing stock in the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, and the Bronx's renter share is reported at 79.7 percent.
  • Old, heavy walls: more than 80 percent of housing is over 50 years old and about two million units predate 1947; the median Manhattan building dates to 1910.
  • Small buildings are common: in one listings sample, 47.7 percent of prewar buildings had fewer than 10 apartments and only 41.5 percent listed an elevator, so many Arlo jobs are in walk-ups.
  • Where the homes are: CHPC reports half of the city's housing is in Brooklyn and Queens, Manhattan holds about a quarter of the units on 7.5 percent of the land, and Staten Island holds about five percent.
  • Landmarks everywhere: 1,460 individual landmarks and 157 historic districts and extensions, with LPC rules that restrict cameras on primary facades.
  • Arlo needs 2.4 GHz: almost every Arlo camera sets up on 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, and the Arlo Pro 3, Ultra and Ultra 2 need a SmartHub or Base Station.

Manhattan: a lot of radios in a small space

Manhattan's problem is not only old walls; it is the number of routers within reach of your apartment. In a walk-up or an elevator building with dozens of units per floor line, every apartment broadcasts its own 2.4 GHz network, and our own wireless camera notes for the city list dense Wi-Fi environments among the main things that reshape a wireless install here. Arlo cameras need that crowded band. When a battery camera has to shout through a masonry wall over the noise of thirty neighbors, it uses more battery and drops more connections. In Manhattan we spend more time than anywhere else on channel selection and on where the router or the SmartHub sits, and less time on brackets. The other constant is paperwork. Doorman buildings, condos and co-ops often want a certificate of insurance and a scheduled time before a contractor goes upstairs, and our Manhattan doorbell notes describe managing agents asking for it 48 hours ahead. We send that early so the camera does not wait in the box at the front desk.

Brooklyn: stoops, garden floors and back yards

Brooklyn's typical Arlo job is a brownstone or rowhouse: a parlor-floor door at the top of a stoop, a garden-level entrance under it, and a rear yard behind a wall of brick and an extension or two. The camera that matters most is usually the one at the back or the one at the garden door, and those are exactly the spots where the signal from a router on the parlor floor arrives weakest. In these houses we often move the router or the SmartHub rather than the camera, or add an access point closer to the yard with a properly run cable. Brooklyn is also where many of the package-theft stories in our research came from: a Lexington Avenue hallway, a Park Slope lobby, a Crown Heights stoop near Montgomery Street and New York Avenue. A doorbell or a camera covering the stoop and the vestibule door is the common first request, and it usually needs a decision about whether the camera's view stops at the property line or takes in the sidewalk.

Queens: houses, garden apartments and longer distances

Queens mixes detached and attached houses with garden apartments and co-op buildings, and its median building dates to about 1935. In houses, the issue is distance more than density: a router in the front room and a driveway or side gate forty feet away through two walls. A SmartHub placed centrally, or a camera that connects to the router directly with a good signal, solves most of it. In the co-op buildings, the same board rules apply as in Manhattan, and a shareholder should get the house rules in writing before anything goes on a hallway door.

The Bronx: renters first

With the highest renter share in the city and many large prewar buildings, Bronx Arlo jobs are more often inside the unit than outside it. A camera facing the apartment door from inside, a peephole-style camera where the building allows it, or a battery camera on a shelf rather than a screwed bracket keeps the tenant inside the line that Connextivity describes, where no drilling and no permanent wiring generally means no issue with the landlord. We are careful in the Bronx, as everywhere, with buildings run by NYCHA, where the AOL report from Parkside Houses shows that the housing authority treats unapproved cameras as a lease issue. We will not mount anything in a common area of a public housing building without written approval.

Staten Island: closest to the box instructions

Staten Island's median building dates to 1970, and much of its housing is one- and two-family houses, more of them wood-framed and owner-occupied. That makes it the borough where an Arlo install looks most like the one on the packaging: the owner approves the work, wood-frame walls pass more signal than brick, and yards and driveways get enough sun for a solar panel. The work there is mostly about coverage, angles and making sure the doorbell transformer is right, and less about boards and walls.

Before You Book

Lease, Router, Sunlight: What to Check in Your NYC Home Before an Arlo Visit

Most of the time lost on an Arlo job is lost before the first camera goes up: a hallway door the board has not approved, a router locked by the internet provider so its 2.4 GHz band cannot be changed, a SmartHub with nowhere to plug in near the router, or a solar panel planned for a yard that never sees direct sun. A few minutes with your phone prevents nearly all of it. Please do not take down a camera the building has objected to or open any walls yourself; just look, check and send us what you find.

Look at the view from the camera, not just the spot

Stand where the camera would go and look at what it would see. In an apartment hallway, Connextivity's rule of thumb is that a camera should cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as their doors open. On a stoop, decide whether you want the sidewalk in the picture or only your steps. In a back yard, think about the neighbor's windows across the fence. A camera that sees too much is the one that produces a complaint, and in a co-op that complaint goes to a board that may already be thinking about a house rule. Taking a photo from the exact height and angle the camera would sit at, roughly six to seven feet, the height SafeWise suggests for Arlo cameras, tells us more than any floor plan.

Find out what your internet provider controls

Many New York apartments run on a router supplied and managed by the internet provider. Some of those combine the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands under one name, some let you split them, and some lock the settings entirely. Since almost every Arlo camera needs 2.4 GHz to set up, it helps to know before the visit whether you can log in to the router, and whether you are allowed to add your own access point or mesh unit. If you cannot, we plan around it, usually with a SmartHub for the models that use one, but knowing in advance saves an hour on the phone with the provider while we stand in your kitchen.

Decide about recording before we arrive

CCTVinfo notes that Arlo cameras sold in recent years ship with no free cloud storage, so you will need either local storage or an Arlo Secure plan to keep recordings. Arlo's own support article says a subscription is not required to use local storage, which saves automatically triggered recordings to a USB device connected to the SmartHub. If you already know you want local storage, buy or bring a suitable USB drive and we set it up during the visit. If you want the cloud plan, have your Arlo account login ready. Either way, we will show you how to find a clip before we leave, because the time you need it is usually the morning after something has happened.

Building Specialists

Arlo Camera Installation for NYC's Architecture

The five boroughs hold almost every kind of home there is, and each changes three things about an Arlo job: what the camera is fixed to, how the signal gets from the router to the camera, and who has to agree before anything goes up. These are the building types the public record and real estate guides describe, and how we handle each one.

Prewar Walk-Up Tenements

Four to six stories, built before 1929 under the earlier multiple-dwelling laws. Masonry party walls, narrow stairs, no elevator, and a landlord or co-op board who decides whether the hallway side of a door can hold a doorbell.

Prewar Elevator Apartment Houses

Eight to twelve stories from the 1910s and 1920s. Long corridors, thick walls, a super who knows the building, and Wi-Fi that has to reach from a unit's router to a door at the end of a hall.

Prewar Doorman Buildings and Co-ops

The larger buildings of the 1920s and 1930s. Managing agents, house rules, alteration agreements, certificates of insurance and scheduled hours before any contractor goes upstairs.

Brownstones and Rowhouses

From the early 1860s rowhouses at 208–218 East 78th Street onward. Stoops, garden-level doors, rear yards behind brick, and in historic districts, LPC rules on what goes on the street facade.

HDFC and Small Co-ops

The city comptroller describes HDFC co-ops as largely prewar walk-up buildings. Small boards, shareholders who know each other, and rules that are sometimes unwritten until someone puts up a camera.

Houses and Newer Buildings

Staten Island and outer-borough houses with owners who approve their own work, and the small share of buildings built after 2010, which one housing report puts at about five percent of the stock.

On prewar plaster and masonry. Our own notes on New York wireless installs list prewar brick and plaster over metal lath among the conditions that reshape the job. Plaster over metal lath is hard to anchor into and blocks signal more than drywall does, because the metal mesh behind it acts like a screen. Brick takes masonry anchors, set into the face of the brick rather than the mortar joints wherever the mount allows, because the joints hold less and crumble sooner. Most Arlo cameras are light, so the fixing problem is small; the bigger problem is that the same walls that hold an anchor well also stop a 2.4 GHz signal. That is why, in a prewar building, we measure signal before we choose the wall, not after.

On brownstones in historic districts. Brooklyn Heights became the city's first historic district on November 23, 1965, and the Greenwich Village Historic District, designated in 1969, is the largest by number of buildings, with nearly 2,200. On walk-ups without elevators. The RentReboot listings sample found that only 41.5 percent of prewar buildings listed an elevator. Where a battery camera fits in a shop, and where it does not

A battery Arlo camera records when motion triggers it and sends clips over Wi-Fi. That suits a quiet stockroom door, a back entrance after hours, a register area in a small shop, or a package shelf in a building lobby. It suits less well a busy sales floor where motion is constant, because every customer triggers it, the battery drains faster, and the clips pile up. Arlo's local storage on a SmartHub, as Arlo's knowledge base describes it, saves only automatically triggered recordings, so a shop that wants continuous footage for insurance claims needs a different design. We walk the space with the owner and tell you which cameras make sense as Arlo and which do not. The Slickdeals post in our research, where an owner reported that an Arlo pointed at a car recorded nothing when the car was broken into, is the kind of outcome we plan around: angle, distance and motion settings decide whether a battery camera wakes up in time.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • Storefront grilles must stay see-through. NYC Council's June 2026 materials state that from July 1, 2026, all existing and new security grilles on buildings in Occupancy Groups B (Business) and M (Mercantile) must have at least 70 percent visibility from the sidewalk. A camera behind the glass now has to be planned around a mostly open grille, which helps sightlines but also exposes the camera to view.
  • Cameras behind glass and motion. A user complaint in our research reports that an Arlo camera could not detect motion through a glass window. We test a camera's motion detection through your specific storefront glass before we promise coverage of the sidewalk.
  • Landmarked facades. LPC Rules Section 2-20 restricts cameras on primary facades. Inside the glass or on a freestanding fixture is usually the path for a landmarked storefront; we check designation before quoting.
  • Sharing with the NYPD. NYC 311 says any business with a security camera can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies and register camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Registration is voluntary; a state bill to make a registry mandatory has been introduced but we found no record that it passed.
  • Audio stays off by default. Because sources disagree on how New York treats passive audio recording, and recording staff or customers' conversations carries its own risk, we leave Arlo microphones off in business spaces unless the owner has taken advice and asks for them.
  • Cabling in commercial ceilings. Where a shop needs an access point cabled through a ceiling or wall to carry Arlo traffic, a vendor source notes that commercial cabling through fire-rated walls or ceilings may need a permit. Field Notes

Field Notes: Why We Walk the Signal Before We Open the Box

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's apartment unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow on a typical New York Arlo job: a prewar apartment or a brownstone, a router in a closet or on a shelf, and a box of cameras that worked perfectly on the kitchen table the night before.

We leave the cameras in the box at first. We ask to see the router, read its label, and check whether we can reach its settings or whether the provider controls them. Then we walk the route each camera's signal will have to travel, with a phone showing signal strength, stopping at the apartment door, the hallway, the stoop, the garden door and the far fence of the yard. We count the neighboring networks the phone can see, because in a dense building that number tells us how crowded the 2.4 GHz band is. If a model needs a SmartHub, we find where it can sit near the router and still reach the cameras. We hold a camera at each spot at about the height it will hang and look at what it sees, including the neighbor's door and the neighbor's windows. Only when the signal is good enough, the view is fair and the building has said yes does the first mount go up. That walk takes a few minutes, and it is the difference between a system that stays online and one that sends you offline alerts for a month.

Your Questions Answered

Arlo Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

Co-op and condo boards

My co-op says an Arlo doorbell on the hallway side of my door needs board approval. Is that allowed if it is only battery-powered?

In most co-ops, yes, the board can require it. Guzov LLC notes that New York City has no law banning doorbell cameras in apartment hallways, but that a co-op's own rules can prohibit a shareholder from placing one in a common area, and the hallway is a common area. KS Renovation Group reports that most proprietary leases treat door-mounted devices as alterations needing board approval, even battery models facing shared halls. Being wire-free removes the electrical question; it does not remove the board's control of the hallway. Ask the managing agent for the house rules and the alteration process in writing, and send us a copy. We can supply a certificate of insurance and a short description of the device and its view, which often makes approval simpler because the board can see the camera will not look into anyone's apartment.

The board turned down the outside mount. What Arlo setup can still watch my door?

The usual fallback is to move the camera inside your own apartment. An Arlo camera placed inside, facing the door, shows anyone who opens it or comes in, and it sits entirely in your space. Our Manhattan doorbell notes describe the same fallback for denied exterior mounts: an interior-of-door or peephole-style camera, which needs no approval in most buildings. A peephole-style camera is not an Arlo product, so if you specifically want Arlo, the inside placement is the choice; if you want to see the hallway before opening the door, a peephole camera that replaces the existing viewer without altering the door tends to raise the fewest questions, as Connextivity notes. We can set up either and leave the hallway exactly as the board wants it.

Our board passed a new rule after my doorbell went up. Can it make me remove it?

Very possibly. Brick Underground's co-op lawyer column explains that a board could ban video doorbells through a new house rule without any shareholder vote. KS Renovation Group's FAQ asks the same question, which tells you it happens. Whether a rule applies to a camera installed earlier depends on how the rule is written and on your proprietary lease, and that is a question for your own lawyer, not for an installer. What we can do is remove the doorbell cleanly, patch any marks from its mount, and set up a replacement inside your apartment so you keep coverage of the door. If the dispute is about what the camera sees rather than the device itself, re-aiming it so it no longer looks at a neighbor's door, as in the board member complaint Brick Underground described, sometimes settles it.

Renting

I rent. Can I set up Arlo cameras inside my apartment without asking the landlord?

Inside your own apartment, generally yes. Connextivity's guide says Wi-Fi cameras that need no drilling and no permanent wiring generally raise no issue with a landlord or a board, and that living rooms and bedrooms are the resident's call. Imperra Security, an NYC installer, writes that tenants can install cameras inside their own apartment without landlord permission but generally cannot put them in common hallways, lobbies or shared spaces without it. That makes Arlo a good fit for renters: a battery camera on a shelf or a removable mount, plugged into nothing and screwed into nothing. Where we would need to drill, run a cable or fix anything to the door, we check your lease first. Two rules stay firm: no camera aimed where another person has an expectation of privacy, and none hidden in a shared space.

Could my landlord or building manager watch what my Arlo cameras record?

Not unless you give them access. Arlo cameras send their video to your Arlo account, or to a USB drive on your own SmartHub, and only people you invite in the app can see it. Your landlord's own cameras in the hallways and lobby are a separate system that they control. The point where things can blur is the network: if your apartment's internet comes through a building-managed router, the building can see that devices are connected but not watch your video. We set up your Arlo account under your name, add only the users you choose, and show you where to check and remove access. If you share the apartment, we can set different permissions so a roommate sees the front door but not the camera in your room.

Instead of a doorbell, can a regular Arlo camera go outside my apartment door?

It can, if the building allows it, and the same rules apply as for a doorbell. Connextivity notes that a camera on a hallway wall sits in a common area, so it typically needs building approval in a co-op or condo, and JustAnswer's attorney reply to a Brooklyn renter said that a landlord can remove a camera installed in common areas without consent. If approval comes through, a camera above the door frame usually gives a better view of the corridor than a doorbell at chest height, and it can be angled so it covers corridor traffic without seeing into other apartments as their doors open. Battery placement means no wiring in the hall. We mount it with fixings the building accepts, often removable ones, so it comes down cleanly when you move.

Privacy and the law

My Arlo doorbell catches part of the neighbor's door across the hall. Is that against the law?

Capturing a hallway that both of you use is generally not illegal in itself. Connextivity's guidance is that hallway cameras are acceptable when positioned to cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open, and an attorney's answer on Justia says video in common areas is legal as long as the camera is not aimed to look into an apartment when its door is open. The line that matters is the inside of someone's home: Penal Law 250.45 makes covert recording where a person expects privacy a Class E felony. The practical risk is less a prosecution than a complaint to the board. We re-aim the doorbell, set motion zones so the neighbor's doorway does not trigger recordings, and where Arlo's settings allow, mask the area so the view stays on your side.

Should the microphone on my Arlo be switched on in a shared hallway or a shop?

Our default is off in shared spaces. RecordingLaw reads Penal Law 250.00 as setting a one-party consent rule for cameras that record sound, but Connextivity describes passive hallway audio as unsettled, and other installer pages in this city describe the rule differently. When the sources disagree, the cautious choice is to leave audio recording off in hallways, lobbies and stores, where you are not part of the conversations the camera would pick up. Inside your own home, the decision is yours. Two-way talk on a doorbell, where you are speaking with the visitor, is a different situation from a camera silently recording neighbors. If audio matters to you for a business or a building, take advice from a lawyer first, and we will configure the cameras to match what they tell you.

Permits, licenses and landmarks

Is a permit needed to put up Arlo cameras in New York City?

For a typical battery Arlo install in a home, our own notes and the sources we reviewed say no permit is generally needed. The exceptions are worth knowing. On a landmarked building or in a historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules apply to cameras on facades, and LPC permits come before any Buildings Department permit; the NoHo BID guidance notes that fixtures which do not physically attach to the building do not need an LPC permit. In commercial spaces, cabling through fire-rated walls or ceilings may need a permit. Inside co-ops and condos, the board's alteration process can work like a private permit. None of the sources we found addressed whether a pure battery, no-wire Arlo install falls under the city's low-voltage rules, so for anything beyond a simple home install we check before we quote.

Does the person installing my Arlo need a New York State license?

If they are installing security cameras as a business, the state says yes. The NYC Business portal explains that a license from the New York State Department of State is required to install, service or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and a hiring guide cites General Business Law Article 6-D. The licensing course provider says that license covers security cameras and CCTV, and that unlicensed installers can be fined. Homeowners installing their own Arlo cameras do not need a license; the license is about doing it for pay. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431. When you hire anyone, ask for the license number and check it, and ask for a certificate of insurance if your building needs one. Some sources also discuss city electrician licensing for certain low-voltage work; those rules have been changing, and we follow them on wired jobs.

Our brownstone is in a historic district. Can an Arlo camera go on the front of the house?

Often not on the street facade without LPC approval. The commission's 2018 rules prohibit many security objects, cameras included, on a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, and its Permit Guidebook has a section on installing security cameras under Rules Section 2-20. The guidebook's examples favor small cameras finished to match the facade, which most Arlo cameras are not. The practical options are a camera inside the vestibule or behind the parlor window, a mount on the rear or a secondary facade, or a freestanding fixture that does not attach to the building, which the NoHo BID guidance says does not need an LPC permit. We check your building's designation first, and if you want a camera on the facade itself, we tell you that an LPC application is the route.

Wi-Fi, walls and hubs

Will an Arlo camera hold its Wi-Fi through the thick masonry of a prewar building?

Sometimes, but you cannot assume it. Prewar walls of 12 to 18 inches of masonry, plaster over metal lath, and dozens of neighboring routers all weaken the 2.4 GHz signal Arlo cameras rely on, and our Brooklyn notes record rear yards and basement units around -85 dBm, too weak for a wireless camera. The fixes, in order of how often we use them, are moving the router or SmartHub to a more central spot, changing the 2.4 GHz channel to a less crowded one, adding an access point on the far side of the wall with a properly run cable, and as a last resort moving the camera. We measure signal at each spot before mounting, so you know before the camera goes up whether it will stay connected.

Which Arlo cameras need a SmartHub or Base Station, and where does it go in an apartment?

Arlo's knowledge base says the Arlo Pro 3, Arlo Ultra and Arlo Ultra 2 require a SmartHub or Base Station, and that cameras should be within three to six feet of it during setup. Many newer models connect to the router directly, but a hub can still help, because it is where local USB storage lives. In an apartment, the hub sits beside the router and connects to it with a cable, so its location is usually decided by where the internet comes in. In a brownstone or a house, a router in a front room and cameras at the back can mean the hub belongs somewhere more central, with a cable run to reach it. We pair each camera near the hub as Arlo instructs, then move it to its mounting spot and confirm it still reports in.

Recording and storage

Can my Arlo cameras save recordings without paying for an Arlo subscription?

Yes, with local storage, within limits. Arlo's knowledge base says an Arlo Secure subscription is not required to use local storage, and that only automatically triggered recordings are saved to the USB device connected to the SmartHub or Base Station. CCTVinfo notes that recent Arlo cameras ship with no free cloud storage, so without either a plan or local storage, you can watch live but not keep clips. Local storage means a USB drive on the hub, kept somewhere safe inside the home; a thief who takes the camera does not take the recording. It does not give you continuous recording, and some app features depend on the subscription. We set up whichever you choose, and we show you how to pull a clip off the drive or out of the app before we leave.

Placement

Can I set an Arlo camera inside the window so it watches the street without going outside?

You can, but expect motion detection to suffer. A user complaint in our research reports that an Arlo camera could not detect motion behind a glass window, which matches what we see: the motion sensing many battery cameras use to wake up does not work well through glass, and night vision can reflect off the pane. For a renter or a landmarked facade, a camera inside the glass is still often the only option, so we test it through your actual window before relying on it, angle it to reduce reflections, and adjust sensitivity. If it will not trigger reliably, we tell you, and we look at whether a camera in the vestibule or on a secondary wall would give you real alerts instead of a view that only records when something else wakes it.

Will an Arlo solar panel keep a camera charged in a shaded Brooklyn or Manhattan back yard?

Only if the panel gets regular direct sun. Arlo's own camera pages say to confirm regular direct sunlight and avoid deep shade, covered porches or eaves that block the panel. Many New York yards are narrow canyons between four- and five-story buildings, under fire escapes or trees, where direct sun reaches the ground for a short part of the day. The panel does not have to sit right next to the camera; on a short cable it can go higher, on a fence top or a rear wall that catches more light. We look at which way the yard faces and where the sun lands at midday before suggesting solar. If no spot gets enough, a regular recharging schedule for the battery is the honest answer.

Fixing what is already up

I set up my own Arlo system and half the cameras keep dropping. Can you fix it rather than start over?

Usually, yes, and we would rather. Most self-installed Arlo systems we see in this city have good cameras in reasonable spots and a network problem behind them: a router that switched bands, a SmartHub tucked in a cabinet far from the cameras, a channel shared with twenty neighbors, or a yard camera on the far side of a brick wall. We start by checking the router and hub, then the signal at each camera, then the firmware and app settings, and only then the camera positions. Often moving the hub, changing a channel or adding one access point brings every camera back without moving a single mount. Where a camera does need to move, we patch the old holes if you want. We also trace any unknown cables in the wall before we use them, so a fix does not create a new problem.

FAQ

NYC Arlo Camera Installation FAQ

Which Arlo doorbell tends to suit a co-op apartment door best?
+

Usually the Arlo Essential Wire-Free Video Doorbell, because it needs no electrical work, and KS Renovation Group names it as one of its two most-installed doorbells for Manhattan co-ops. Battery power does not remove the need for board approval on a hallway-facing door, so get the house rules first, and if the board says no, an Arlo camera inside the apartment facing the door is the usual alternative.

Is my landlord allowed to put cameras in the building's hallways and lobby?
+

Generally yes. An attorney answering on Justia said video surveillance in common areas such as hallways and stairwells is legal as long as the camera is not aimed into an apartment when its door is open and voice is not recorded. A camera hidden where people expect privacy is a different matter under Penal Law 250.45. Tenants with concerns about building security or surveillance can raise them with HPD or 311.

Does New York City law require apartment buildings to have security cameras?
+

No. Seneca Security notes there is no general NYC law mandating cameras in apartment buildings; the legal requirements that apply to building entry are mainly about intercoms and lighting. That is why so many tenants add their own Arlo cameras inside their units, and why building managers who want lobby coverage install it by choice.

How quickly can you schedule an Arlo installation?
+

We book within 2 hours of your request. The visit itself is set for the earliest slot that works for you and, in co-ops and condos, for the building, since some managing agents want a certificate of insurance on file before a contractor goes upstairs. Sending photos and the building rules with your request is the fastest way to a confirmed date.

Arlo is sold as do-it-yourself. When is it worth paying someone to install it?
+

When the building is the hard part. If your cameras are inside an apartment near the router, you may not need us. If they have to reach a rear yard through brick, share 2.4 GHz air with dozens of neighbors, need a SmartHub placed for coverage, sit on a landmarked facade, or need board paperwork, a licensed installer saves time and second attempts.

How high should an Arlo camera be mounted outside a New York home?
+

SafeWise's Arlo guide suggests around six to seven feet above the ground, which is high enough to be out of easy reach and low enough to see faces. On a stoop or in a narrow yard we adjust from there to keep the view on your own property, away from neighbors' windows, and clear of glare from street lights.

Does Arlo have its own installers in New York?
+

Arlo offers professional installation through partners. Forbes reports those partners are OnTech and HelloTech, covering all 50 states. AESS is an independent, locally licensed installer, not an Arlo partner, and we focus on the New York parts of the job: masonry Wi-Fi, building rules, landmark facades and the network behind the cameras.

Can a wired Arlo doorbell connect to the old doorbell wiring in a brownstone or house?
+

Only if that wiring supplies proper doorbell power. One buyer review warns that without standard 16 VAC doorbell wiring already in place, you will have to wire it. Many older New York houses have old buttons on buzzer or chime systems that may not match. We check the existing transformer and wiring from photos before booking, and if it will not work, a wire-free Arlo doorbell avoids the problem.

Can footage from my Arlo cameras be shared with the NYPD?
+

Yes, if you choose. NYC 311 explains that any business with a security camera can share live feeds with the NYPD during emergencies and register camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org. Homeowners can also download a clip from the Arlo app or the local USB drive and give it to police after an incident. Registration is voluntary.

Do you cover all five boroughs, and does the price change from one to another?
+

We cover all five. Labor follows borough multipliers: Brooklyn and the Bronx at the base rate, Queens at 1.05 times, Staten Island at 1.10 times and Manhattan at 1.25 times. Equipment is priced the same everywhere. Each borough has its own Arlo price page listing every line.

What should I have ready on the day of the visit?
+

Your Arlo account login, the Wi-Fi name and password, router admin access if you have it, any USB drive you want for local storage, the cameras charged if you bought your own, and any board or landlord approval in writing. If the building wants a certificate of insurance, the managing agent should already have it from us.

Does the 2026 storefront grille rule affect where a shop's Arlo camera can go?
+

It can. City Council materials say that from July 1, 2026, security grilles on business and mercantile buildings must have at least 70 percent visibility from the sidewalk. A camera inside the glass may now see out better through an open grille, but it is also easier to see, and motion detection through glass is limited, so we test placement before relying on it.

Why might an Arlo camera miss an event right in front of it?
+

The usual causes are distance, angle, glass and settings. One owner reported an Arlo aimed at a car that recorded nothing during a break-in. A battery camera has to detect motion to wake up, so a subject too far away, crossing at the wrong angle, behind a window or outside the motion zone can be missed. We set distance, angle and zones during the walk-test so the camera triggers where it matters.
