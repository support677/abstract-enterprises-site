The Neighborhood

Why Starrett City Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Starrett City, renamed Spring Creek Towers in 2002, sits on a peninsula at the edge of Jamaica Bay between Fresh Creek and Hendrix Creek, with Flatlands Avenue to the north, Seaview Avenue and the Belt Parkway to the south and Pennsylvania Avenue running through the middle. It is 5,881 rental apartments in 46 buildings of 11 to 20 stories, grouped into eight sections from Ardsley to Hornell, each with its own five-story garage. That is the short version of the place. For a camera repair, the part that matters is who owns what, what the power does, and what a camera has to look through or past to see anything.

Ownership first. One company owns the whole residential complex and another manages it, and the grounds are patrolled around the clock by the Spring Creek Towers Department of Public Safety, whose officers are certified, armed peace officers. Management has also been adding its own cameras: its October 2021 update to residents in the Spring Creek Sun promised "over 30 additional cameras" by the end of that year, alongside mock-ups of new hallways and lobbies and plans to replace building frontages. Those cameras, and every other camera in a lobby, corridor, elevator, garage or path on the grounds, belong to the owner. If one of them is dark, the people who can fix it are management and its contractors, and the person to tell is the management office or Public Safety. We say that plainly because it saves a resident a service call that cannot fix the problem.

What is left is still a lot of cameras. Residents put indoor cameras on a shelf facing the apartment door, in a child's room, or near a window, and a small number mount a doorbell camera at their own door where the lease and management allow it. The retail center facing Pennsylvania Avenue has stores, a supermarket and second-floor offices, and several ground-floor spaces on the avenue are built out as medical and community-facility suites, each with its own small system. East of Hendrix Creek, the Nehemiah Spring Creek houses have stoops, raised front doors and parking on rear alleys, and their owners run their own cameras on all three. Those are the systems this page is about, and they fail in a few predictable ways here.

The power is the second thread, and it is unusual. The complex opened in the 1970s with its own power plant, and reporters have covered it going dark several times since: more than 1,000 apartments without power on a December night in 2014, a morning in April 2015 when a failure at the plant left stairwells dark, thousands without power from about 5 a.m. on a July morning in 2018, and at least an hour in the dark on an October Tuesday in 2023, with elevator rescues more than once. In 2017 an FDNY deputy chief told ABC7, "This is not an unusual occurrence for this complex," and, "We come here once a year for a power problem." Management's 2021 update said it had installed 8 megawatts of permanent backup power to avoid some outages and recover from others faster. For a camera owner, each of those events is the same test: a recorder, a switch and a few small power adapters going down hard and coming back on a surge. Some do not come back right, and the owner often finds out weeks later, the first time someone tries to play something back.

The third thread is what the cameras look through. The towers are concrete, so a Wi-Fi camera by an apartment door may sit three solid walls away from the router in the living room. A store camera in the retail center looks out through glass at a parking area or the avenue, bright by day and lit by fixtures at night. A Nehemiah house camera watching the rear alley looks down a lane lit by whatever its neighbors leave on. And the whole site is open to the wind off the bay, which works loose any camera mounted on a long arm or a thin bracket. Many of our "the camera is broken" calls from here turn out to be one of those conditions rather than a dead camera, and they are fixed with a better spot, a setting or a small part.

So a camera repair here is rarely only "make the black square come back." It is getting your own cameras to record usable video at the hours that matter, getting the recorder or app to keep it, and making sure the system warns you when it stops, so the next outage does not leave you with a gap you find out about too late. Many store owners pair the repair with a check of the alarm or the door entry at the same counter, and homeowners on the Nehemiah blocks often ask us to look at the doorbell at the same visit.

Tower apartments: three concrete walls between the camera and the router

The usual resident system in a Spring Creek Towers apartment is one to four indoor cameras on Wi-Fi: one facing the inside of the front door, one in the living room, sometimes one in a child's room or near a window on a low floor. The router is wherever the internet provider's line enters, often in the living room or a bedroom. Between the two there can be several concrete partitions, and concrete eats a Wi-Fi signal. The camera by the door shows a full signal when the apartment is quiet at noon and drops out every evening when every neighbor's network is busy. Owners read that as a failing camera. Usually it is placement. We test the signal at the camera with the camera's own tools, try the other band, move the camera a few feet or the router a room, and only add a small access point when the apartment genuinely needs one. Nothing is drilled, screwed into a wall or run through the hallway, because none of that is ours or yours to change.

The second tower problem is the account. Indoor cameras are tied to a cloud login, and in a household where a grown child set up the cameras for a parent, the account may belong to someone who has since moved, changed phones or forgotten the password. Then a camera that works perfectly looks dead to the person who lives with it. A repair here is often recovering or resetting the account, putting it in the resident's name, adding the family members who should see it, and removing anyone who should not.

Section garages and service spaces: heat, exhaust and the owner's say

Each of the eight sections has a five-story garage, and since the boiler decentralization project each garage also houses one of the eight modular boiler plants that replaced the complex's old central heating plant. That changes the conditions for any camera in or near those spaces: warmer, drier air near the mechanical rooms, exhaust and road grime on lenses near the ramps, and a lot of moving metal that confuses motion detection. The garages and their cameras belong to the owner, so we work there only when the owner or its contractor engages us. The exception is a tenant with its own space inside a garage building, such as a gym, that runs its own cameras inside its own rooms. For those, the job is the same as any small commercial system, with the building's contractor rules on top: insurance certificate, sign-in, and the times management allows work.

Retail center storefronts: glass on one side, stockroom on the other

Stores in the retail center usually have a camera over the register, one or two over the aisles, one on the stockroom or back door and one looking out through the front glass. The recorder sits in a back office or on a shelf in the stockroom, often under a pile of cartons, beside the router and the alarm panel. The front camera has the hardest job. By day it looks from a dim store out at a bright walk; at night it looks out at whatever the center's fixtures light, and its own infrared reflects off the inside of the glass. The stockroom camera has the next hardest: boxes stacked higher every season until the lens sees only cardboard. A storefront repair here is often a re-aim, a hood or a move of a few inches away from the glass, plus a recorder that has been quietly failing for months, which nobody noticed because the live screen on the counter still showed pictures.

Second-floor offices and medical suites: small systems, careful views

Offices above the stores are reached by a stair shared with the other upstairs tenants, so an office's own cameras usually cover only its door, its reception area and perhaps a records room, with a small recorder or a cloud service. Nehemiah Spring Creek houses: stoop, door and alley

The houses across Hendrix Creek were assembled from factory-built sections, with stoops leading to raised front doors in a modern take on the Brooklyn row house, and parking along rear alleys inside each block. A typical owner's system has a doorbell camera, a camera over the stoop, one or two at the back over the parking space, and a recorder in the basement or a front room. Three things fail most. Cable run along the outside of a factory-built wall and into a small hole lets water follow it in, and the connector corrodes. The rear cameras depend on alley lighting that neighbors switch on and off. And on a two-family house, the cameras are sometimes powered from the tenant's outlets, so a tenant's breaker or closed electric account takes half the system with it. Part of a house repair is sealing what we open, moving power onto the owner's circuit, and putting the recorder somewhere the owner can reach.

Field Notes: The Three Questions We Ask Before Booking a Starrett City Visit

This is the way our technicians handle the most common call from Starrett City, written as our method rather than a story about any one apartment or store. The caller says the cameras "stopped working." Before booking, we ask three things. First: whose cameras are they? If the answer is "the ones in our lobby" or "the garage cameras," we explain that those are the owner's and that the management office or Public Safety is where to report them, and we do not take the caller's money for a visit that cannot fix anything. If they are the caller's own, we go on.

None of this costs the caller anything, and it means the technician arrives with the right part, or tells the caller before the visit that no part is needed.

Fault by Fault

Power Returns, Concrete, Fiber, Bay Wind and the Overwrite Clock: How Starrett City Camera Systems Go Wrong

Power returns: the damage happens when the lights come back

An outage itself rarely hurts a camera system. The return does. When power comes back to a whole section at once, every refrigerator, air conditioner, elevator motor and router starts in the same second, and the voltage in an apartment or store can swing before it settles. Small plug-in camera adapters and cheap power strips are the first to go. A recorder that lost power mid-write can come back with its drive unmounted, its recording schedule wiped or its clock reset to the factory date. A PoE switch can lose one port, so a single camera stays dark while the rest return. Because the live view often looks normal, the first sign can be a gap in playback found weeks later. After any outage call here we check every link in the chain, set the recorder's clock to sync automatically, and recommend a properly sized surge protector or battery unit where the system has none.

Starrett City adds a twist: backup power. Management reported installing 8 megawatts of permanent backup in 2021 so that some outages are avoided and others end sooner. That is good news for residents, but a switch from one source to another can still mean a brief drop or a flicker, and a recorder without a battery unit restarts each time. A restart a week is enough to wear a drive and leave short gaps. A small battery unit that only has to carry the recorder and a router through a few seconds or minutes is cheap protection, and we size it to that job rather than pretending it will carry a system through a long outage.

Concrete: why the camera nearest the door drops first

The towers' concrete floors and walls are excellent for fire separation and terrible for Wi-Fi. A camera on a shelf beside the apartment door is usually the farthest device from the router, and the signal it gets has passed through one or more solid partitions. At quiet times that is enough. In the evening, when dozens of networks on the floors above and below are busy, the camera starts dropping, buffering or saving clips with gaps. The most reliable fixes are moving the router toward the middle of the apartment, moving the camera to where the signal is better while still seeing the door, using the band that reaches farther through walls, or adding a small mesh point. A new camera of the same kind in the same spot just fails the same way.

Fiber in the building: a different kind of cable fault

Honeywell's 2013 announcement described a passive optical LAN installed at Spring Creek Towers to carry building systems including video surveillance and phone service. Most residents never see it, and the owner's system is the owner's business. But it matters for some stores, offices and program spaces whose cameras or internet connect through equipment the building provides. On an optical network the "cable" between a camera switch and the rest of the network can be a fiber jumper and a small optical terminal, not a copper Ethernet run. A copper tester says nothing about it. When a tenant's cameras go offline and the cause points upstream of the tenant's own switch, we test what is ours to test, show the result, and tell the tenant exactly what to ask the building or the provider to check, rather than opening equipment that belongs to someone else.

Bay wind and salt air: mounts loosen, domes haze

Starrett City is open to Jamaica Bay on the south, with the Belt Parkway running along the shore. A store-front camera on a long arm or a Nehemiah house camera on a thin vinyl-siding bracket takes that wind every day. Over a season the screws work loose, the camera drifts a few degrees, and the view that used to show the walk now shows the sky or the wall. Salt in the air and grime from the parkway film the dome or the lens, and a dome left in full sun turns cloudy. Most of these repairs are small: solid backing behind the mount, stainless hardware, a re-aim, a cleaned or replaced dome, and a shorter arm where a long one is not needed. When the camera itself is past saving, we say so and replace it with one rated for outdoor wind and weather.

The overwrite clock: footage is not kept forever

Every recorder keeps a fixed amount of video and then records over the oldest. Building managers generally keep footage for 30 to 90 days, according to FirstService Residential, but a small store recorder with a worn or undersized drive may hold only a few days, and a cloud camera on a free plan may keep less. That is why, when an incident happens, the first hours matter. If a store owner waits a week to pull a clip of a shoplifting or a break-in, it may already be gone. A repair is a good moment to check how many days the system actually keeps, at what quality, and to size the drive and the recording schedule so the answer is at least a week of usable video.

Glass and glare: the store camera that looks out the window

Front cameras in the retail center and in apartments that look out a window share one trap. A camera inside glass sees its own reflection at night, lit by its infrared, and sees the bright outdoors by day while the person at the door stays dark. The fixes are simple once the cause is named: a lens hood or shroud pressed to the glass, the camera's infrared turned off with the outside lit instead, a few degrees of tilt, or a camera with better handling for strong backlight. We test in the light the problem happens in, which sometimes means booking the visit for the afternoon or checking the night picture before we leave.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Starrett City: Questions Answered

Your own cameras in a tower apartment

My mother lives alone in a tower on Bethel Loop, and I keep an eye on her through an indoor camera from my own apartment in another section. Every few days it shows offline for an hour and then comes back by itself. What is going on?

A camera that drops and recovers on its own is almost never dead. The usual causes are a weak Wi-Fi signal that fails when the building's networks are busiest, a router that restarts itself overnight for updates and takes a while to come back, or a cheap plug-in adapter that sags when the voltage dips. We check the signal at the camera, look at the router's restart log, swap the adapter, and set the camera to alert your phone when it goes offline so you know right away rather than guessing. If your mother cannot reach the camera or router, we place them where they work without her having to touch anything.

After our section lost power, my apartment camera came back, but the clips it had saved on its memory card from before the outage are gone. Can they be recovered?

Sometimes. A memory card that was being written when the power dropped can end up with a damaged file table, so the camera formats it or cannot read it. Stop using the camera and take the card out before it records over anything. Recovery tools can often rebuild clips from a card that has not been overwritten, and we can try that on a visit. Going forward, a camera that also saves to a cloud plan or to a small recorder on a battery unit keeps a second copy, so one bad card does not take everything.

Cameras that belong to the complex

Something happened on the path outside our building, and I want to know whether the complex's cameras caught it. Can you get that footage for me?

No, and nobody outside the owner can. The cameras on the grounds, in the lobbies and in the garages are the owner's, and only management, its contractors or someone with legal authority can pull their video. Brick Underground has reported that landlords generally are not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process. What you can do is ask quickly and in writing: tell management and Public Safety the date, the time, the place and why you need it, because building systems typically keep video for weeks, not months, and then record over it. If the police are involved, tell the detective which cameras you think saw it.

The building's cameras do not cover my apartment door. Can I watch my own door with a camera behind the peephole, or put one in the hallway?

A small camera inside your apartment, aimed through or beside the peephole or simply facing the inside of the door, is yours to place and fix, and it does not touch the building. A camera mounted in the corridor is a different matter. Habitat has noted that New York City has no law banning hallway doorbell cameras and that building rules decide, so in Spring Creek Towers the lease and management's rules are what count. Ask management in writing before you put anything on the door or in the hall. We will repair or set up whatever you are allowed to have, and we will not drill a door or a corridor wall without written approval.

I live on the 18th floor, and my camera looks out the window at the parking lot far below. From that height can it identify anyone, or is it the wrong camera for the job?

From that height a standard wide indoor camera shows cars and figures, not faces or plates. It can still tell you when something happened, which is useful, but identification needs pixels on the target. A camera with a zoom lens aimed at the one spot you care about, such as your own parking space, does far better than a wide view of the whole lot. Shield it against the glass so it does not record its own reflection at night. For plates and faces you are relying on the owner's ground-level cameras, and that is a question for management.

Stores, offices and suites

Our store in the retail center shares a back service hall with the stores beside it. Our back-door camera catches every delivery they take, and our phones buzz all day. Can it watch only our door?

Yes. Most cameras and recorders let you draw a detection zone, so alerts fire only when a person crosses the area in front of your own door, and some add a line that triggers only when someone crosses in one direction. Re-aiming the camera so your door fills more of the frame helps too, and it makes the clip more useful when you need it. We keep the recording continuous so nothing is missed, and limit only the alerts. The neighbors' deliveries stay on your recording, which is fine; what stops is the noise on your phone.

Our medical suite on Pennsylvania Avenue wants the entrance and the waiting room on camera, but not the treatment rooms or the staff room. Can the system be set so each person sees only what they should?

Yes, in two ways. Cameras are placed and aimed so no lens points into a treatment room, a bathroom or a changing area, and where a view would catch part of a doorway, a privacy mask blacks out that part of the picture permanently. Then the recorder or cloud account gets separate logins: the front desk sees the entrance live, the office manager sees everything and can export clips, and nobody shares a password. We ask who should have which access before we start and write it down.

Our program space's cameras connect through network jacks the building provides. When the cameras dropped, the building said its network was fine. How do we find out where the fault really is?

By testing your side first and putting the results in writing. We test each camera, its cable and your own switch, and check whether your equipment gets a network address and a working link from the building's jack. If all of that passes on your side and the link from the jack does not, the fault is upstream, and on an optical network like the one Honeywell described installing here in 2013 that can mean a fiber jumper or an optical terminal we do not touch. You hand the building our results instead of an argument, and we stay off its equipment.

Nehemiah houses across the creek

Behind our Nehemiah house the parking alley is lit only by whatever the neighbors leave on. Some nights our rear camera shows our car clearly, other nights almost nothing. Do we need a better camera or a light of our own?

Usually a light of your own, set on a timer or a motion sensor and mounted so it lights the car and not the lens. A camera that depends on a neighbor's light is only as good as that neighbor's habits. Infrared alone reaches a limited distance and shows no color, and it bounces off the nearest car or fence. With your own light, a decent camera gives a steady picture every night. If the camera is also old or set wrong, we fix that at the same visit, but the light comes first.

The builder left a capped cable and an empty bracket beside our front door, but no camera was ever mounted. Can a camera go there, and is the cable connected to anything?

Often, yes. We trace where the cable goes, usually toward a basement or a closet, check what kind it is and whether it is intact, and test it end to end before anything is bought. If it is a sound network cable, a powered network camera can use it directly, and the recorder or switch goes at the other end. If it turns out to be cut, too short or the wrong type, we tell you before you spend money on a camera that cannot use it.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Starrett City

Search summaries and home-service sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give general advice about camera repair. Much of it is fine anywhere. Some of it misses how a place like Starrett City works. These are the claims we hear repeated most, and what we find on repairs here.

"Call a camera repair company to fix your building's cameras"

In a building with many small owners, maybe. In Spring Creek Towers one owner controls the lobbies, corridors, elevators, garages and grounds, and its own Department of Public Safety patrols them. A resident who hires a company to fix a lobby camera has paid for a visit that cannot legally touch it.

The right move for a dark building camera is a written report to management, with the building, the camera's location and the date it went dark. That creates a record and puts the repair with the people who own the system.

What a resident can hire out is the repair of their own equipment, and that is where we start every Starrett City call: deciding whose camera it is before anyone books anything.

"Wi-Fi cameras work anywhere in an apartment"

Wi-Fi range figures assume open rooms and light walls. A tower apartment here has concrete partitions and dozens of neighboring networks on the floors around it, and the camera people most want, the one by the front door, is usually the farthest from the router.

The result is a camera that tests fine at noon and drops in the evening. Replacing it with another camera of the same kind in the same place changes nothing.

Moving the router or the camera, using the band that reaches farther, or adding a small access point fixes it. We measure the signal where the camera sits before recommending anything.

"A battery backup keeps your cameras running in a blackout"

A small battery unit keeps a recorder and router alive through a flicker or a few minutes. It does not carry a system through an outage that lasts an hour or more, like the ones reported at the complex in 2014, 2015, 2018 and 2023.

Its real value here is the short drops: the moment of switch-over when backup power takes over, or the surge when power returns. Those are what corrupt drives and kill adapters, and a modest battery unit handles them well.

The honest plan is to ride through short drops, shut down cleanly on long ones, and check the whole chain after any outage. That is what we set up, rather than promising a battery will do what it cannot.

"If the live picture looks fine, the system is recording"

Live view and recording are separate jobs. A recorder can show every camera live while its drive has stopped writing, or while it saves a low-quality stream that turns faces into smudges.

In stores here we often find a counter monitor showing sharp live video from a recorder whose timeline ends weeks earlier, usually right around an outage. Nobody noticed because nobody needed a clip until something happened.

The test is simple: play back a random hour from yesterday, from the camera that matters most. We do that on every repair, and set alerts so a stopped recording reaches a phone someone reads.

"A patrolled complex does not need private cameras"

Public Safety officers patrol the grounds, and the owner has added cameras, but neither covers the inside of an apartment or a store's register. Those are the views residents and shopkeepers are responsible for.

The 75th Precinct's own figures show burglaries falling, 145 year to date in mid-October 2026 against 192 a year earlier, but falling is not zero, and that precinct covers a large part of East New York beyond the complex.

A working private camera also helps the patrol and the police. When something happens near a store, a clear clip of the door with the right timestamp is the most useful thing an owner can hand over.

"Any handyman or electrician can fix a security camera system"

In New York, companies that install or service security and CCTV systems for other people are licensed by the Department of State. An electrician can fix the outlet the recorder plugs into, but cameras, cabling, recorders and their networks are low-voltage work.

The tools differ too. Most faults here are found with a PoE tester, a cable tester, sometimes an optical power meter, the recorder's logs and a signal reading at the camera, not with a voltage meter on the house wiring.

If someone has already tried a fix, tell us what was changed. It saves time, and a well-meant fix often moves the problem rather than solving it.

"Camera repair costs about $100 to $300"

That figure describes a single consumer camera somewhere simple. HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, typically $680 to $2,145 across all job sizes.

On our Brooklyn list, a setting or account fix is often covered within the $150 service call, a connector or PoE port runs $150 to $350, a single camera replacement $250 to $600, and a recorder replacement $700 to $1,500. You see the price in writing first.
