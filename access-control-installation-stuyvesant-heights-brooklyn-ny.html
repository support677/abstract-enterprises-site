The Neighborhood

Why Stuyvesant Heights Properties Need Access Control

Stuyvesant Heights is the eastern, historic heart of Bedford-Stuyvesant, and most of its housing was built for a single family or a few households to share one front door. The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s 2013 designation of the Bedford-Stuyvesant/Expanded Stuyvesant Heights Historic District describes about 825 buildings between Halsey and Macon Streets on the north, Fulton Street on the south, Malcolm X Boulevard on the east and Tompkins Avenue on the west, wrapped around the smaller district the city first protected in 1971. Most of it went up between 1880 and 1920: speculative rowhouses in Queen Anne, neo-Grec, Romanesque Revival and Renaissance Revival dress, four- and five-story “French flats” from the 1890s, a scattering of wood-frame houses older than any of them, and churches such as Bethany Baptist.

None of those buildings was designed for electronic entry. A brownstone stoop leads to a pair of tall wooden outer doors, then a vestibule, then an inner door with glass, and below the stoop a garden-floor door behind an iron gate. A French flat has a street door, a vestibule door, a stair hall that climbs four or five floors, a cellar door and a roof door. Each of those openings has its own key today, and in a house divided into apartments over the decades, nobody is quite sure how many copies are out there. That is the first reason owners call us: they want to stop counting keys.

The second reason is who comes to the door. Package deliveries, home health aides, dog walkers, contractors on a renovation, tenants’ guests, the mail carrier and, in a church or community hall, dozens of people on a schedule. News 12 Brooklyn reported in February 2025 that police said a DoorDash courier took a package worth about $1,500 from in front of a resident’s door in a building on Bainbridge Street. The report does not say how the courier got in, and that is exactly the question an access plan has to answer for every person who is allowed in the front door but not past it.

The third reason is the paperwork that comes with a changeover. In a rent-stabilized walk-up, swapping metal keys for fobs is a change in building services that the state housing agency, DHCR, has approved only with conditions: free fobs, a keyed way in for emergencies, a battery that lasts 48 hours, help for tenants who do not use electronics on the Sabbath, and limits on what the system records. In any multiple dwelling that uses keyless entry, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy law adds its own rules about consent, data and deletion. In the districts, a new panel at a door opening is a Landmarks permit item. A good installation handles all three before the first hole is drilled.

HPD’s complaint data shows how often entry hardware here simply stops working. In the three ZIP codes that hold the neighborhood, residents logged 139 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints in 2025 and 134 in 2026 through November 11, and the complaints cluster on Fulton Street, Stuyvesant Avenue, Tompkins Avenue and Chauncey Street, where the apartment and mixed-use buildings stand, rather than on the brownstone blocks of Decatur and Macon. That pattern shapes what we sell on each street, and it is covered building by building further down this page.

Stuyvesant Heights facts we checked before writing this page

  • Two districts, one neighborhood. The Stuyvesant Heights Historic District was designated in 1971; the Commission voted on April 16, 2013, to designate the expanded district of about 825 buildings around it.
  • Age of the doors. Most homes in the expanded district were built between 1880 and 1920, so the typical front door is a century old or more and hangs in a wood frame set into brownstone or brick.
  • Building mix. Rowhouses dominate, with four- and five-story French flats from the 1890s and some mid-nineteenth-century frame houses that survived the rowhouse boom.
  • Entry complaints. HPD logged 94 bell, buzzer and intercom complaints in 2023, 134 in 2024, 139 in 2025 and 134 in 2026 through November 11 across ZIP codes 11216, 11221 and 11233; Fulton Street (54) and Stuyvesant Avenue (48) led the streets from January 2024.
  • The keys-to-fobs rules. DHCR has approved fob systems in rent-stabilized buildings with conditions that include free fobs for occupants, up to four extra free fobs, replacements capped at $25 and a 48-hour battery backup.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Stuyvesant Heights’ Architecture

On these blocks the house tells us most of the plan before we open a toolbag. The number of households, the age of the doors and whether a wall was added inside the hall decide where the reader goes, which lock it releases and where the controller hides.

Brownstone kept as one home

One family, a stoop entry and a garden-floor entry. Owners want phone keys for themselves, codes for helpers and nothing visible from the sidewalk beyond what the district allows.

Owner’s duplex with rentals above

The owner lives on the garden and parlor floors and rents the top. The front door becomes shared, the parlor hall often gets a dividing wall, and the owner needs to run the tenants’ access without giving them the owner’s doors.

Three- and four-family rowhouse

Three or more apartments make the house a multiple dwelling under state law, and once keyless entry is used, the city’s tenant privacy rules apply too. A fob system here is a small building system, not a gadget.

1890s French flat

Four or five stories, eight to twenty apartments over a single stair, a street door and vestibule, a cellar door and a roof door that must open from inside. Usually rent stabilized, usually with an old buzzer riser that video entry can replace.

Wood-frame survivor

Some of the district’s oldest houses are wood, with doors set in frames that move with the seasons. Strike alignment and closer force get rechecked after the first winter.

Churches and community halls

Heavy doors, several entrances, many keyholders and a calendar. The access plan is mostly a schedule: which door opens for whom, when, and who can override it during a service.

What the materials mean for the install

Original stoop doors here are thick, heavy and often slightly out of square after a century of swelling and paint. A strike only works if the latch meets it in the same spot every time, so we rehang or shim a dropped door before we cut the frame, and we size the closer to the door’s real weight. The masonry around the opening is brownstone, brick or limestone, and the district rules want any attachment in the mortar joints or plain masonry rather than in carved stone, which suits us: mortar is easy to repair if the device ever comes off.

Cable is the other material question. Most houses have an old bell or buzzer pair running from the front wall to the cellar, and sometimes a conduit we can reuse. Where there is none, the route runs down inside the vestibule wall or under the stoop into the cellar, which keeps the facade clean and keeps the controller indoors, where it belongs. In French flats, the cellar ceiling is usually open joists, so the run from the street door to a controller beside the meters is short.

From Metal Keys to Fobs in a Rent-Stabilized Walk-Up: The Conditions DHCR Has Set

Many of the French flats and larger rowhouses in Stuyvesant Heights are rent stabilized. In those buildings, the front-door key is a service the owner provides, and changing how tenants get in is a change in services. Owners who want fobs on the street door file a service modification application with DHCR, the state’s housing agency. We do not file it, and we do not give legal advice, but we design the system so that it can meet the conditions DHCR has attached to past approvals, because a system that has to be rebuilt after the order comes back is the most expensive kind.

One published decision is especially useful because the building sounds like many here: no doorman, and a superintendent who lived three blocks away. DHCR’s rent administrator found that swapping metal keys for fobs or key cards was an “adequate substitution of services,” left the legal rents unchanged, and attached fourteen conditions. Tenants appealed, saying among other things that recording entries invaded their privacy, and DHCR held that recording entrances only was consistent with its earlier rulings. The conditions read like a specification, so we treat them as one.

Those conditions come from one case; DHCR decides each application on its own record, and other orders have carried different lists. A later decision, from 2023, approved an owner adding fobs alongside the existing metal keys and limiting the back service entrance to deliveries for every tenant, with thirteen conditions. Brick Underground’s summary of earlier rulings adds three points owners here ask about: a new key card system on its own has not counted as a major capital improvement that raises rents, landlords may not use the changeover to collect new personal details such as driver’s license numbers, and fobs must go free to every lawful occupant, including relatives, roommates and approved subtenants.

Where city law adds to the state conditions

New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to any multiple dwelling that uses a smart access system such as fobs, cards, apps or biometrics, rent stabilized or not. HPD’s summary says owners need tenants’ express consent, must give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, may collect only a minimum set of data, must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, may not sell it, may not track tenants outside the building, may not require smart access to enter an apartment itself, and may not use the data to harass or evict. We set the controller’s log retention and user fields to match, and we hand the owner a plain list of what the system stores so the policy they write is accurate.

Two Doors Under One Stoop: Outer Doors, the Vestibule and the Mail Carrier

The brownstone entry is two doors deep, and the most common planning mistake is to treat it as one. A three-family owner on Brownstoner laid out the dilemma plainly: the house has bi-fold outer doors and a single inner door across the foyer, and the choices are to lock both, lock only the inner door and leave the outer one open for the mail, give the carrier a key, or lock only the outer door. As the owner put it, “There seem to be so many variations.”

The replies from that thread match what we do. One suggested an electric strike on the outer door wired to a USPS lock, so the carrier can be let in for a few seconds, with the same strike also opening by app, fob or card. Another argued for keying the two doors differently, so one lost key means changing one cylinder, for dead latches rather than deadbolts because tenants pull the door shut and walk away, and for keeping a key lock on the inner door in case the electronics fail. Each of those is a design decision we put in writing before the install.

Our usual answer for a Stuyvesant Heights stoop

For most multi-family rowhouses we put the reader and the strike on the outer stoop doors, because that is the door strangers reach first and the one deliveries stop at, and we leave the inner vestibule door on a dead-latching mechanical lock keyed differently from the outer door. The mailboxes stay in the vestibule. The carrier gets in through a postal lock or, where the Postal Service’s local office prefers it, a timed code. Packages wait in the vestibule, out of sight from the sidewalk, and do not go up the stairs.

A contact on each door lets the controller know when both stand open together, which is what happens when a mover props the outer door and someone leaves the inner one ajar. Rather than lock anyone in or out, the system chimes in the hall and, if the owner wants it, texts the owner. Brownstone Boys, a pair renovating a Bed-Stuy brownstone after a break-in, wrote on Brownstoner that they added a lock to the interior vestibule door as one of their first fixes; we agree with the instinct and simply give that inner door a role in the system.

When the house is one family again

If the rowhouse has gone back to a single household, the logic flips. The outer doors often stay open during the day so the vestibule works as a porch, and the inner door is the one that locks. The reader then goes on the inner door frame, or, if the owners want the outer doors locked at night, on a schedule that locks the outer pair at a set hour. Either way the parlor-floor buzzer button stays where it has always been, wired to the new system, so visitors find it.

Original Double Doors: Electric Strikes on a Century-Old Pair

Owners are sometimes told that an electric strike cannot go on tall paired rowhouse doors. A Brownstoner thread on exactly that question drew a firm answer from a firm that rebuilds such doors: about half of the double doors they work on get an electric latch, the job is easier with the door on the bench and the glass out but can be done in place, and claims that it can’t be done mean the installer is “letting their own limitations get in the way of their creativity.” Another reply listed the options we use: cable through the passive door or surface-mounted, or an electrified mortise lock on the active door.

Our order of preference on a Stuyvesant Heights pair is simple. First, an electric strike in the frame for the active leaf, if the active leaf latches into the frame or into a solid astragal. Second, where the latch meets the passive leaf, a strike cut into the passive leaf’s edge with the cable carried through that leaf and up into the head of the frame through a concealed transfer at the hinge. Third, where the original lock is beyond saving or the owner is replacing it anyway, an electrified mortise lock in the active leaf, which keeps the outside of the door looking like a normal key-and-knob door.

The passive leaf matters as much as the active one. It is held by flush bolts top and bottom, and if those bolts do not seat firmly, a hard push opens both leaves together no matter what the strike does. Part of every double-door job is checking those bolts, the strike holes in the floor and head, and the astragal that covers the gap. On doors that still carry their original glass, we protect the glass during drilling and do not take it out unless the owner’s door restorer is on site.

Keeping the original lock and key

Many owners want to keep the old mortise lock because it is part of the door. That works: the strike releases the latch electrically while the original lock and key stay in service as the mechanical override. A Brownstoner reply about smart buzzers in a three-family made the same distinction, noting that smart locks usually work the lock side while older mortise locks open the latch. The strike approach respects both.

Inside the 1971 District and the 2013 Expansion: What Landmarks Asks of a Reader

Almost every brownstone block in Stuyvesant Heights is landmarked, either in the original 1971 district or in the 2013 expansion around it. That does not rule out access control. It means the parts you can see from the street follow the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s rules, and the owner applies for a permit before new equipment goes up at the door. The Commission says 95 percent of its permit approvals are issued at staff level, as a Permit for Minor Work or a Certificate of No Effect, when the work conforms to its rules, so a well-placed reader rarely sees a public hearing.

The Commission’s Permit Guidebook chapter on health and safety equipment is the one that applies. It says intercom panels and doorbells need a permit when they are installed within or adjacent to a door opening, that a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can go at each door opening, that an intercom can instead go on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway, and that design, size and finish must be compatible with the building. Equipment should not go on the primary facade unless there is no other option, must attach at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, must not be attached through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and any conduit must be concealed through plain masonry or cladding behind the device.

The guidebook does not name card readers or keypads. In practice we treat a reader at the door the way the chapter treats an intercom panel, which has a useful consequence: if the stoop already carries an intercom panel and a fob reader is added beside it, the door now has two devices where the rules allow one. That is why we prefer a single combined station that holds the camera, the call button and the reader. The barrier-free chapter adds that staff can approve a door actuator or push plate that is as small as possible and sits on plain masonry, wood or metal, not cast iron, and that replacing minor hardware in kind needs no permit at all.

What we prepare for the owner’s application

The application is the owner’s, but the pieces come from us: the device’s cut sheet with its dimensions and finish, a photo of the door with the proposed position marked, the attachment method, the cable route showing nothing on the facade, and a note on what is being removed. Strikes in the frame, controllers in the cellar and readers on the inner vestibule door, which cannot be seen from the street, generally do not change the facade at all; we flag that in the package so the owner can describe the work accurately.

Areaway gates and the garden door

The iron gate under the stoop is often the first thing a burglar tries and the last thing an owner thinks to control. A keypad on the gate itself, mounted to the ironwork rather than through it, or a reader on the wall of the areaway beside the garden-floor door, keeps the under-stoop entrance on the same list as the front. We avoid drilling original cast iron and use clamps or the newer metalwork where a gate has been replaced.

Churches, Fellowship Halls and Community Rooms: Doors That Run on a Calendar

Stuyvesant Heights has an unusual number of congregations for its size, from Bethany Baptist Church, named in the 2013 designation, to churches in converted rowhouses and storefronts along the avenues. Their doors carry more traffic in two hours on a Sunday than a brownstone does in a month, and they stand empty for days in between. In February 2026, the state Attorney General’s office and local elected officials condemned an intrusion at Antioch Baptist Church in Bedford-Stuyvesant, where, according to an Assemblymember quoted in the release, a man “claiming to be an Uber driver” walked through the fellowship hall and sanctuary during an MLK Day celebration before he was escorted out. No one was physically harmed, and the release does not say how he got in.

Access control cannot decide who is welcome at a service, and it should not try. What it can do is make sure the doors that are meant to be open are the only ones open. During a service the main doors stand open with greeters; the side door to the fellowship hall, the door to the office and the door to the stair down to the kitchen stay locked from outside and open from inside with one motion. After the service the main doors lock on schedule. The pastor, the administrator and the ministry leaders have fobs that work on their days, and a youth program’s volunteers have fobs that work on Tuesday evenings and nowhere else.

What a congregation needs to hand over

The hardest part of a church job is rarely technical. It is collecting the list of everyone who holds a key today and agreeing who gets a credential. We give the trustees a one-page sheet to fill in, one line per person, with the doors and days each one needs. Old keys stop mattering on the day the strikes go live, so the sheet replaces years of keys handed between ministries.

Assembly doors and exits

Sanctuary and hall doors are exits for large groups, and the NYC Building Code is strict about electrically locked egress doors. Any electrified hardware on those doors must release on a power loss and on the fire alarm, and panic hardware must open the door with a single push. We use electrified exit devices there, not magnetic locks, and leave the code questions about occupancy to the congregation’s architect or expediter where a permit is involved.

Rules that apply to Stuyvesant Heights business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on the way out must unlock when a sensor detects someone approaching or when a push-to-exit button 40 to 48 inches high and within five feet of the door is pressed, stay unlocked for at least 30 seconds, unlock on power loss and release when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate. Magnetic locks need door hardware with a built-in switch that releases them with one hand.
  • Listed equipment. The same code sections call for access control equipment listed to UL 294 on those doors, and we install listed controllers and locks.
  • The apartments upstairs. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after 1968 and an intercom with voice and remote release in buildings of eight or more apartments; older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants request it in writing.
  • Roof doors. The same section says the roof door is self-closing but not self-locking and must open from inside without a key. We never put a reader that locks the inside of a roof door.
  • Landmarked storefronts. A shop inside the historic districts follows the Commission’s storefront and health-and-safety guidance for anything new at the door opening.
  • Electrical work. AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431. A new outlet or circuit for a controller is electrical work, listed on the quote for a licensed electrician.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Stuyvesant Heights: Questions Answered

Brownstones with an owner and tenants

Our brownstone’s outer stoop doors fold back during the day and the inner door has glass. Which one should carry the reader if the tenants want deliveries left inside?

The outer doors. If the vestibule is where packages wait, the outer pair has to be the locked door, otherwise anyone can walk up the stoop and take what the courier left. The reader and strike go on the outer active leaf, the folding leaf is bolted shut at night, and the inner door keeps a mechanical dead latch keyed differently.

Couriers get a code that opens the outer door only, only during delivery hours, and the system logs which code was used. If a package goes missing, the log narrows it to a time and a code instead of “someone.”

We added a wall across our parlor hall when we rented out the top two floors. Can the door in that wall be on fobs too, or is that a Buildings Department question?

Both. Technically a reader can control that door like any other. Whether that door may be locked against the tenants depends on how the wall was filed with the Department of Buildings, a point Brownstoner readers raised in a thread about exactly this layout, so we ask to see the filed plans or the certificate of occupancy before quoting it.

If the door is shown as a required exit for the upper floors, it cannot be locked against them, and the right answer is a lock that the tenants can always open from their side. If it is a private connecting door, it can be the owner’s door only, on the owner’s group.

Our house has three apartments and we live in one. Do we really need a written privacy policy just to give the tenants fobs?

If the house is a multiple dwelling and uses keyless entry, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy law applies, and HPD’s summary of it calls for tenants’ consent and a written data-retention and privacy policy. Three apartments is where state law starts treating a house as a multiple dwelling, so most three-family brownstones here are in scope. Only a lawyer can tell you for certain about your house.

What we do is make the policy easy to write honestly. We switch off any logging the owner does not need, set entry logs to delete on a short schedule, and give you a one-page description of exactly what the system stores, which is most of what the policy has to say.

Walk-ups and rent-stabilized buildings

We are filing with DHCR to replace keys with fobs in our Chauncey Street walk-up. Can you install before the order comes back?

We can do the parts that do not change the tenants’ service, such as repairing the closer, rehanging the door and running cable to the cellar, but we do not switch the street door over to fobs until the owner has the order. In the decision summarized above, tenants argued the system had gone in before approval; DHCR found no proof of that, but it is an argument worth never giving anyone.

Running the cable early also lets us show the managing agent the exact equipment list for the application, which makes it easier to describe the change accurately.

Several elderly tenants in our French flat have home attendants who change from week to week. How do the extra fobs work without the list getting out of hand?

DHCR approvals have allowed up to four extra free fobs per apartment for guests or employees, and Brick Underground’s summary of the rulings says the same. We issue those extras as credentials tied to the apartment, not to a named attendant, so when an agency sends a new aide the tenant hands over the fob and the log still shows the apartment.

If a tenant would rather not carry an extra fob around, the attendant can have a code that works on set days instead. The owner’s administrator can switch either off in a minute if it is lost.

A tenant’s lawyer asked whether our fob logs could be used in a non-primary-residence case. What does the system keep?

Whatever the owner configures, within the law. Under the city’s privacy rules authentication data has to be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and owners may not use it to harass or evict. Practitioners quoted by Brick Underground also said fob data is weak evidence on its own, since a fob can be handed over and doors are held open.

We set log retention at handover and write it down. If the owner wants a shorter period, we shorten it. We do not keep copies of building logs on our side.

Our walk-up’s roof door is chained shut because people kept going up there. Can a reader replace the chain?

Not in a way that locks people in. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a says a roof door in these buildings is self-closing but not self-locking and must open from inside without a key, and a chain on it is a hazard. What a reader can do is control the door from the roof side, so nobody gets in from the roof, while the inside stays free.

The usual fix is a door contact and a local alarm that sounds when the roof door is opened from inside, with an alert to the super. People stop treating the roof as a lounge when the door announces them.

Landmarked fronts and old doors

Our MacDonough Street house has an iron fence and gate in front of the stoop. Could the reader go on the fence instead of on the brownstone beside the door?

Sometimes, and the Commission’s guidebook allows for it: it says an intercom can be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway rather than at the door. For a reader, that works best when the gate is the door you actually want to control, for example where the areaway and the stoop are both behind one gate.

The catch is cable. The run has to reach the fence without trenching through historic paving, so we look for an existing conduit, a sleeve under the stoop or a route through newer paving before we suggest it. Where there is none, the reader stays at the door, in a mortar joint.

Our outer stoop doors are original, but a previous owner swapped the inner vestibule door for a modern glass one. Which door should get the strike?

For a single-family house where the vestibule is used as a porch, the modern inner door is the easy one: its frame is usually square, the strike cuts in cleanly, and nothing on the facade changes. For a multi-family house where packages wait in the vestibule, the outer doors still need the strike, because they are the line between the street and the mailboxes.

Many owners end up doing both: a strike on the outer pair for tenants and couriers, and a reader on the inner door for the owner’s own household, both on one controller with the two door contacts.

Our wood-frame house near Stuyvesant Avenue has a front door that sticks every August and swings free every January. Will a strike work on it?

It will if the frame is made stable first. Wood-frame houses move with the seasons more than masonry ones, so we re-set the frame, fit the strike with an adjustable keeper and choose a strike that tolerates a little gap between the latch and the frame. Then we come back after the first season change to adjust it.

If the frame is too soft to hold a strike, a motorized lock in the door itself is the better choice, because it does not depend on the frame lining up.

Churches, offices and shops

Our church’s fellowship hall has its own street door that the youth program uses on weeknights. Can that door work for the program volunteers without opening the sanctuary?

Yes. The fellowship-hall door gets its own reader on a schedule, the volunteers get fobs that work on program nights at that door only, and the interior door from the hall to the sanctuary stays locked from the hall side. The hall’s exits still open from inside with one push.

The administrator can see which fob opened the hall door and when, which helps trustees answer the question that usually starts these projects: who has keys.

We rent the storefront on Fulton Street and the landlord controls the building door. Can our back door be on fobs if the cellar is shared?

Your back door can, with the landlord’s written consent if the lease requires it, which most commercial leases do for alterations. The shared cellar door is the landlord’s to control, and the cleanest arrangement is for the landlord to give your staff a credential on the building system for the hours you need the cellar.

If the landlord has no system, we can quote one door on each side as two separate small systems, so neither party depends on the other’s administrator.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Stuyvesant Heights

These are generalizations we see repeated in quick online answers and in cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, which price security doors and smart locks rather than wired access control. Each one is followed by what we actually find on Stuyvesant Heights doors.

“A landlord can switch the building from keys to fobs whenever it likes.”

In a building with rent-regulated apartments, the front-door key is a required service, and replacing it is a service modification that DHCR reviews. Published approvals have come with long lists of conditions, from free fobs to a keyed way in for emergencies.

Owners who skip that step risk tenants filing reduction-in-services complaints, and a system that has to be rebuilt to meet an order after the fact. Many French flats and larger rowhouses here have stabilized apartments, so this is the first question we ask a walk-up owner.

In a building with no regulated apartments the owner has more freedom, but the city’s Tenant Data Privacy law still applies to keyless entry in any multiple dwelling.

“Fob logs show exactly who lives in each apartment.”

They show which credential opened which door at what time, and nothing more. A fob can be lent, and a door can be held open by the person ahead, which is why attorneys quoted by Brick Underground said fob data is not that useful on its own in residency disputes.

City law also limits what can be kept: authentication data must be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, and it may not be used to harass or evict tenants. In one DHCR approval, the system could record entries but not exits.

We configure logs for the job they do well, investigating a forced door or a missing package, and we keep them short.

“Landlords can charge whatever they want for fobs.”

In rent-stabilized buildings, DHCR has required free fobs for every lawful occupant, including children, and up to four extra free fobs for guests or employees, with replacements for lost fobs capped at $25.

That changes the purchase. Ordering a few dozen fobs at install costs far less than issuing them one at a time later, and fobs that cost the owner more than the cap make every lost one a loss.

For owner-occupied houses with no regulated apartments, the fob price is between the owner and the tenants, but most owners here simply include them.

“You can’t put an electric strike on old double doors.”

Installers who say this usually mean they only carry single-door hardware. A door-rebuilding firm on Brownstoner said about half the double doors it works on get an electric latch.

The real questions are where the active leaf latches, whether the passive leaf’s flush bolts hold, and how the cable gets into the frame without crossing the face of the door. Each has a standard answer.

What does rule out a strike is a door that is out of square or dropping on its hinges, and that is a carpentry repair we do before the strike goes in.

“Anything new on a landmarked house needs a public hearing.”

The Commission says 95 percent of its permit approvals are issued by staff, and work that conforms to its rules, such as a small panel at a door opening attached in a mortar joint with concealed conduit, is the kind staff handle.

The guidebook is specific about limits: one intercom panel per door opening, nothing on decorative surfaces or cast iron, and conduit concealed. Proposals that break those limits are the ones that go to a hearing.

Much of an access job, the strike in the frame, the controller in the cellar, the reader on the inner vestibule door, is not visible from the street at all.

“A battery smart lock on the stoop door is all a small building needs.”

On a one-family house with light traffic, a good smart lock on one door can be a fair choice. On a multi-family brownstone it runs into the two-door problem: it cannot see the vestibule door, cannot let the mail carrier in without sharing a code, and most models need a single-leaf deadbolt that original paired doors do not have.

Battery locks also depend on someone noticing a low battery. A Brownstoner reply on brownstone entries suggested keeping a key lock on the inner door in case the electronics fail, which is good advice whatever the outer door uses.

A wired controller keeps credentials on site, runs on a backup battery sized for the building, and reports a door that fails.

“Face recognition is the modern replacement for fobs.”

In 2019 tenants of a rent-stabilized complex in Brownsville filed legal opposition with DHCR to their landlord’s application for a facial recognition entry system, and the case became a citywide argument about biometric entry.

The city’s Tenant Data Privacy law that followed treats biometric data as part of smart access, with consent, minimum-data and deletion rules. In a walk-up, that makes face recognition a legal project as much as a hardware one.

Some panels we install have the feature. In residential buildings here we leave it off unless the owner has decided, with counsel, that it fits the law and the tenants have agreed.
