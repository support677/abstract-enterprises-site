The County at a Glance

Where Ulster County's Elevators Actually Are

Ulster County is mostly houses. The U.S. Census Bureau counts 87,657 housing units in the county and puts the owner-occupied rate at 69.9 percent, which tells you that most people here live in single-family homes, farmhouses and small multi-family buildings with stairs, not elevators. That is exactly why elevator buildings matter so much where they do exist: they are where the county's older residents, its supportive-housing tenants, its students, its patients and its government workers are concentrated, and they are where one unlocked car can let a stranger reach every floor of a building full of people who cannot easily deal with that. Almost a quarter of the county's residents, 23.5 percent, are 65 or older, and a large share of the new elevator buildings opened since 2022 are senior or supportive housing.

Kingston

Kingston: Stockade Stone, Midtown Lofts and a Wave of New Mid-Rise Housing

Most of the elevator access control work in Ulster County happens in and around the City of Kingston, because that is where the elevators are. The city's building stock falls into three groups that need quite different thinking: older masonry buildings in Uptown and along the Rondout that had elevators added or modernized long after they were built; former industrial buildings in Midtown being turned into apartments; and the new four- and five-story buildings that have gone up since 2020, many of them affordable, senior or supportive housing.

Uptown and the Stockade District

Uptown Kingston is the old Stockade, and the city protects it. Kingston's Historic Landmarks Preservation Commission has overseen the Stockade as a local landmark district since 1974, alongside the Rondout-West Strand, Fair Street, Chestnut Street and Wilbur districts. The commission's own guidance says it reviews exterior work visible from the public right-of-way, while interior changes that do not affect how the building reads from the street can be handled at the desk. For elevator access control that is usually good news: a reader inside the cab, a reader by the lobby call button and an access panel in a basement machine room are interior work. What can draw review is anything outside, such as a new entry intercom panel on a street-facing facade or exposed conduit up the front of the building. When a project includes a new front-door station, we plan its placement with the building so that any landmark review happens before the work is scheduled, not after.

Older Uptown buildings also bring physical quirks: thick masonry walls, plaster ceilings, elevator shafts squeezed into spaces that were never designed for them, and machine rooms in basements that see water in a heavy storm. We route cable through existing chases where we can, mount panels high and away from floor drains, and leave a labeled drawing so the next person does not have to open walls to find our wires. Proposed Uptown projects, such as the long-planned Kingstonian with its 420-space garage and the roughly 50 affordable apartments the city has chosen developers for on the North Front Street municipal lot, are not built yet, and Hudson Valley One reported in June 2026 that the Kingstonian had not left the planning stage. If either goes ahead, garage-level elevator stops shared with the public will be the main access question, and the time to answer it is during design.

Midtown: Energy Square and the Barrel Factory

Midtown is where Kingston's industrial past and its new housing meet. Energy Square, at the corner of Cedar Street and Iwo Jima Lane, is a five-story building with 57 apartments and two elevators above about 11,000 square feet of ground-floor commercial and community space. A few blocks away, the Barrel Factory project restored a 120-year-old barrel-factory warehouse that had stood vacant for 15 years and is adding a four-story, 100-unit apartment building next to it. Buildings like these put members of the public in the lobby by design: shoppers, people using a community program, visitors to an arts space. The elevator is then the line between public and private, and a reader in the car or at the lobby call button keeps the residential floors for residents while the ground floor stays open.

Mixed-use buildings also raise the question of schedules. A community space may be open to the public on weekday evenings and closed on Sundays; a commercial tenant may need its staff to reach a second-floor office early in the morning. Elevator access control with time-based floor permissions handles both, but someone has to write the schedule down, and the person who knows the hours is the building manager, not us. Our job is to turn that schedule into programming and to test it floor by floor in every car before it goes live.

Golden Hill and the former county jail site

Golden Hill Apartments, at 2106 Fernway Road on the site of the former county jail, opened in November 2025 with 164 units in two mid-rise apartment buildings, four townhomes and a central community building. Nearly half the apartments are reserved for residents 62 and older, and 48 are supportive housing. The community hub includes a fitness center and a child-care facility that is open to the public. A campus like this is a good example of why a property manager should think in terms of one credential list rather than one system per door: the same resident needs the front door of their own building, the elevator to their own floor, the community building and perhaps the fitness room, while a child-care parent needs the community building and nothing else. A single access platform with permission groups does this cleanly; separate systems bought one at a time do not.

The Kingston Housing Authority's new senior building

The Kingston Housing Authority manages several hundred apartments across the city, including senior and disabled housing at Wiltwyck Gardens, the Colonial Gardens Addition and Brigham Senior Housing. In July 2026 the Governor's office announced the start of Phase II of the authority's redevelopment: Penn Court is being replaced by a new four-story building with 80 one-bedroom apartments for seniors, 30 of them supportive housing for formerly homeless seniors. A new building at this stage is the cheapest place to add elevator access control, because the elevator contractor has not yet built the controller and car operating panel, the conduit can go in while the walls are open, and the access system can be tested with the elevator during acceptance rather than as an afterthought.

The Rondout and the waterfront

Along the Rondout Creek, the building question is water. Older buildings near the creek and the Hudson often keep their elevator machine room in the basement, which is the worst place for the access panel's battery and power supply. Where the building allows, we put our panel and battery on the first floor or higher and run only cable down to the machine room, so a wet basement means a cleanup rather than a dead elevator system. The same thinking applies to any building in the city's floodplain, where Kingston's own permitting includes a separate floodplain permit for development.

New Paltz

New Paltz: Residence Halls, Student Apartments, Net-Zero Condos and Senior Housing

New Paltz is the county's second center of elevator buildings, and it is a very different place from Kingston. The SUNY New Paltz campus has 14 residence halls across the Parker, Peregrine and South complexes, and the college keeps upgrading them; when Bevier Hall, a 212-bed hall, was renovated, the work included a new elevator along with a new roof, entrance and fire-protection upgrades. According to U.S. News, about 38 percent of the college's students live in college housing and the rest live off campus, which means the village and town also hold apartment buildings whose tenants change almost entirely every year.

Campus buildings run on the campus card system

A college residence hall almost always belongs to a campus-wide card system that the college's own staff administers. University elevator access control in that setting is a question of integration rather than a stand-alone purchase: the elevator reader and the floor-release interface have to report to the platform that already controls the hall's doors, so a student who is moved to another hall, or who leaves mid-semester, loses elevator access in the same step as door access. When a campus or a campus contractor brings us in, we install and wire the elevator-side hardware to the campus platform's specification and coordinate the controller tie-in with the elevator contractor; we do not set up a parallel system with its own card list.

Off-campus student buildings and the May turnover

Privately owned student and young-professional apartment buildings near campus have a problem most residential buildings never face: a large share of the credentials in circulation stop being valid on the same week every spring, and a new set has to be issued in late summer. If the building still uses a shared elevator code or a key switch, last year's tenants keep access forever. A credential-based elevator access system with expiry dates set at enrollment solves this without anyone remembering to do it: every fob, card or phone key issued to a lease ending May 31 simply stops working on June 1, and the audit log shows that it did. That is one of the strongest arguments for elevator access control in a college town, and it is why we set up permission groups by lease term, not just by floor, in buildings like these.

Zero Place and small mixed-use condominiums

Zero Place on North Chestnut Street, which its owners formally opened in May 2022, is a four-story, net-zero-energy mixed-use building with 46 residential units over more than 8,000 square feet of commercial space. Small condominium elevator buildings like this one are usually run by a volunteer board and a part-time manager, so the system has to be simple enough to administer from a phone or laptop without training sessions every time a board member changes. Condominium elevator access control in a building of this size is nearly always Level 1: one reader in the car, every resident credential enabling the car, and the commercial tenants' customers kept on the ground floor.

Harmony Hall and senior residences

Harmony Hall, a 51-unit affordable senior building in the Village of New Paltz, was completed in June 2026. The state's announcement notes that its apartments are accessible and adaptable, with six units for residents with mobility disabilities and three for residents with sensory disabilities. Senior buildings like this are where elevator access control has to be most forgiving. We pick readers with a large target, a clear light and an audible tone, mount them where a seated rider can reach, and agree in advance what happens when a resident forgets or cannot use their credential, whether that is a staff override, a keypad code as backup, or an intercom call to the office that releases the car for one trip.

Industrial Reuse

Big Floor Plates, New Tenants: Elevators in Converted Plants and Mills

The largest building complex in the county is the former IBM Kingston plant, now marketed as iPark 87, on both sides of Enterprise Drive in the Town of Ulster. Its owners describe roughly 200 acres and more than a million square feet of existing buildings, with four buildings on the East Campus covering warehouse, manufacturing, data center and office uses, and plans that include multifamily housing and media studios. The Ulster County Comptroller published a report in April 2026 on the site's redevelopment, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has a site profile for cleanup work at the former IBM facility. Whatever happens there next, the pattern is common across the Hudson Valley: a building designed for one company is being split among many, and the elevators that once moved one company's staff and freight now serve several tenants who should not be able to wander onto each other's floors.

Freight and service cars shared by several tenants

Converted industrial buildings tend to have a few large freight or service elevators rather than banks of passenger cars. Restricting a freight car is still elevator access control, but the design is different: the car may be run by attendants with keys, may have manual gates, and may need to stop at a loading dock level for anyone with a delivery. In those buildings we typically put the reader where the operator or rider can reach it without stepping into the path of a pallet jack, give each tenant a permission group for its own floors plus the dock, and leave the building manager a way to open the car fully for a move-in day. Employee-only elevator access for a lab, data hall or production floor works the same way, with a tighter list and, if the tenant wants it, a biometric reader.

Old buildings, hidden hazards

Industrial buildings from the mid-twentieth century can contain materials that should not be drilled or disturbed without testing, and the EPA site profile for the former IBM facility mentions cleanup involving regulated asbestos-containing material. Before we drill or pull cable through an old plant building, we ask the owner or tenant for any hazardous-materials survey, use existing chases and cable trays where we can, and stop and ask if we find anything unexpected. That can add a step to the schedule, but it is the right way to work in these buildings, and it is part of why our proposals for converted buildings list the cable route explicitly.

Medical and Public Buildings

Hospitals, Medical Offices and County Buildings: Open Floors and Staff Floors

Healthcare and government buildings share one design problem: the public must be able to reach some floors easily, and must not be able to reach others at all. Getting that split right, and keeping it right as departments move, is most of the work.

Medical buildings around Kingston and Ellenville

The county's two hospital campuses, HealthAlliance Hospital on Mary's Avenue in Kingston and Ellenville Regional Hospital on Healthy Way in Ellenville, anchor clusters of doctors' offices, clinics and outpatient services. HealthAlliance reopened in December 2022 after a $113 million project that added a new two-story, 79,000-square-foot building, renovated 48,000 square feet of the older structure and built a 25,000-square-foot emergency care center, and its former Broadway campus is planned as a walkable health village. We name these hospitals only as landmarks: large hospitals generally have their own security departments and access platforms, and we do not claim them as customers. The buildings we are usually asked about are the medical office buildings, clinics and practices nearby, where healthcare elevator security means keeping patients on the floors of the practices they are visiting, keeping records and supply floors staff-only, and opening floors to patients only during office hours.

County and municipal buildings in Kingston

The Ulster County Office Building at 244 Fair Street is a useful example of the split. According to the county comptroller's facilities report, its basement and first floor hold the DMV and mail room, the second floor the County Clerk, and the upper floors offices, the County Attorney and Comptroller, and on the sixth floor the legislative chambers, budget office and County Executive. Government building elevator access control in a building laid out like that usually leaves the public floors open during business hours, puts staff-only floors on credentials at all times, and opens the chambers floor to the public on meeting nights by schedule. Municipal building elevator security in a smaller town hall or court building follows the same logic on a smaller scale, and the agency's own security office normally sets the rules. Our part is to install, wire and program the elevator side to fit the platform the agency already uses wherever that is possible, so staff carry one badge and one audit trail covers both doors and elevator.

The Mechanics

What a Reader in the Elevator Actually Controls

An elevator access control system does not drive the elevator. The elevator's own controller still decides when the car moves, which way, how fast and where it stops. What our equipment does is tell the controller, through a set of dry-contact relay outputs or an interface board, which calls it should accept right now. When nobody has presented a valid credential, some or all of the floor buttons in the car, or the call button in the lobby, are simply ignored. When someone presents a credential the system recognizes, the outputs for the floors that person is allowed to visit are released for a short window, usually a few seconds, and the rider presses a button as normal. That is how elevator floor restriction works in almost every building, from a single car in a village to a high-rise bank.

There are three basic ways to set it up, and most Ulster County buildings use one or two of them.

  • Car enable. One output tells the controller to accept car calls at all. Before a credential is presented, the floor buttons are dead; afterward, the rider can choose any floor the car serves. This is the simplest form of elevator access control and the one most four- and five-story residential buildings need, because the goal is to keep outsiders off the upper floors, not to keep residents off each other's floors.
  • Floor-by-floor lockout. Each controlled floor has its own output. A credential releases only the floors its holder is allowed to reach. This is what elevator floor access permissions mean in practice: the resident of the third floor can choose 3 and the lobby, the office tenant on 4 can choose 4, and the roof stays locked for everyone except maintenance staff. It needs one relay or I/O point per controlled floor in every car, which is why it costs more and is quoted per building.
  • Landing (hall call) control. A reader at the call button on a particular landing, often the lobby, a garage level or a basement, decides whether that call button works at all. It stops someone walking into a parking level or a back entrance and calling the car without a credential. It can be combined with either of the other two.

Elevator destination dispatch, where riders enter their floor at a kiosk in the lobby and the system assigns them a car, works differently. There are no buttons in the car to restrict; instead the access system tells the dispatch system which floors a credential may request. That integration is done through the elevator manufacturer's interface and is always coordinated with the elevator contractor. It is rare in Ulster County because destination dispatch usually appears in large office towers, but the same principle applies if a building here installs it: our system decides who may go where, and the elevator system decides how to get them there.

Two details catch boards out. First, a reader that only enables the car still lets an authorized rider take a guest anywhere the car goes; if that matters, the building needs floor-by-floor control. Second, the stairs are not part of the elevator system. Restricting the elevator without thinking about stairwell doors, roof doors and fire exits can create a false sense of security. We point out on the survey where the stair doors leave a gap, and if the building wants those doors on the same system, we can quote them as access control installation for the doors themselves.

Two Trades, One Job

Our Wires, Their Controller: How the Work Is Split Under New York's Elevator Licensing Law

Every elevator access control job has two halves, and in New York State they belong to two different licensed trades. Article 33 of the New York State Labor Law, administered by the Department of Labor, requires businesses and individuals who install, alter, maintain, service or repair elevators to be licensed by the state as elevator contractors and elevator mechanics. Connecting anything to an elevator controller, or changing how the car operating panel behaves, is work on the elevator. We are a licensed low-voltage security contractor, NYS license #12000287431; we are not an elevator contractor, and we do not open the controller or rewire the car's buttons.

Building permits and code enforcement in Ulster County

Outside New York City, the New York State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code is enforced locally. In the City of Kingston, the Building Safety and Zoning Enforcement office, at the back of the Rondout fire station on Garraghan Drive, handles all building inspection and permitting, and the city code requires a building permit for work that must conform to the Uniform Code or the Energy Code. In the towns and villages, it is the local code enforcement officer. Whether a particular elevator access project needs a permit, and whether the elevator side triggers an inspection, depends on the scope and on how the local office reads it; the elevator contractor's work is the part most likely to draw attention. We do not guess on your behalf. We tell you what our scope involves, the elevator contractor tells you what theirs involves, and the building checks with its code office before work starts when either of us thinks it might be needed.

New York City rules that do not apply here

Much of what is written online about elevator access control in New York is about New York City. NYC's Department of Buildings bulletins and its Local Law 63 of 2021, the tenant data privacy law for smart-access systems in residential buildings, apply in the five boroughs. They do not apply in Kingston, New Paltz or anywhere else in Ulster County. We still borrow their good ideas where they make sense: we recommend that any residential building using fobs, phones or face readers keep a short written policy on what the system records, who can see it and when it is deleted, and that any building offering face recognition also offers a fob or card to anyone who prefers not to enroll.

When Things Go Wrong

Firefighters' Service, Long Outages and a Dropped Internet Line

A board's first worry about elevator access control is usually safety: what happens in a fire, what happens when the power goes out, what happens when the system itself fails. The answers are decided in writing before installation, not discovered afterward.

Fire recall always wins

When a building's fire alarm recalls the elevators, or a firefighter takes control of a car with the fire-service key, the elevator's own fire-service operation takes over. The New York State Uniform Code references the ASME A17.1 elevator safety code, and New York City's Department of Buildings spells out the principle for its own buildings: equipment added to a car or hall station must not impede normal or emergency operation and must go inoperative during firefighters' service. We design every Ulster County installation on that principle. The elevator contractor confirms during the connection that recall and firefighters' operation behave exactly as before, and we test with them before the system is handed over. Elevator access control fire recall coordination is not optional, and it is one more reason the elevator contractor is part of the job.

Power outages and two utilities

Ulster County is served by two electric utilities, Central Hudson and NYSEG, and both report outages here after storms. If the elevator has emergency power, our panel's backup battery keeps the access system in its normal state through a typical outage. For a long one, the building chooses ahead of time whether the car should fail safe, meaning every floor frees up when our battery runs down so nobody is stranded, or fail secure, meaning floors stay locked until power returns. Most residential and senior buildings choose fail-safe for the elevator. Some commercial buildings with a staffed desk choose fail-secure for specific floors. Elevator access control fail-safe design is a decision for the building, not for us, and we write it into the handover documents.

When the internet drops

Many modern systems are cloud-based elevator access control platforms: the manager adds and removes credentials from a browser or phone app, and the panel in the building syncs with the cloud. In a rural building where the internet connection is unreliable, the important question is what the panel does when the connection drops. A good panel keeps its credential list and schedules locally and goes on making decisions on its own, then catches up when the link returns; only changes made in the app wait until the connection is back. We specify panels that work that way for Ulster County buildings, and where the machine room has no reliable wired internet we can price a cellular link as its own line rather than leave the system stranded. Elevator access control communication failure is then an inconvenience for the manager, not a lockout for residents.

Accessibility and the human override

Every building gets a documented way to let a rider up who cannot use the reader at that moment: a staff override at the desk or in the office, an intercom call that releases the car for one trip, a backup keypad code, or a key switch kept for staff. Senior and supportive buildings such as the newer ones in Kingston, New Paltz and Highland need this most, and we rehearse it with staff before we leave.

Credentials

Fob, Card, Code, Phone or Face: Picking What Riders Carry

The credential is the part residents and staff actually touch, so it decides whether the system feels like a help or a hurdle. Most elevator readers sold today are multi-technology readers that accept more than one type, which lets a building mix credentials: phones for younger residents, fobs for everyone else, a keypad code for the aide who visits twice a week.

A word on proximity fobs. Many buildings in the county already have 125 kHz proximity fobs at the front door, and an elevator reader can usually read them, so residents keep one fob. When a building is replacing its system anyway, elevator encrypted credential systems with secure smart-card technology are worth the modest extra cost, and phone credentials can be issued alongside them. Cold weather is a practical issue in a county where winter is real. Readers inside a heated lobby or car are not affected, but a reader at an unheated garage or vestibule stop should be rated for the temperature range, and riders wearing heavy gloves will find a card or fob easier than a phone or a keypad. We take that into account when we choose where each reader goes.

Visitors, Aides, Couriers

Letting the Right Visitor Ride: Intercom Release, Timed Passes and Deliveries

A locked elevator is only half a system. The other half is how people who do not live or work in the building get where they are supposed to go: family visiting a resident in a senior building, a home health aide on a weekday morning, a plumber called to the fourth floor, a food courier with a bag going cold. Elevator access control with visitor management decides that, and in practice the options are few and well understood.

  • Intercom floor release. The visitor calls the resident from the front-door intercom or video station. When the resident answers and lets them in, the system releases the elevator for that resident's floor only, for one trip, instead of opening the car to every floor. This is the most useful single feature in a residential building, and it is how elevator access control with video intercom works on platforms such as Akuvox, ButterflyMX and 2N that we install and integrate.
  • Timed credentials for aides and caregivers. An aide who visits a resident three mornings a week gets a personal code, fob or phone pass that works only on those days, only during their shift hours and only for their client's floor, and expires when the assignment ends. Nobody shares a resident's fob, and the log shows who came and went.
  • Temporary passes for contractors and guests. A one-day or one-week pass, issued by the manager from the app, for a contractor working on a particular floor or a guest staying over a weekend. The pass ends on its own.
  • A delivery plan. Couriers complain online, on delivery-driver forums, about buildings where the elevator needs a fob and nobody comes down. Most Ulster County buildings solve this with a package room or lockers on the ground floor, and resident-approved intercom release for anything that has to go upstairs. Deciding this up front saves a stream of complaints in the first month.
  • A move-in and move-out mode. The manager can put one car into an open or attendant mode for a scheduled move, then return it to normal, without calling anyone.
Records and Turnover

Turnover, Audit Trails and Keeping the Credential List Honest

The most common failure of an elevator access system is not a broken reader. It is a credential list nobody maintains: fobs belonging to tenants who moved out years ago, staff who left still enrolled, a contractor's code that never expired, a shared "super's code" half the town seems to know. An elevator access control system with audit trail and expiry dates fixes this, but only if someone uses those features.

Permission groups instead of one-off settings

We set up elevator access control permission groups so that the manager assigns a person to a group, not to a list of floors. "Residents, floor 3" or "Medical suite 210 staff" or "Maintenance, all floors, 7 a.m. to 6 p.m." is easier to manage and audit than hundreds of individual settings. When a resident moves from the second floor to the fourth, the manager changes their group, and their elevator floor access key fob updates with it.

Expiry dates at enrollment

For leases, student tenancies, supportive-housing placements, contractors and aides, we set an end date when the credential is issued. Elevator access control with time-based floor permissions works at two levels here: the schedule of the floor itself, such as a community room open until 9 p.m., and the validity dates of each person's credential. Both together mean the system stays clean without anyone remembering to tidy it.

Using the event log

Every elevator access control event logging system records which credential was used, at which reader, at what time and whether it was accepted or denied. That record is useful after an incident, such as damage on an upper floor or a complaint about a stranger in the hallway, because it shows which credentials released that floor in the relevant window. It is also a quiet health check: a reader that has logged nothing for two days is probably broken, and a credential that is rejected every morning probably belongs to a resident who needs help. Tracking elevator access activity this way should be governed by the building's written policy on who can see the log and for what.

Repair and Replacement

When an Existing System Fails: Troubleshooting, Repair and Replacement

We are called as often to fix an elevator access system someone else installed as to install a new one. Elevator access control repair in Ulster County usually starts with a building manager who has a reader that stopped lighting up, residents whose fobs work at the front door but not in the car, or a software login nobody remembers.

The usual causes, in the order we check them

  • Power. A failed power supply, a tripped circuit or a battery that has reached the end of its life is behind a large share of "elevator card reader not working" calls. It is also the cheapest fix.
  • The reader. Readers in cars take knocks from carts, luggage and cleaning, and readers near doors can get wet. A damaged reader is swapped like for like if the model is still available.
  • Wiring and the traveling cable. A conductor in the traveling cable can fail with the car's constant motion. Diagnosing that is shared work with the elevator contractor, because the traveling cable is part of the elevator.
  • Programming and the credential database. A schedule set wrong, a permission group edited by mistake, or a database that was never backed up. This is an elevator access control programming issue, not a hardware fault, and is often fixed remotely once we have access.
  • Communication failure. The panel lost its network link or cloud subscription. Residents may still ride if the panel works offline, but nobody can change anything until it is reconnected.
  • The relay interface. A floor-release relay or I/O board that has failed leaves one floor unreachable or permanently open. Elevator access control relay troubleshooting is done with the elevator contractor present, because the other side of the relay is in the controller.

Repair or replace

When the original installer has gone and the software is obsolete, repair can cost more than it is worth. We document what is there, tell the building what it would take to keep it running, and price an elevator access control system replacement and upgrade alongside it, so the board can compare honestly. Often the cable and sometimes the relays can be reused, which keeps the cost of replacing outdated elevator access control well below a new installation. Elevator access control retrofit installation in an older car is also the moment to move from a shared code or brass key switch to individual credentials.

Taking over someone else's system

If you want us to take over maintenance of an existing system, we start with a paid assessment visit: we confirm the make and model of every component, recover or reset administrator access with the building's authorization, check the battery and power supply, test every floor in every car, and write down what we found. Myths

Four Things Ulster County Boards Hear That Are Not Quite Right

1. "Our building is too small to bother."

Small elevator buildings are often the most exposed: no doorman, no staff on evenings and weekends, and an elevator that will take anyone who slips in behind a resident to any floor. A single Level 1 reader in a four-story building is one of the cheapest security improvements a board can make, and it is the main reason many new senior and supportive buildings are specified with one from the start.

2. "If the elevator is locked, people will be trapped in a fire."

The elevator's fire-service operation overrides the access system, and riders inside a car can always choose the lobby or the exit floor. The stairs are not controlled by the elevator system at all. A properly installed system changes nothing about how the building is evacuated.

3. "We need floor-by-floor control everywhere."

Most residential buildings in the county need the car enabled for residents and nothing more. Floor-by-floor control earns its cost in mixed-use, medical, office and government buildings, and in residential buildings with private amenity, roof or storage floors. Buying it where it is not needed adds cost and complexity for nobody's benefit.

4. "Once it is installed, we can forget about it."

The hardware is the easy part. The credential list, the schedules and the battery need attention. A building that reviews its user list once a month and replaces its backup battery on schedule will have a system that keeps working; a building that does not will, in a few years, have a system that keeps out nobody.

Building New

Specifying Elevator Access in a Building That Is Not Built Yet

Much of Ulster County's new elevator housing has been built or started in the last few years, and more is planned, from the Kingston Housing Authority's new senior building to the multifamily housing proposed at iPark 87. Day to Day

Running the System After Handover

An elevator access system needs a little attention every month and a little more once a year. None of it requires a technician if the building's manager has been trained properly.

  • Monthly: review the user list. Remove anyone who has moved out or left, check that aides' and contractors' passes have end dates, and look for credentials that have not been used in months.
  • When a fob is lost: cancel it from the software. Then issue a replacement from the spares kept on site.
  • Twice a year: test the override. Make sure whoever is on duty knows how to let a rider up who cannot use the reader.
  • Yearly: check the backup battery. Batteries in access panels wear out over a few years; replace them on schedule rather than after an outage shows they were dead.
  • When the elevator company changes: tell the new one. Show their mechanic the diagram in the machine room so they know what our relays do and leave them alone.
  • After any incident: read the log. It is often the fastest way to understand what happened.

FAQ

Ulster County Elevator Access Control: Questions and Answers

If the internet connection drops at our Ulster County building, do the elevator readers stop working?
+

Not if the panel is chosen properly. We specify panels that keep the credential list and schedules locally, so residents keep riding while the link is down; only changes made from the app wait until it returns. If the machine room has no reliable wired internet, we can price a cellular link as its own line after the survey.

Our residents keep several cards in one wallet or phone case. Will the elevator reader still pick out the right one?
+

Usually, but not always. When two or more contactless cards sit together, the reader can see more than one and fail to read any of them. The simple fix is to hold the elevator fob or card on its own, or keep it on a key ring. Choosing a different credential technology from the building's other cards also helps, and we check this on site.

Our New Paltz student apartment building turns over most of its residents every spring. How do we retire last year's credentials without reissuing everyone's?
+

Set an end date on every credential when it is issued, matched to the lease. A fob, card or phone key tied to a lease ending May 31 stops working on its own on June 1, and the audit log shows it. Residents who renew simply get their date extended from the management software; nobody else needs a new credential.

After a break-in on an upper floor, can the system show which credentials took the elevator to that floor?
+

With floor-by-floor control, yes: the event log records which credential released which floor, in which car and at what time. With car-enable only, it shows who enabled the car, not which floor they chose. Who may read the log, and why, should be set out in the building's written policy before an incident happens.

Can one resident's credential keep working after the building's overnight floor lock-down begins?
+

Yes. Floor schedules and personal permissions are separate settings, so a resident who works nights, or a staff member on an early shift, can be placed in a permission group that ignores the overnight lock-down for their own floor while everyone else follows the building schedule. The manager can make that change from the software.

Will a Level 2 face reader still recognize residents wearing winter hats, scarves or medical masks?
+

Face readers vary, and heavy hats, scarves and masks reduce how reliably any of them match. That is why we pair Level 2 readers with a second credential, such as a card or PIN, and why we recommend biometrics mainly for staff-only or restricted floors. We test with real users on site before handover.

How many spare fobs or cards should an Ulster County building keep on hand?
+

Enough to cover lost credentials and new arrivals until the next order arrives. For a small residential building that is usually a handful; for a larger or high-turnover building, more. Keep them locked in the office, unassigned, so the manager can enroll a replacement in minutes instead of waiting for a delivery or a visit.

Our Ulster County building is changing property managers. How do we make sure the new manager takes over the elevator system cleanly?
+

Make sure the administrator accounts are in the building's name, not the outgoing manager's or the installer's. Before the handover, the outgoing manager should export the user list and schedules, and the new manager should be added as an administrator and the old accounts removed. We can help with that transfer if records are missing.

In a senior building, can the system flag a resident whose fob has not been used for several days?
+

Most platforms can produce a report of credentials with no recent activity, and some can send an alert. A few senior and supportive buildings use that as a prompt for a friendly check-in. It only works if residents are told about it and agree, so put it in the building's written policy and treat it as a prompt, not proof.

How long does elevator access control equipment usually last before parts need replacing?
+

Backup batteries wear out first and should be replaced every few years. Readers in busy cars take knocks and are sometimes replaced after damage. Panels and relay boards usually last much longer, and software or cloud platforms need updates over time. A yearly check of the battery and every floor keeps surprises to a minimum.

Our Ellenville building is far from most service companies. What can our own staff handle without waiting for a technician?
+

Quite a lot, with training and spares on site: enrolling and cancelling credentials, issuing aide and contractor passes, changing schedules, reading the log, swapping a damaged reader for the spare, and using the override to let someone up. We confirm changes remotely where the platform allows, and come out for anything beyond that.

What signage and instructions should riders see so they know how to use the elevator reader?
+

A small sign at the reader showing where to tap and what the light and tone mean, a printed sheet given with each credential, and a sign in the lobby with the office number for help. In senior buildings we also walk residents through it in person at changeover. Clear signage cuts the number of calls in the first weeks.
