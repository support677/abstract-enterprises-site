Brooklyn Condos

Most Brooklyn Condo Boards Inherited Their Cameras From a Developer

Ask a Brooklyn condo board who chose its cameras and the honest answer is usually "the sponsor." A large share of the borough's condominium buildings are less than twenty years old. They were designed, wired and outfitted by a developer who sold the units, collected the closing checks and handed the building to an elected board of managers along with a binder, a few keys and, if the board was lucky, a sheet with the recorder password. The cameras in that building were picked to pass a punch list and look tidy in a sales brochure. Nobody asked how many days of footage the association would need when a package walked out of the mailroom, or who would be allowed to export a clip.

That history explains most of the calls we get from Brooklyn condos. The board does not want a camera system; it already has one. It wants a camera system it controls. The integrator who installed it has moved on to the next tower. The recorder sits in a telecom closet nobody has a key to. Half the cameras are aimed at features the sales office wanted to show off, like the amenity lounge, while the bike room and the garage gate, where the actual losses happen, have one fuzzy view between them. Our first job in a Brooklyn condominium is usually an inventory: what exists, what still works, who can log in, and what the board would change if it were starting over.

Three rezonings built most of the borough's condo towers

The towers came in waves, each one set off by a land-use change. On May 10, 2004 the City Planning Commission approved the Downtown Brooklyn plan, which was written around office space and roughly a thousand homes. The homes arrived anyway, and in far greater number: Planetizen, citing a 2016 Borough President report, put the count at more than 11,000 housing units in the first dozen years, against a city projection of 979. A year later the city rezoned 175 blocks of the Williamsburg and Greenpoint waterfront, the action CBS New York says "reshaped the skyline" of north Brooklyn. In November 2021 the City Council approved the Gowanus plan, covering 82 blocks and nearly 8,500 expected homes around the canal.

Many of the buildings those plans produced are rentals, and we do not pretend otherwise. But the condominiums among them share a recognizable camera profile. They were wired for network cameras from day one, with power over Ethernet run to each device and a recorder in a closet or on a rack beside the building's internet equipment. They have glass lobbies, a garage or a valet entrance, a package room, a bike room, a gym, a roof terrace and sometimes a pool. Their cameras are typically from whatever line the integrator carried that year, set up on the integrator's account, with retention left at the factory default. When a board in one of these buildings calls us, the cable is usually good and the problem is control, coverage and documentation.

The other end of the market: split brownstones and boutique buildings

Brooklyn also has thousands of small condominiums that never had a sales office. A row house in Bedford-Stuyvesant, Clinton Hill or Crown Heights becomes three condo units; a four-story walk-up in Park Slope becomes six; a corner building in Prospect Heights gets a new top floor and becomes a ten-unit boutique condo with a retail space at grade. These associations often have no managing agent. The board of managers might simply be every owner in the building, and the person who handles the cameras is whoever is home during the day.

A small condo needs a different kind of plan. Four to eight cameras usually cover the stoop, the vestibule, the garden-level door under the stoop, the rear yard and the cellar. The recorder fits on a shelf. The question that matters most is not resolution but agreement: in a building where neighbors are also the entire board, a camera aimed at the shared rear yard can become a dispute if three owners want it and the garden-unit owner, whose patio it overlooks, does not. We draw the plan so each owner can see exactly what each camera sees before anyone votes.

A condo full of renters still needs owner-level decisions

A Brooklyn condo is rarely a building of owners alone. The New York City Comptroller's March 2024 spotlight on the homeowner housing market counted 318,000 occupied condominium apartments in the city and found that 60% of condo units are occupied by renters, a share it says has risen since the pandemic. That matters for cameras in two ways. First, the people walking through the lobby and the garage are often not the people who voted on the cameras, so notice and signage carry more weight. Second, footage requests often come from renters through their landlord, the unit owner, which means the board needs a written process for who may ask for a clip and through whom.

What Brooklyn condo boards actually lose

The camera requests we see from Brooklyn condominiums follow a pattern, and it is not the dramatic break-in people picture. It is small, repeated loss in shared spaces: a box taken from an open package shelf, an e-bike battery unclipped in the bike room, a car door dinged on a garage ramp with no view of the ramp, a roof deck furniture set scratched after a party nobody will admit to, a dog left to foul the courtyard, a tradesperson who propped the service door open and left. Each of those incidents ends with a board member asking the same question: can we see who did it? A camera plan is a list of those questions with an answer for each one.

Waterfront glass towers

The towers along Kent Avenue, the Greenpoint shoreline and the DUMBO waterfront face west and south over the river, which is lovely for residents and hard on cameras. A lobby camera looking toward a glass wall sees a silhouette every afternoon unless it has strong wide dynamic range and is placed to look across the light rather than into it. These buildings also tend to have several separate amenity floors, a garage with a ramp and a gate, a loading dock and a package room large enough to need two views. The recorder is often a rack unit sized when the building opened, now short on channels. The usual job is to add channels or a second recorder, re-aim what exists, replace the handful of cameras that never produced a usable image, and move every account from the integrator's name to the association's.

Downtown Brooklyn high-rises

Downtown condos sit among offices, a university campus, a courthouse district and the busiest shopping streets in the borough, so their lobbies see a steady flow of couriers, food deliveries and visitors. The desk staff, where there is a desk, need cameras that show a face at the door and a full figure at the package counter, plus a monitor they can actually read. Many of these buildings share a ground floor with retail condo units. The declaration usually decides which doors belong to the residential association and which to the commercial owner, and the camera plan has to follow those lines so that each party records only what it owns.

New mid-rise elevator condos

The eight-to-twelve-story condos that filled in Gowanus, Fourth Avenue in Park Slope, Prospect Heights and parts of Crown Heights and Bushwick are where we do the most complete camera installations, because their sponsors often installed only a few cameras or none. There is rarely a doorman. A part-time super or a managing agent handles problems remotely. Here the essential views are the street door and vestibule, the package area, the elevator cab or landings, the stair doors to the roof, the bike room and the rear or side yard. Remote viewing for the agent matters more than a lobby monitor, because nobody sits in the lobby.

Loft and factory conversions

Converted warehouses and factories in Williamsburg, Clinton Hill and along the Gowanus have walls of brick or concrete two feet thick, ceilings of twelve feet or more and corridors that run the length of a block. Cable routes are the hard part. We look for the old freight shaft, abandoned conduit and the paths the conversion contractor used for the intercom and fire alarm, then plan cameras around routes that already exist. Where the ceiling is exposed, cable runs neatly in conduit and nobody minds; where it is finished, we price the concealed runs as an extra and show them on the plan before the board orders anything.

Brownstone and row-house condos

A three-to-six-unit condo carved out of a brownstone has no lobby to speak of. The front camera covers the stoop and the sidewalk approach; a second covers the garden-level entrance under the stoop, where packages often get left; a third or fourth covers the rear yard and the back door; one more goes in the cellar near the meters and storage. The recorder lives in a closet or in the cellar on a shelf off the floor. In a historic district, the front camera's position has to respect the Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidance, which we cover in the privacy section below.

Southern Brooklyn elevator condos

Condos in Bay Ridge, Sheepshead Bay, Brighton Beach and Coney Island are often mid-century buildings converted from rentals or newer buildings with surface parking lots. Lot coverage with full-color cameras, entrance coverage for buildings without staff, and simple phone viewing for board members who are not tech enthusiasts come up most. Near the water, we specify housings and mounts rated for salt air and steady wind, and we avoid cheap brackets that rust within a couple of winters.

Coverage

The Shared Spaces Brooklyn Condo Boards Ask Us to Cover

Every condo camera on our plans has a job written beside it: identify faces at the street door, follow a person from the package shelf to the exit, read the time a car came down the ramp. A camera without a job is a camera nobody watches. Here is how those jobs play out in the spaces Brooklyn associations bring up most, with what we have learned about each.

Garage ramps, gates and parking levels

Garages are where Brooklyn condos lose the most money per incident: a scraped car, a stolen catalytic converter, a bicycle taken from a rack by the wall. The gate itself needs one camera from inside looking out, aimed so a car's front plate and the driver's window are both in the frame as it waits for the gate. The ramp needs a view of the full slope. Each parking aisle needs coverage of the drive lane, not the individual spaces, because a lane view shows every person who walked to every car. Full-color low-light cameras earn their price here; a black-and-white night image cannot tell a residents' grey sedan from a visitor's silver one. Where a garage has EV chargers, a camera on the charging row answers the disputes about who unplugged whom.

Package rooms and parcel lockers

The package room is the single most requested camera in a Brooklyn condo. One camera at the door is not enough; it shows who entered but not what they took. A second camera from the far corner, looking back across the shelves toward the door, shows hands on boxes. If the building has parcel lockers in the lobby instead of a room, a camera above and slightly to the side of the locker bank shows who opened which bay. We set the recorder's motion zones on the shelves, so a board member searching for a missing parcel can jump straight to every event at that shelf instead of scrolling through hours of an empty room.

Bike rooms and storage cages

Brooklyn condo owners ride, and e-bikes have made bike rooms a target. A bike room camera belongs at the door, covering everyone who comes in, plus one view down the length of the racks. In storage-cage rooms, we aim along the aisle rather than into individual cages: owners keep personal belongings there, and a camera that shows the inside of a neighbor's locker invites complaints. Battery charging in bike rooms is a fire-safety question for the board and the building's insurer, not something cameras solve, but a clear camera view does show whether the room's rules are being followed.

Lobbies, vestibules and the street door

At the street door we want two things: a face, and a full figure. The vestibule camera sits at roughly head height off to one side so it sees faces as people reach for the door, not the tops of their heads. A second, wider camera covers the lobby itself and the path to the elevators. In glass-front Brooklyn lobbies, placement fights the sun; we often mount the face camera inside the vestibule looking out at an angle, or recess it into a soffit, so the bright street is not filling the frame. Where the building has a video intercom, its built-in camera is useful but not a replacement; it looks at whoever presses the button, not at the person who slips in behind them.

Elevator cabs and landings

Elevator cameras are a common request in Brooklyn condo towers and in mid-rises without a doorman, because the elevator is the only route to every floor. A camera in the cab ceiling corner sees everyone who rides. Getting its video out of a moving car is the challenge. We use wireless elevator bridge kits that send the picture from the cab roof to a receiver in the shaft or machine room, which avoids touching the traveling cable. Anything that involves the elevator controller, the car's own wiring or the shaft beyond the bridge mounting belongs to the building's elevator contractor, and we coordinate the visit with them. Landing cameras on each floor are an alternative where the board prefers not to put a camera in the cab.

Stairwells, roof doors and service entrances

The doors people prop open are the doors that need a camera. Roof access doors in buildings with a shared terrace, the service entrance by the trash room, the stair door at the garage level and the side gate to the alley all fall into that group. A camera on the inside of each, aimed at the door and the first few feet beyond it, shows who opened it and whether they let anyone in. Pairing these cameras with a door contact that flags a door left open is possible through the recorder's alarm input on many models; it is a simple add-on that flags the event on the recorder, not a service where someone watches for you.

Roof decks, courtyards and rear yards

Roof decks and courtyards are amenities owners pay for, and boards want them protected without feeling watched. We place amenity cameras at the entrance to the space and along its edges, aimed inward over the shared deck or lawn. A roof camera in Brooklyn almost always has a view of neighboring buildings, terraces and windows; it gets aimed and, where the recorder supports it, privacy-masked so that neighboring windows are blacked out in the recording. In a brownstone condo where the rear yard is divided among units or assigned to the garden unit, the declaration may make the yard a limited common element, and the board should settle who decides before a camera goes up.

Laundry rooms, mail areas and corridors

Cellar laundry rooms produce a surprising share of condo complaints, from vandalized machines to disputes over clothing left in dryers. A single camera at the door covering the machines is usually enough. Mailboxes get one camera, aimed at the box bank from the side. Residential corridors are where boards should think hardest: a corridor camera can show the doors of every unit on that floor, and some owners find that intrusive. Many Brooklyn boards stop at the elevator landing and the stair doors and leave the corridor uncovered; others cover long corridors in towers where a fire stair opens mid-hall. Either is a defensible choice; the point is to make it on purpose.

Spaces we never cover

  • Private homes: no lens looks into an apartment, whether past an open entry door or through glass from a terrace.
  • Restrooms, locker or changing rooms at a gym or pool, and any staff changing area.
  • A neighboring building's windows, which get masked out of any roof or rear-yard camera.
  • Audio, which stays off on every common-area camera we install.

Resolution, lenses and low light

Boards often ask for the highest megapixel number available. A better question is what each camera must show at what distance. A 4-megapixel turret with a good sensor recognizes a face at a lobby door; the same resolution across a seventy-foot parking aisle only shows shapes. For long views we use varifocal lenses or move the camera closer. For dim garages and cellars we lean on cameras with larger sensors and full-color low-light performance, plus a modest light source where the board agrees to one. Wide dynamic range is non-negotiable at any glass entrance in Brooklyn's newer buildings.

Recorders, storage and how long footage lasts

A condo's recorder is sized by three numbers: how many cameras, how much detail each records, and how many days the board wants to keep. A 16-camera mid-rise keeping 30 days at sensible settings needs very different drives from a 48-camera tower keeping 60. We calculate it, show the board the math in the proposal and set retention to the agreed figure rather than letting the drives simply fill up. Motion-based recording stretches storage in quiet spaces like storage rooms; continuous recording is the right setting at the street door and the garage gate, where the board will want every second.

Network design and keeping the system private

Condo cameras should sit on their own network segment, separate from the building's Wi-Fi and from any owner's devices. The recorder reaches the internet only for remote viewing, through the manufacturer's secured app connection or a properly configured remote-access path, never through ports opened on the router for anyone to find. Default passwords get changed on day one. Firmware gets updated at installation, and the board's documentation lists how to check for updates. A camera system that anyone on the internet can stumble into is worse than no system at all.

Viewing: the desk, the agent and the board

In a staffed Brooklyn condo, the desk gets a monitor arranged with the views staff actually use: street door, package room, garage gate, elevator. The managing agent gets phone and computer access to live and recorded video. Board members get the access the board decides they should have, which is often live view of common areas without the ability to export. Every person gets a named account. Shared logins make it impossible to know who exported a clip, and in a building where 60 percent of units may be rented, knowing who saw what is part of protecting the association.

Power without electrical work

Network cameras draw their power through the same cable that carries their video, from the recorder or a PoE switch, so most condo camera installations need no new outlets at the camera. The recorder, switches and a battery backup plug into existing outlets in the equipment closet. Where a closet has no outlet at all, or the board wants a dedicated circuit, that is work for the building's licensed electrician; we do not do electrical work, and we coordinate with whoever the association uses.

Privacy and Rules

Condo Camera Privacy in Brooklyn: What the Law and the Condo Documents Say

We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing on this page is legal advice. A board should have its attorney review any camera policy and any question about its own declaration and bylaws. What we can do is point to the sources that shape how we install, and explain the settings we use because of them.

The board's authority over common elements

New York's Condominium Act requires every condo's bylaws to address, among other things, the powers and duties of the board, the operation of the property and the payment of common expenses, and reserves for repairs, improvements and replacements (Real Property Law §339-v). In practice, that usually makes cameras in the lobby, garage, package room and amenity spaces a board decision paid from common charges or reserves. "Usually" is the honest word. Some declarations require an owner vote for improvements above a set cost, and some assign parts of a building, like a rear yard or a roof terrace, to particular units. The documents decide.

The limits run the other way too. In a Habitat Q&A on whether a condo board may watch staff and owners, attorney Andrew Lieb wrote that "a house rule that is unauthorized by these foundational documents is unenforceable" (Habitat, August 2023). A camera policy that rests on solid authority in the bylaws holds up; one invented at a single meeting may not.

Audio stays off

New York's eavesdropping law makes the mechanical overhearing of a conversation without the consent of at least one party a crime (Penal Law §250.00 and §250.05). A microphone in a Brooklyn condo lobby records residents, guests and couriers who never agreed to anything. Many modern cameras ship with a built-in microphone switched on, so turning audio off on every camera is part of our installation checklist, not an afterthought.

Rooms where cameras do not belong

The state's unlawful surveillance statute covers recording people without their knowledge or consent, for no legitimate purpose, in places such as a "bedroom, changing room, fitting room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom, shower" (Penal Law §250.45). Brooklyn condo gyms, pool decks and spa rooms frequently include locker and changing areas; our cameras cover the doors to those rooms from outside and never the inside.

Owners' own cameras and the neighbor's door

Unit owners in Brooklyn condos regularly mount doorbell cameras in common corridors or point a camera from a terrace at a shared roof deck. CooperatorNews, quoting attorney Ricardo Vera, notes that condos "typically prohibit unit owners from unilaterally altering" the common areas and that "a request for board consent is advisable" before an owner mounts a device there (CooperatorNews). A board that wants consistent hallways can adopt a policy, and if it chooses a single approved doorbell model and mounting position, we can install it to that standard for any owner who wants one.

Landmarked facades in Brownstone Brooklyn

Brooklyn has more historic districts than most people realize, and it holds the city's first: the Brooklyn Heights Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on November 23, 1965 (DNAinfo). Park Slope, Fort Greene, Clinton Hill, Cobble Hill, Bedford-Stuyvesant and many other neighborhoods contain districts too. A camera on the front of a building in one of them is a change to a landmarked facade. The LPC's Permit Guidebook prefers cameras off the primary facade where possible and asks that cameras be "well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance." For a brownstone condo, that usually means a small camera tucked under the stoop, inside the vestibule or under a cornice, mounted into mortar joints rather than brownstone, with the application handled before the work. Rear facades are generally treated more flexibly, but the board should confirm with the LPC.

Face recognition and fob data

Some sponsor-installed Brooklyn condo entry systems already use fobs, phone credentials or face recognition. NYC Local Law 63 of 2021 sets rules for those "smart access" systems in class A multiple dwellings, including limits on what data is collected and how long it is kept (intro.nyc, Local Law 63 of 2021). Recording cameras are not entry systems, but a camera feature that identifies residents to unlock a door raises the question. How the law applies to a condominium's owner-occupied units is a question for counsel, and we do not switch on face-matching features without the board's attorney approving it.

Who is allowed to install

New York's Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers, and its rules for the trade name closed-circuit television and video surveillance used to detect or monitor intrusion among the systems covered (19 NYCRR Part 195). Abstract Enterprises Security Systems holds NYS license #12000287431. A board can and should ask any bidder for its license number before signing.

Footage Policy

Writing a Footage Policy a Brooklyn Condo Board Can Defend

The camera system is half the project. The other half is a short written policy that says who sees recordings, who may ask for them and how long they last. Without one, the first contested request, a renter asking for garage footage after a fender-bender, or an owner demanding to see who complained about their dog, turns into an argument at a board meeting. We hand every Brooklyn condo a draft outline at handover; the board and its attorney finish it.

What the industry guidance says

FirstService Residential, one of the largest managers of New York condos and co-ops, published best practices for building cameras in September 2024. It notes that footage is "generally stored for 30 to 90 days depending on the building size," that access usually sits with the property manager or a third-party provider, and that buildings "should maintain a log of requests and a list of anyone who accessed footage" (FirstService Residential). Those three points make a good spine for a Brooklyn condo policy.

Six decisions to put in writing

  1. Retention. A fixed number of days, set on the recorder, with no informal "we'll keep it as long as the drives allow."
  2. Live view. Who may watch live: desk staff, the super, the managing agent, board members.
  3. Playback and export. A smaller group, often only the agent and one board officer, with every export logged.
  4. Requests. How an owner, or a renter through the unit owner, asks for a clip, what information they give, and how fast the building answers before the footage is overwritten.
  5. Police and insurers. Who releases footage to them, and on what basis; the board's attorney should set this.
  6. Accounts. Named logins, removed when a board member leaves office or the agent changes, reviewed once a year.

Signs and owner notice

Signs at the entrances saying the building's common areas are recorded are inexpensive and set expectations for residents, guests and the many renters living in Brooklyn condo units. A short notice to owners when cameras go in, with a map of covered areas, answers most questions before they become complaints. We supply the camera map; the managing agent or board sends the notice.

Exporting a clip the right way

When something happens, the person exporting should save the clip in the recorder's native format and a common video format, note the camera name and time range, and record the export in the log. We show the agent and the designated board member how to do this during handover, and our documentation includes the steps for the exact recorder installed.

Existing Systems

Upgrading, Repairing or Taking Over a Brooklyn Condo's Camera System

Most Brooklyn condo associations that call us already have cameras. The decision in front of the board is whether to fix what is there, upgrade it, or start over. We answer that by testing, not by assuming a newer system is always the answer.

Taking control of a sponsor-installed system

When the developer's integrator installed the cameras, the first task is administrative. We locate the recorder, confirm its make and model, and work with the board to recover or reset the administrator account so the association owns it. That sometimes means a factory reset with the manufacturer's procedure, which wipes stored footage and settings, so we export anything the board needs first. Then every account is rebuilt in the association's name: a primary administrator held by the managing agent or a board officer, a backup administrator, and named user accounts. Cloud services tied to the sponsor or the integrator are moved to the association's email address where the platform allows it.

When repair is enough

A sound system with a few failed parts needs repair, not replacement: a camera with water in the housing, a recorder drive that died, a PoE switch that overheated in an unventilated closet. Recorders in Brooklyn condo closets fail most often from heat and from drives that have run continuously for years. For single-camera and recorder faults outside a larger project, our Brooklyn security camera repair service handles the diagnosis and fix.

When an upgrade makes sense

If the cable is good but the picture is poor, the board can keep the cable and replace cameras and the recorder. Network cable installed by a sponsor in the last fifteen years is usually reusable. Older coaxial cable in converted buildings can often carry modern high-definition analog cameras, or network video over coax with adapters, which saves opening walls. We test each run before recommending either path, and the proposal shows which cable stays and which gets replaced.

When replacement is the answer

Replacement is right when the recorder is unsupported, the cameras cannot be updated, the cable is damaged or undocumented throughout, or the system cannot be secured. In that case we design the new system from the coverage the board needs, reuse whatever conduit and pathways exist, and remove the old equipment so the building is not left with dead cameras on its walls.

Keeping it running

A condo camera system needs a yearly look: clean the lenses on exterior cameras, check every camera is still aimed where the plan says, confirm the recorder is keeping the agreed number of days, check drive health, update firmware and remove accounts for people who have left. A board can book that check-up whenever it likes. There is no monthly contract to sign.

Brooklyn Conditions That Change Where Condo Cameras Go

The same camera behaves differently on a Kent Avenue tower, a Carroll Gardens brownstone and a Brighton Beach elevator building. These are the local conditions we plan around.

River light and glass

West-facing lobbies along the East River get direct low sun in the afternoon and reflected light off the water all day. Cameras aimed toward that glass record dark silhouettes unless they are placed to look across the entrance rather than straight out, and set up with wide dynamic range tuned for the scene. We test the image at the time of day the problem happens, not just when we are installing.

Wind, salt and winter on the waterfront

Roof and corner cameras on waterfront buildings in Greenpoint, Williamsburg, DUMBO, Red Hook, Sheepshead Bay and Coney Island face steady wind and salt air. We use weather-rated housings, stainless or coated hardware and junction boxes that seal the cable connection, and we avoid long, flexible arms that vibrate in wind and blur the picture.

Low-lying cellars and garages

Some Brooklyn condos sit in low-lying blocks near the water or the Gowanus Canal, with garages and cellars below street level. Recorders and switches do not belong on a cellar floor anywhere, and in these buildings we look for an equipment location on a higher floor or at least on a wall shelf well off the ground, with cable entering from above. Garage cameras are mounted high on walls and columns rather than at bumper height.

Brownstone, brick and mortar joints

Brownstone is soft and spalls if drilled carelessly, and a bracket anchored into a crumbling block will not stay aimed. On row-house condos we anchor into mortar joints or sound brick, keep exterior cable runs short and tidy, and bring cable through the wall at the least visible point. Where the facade is landmarked, the mounting detail is part of the approval the board seeks.

Finished tower interiors

Newer Brooklyn condos have stone lobbies, wood-paneled elevator cabs and plaster corridors that owners paid for. We plan routes through ceiling spaces, closets and the paths the original low-voltage contractor used, use low-profile cameras in finished areas, and mark every concealed run on the plan. When a route would require opening a finished surface, the board sees that on paper before it approves.

Construction next door

Brooklyn keeps building, and a new tower next door can block a camera's view, dust a lens or, in the worst case, damage a mounting. Boards in fast-changing neighborhoods should expect to re-aim a camera or two over the years. Our yearly check-up includes confirming every view still matches the plan.

Power outages and battery backup

A short power interruption should not leave a gap in footage. We put the recorder and the PoE switches on a battery backup sized to carry them through brief outages and to let the recorder shut down cleanly in a longer one, so drives are not damaged. The backup plugs into an existing outlet in the equipment room.

Questions We Hear

Brooklyn Condo Camera Questions, Answered Plainly

Our Williamsburg lobby faces the river. Why are faces black on the footage every afternoon?

The camera is looking into the light. When the sun is low over the water, the glass wall is many times brighter than a person's face, and a camera without strong wide dynamic range exposes for the bright background. Moving the face camera so it looks across the entrance from the side, adding a second camera inside the vestibule facing in, and enabling true wide dynamic range usually solves it. We check the result in the late afternoon, when the problem actually happens.

Couriers follow residents into our Downtown Brooklyn lobby. Can cameras help the desk show who came in?

Cameras cannot stop tailgating, but they can make it visible and provable. A face-height camera at the inner door, a wide lobby camera and a package-counter camera together show who entered behind whom and where they went. Some boards add a door-held-open alert through the recorder. If the board wants to physically control entry, that is an access control conversation covered on our condo security systems page.

We are six owners in a Bed-Stuy brownstone with no super. Where should the recorder go?

Somewhere dry, ventilated, lockable and reachable by more than one owner. A hall closet on a middle floor is often best; the cellar works if the recorder sits on a shelf well off the floor and the room stays dry. Avoid putting it inside any single owner's unit, because the association loses access when that owner is away or sells.

Our roof deck looks onto neighbors' terraces. How should a roof camera be aimed?

Aim it down and inward across the deck, mounted at the stair bulkhead or a parapet corner, so the frame is mostly your own floor. Then apply privacy masks on the recorder to black out any neighboring window or terrace that remains in view. The masked areas are never recorded.

Water collects in our garage during heavy storms. Where should the cameras and cable run?

High. Cameras go on upper walls and columns, cable runs along the ceiling in conduit and drops from above, and nothing electronic sits near the floor. The recorder lives on a higher level. If the garage has a sump or drainage issue, that is a building repair; our part is making sure the camera system is out of the water's reach.

The rear yard of our Park Slope condo belongs to the garden unit. Can the board still put a camera on it?

That depends on the declaration. If the yard is a limited common element assigned to the garden unit, the board may need that owner's agreement, or may be limited to a camera covering only the rear door and the building wall. The board's attorney should read the documents. We can draw options for each answer so the decision is easy once the legal question is settled.

A new building next door now blocks our side camera. What can we do?

Usually the camera can be moved a few feet or swapped for a different lens to regain the view of the path that matters. If the old view is gone entirely, we look at what risk that camera was covering and whether a camera somewhere else covers it better. Re-aiming or relocating a camera is a small job priced on its own.

Can our cameras see a delivery van blocking the garage door on the street?

A camera at the garage door looking out will show the apron and the curb directly in front of it, which is often enough to show a vehicle blocking the entrance. We aim building cameras at the building's own entrances and approaches, not up and down the block.

Our board meets monthly. How long does a camera proposal stay valid while we decide?

Our proposals state a validity period, and equipment prices can change after it, but we will refresh a proposal at no charge if the board needs another meeting. Nothing is ordered until the agreement is signed and the deposit is received, so a board never commits by asking for a revision.

We are switching managing agents. How does camera access move to the new firm?

The association's administrator, ideally a board officer, removes the old agent's accounts and creates new named accounts for the new firm. If the outgoing agent held the only administrator login, recovering it is the same process as a sponsor takeover. We can do the handover in one short visit or remotely when the recorder's remote access is set up.

Reality Check

Things Brooklyn Condo Boards Hear About Cameras That Do Not Hold Up

"New buildings come with good cameras, so there is nothing to plan"

New buildings come with cameras chosen by the developer, aimed where the sales office wanted them and set up on someone else's account. The cable is often excellent; the coverage and control frequently are not. A board taking over a new Brooklyn condo should audit the system in its first year, while the sponsor's people can still be reached.

"The intercom camera covers the front door"

An intercom's camera wakes up when someone presses a button and looks at that person. It does not record the person who walks in behind, the courier who props the door, or the lobby. It complements a door camera; it does not replace one.

"More cameras means more security"

Ten well-placed cameras with jobs beat thirty aimed at walls. Extra cameras cost storage, attention and money every year. The plan should start with the incidents the board wants to answer and add cameras only where they answer one.

"Cloud cameras mean no recorder to worry about"

Cloud systems move the recorder off-site and replace it with a subscription per camera, forever, and they depend on the building's internet connection for every frame. For a few cameras that can make sense. For a condo with twenty or forty, a local recorder with secure remote viewing usually costs far less over the life of the system and keeps recording when the internet drops.

"Anyone handy can mount a camera"

Mounting is the easy part. Licensing, cable testing, network security, retention settings, privacy masking, account setup and documentation are what separate a system the board can rely on from a collection of cameras. New York requires security installers to be licensed by the Department of State, and a board can ask for the number.

"Footage is kept until someone deletes it"

Recorders overwrite the oldest footage when drives fill, so the real retention is whatever the drive size allows, often far less than boards assume. Setting a fixed retention period, and sizing the drives to meet it, is the only way to know what will still be there when an owner reports a problem a week later.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Condo Camera Work Across Brooklyn

These are patterns our technicians see again and again in Brooklyn condominiums. No building or client is identified.

  • The recorder is in the hottest closet in the building. Sponsor integrators often put the recorder in the telecom room next to the internet equipment, with no ventilation. Drives that run hot fail early. A small change, a vented shelf or a different closet, adds years.
  • Half the cable labels are wrong. When we audit a sponsor-installed system, it is common to find cameras labeled for the wrong floor or the wrong door. We relabel every camera and every cable end so the next person does not have to guess.
  • The camera everyone relies on is pointed at the ceiling. Cameras get bumped by movers, cleaners and ladders. A five-minute check of every view, once a year, catches it.
  • Bike rooms get one camera and need two. One at the door shows who entered. One down the racks shows what they touched.
  • The board's "admin" is a former board member. Accounts outlive terms of office. Removing old accounts is as important as adding new ones.
  • Brownstone owners want small and quiet. In row-house condos the best camera is the one neighbors barely notice: compact, matte, tucked under the stoop or inside the vestibule.

A checklist before the board signs

  • Does every camera on the plan have a written purpose?
  • Are concealed runs listed and priced separately?
  • Is retention stated in days, and are the drives sized to meet it?
  • Will every login be in the association's name, with named user accounts?
  • Is audio off on every camera?
  • Are ongoing fees, if any, stated in the proposal?
  • Does the contractor hold a New York State installer license, and will it provide an insurance certificate naming the condominium once the job is awarded?
  • Will the board receive a labeled camera map and export instructions at handover?

Brooklyn Condo Security Camera FAQ

Do the building's cameras treat renters in our condo any differently from owners?
+

No. Common-area cameras record everyone who uses the shared spaces the same way. What differs is paperwork: renters usually learn about the cameras through their landlord, the unit owner, or a lobby notice, and a renter's request for footage normally goes through that owner under the board's written policy.

Will a camera on our brownstone condo's stoop need Landmarks approval?
+

If the building is in a historic district or is an individual landmark, a camera on the front facade is a change the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews. Its Permit Guidebook prefers cameras off the primary facade where possible and well spaced. We plan a small, discreet mounting, often under the stoop or inside the vestibule, and the board files before work starts.

Can the garage cameras capture license plates as cars come down the ramp?
+

A camera dedicated to the gate, aimed at the lane where cars stop and set up for moving vehicles, can record readable plates in most Brooklyn condo garages. A general overview camera usually cannot. We plan one camera for plates and drivers at the gate and separate cameras for the ramp and drive aisles.

Can cameras cover a bike room without filming the inside of owners' storage cages?
+

Yes. We place one camera at the door and aim a second down the aisle between racks or cages, not into them. Where part of a cage interior still falls in the frame, the recorder's privacy mask blacks it out of the recording.

How do you run camera cable in a finished Brooklyn condo tower without opening corridor ceilings?
+

We use the routes the building already has: ceiling access panels, telecom risers, closets and the pathways the original low-voltage contractor used, and we reuse sponsor-installed network cable that tests good. Where a run truly needs a finished surface opened, the plan shows it and prices it before the board approves anything.

Can one recorder handle two buildings of a Brooklyn condo complex across a courtyard?
+

Usually. If network cable or conduit already connects the buildings, cameras in the second building can reach the main recorder through a switch. If not, a point-to-point wireless bridge or a new underground or aerial run can link them, or each building can have its own recorder viewed together in one app.

Can we change the cameras while the sponsor's construction warranty is still running?
+

Often yes, but the board should first read the offering plan and any warranty terms, because altering sponsor-installed systems can affect warranty claims. Its attorney is the right person to confirm. We document the existing system before changing anything, so the board has a record either way.

What happens to recordings if the building loses power?
+

Cameras powered from the recorder or a PoE switch stop when those devices lose power. We put the recorder and switches on a battery backup sized to ride through short outages and to let the recorder shut down cleanly in a long one, so footage already saved is protected.

Can the lobby intercom's camera image be saved on the condo's recorder?
+

With many current video intercoms it can, because their panels can share a video stream with a network recorder. Older or closed systems may not allow it. We check the model during the survey and, where it works, add the panel as a channel next to the vestibule camera.

Can the camera survey happen on a weekend when board members are home?
+

Yes. We book seven days a week, and weekend surveys are common for self-managed Brooklyn condos whose board members work weekdays. Visits are scheduled in two-hour arrival windows early in the day.

Will you take out the old cameras and recorder the developer left behind?
+

Yes. When a system is replaced, our proposal includes taking down the old cameras, brackets and recorder and reusing any cable that tests good. Footage the board wants to keep is exported before the old recorder goes. Patching and painting the old mounting spots is left to the building's painter.
