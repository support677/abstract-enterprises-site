The Neighborhood

Why City Line Properties Need Access Control Installation

City Line is small, and it is easy to drive through without noticing where it starts. Definitions differ a little depending on who draws the map: an encyclopedia entry describes it as a sub-section of East New York bordered by Cypress Hills to the north and Ozone Park, Queens, to the east, while a neighborhood guide puts Euclid Avenue on its western side and Atlantic Avenue and Conduit Boulevard at its top and bottom. Everyone agrees on the eastern edge, because it is the borough line itself. The name is older than the five-borough city: it marked the boundary between the City of Brooklyn and Queens County before consolidation in 1898. Liberty Avenue is the shopping street, and Brooklyn Community Board 5 is the local board, the same board that covers East New York, Cypress Hills, New Lots, Highland Park, Spring Creek and Starrett City.

The houses are the reason access control here looks the way it does. Walk the side streets off Liberty and Pitkin and you see brick two-family houses, some three-families, small apartment buildings and corner buildings with a store at street level. A house at that price is usually carried partly by the rent from the other floor, which makes the owner a landlord as well as a resident — and makes the front door a shared door.

The community has changed several times and the doors show it. City Limits reported in 2013 that City Line had been a middle-income Italian neighborhood, that halal groceries and South Asian clothing stores had replaced older Latino and Italian businesses along Liberty Avenue, and that more than 2,000 Bangladeshi residents lived in the area, with seven mosques drawing new arrivals. The encyclopedia entry adds residents from the Dominican Republic, Guyana and Puerto Rico. In practice that means many City Line houses are multigenerational: grandparents downstairs, grown children upstairs or in the basement, cousins with keys, and a paying tenant somewhere in the mix. Keys multiply fast in a house like that, and nobody keeps a list.

So the problems we are called about in City Line are specific. A tenant moved out and nobody is sure how many copies of the front-door key went with them. A side gate to the driveway gets left open because the latch is awkward and the padlock key is lost. The basement entrance under the stoop has its own key that only one relative ever had. The store downstairs needs to open at seven in the morning, but the apartment door next to it should never be propped. An older parent cannot manage keys any more and an aide comes four days a week. None of these needs a lobby system or a monthly contract. Most of them need one or two well-chosen doors, codes or fobs that can be deleted in a minute, and someone who explains what the law expects of a small landlord.

What We Install

Access Control Installation Services in City Line

Every service on this list begins with the same City Line question: who lives here, who visits, and which door are they actually using? In a house where the owner’s family uses the side door and the tenant uses the front, the front door may matter less than you think.

Front-door keypads with separate codes per floor

A keypad or keypad-and-fob deadbolt on the shared street door, with a code for each household and each regular visitor. When the upstairs tenant leaves, their code is deleted and nobody else has to change anything.

Wired readers with an electric strike

For heavier doors, doors that take a beating, or houses that want a reader on more than one entrance: a reader outside, a fail-secure electric strike in the frame, a small controller and power supply inside, and the door handle on the inside still opening it every time.

Driveway and side-gate locks

An electric lock or strike on the gate to the driveway or the walkway beside the house, with a weatherproof keypad on the post and a button or app to let in a delivery or a contractor. Gates are where we spend the most time on hinges and posts before any electronics go on.

Basement and rear-yard entrances

The door under the stoop or at the back of the yard, put on the same app as the front so the owner can see when it is used and switch off a code without going downstairs. Often a battery keypad lock is enough here.

Store-plus-apartment setups on Liberty Avenue

Two zones on one controller: the shop door on a business schedule for the owner and staff, the residential street door on the tenants’ fobs only, and the cellar hatch or rear door on its own rule. One login for the building owner, separate access for each group.

Takeovers, repairs and fob replacements

Systems left behind by a previous owner or installer: we find the controller, recover or reset the administrator account, list every code and fob that still works, and give you back control. Lost-fob calls are usually handled the same day.

If the front door already has a buzzer that half works, the right first step may be replacing the Brooklyn door buzzer with a unit that includes a keypad, rather than adding a second box beside the old one. We look at both before quoting.

Building Specialists

Access Control for City Line’s Architecture

There are four building types we see again and again in City Line. Each one has a different weak door, a different place to hide the wiring, and a different answer to whether the city treats it as a private house or a multiple dwelling.

Brick two-family with a stoop

Owner on one floor, tenant on the other, one street door into a small vestibule and separate apartment doors inside. Legally a private dwelling. The usual plan is a keypad or fob lock on the street door, a keyed deadbolt kept on each apartment door, and a battery keypad on the basement or yard entrance.

Three-family house

Three households, and under city rules a multiple dwelling. The street door and any shared rear door are where access control goes; apartment doors keep the heavy-duty deadbolt the Housing Maintenance Code requires. The data rules for keyless systems apply here, so we set the system up to collect only what the law allows.

Attached row with a driveway or side gate

Where a house has a curb cut or a walkway gate, that gate often sees more traffic than the front door: cars, bins, deliveries, workers. A gate lock on its own keypad, separate from the house door, is a common first project.

Liberty Avenue mixed-use building

A store at street level, one or two apartments above reached by a separate street door, a cellar under the store. Two groups of users with nothing in common except the building owner. We zone the doors so neither group can open the other’s.

Doors, frames and where the wires go

Brick houses with wood street doors. Many City Line front doors are wood or wood-and-glass doors in a wood frame set into brick. The frame is usually solid enough for an electric strike, but the space behind it is not always free: older houses can have a cavity filled with plaster and lath, or a steel lintel just where the strike body needs to go. We open a small inspection hole before committing, and if the frame cannot take a strike we use a keypad lock that keeps all its electronics in the door.

Steel doors and storm doors. Many owners replaced the original wood door with a steel door, and many added an outer storm or security door. The second door changes everything: a keypad on the inner door is useless if the outer one is locked with a key. We decide together which of the two is the controlled door and set up the other to stay latched but unlocked, or to carry the keypad itself.

Getting cable from the door to the controller. In a two-family the controller and power supply usually go in the basement under the vestibule or in a closet off the hall. The cable goes down through the floor at the hinge side of the door, not through the brick, so nothing is drilled through the façade. Where there is no basement access under the entry, we run a small surface raceway painted to match the trim and keep it on the inside.

Attached houses with no spare space. In a house attached on both sides, with the basement rented or full, the controller can be a slim wall-mounted unit in the vestibule itself, behind a locked cover, or the whole job can be done with battery hardware that needs no controller at all. We will tell you which you are getting and what it means for changing batteries.

Gates. Chain-link gates on steel posts and ornamental iron gates both work with electric locks, but the gate has to close on its own and line up the same way every time. If it drags, sags or bounces, we fix that first — a new hinge, a gate closer, a drop rod — because no lock will be reliable on a gate that does not close properly.

Most City Line owners who put a reader on the street door also want a camera on it, so a tenant’s claim that the door was left open can be checked against video. We often install both on one visit with security camera installation in Brooklyn running on the same low-voltage wiring.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in City Line

City Line’s businesses are small and mostly owner-run, and they sit in buildings where people also live. That shapes everything about a commercial job here. The questions are rarely about badge printing or dozens of doors; they are about keeping the shop door, the cellar and the tenants’ entrance separate, and about a system the owner can run from a phone between customers.

Where City Line businesses are

  • Liberty Avenue at the Queens line. The main commercial street, where City Limits described halal groceries and South Asian clothing stores replacing older Italian and Latino businesses, with apartments above many storefronts, near the elevated A train east of Grant Avenue. The typical job is a store door, a separate residential street door and a cellar or rear door.
  • Pitkin Avenue between the two stations. Euclid Avenue station sits at Euclid and Pitkin and the Grant Avenue entrance is just north of Pitkin, so a corner shop near either one is the kind of business that asks for a door that unlocks itself at opening time and locks itself at closing.
  • Atlantic Avenue along the northern edge. A wide avenue that some maps use as City Line’s boundary. Where a business lot fronts it, the job tends to be a vehicle gate or a rear yard door as much as the front entrance.
  • Forbell Street and the blocks around Liberty Avenue. City Limits counted seven mosques in the area in 2013; houses of worship and community rooms need doors open on a schedule for prayers, services or meetings and locked the rest of the time.
  • Fountain Avenue and Euclid Avenue edges. The streets beside the two NYCHA developments, where any small office or service business near the corner tends to want named staff codes rather than shared keys.

For storefronts the reader is usually a keypad or card reader at the shop entrance, scheduled to unlock during business hours if the owner wants walk-in traffic, or to stay locked with staff codes if they do not. Staff codes are named and can be limited to certain hours, so an evening worker cannot open the shop on a Sunday morning. The residential door next to it stays on the tenants’ fobs, and the shop staff never get those. For a store with cash on site, we add a camera on the door so every open has a picture to go with it, through commercial security camera installation in Brooklyn.

For houses of worship and community rooms, the need is usually a side door that opens on a timetable and a short list of people who can open it at other times. A keypad with time-based schedules handles that well, and the administrator can add a code for a visiting speaker or a caterer for one day and let it expire on its own. Where a congregation’s main doors must open freely during services, the schedule simply holds the lock open for those hours.

Compliance callouts for City Line doors

Free exit always comes first. Whatever we put on a door, a person inside must be able to leave without a key, a code or special knowledge. On ordinary entry doors we use electric strikes or electrified locks where the inside lever always opens the door. Electromagnetic locks, which hold the door shut with a magnet, are a different category: building codes allow them on doors in an exit path only with specific release methods — typically a motion sensor that releases the lock as someone approaches, a clearly marked “Push to Exit” button that cuts power directly, release on power failure, and release on fire alarm where the building has one. New York City’s current construction codes, which took effect in November 2022, are based on the 2015 International Code Council model codes. We avoid magnetic locks on City Line street doors in most cases for exactly this reason.

Fire-rated doors keep their rating. A fire-rated door, such as the door from a store into a shared stair or a cellar door in some buildings, must keep its label. Electric strikes used on fire doors must be fail-secure and listed for fire doors; we do not cut a rated door or frame without matching listed hardware.

Accessibility. Where a business door is required to be accessible, the reader goes at a height and position a wheelchair user can reach, and the door still has hardware that can be operated without tight grasping or twisting.

Multiple dwellings above stores. If the apartments above a Liberty Avenue store add up to three or more households, the building is a multiple dwelling for city housing purposes, and the residential part brings its own rules on entrance doors, apartment door locks and keyless-entry data. We cover those in the questions below. This section is general information, not legal advice; a lawyer or the city agency has the final word on your building.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control Installation in City Line: Questions Answered

These are the questions City Line owners ask once they are serious about taking the keys out of circulation. They are grouped by what sits behind them: the two-family-or-three-family line, family and tenants sharing doors, gates and side entrances, the store downstairs, power and internet, and money. Each is answered once.

Two Units or Three: Which Rules Reach Your Door

I live on the first floor of my City Line two-family and rent the top floor. Does the tenant data privacy law apply to me if I put a keypad on the front door?

Very likely not, but the habits it requires are still worth copying. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, is written for owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems — key fobs, cards, phone apps, biometric readers and similar keyless entry — to let people into a Class A multiple dwelling, its common areas or its apartments. The city’s housing agency, HPD, treats a building with three or more residential units as a multiple dwelling and a building with one or two units as a private dwelling. A two-family where you live on one floor and rent the other is on the private-dwelling side of that line.

That does not mean anything goes. You still have a tenant with a lease and ordinary legal protections, and a keypad log that shows exactly when your tenant comes and goes is sensitive information. Our default setup for a City Line two-family follows the spirit of the law anyway: the lock records entries for security, not for checking on anyone’s personal life; logs are kept for a limited time; the tenant’s code is theirs alone; and the tenant still has a key to their own apartment door. If you later add a third unit, the system is already set up the way the law expects. This is general information, not legal advice.

We converted to three legal units in City Line. What changes once the house counts as a multiple dwelling?

Three things change for access control. First, the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to any keyless system you use. In short: collect only the minimum the law lists (name, unit, which doors the person may use, contact method, the credential number, lease dates, and time and method of entry for security), get each tenant’s express consent in writing or through the app, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never use the data to track where people go outside the building or to harass or evict anyone, do not collect data from guests who are not tenants, and do not require a tenant to use the smart system to get into their own apartment. Data cannot be sold, and the law gives tenants a private right to sue over unlawful sale.

Second, the Housing Maintenance Code’s rules for apartment doors in a Class A multiple dwelling apply: each apartment entrance door needs a heavy-duty latch set, a heavy-duty deadbolt that is keyed outside with a thumb-turn inside, and a chain door guard, and the owner must provide at least one key. Third, the Multiple Dwelling Law’s entrance-door rule may reach the street door, depending on when the building was built or converted; we explain that in the store question below. HPD also expects multiple dwellings to register every year, with a September 1 deadline. We set the system up for all of this on the first visit, so there is nothing to redo later. Confirm the specifics for your building with a lawyer or HPD.

Will a keypad deadbolt on the apartment door upstairs in City Line still meet the deadbolt and chain rules for a three-family?

It can, if it is the right kind of keypad deadbolt and the chain guard stays. The code asks for a heavy-duty deadbolt operated by a key from outside and a thumb-turn from inside. Many electronic deadbolts keep a keyed cylinder on the outside alongside the keypad and a thumb-turn on the inside, so they meet that description as hardware; what we check is the grade of the lock, the length and strength of the bolt, and whether the tenant still has a working key. The chain guard is a separate item and stays on the door.

The bigger point is the privacy law: an owner of a multiple dwelling may not require a tenant to use a smart access system to get into their own apartment. So even if the apartment door has a keypad, the tenant must be able to use a key instead, and many owners simply leave apartment doors on keys and put access control on the shared doors only. In most City Line three-families that is the simplest and cheapest plan: control the street door, the yard door and the basement, and leave each household in charge of its own apartment door.

Is it legal in New York City to use fingerprint readers for tenants in a City Line three-family?

Biometric readers are among the technologies the Tenant Data Privacy Law covers, and the law lists biometric identifiers as data an owner may collect only if the system actually uses them, with express consent and the 90-day destruction rule for authentication data. So a fingerprint reader is not banned outright in a multiple dwelling, but it brings the most paperwork and the most risk for a small landlord, and the tenant still cannot be forced to use it to reach their own apartment.

Our advice in City Line is plain: for tenants, use codes, fobs or phone credentials, which do the job without storing anything about a person’s body. Keep fingerprint locks for the owner’s own household door, where the people enrolling are family members who chose it. If you already have a fingerprint reader on a shared door, we can switch tenants to codes or fobs and delete their enrolled fingerprints from the device while we are there.

Family, Tenants and Who Can Open What

Can my tenant upstairs and my family downstairs share one front door reader in a City Line house without seeing each other’s comings and goings?

Yes. Every person gets their own code or fob, and the entry log is visible only to the account administrator, which is the owner. The tenant’s app, if they have one, shows only their own credential; it does not show when the owner’s family uses the door. The owner can see the whole log but should treat it as a security record, not a way to keep tabs on the tenant. We set the log to keep entries for a limited period so it does not become a years-long diary of someone’s life.

In practice most City Line owners never look at the log except after a problem: a door left open, a package missing, a stranger in the hallway. Then it answers one question — which credential opened the door at that time — and that is usually enough. If you want a camera to go with it, we put the camera on the outside of the door looking at the entrance, not into the hallway toward anyone’s apartment.

My parents, my siblings and a cousin all have keys to our City Line house. How do we move everyone to codes without someone getting locked out?

We do it in stages, with the old key still working until everyone has been moved over. On install day the new lock or reader goes in with the existing key cylinder kept working, or a new cylinder keyed to a single key that the owner holds. Each family member gets a personal code or fob that day and tries it in front of us. For an older parent who will never remember a code, a fob on their keyring, or a keypad with large lit buttons, is usually easiest; we can also set a longer unlock time so they are not rushed.

After a week or two, once everyone has used the new credential, the old keys are retired: the cylinder is rekeyed or swapped so the dozen loose copies stop working. From then on, adding or removing someone takes a minute in the app. We write down who has which credential in a simple list for the owner, because in a multigenerational house the hardest part is remembering who was given what, not the technology.

How should a City Line landlord handle codes when a tenant moves out on short notice?

Delete the tenant’s code or fob the moment the keys are handed back or the lease ends, and check the log for any use after that. That is the whole advantage of access control in a rental house: there is no lock change and no locksmith visit at turnover. The new tenant gets a new code on move-in day. If the departing tenant had given their code to a friend or relative, deleting it shuts that out too, which is something a key change never fully guaranteed when copies were floating around.

Two cautions. First, do not use the lock to force anyone out: changing access to put a tenant out without going through the courts is illegal in New York, and for multiple dwellings the privacy law separately forbids using access data to harass or evict. Second, do not delete a code early on the basis of a rumor that the tenant has left; confirm the move-out first. We can set a code to expire automatically on the last day of a lease if you know it in advance.

Gates, Basements and the Side of the House

The side gate to our City Line driveway is chain-link on a steel post. Can it take an electric lock and a keypad?

Usually, yes, once the gate itself behaves. Chain-link gates sag over the years and often close a different way each time, so the first job is mechanical: tighten or replace the hinges, add a gate closer so it swings shut on its own, and make sure the latch side meets the post in the same place every time. Then we fit a gate lock designed for outdoor use, either a lock that mounts to the face of the post or an electric strike in a steel keeper, with a weatherproof keypad on the street side at a height you can reach from the car window if the gate is for a driveway.

The cable is the other half. Low-voltage cable runs from the lock back to a power supply indoors, usually through a short conduit along the house wall or under the walkway; we avoid leaving loose cable across a driveway where it will be driven over. Where running cable is impossible, a battery-powered gate lock with its own keypad is an option, with the trade-off that batteries need changing and cold weather shortens their life. Either way, the gate must still open from the inside without a code.

Our City Line basement has its own entrance under the stoop. Should it be on the same system as the front door?

If you want to know when it is used and to control who can open it, yes; if it is used once a month by the owner, a good mechanical lock may be enough. In many City Line houses the basement entrance under the stoop is the forgotten door: one old key, a weak lock, and a direct route into the house that nobody watches. Putting it on the same app as the front door means one place to add or remove people and one log for every way into the building.

If the basement is a rented unit, the question changes: that door is a tenant’s own entrance, and the tenant must keep a key to it. If the basement is storage or the boiler room, a battery keypad lock with codes for the owner and the heating technician is a cheap and effective upgrade. We also look at the door itself, because basement doors under stoops are often short, damp and swollen, and an electronic lock on a door that binds will not last.

Our City Line house is attached on both sides. Where does the controller go when there is no closet or basement space to spare?

We have three options and choose based on how many doors you are controlling. For one door, skip the controller altogether: a battery keypad or keypad-and-fob lock keeps all its electronics in the door and needs no wiring. For two or three doors on one system, a compact controller can mount in the vestibule or the inside of a hall closet behind a locked metal cover the size of a shoebox, fed from a nearby outlet through a plug-in power supply with a battery backup.

For a store-plus-apartments building, the controller goes in the cellar or the back of the store where the owner already controls access, with a separate cable up to the residential street door. In every case the controller sits on the protected side of the door it controls, so someone outside cannot reach the wiring that releases the lock. We do not put controllers in shared spaces that a tenant can open without the owner.

The Store Downstairs on Liberty Avenue

I own a Liberty Avenue building in City Line with a store downstairs and two apartments upstairs. Should the shop door and the apartment door share one system?

One system, two separate groups of people. Running both doors from a single controller and app is cheaper and easier for you as the owner: one login, one place to see what happened, one set of hardware to maintain. But the store staff and the tenants should never share credentials. The shop door has its own schedule and its own list of staff codes; the residential street door has its own list of tenant fobs. Nobody in one group can open the other group’s door, and you, as owner, can open both.

A building with a store and two apartments has two residential units, so under HPD’s definitions it is not a multiple dwelling; if there are three or more apartments above the store, it is. In that case the residential door falls under the tenant data rules, and if the building was erected or converted after January 1, 1968, the Multiple Dwelling Law expects its street entrance door to be automatically self-closing and self-locking; for older buildings that requirement kicks in when a majority of tenants ask for it in writing. Buildings with eight or more apartments also need an intercom at that door that lets each apartment talk to visitors and release the lock. We check the closer and latch on the residential door as part of any job, because a self-locking door that does not close is not locked.

The shop on my City Line ground floor closes late and opens early. Can the store door lock on a schedule while the tenants’ door stays on fobs?

Yes. The shop door can be set to unlock automatically at opening time and lock at closing, every day or with different hours for different days, while staff codes still work outside those hours for whoever opens or closes. Many owners prefer a “first person in” rule: the door stays locked until a staff member with a valid code arrives, then it switches to its open schedule, so the store never unlocks itself on a day nobody shows up. The residential door ignores the shop’s schedule entirely and stays locked, opening only for tenants’ fobs and the owner.

Holidays are the usual trap. Religious holidays, store closures and family emergencies all mean a day when the schedule should not run. We show you how to pause the schedule from the app in a few taps, and we set up a holiday list if your closing days are predictable. If the store has a roll-down gate outside the glass door, the electronic lock controls the glass door only; the gate stays on its own key or motor control.

Can a maglock go on the residential street door above a City Line storefront?

It can be done, but it is rarely the right choice here, and we usually talk owners out of it. A magnetic lock holds the door with a powered magnet, so it must release when the power fails and must have approved ways for people to exit: typically a sensor that releases it as someone approaches from inside, a marked push-to-exit button that cuts power directly and holds the door unlocked for a time, and release from the fire alarm if the building has one. On a narrow residential door at the bottom of a stair, with tenants carrying bags and children, every one of those parts is something that can fail or be misused, and a maglock that does not release is a life-safety problem.

An electric strike or an electrified lock does the same job better on that kind of door. The inside handle always opens it mechanically, so exit never depends on electronics, and a fail-secure strike keeps the door locked from outside even if the power is out. Maglocks still have their place, such as glass doors where no strike will fit, but those are uncommon on City Line’s residential entrances. Where one is genuinely needed, we install it with the full release set and test each method in front of you.

Power, Internet and What Happens When They Drop

If the power goes out on our City Line block, does the front door stay locked or swing open?

That depends on whether the lock is fail-secure or fail-safe, and you choose. A fail-secure electric strike or lock stays locked from the outside when it loses power and needs power to unlock; a fail-safe device does the opposite and unlocks when power is cut. The terms describe the outside, secure side of the door. With ordinary strikes and electrified locks, people inside can still leave either way, because the inside handle works mechanically. For a City Line street door, fail-secure is almost always the right choice: a blackout should not leave the house open to the street.

To keep the door usable during an outage, we put a battery backup on the power supply, sized to run the lock and reader for hours rather than minutes, and we make sure the owner and every household still have a mechanical key that opens the door from outside. Battery keypad locks keep working through an outage anyway, because they never depended on house power. We pull the plug on the system during handover to prove it behaves the way you expect.

What happens to a City Line access control system if the home internet is down or the Wi-Fi password changes?

The door keeps working; the remote features pause. Codes and fobs are checked by the lock or the controller on site, so they do not need the internet. What you lose while the internet is down is the ability to unlock from your phone when you are away, to receive alerts, and to add or delete users remotely. When the connection returns, the system syncs and catches up on its log.

The Wi-Fi password is the more common trap in a two-family, because the router often belongs to whichever household pays for the internet, and when someone changes the password or swaps providers the lock’s bridge drops off. We prefer to connect the controller with a cable to the router, or to give it its own small network, so a change made upstairs does not take the front door offline. We also write down how to reconnect it, in case the router changes anyway.

Spending Only What the House Needs

We pay out of pocket for every repair on our City Line house. What is the cheapest setup that still stops copied keys?

One controlled door, chosen carefully. For most two-families that is the shared street door, because it is the one every tenant, relative and visitor uses. A single-door installation with a reader or keypad and an electric strike is priced at $1,500 to $2,500 on our published Brooklyn table, including the reader, strike, wiring and programming. The basement and yard doors can stay on good mechanical locks for now and be added later, when the budget allows, onto the same system.

What stops copied keys is the credential, not the price tag. Codes can be shared but are changed in a minute; modern encrypted fobs are much harder to copy than old 125 kHz proximity fobs, which the Brooklyn hub page warns can be cloned with an inexpensive device. If you already have fobs in the house from a previous system, we check what type they are before reusing them. And whatever you choose, retire the old key cylinder at the end so the copies in circulation stop working; otherwise you have paid for a new lock and kept the old problem.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control Installation in City Line

Search summaries and cost-guide sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give confident, general answers about access control. Most of them are written for office buildings or suburban houses. Here is where those general answers part company with a City Line two-family or a Liberty Avenue store with apartments above.

“Access control is a commercial product”

The common framing treats access control as something for offices with badge readers, and smart locks as the home version. In City Line that split does not fit. A two-family with a tenant upstairs has the same core need as a small office: several people, some of whom will leave, sharing a door that the owner has to keep secure.

The right equipment sits in between. A single battery smart lock may be too weak for a heavy shared street door used dozens of times a day, while a full commercial system is too much for two households. A reader, a strike and a one- or two-door controller are the middle ground, and that is what most City Line jobs end up being.

What decides it is not whether the building is “commercial” but how many people use the door, how often they change, and whether the owner wants to manage them from a phone. We ask those questions before naming any hardware.

“Smart locks are covered by tenant privacy law everywhere”

Summaries of New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law often say it applies to “landlords who use smart locks.” The law is narrower than that: it covers smart access systems in Class A multiple dwellings, and HPD defines a multiple dwelling as three or more residential units.

For City Line that line falls right through the housing stock. A two-family is outside the law’s reach; a three-family is inside it. The same keypad on the same kind of door can be lightly regulated on one block and carry consent, retention and data-use rules on the next.

We set up two-family systems as if the law applied anyway, because it is good practice and because houses change. But owners deserve to know which side of the line they are on, and a generic answer does not tell them.

“Install a magnetic lock for maximum security”

Magnetic locks appear in many general answers as the strong option. On egress doors they are the most heavily regulated kind of lock, because they hold the door with power and must release on power loss, with approved exit devices and fire-alarm release where required.

On a City Line street door, a maglock is usually less secure in practice: it unlocks in a blackout by design, it depends on a sensor and button to let people out, and a narrow vestibule door leaves little room to mount it properly.

A fail-secure electric strike keeps the door locked through an outage and lets people out by the mechanical handle. For most residential doors here, that is both safer and more secure.

“Expect to pay a monthly fee”

Many summaries present access control as a subscription. Cloud-managed systems do charge monthly fees, and they are worth it for some multi-site businesses. A City Line owner with one building rarely needs that.

Locally managed controllers and good battery locks are owned outright, with an app that runs without a subscription for the core features of adding users, deleting users and remote unlock. Where an app does carry a fee for extras, we say so before you buy.

Every system we install is yours: no contract, no monthly charge from us, and the administrator account in the owner’s name.

“Installation costs a few hundred dollars”

Cost guides often quote low national figures that assume a single smart lock swapped into a standard interior door. That number can be right for exactly that job. It is not the price of a wired reader and electric strike on a heavy street door, with cable fished down to a basement and a battery-backed power supply.

Our published Brooklyn table starts single-door installations at $1,500 to $2,500, multi-door buildings at $4,000 to $15,000, and full buildings at $15,000 to $40,000 and up. City Line is priced at that Brooklyn base, with no surcharge.

If a one-door battery lock is all your house needs, we will say so, and it will cost far less than a wired system. The point is to match the hardware to the door, not to sell the biggest package.

“Fingerprint readers are the most secure choice for tenants”

Biometric readers are often promoted as the top tier. For tenants in a New York City multiple dwelling, biometrics carry the heaviest legal obligations under the Tenant Data Privacy Law, and tenants cannot be required to use the smart system to enter their own apartments.

A code or encrypted fob gives a small landlord the same practical control with no stored body data. If a tenant’s fob is lost, it is replaced; a fingerprint is not.

We reserve fingerprint locks for the owner’s own household door, where family members choose to enroll.

“Any handyman can wire a strike”

General answers often treat access control as a simple DIY or handyman project. In New York State, installing security and access systems for others falls under the Department of State’s security and fire alarm installer licensing under General Business Law Article 6-D, which is why we display license #12000287431.

More practically, the hard parts of a City Line job are not the wiring diagram. They are finding a path for cable in a brick house without damaging the façade, making a sagging gate close reliably, and keeping egress free on every controlled door.

If a previous installer left you with a system nobody can log into, we can usually recover it; but it is cheaper to have it set up with documented credentials in the owner’s name from the start.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before You Count the Doors

This is a method note, not a story about a particular house. On a City Line walkthrough, before we look at a single door, we sit down with the owner and make a list of everyone who has a key: each person in the household, the tenant and anyone the tenant may have given a copy to, relatives who check on the house, a home aide, the person who reads the meter or services the boiler, a former tenant whose keys were never returned. In a multigenerational two-family the list is often longer than the owner expected, and the act of writing it down is when the case for access control becomes obvious.

Then we sort the list by door. Who actually uses the street door, who uses the side gate, who needs the basement? Very often one door carries almost all the traffic and the others are used by one or two people. That door gets the reader; the others get good locks and a place on the plan for later. Only then do we look at frames, closers, hinges and cable paths. Counting people first keeps the job small, the price honest and the system something the owner will actually use.

FAQ

City Line Access Control Installation FAQ

Do I need to tell my City Line tenants before I switch the front door from keys to fobs or codes?
+

Yes, and in a three-family it is more than courtesy. In a multiple dwelling the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires express consent, in writing or through the app, before collecting a tenant’s data for a keyless system. In a two-family we still recommend written notice with the date of the switch, how the tenant gets their new code or fob, and confirmation that they keep a key to their own apartment door. A tenant who hears about the change on install day is the most common source of friction.

Do City Line installs need a Department of Buildings permit for a keypad or electric strike?
+

Swapping a lock or adding a low-voltage reader and strike to an existing door is usually treated as ordinary hardware work, but we check each job rather than assume. Anything that changes an exit path, a fire-rated door or a building’s fire alarm connection is handled under the code requirements for that door, and any new 120-volt outlet or circuit is done by a licensed electrician. If your building has open violations or a pending alteration, tell us at the walkthrough so we can plan around it.

Which City Line doors should get a camera paired with the reader?
+

The shared street door first, because it is where disputes happen: a door left open, a package gone, someone let in who should not have been. Next is a driveway or side gate if it opens onto the sidewalk. A store door with cash inside also benefits. We aim cameras at the outside of the entrance and the approach, not into hallways or toward any apartment door, so the camera records who came and went without watching the people who live there.

How long does a single-door job in City Line take from walkthrough to working fobs?
+

The walkthrough takes under an hour, and the written estimate comes the same day. Once the hardware is in hand, a single wired door with a reader and strike is usually installed and programmed in one working day, with every household enrolled before we leave. A battery keypad lock on its own takes a few hours. Doors or gates that need repair first, or cable paths that are hard to find, can add a day.

Can a City Line keypad be set for a home aide or dialysis driver who comes only on certain days?
+

Yes. A code or fob can be limited to certain days and hours, so an aide’s code works Monday to Thursday from eight to six and nowhere else, and a driver’s code works only on treatment days. If the aide changes, you delete one code and add another; nobody has to hand back a key. The log shows each visit, which many families find reassuring when an older parent lives on the ground floor.

Can the access app show our City Line front door in Bengali, Spanish or another language besides English?
+

It depends on the product, so we check before you buy. Some lock and access apps offer several languages, and phone credentials work the same whatever language the phone is set to. Keypads themselves use numbers and symbols, which helps in a house where family members read different languages. At handover we can name users and doors in whatever way the family prefers, so the log is readable to the person who manages it.

Will a reader on a City Line stoop survive rain and salt from the sidewalk?
+

Outdoor-rated readers and keypads are built for weather, and we mount them under the door hood or in a recess where possible, sealed at the cable entry. Salt and slush splashed from the sidewalk are harder on gate posts at ground level than on a reader beside a door at the top of a stoop, so on gates we keep the keypad high and use stainless hardware. Battery locks lose capacity in hard cold; we suggest lithium batteries on outside doors.

Yes. If parts are obsolete or the old installer locked the system to their own account, we explain the options before replacing anything. Service visits are $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum.

Can a City Line mosque, church or community room put a reader on a side door used only at prayer or meeting times?
+

Yes. The door can unlock automatically for set times, such as daily prayers or a weekly meeting, and stay locked otherwise, with personal codes for the people who open and close. A one-day code for a visiting speaker or caterer can expire on its own. If the main doors must open freely while the space is in use, the schedule holds them unlocked, and exit hardware on the inside always lets people leave.
