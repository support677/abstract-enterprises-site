The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Properties Need Video Wall Installation

Putnam is a county of about 99,000 people — the Census Bureau’s July 2025 estimate is 99,028 — living in 38,713 homes, 83.8 percent of them owner-occupied. It has no downtown office towers, no arena and no convention hall. What it does have is a dense layer of public rooms that serve those households: a county 911 center that is the only public-safety answering point for the whole county, a legislature, the halls of six towns and three villages, school districts with auditoriums and cafeterias, eight public libraries, firehouses that double as training and meeting halls, and churches in every hamlet. Those are the rooms where a video wall earns its keep here, along with the basements and great rooms of the larger houses. The median owner-occupied home is valued at $471,300, and families who build a media room in Carmel, Garrison or Patterson often want more picture than a single television can give.

The county’s own records show what these rooms need. The Bureau of Emergency Services at 112 Old Route 6 in Carmel runs the Enhanced 911 system and opens the Emergency Operations Center, where, in the Bureau’s words, representatives of state agencies, emergency services, police, highway departments and utilities gather to make assessments and help the County Executive decide what to do. Its 911 center coordinates 8 police agencies, 7 EMS agencies and 13 fire departments, and answered 114,246 emergency calls in 2022. In March 2026 legislators were briefed on transfers to replace two broken monitors on the 911 center’s video wall, at roughly $10,000, and to upgrade equipment at the sheriff’s backup 911 center. A wall in a room like that is not a decoration. When one tile fails, the job is to put the same picture back on the same wall quickly, and that is decided years earlier by which panel was bought and whether a spare exists.

Public meetings tell a similar story. In July 2021 the county’s IT director told a legislative committee that the Historic Courthouse’s sound system had been designed for court proceedings rather than meetings, that any work there had to respect its historic status and be approved by the Office of Court Administration, and that the quotes for the courthouse were $10,000 for video and $11,000 for audio. The same minutes record that the Town of Carmel had invested about $180,000 in its own livestream system, with six cameras and a contracted operator. In May 2026 the legislature was still debating phased audio-visual fixes after repeated complaints about meeting sound and livestream quality. Today the Town of Carmel streams its board meetings from Town Hall at 60 McAlpin Avenue in Mahopac to YouTube, and the county posts its legislative sessions there too. Any display wall in rooms like these has to work with the cameras and microphones, not fight them.

Then there is the power. When Tropical Storm Isaias came through on August 4, 2020, NYSEG reported at 10:15 the next morning that 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers were still without power — 12,717 in Carmel alone — and Central Hudson had 3,621 of its 5,160 Putnam customers out, most of them in Philipstown and Cold Spring. A display wall, its controller and its media player all have to come back on their own when the lights do, in the right order, showing the right thing, without a volunteer climbing behind the screens. And under Putnam County’s Electrical Licensing Law, Chapter 145 of the county code, the work itself is regulated: the county’s licence for low-voltage and limited-data communications technicians names audio/video cabling alongside networking, intercom and video surveillance. That is the local rule book a Putnam wall is built under.

Building Specialists

Video Wall Installation for Putnam County’s Architecture

Putnam’s public rooms were built across two centuries. The wall a display hangs on decides the mounting, the cable path and sometimes whether a fixed wall is possible at all.

Public-safety buildings

Purpose-built rooms with block walls, raised or sealed floors and equipment rooms nearby. The wall goes on a rail anchored into masonry, the controller into the existing rack, and nothing interrupts dispatch: we work a section at a time and hand each screen back before taking the next.

Historic halls and the courthouse

Plaster on lath, tall windows, decorative trim and, in the Historic Courthouse, approvals beyond the county’s own. Displays go on freestanding or floor-supported frames where fixing to the wall is not allowed, with cables in removable raceway rather than chased into old plaster.

School buildings from several eras

Block-walled classrooms and gyms, newer steel-framed additions like Brewster’s 13,400-square-foot cafetorium, and auditoriums such as Mahopac High School’s, where the district’s capital plan includes lighting and audio upgrades. Mounting, conduit and power differ wing to wing, and work is scheduled around the school calendar.

Library buildings

Older reading rooms beside newer additions and lower-level program rooms. Quiet matters: fans and switches go out of the room, and installs happen before opening or on closed days.

Churches and parish halls

High ceilings, stone or plaster, stained glass light and wooden trim. Sightlines from the back pews decide height and size; the mount is reversible where the building’s history calls for it.

Houses: split-levels, colonials and newer builds

Most of Putnam’s homes are detached houses on wooded lots. Basement media rooms have concrete on one side and framing on the other; great rooms have big windows. Each needs its own mounting and light plan.

Materials and methods by building type

  • Block and poured concrete (firehouses, schools, basements): sleeve anchors into solid block cores, surface conduit or raceway for cable, and recessed boxes only where the core allows them.
  • Steel studs (newer additions and offices): backing plates or a horizontal rail spanning several studs, because one stud will not carry a tiled wall’s weight on its own.
  • Wood frame (houses, parish halls): blocking added behind drywall during rough-in, or a rail lagged across studs; we find every stud before marking a hole.
  • Plaster on lath (older halls and churches): no recessed boxes in the plaster; cable in painted raceway or through the floor below; a floor-supported frame when the wall itself is the protected part.
  • Stone and brick (older churches and village buildings): anchors into the mortar joints or the masonry units as the wall allows, and a freestanding frame when the owner prefers no holes at all.

Weight drives these choices. A 2x2 wall of 55-inch commercial panels, with its mounts, weighs a few hundred pounds hung on a single plane; a direct-view LED wall of the same size can weigh more. The mounting plan, with the fixings named for the actual wall, is part of every written proposal.

Compliance callouts for Putnam display work

Your Questions Answered

Video Wall Installation in Putnam County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Putnam agencies, schools, congregations and homeowners raise when a wall is being planned. Each is answered here once.

Rooms That Cannot Go Dark: Dispatch, Emergency Operations and Public Safety

If the original monitor model is discontinued, how do you make a replacement tile match the rest of the wall?

First we look for the same model through the manufacturer’s service channel and distributors, because commercial video-wall panels are often supported for years after they leave the catalogue. If it truly is gone, we pick the current model with the same outside dimensions and bezel width, check that it fits the existing mount, and calibrate brightness and colour on the new tile and its neighbours together. On an older wall the honest answer is sometimes to replace a whole row so the mismatch sits at an edge rather than in the middle. We show the options and prices before ordering anything.

That is why our proposals for public-safety walls name the panel model, its published end-of-life information where the maker provides it, and a recommended number of spares bought at the same time from the same production run.

How many spare tiles should a public agency buy with the wall, and where should they be kept?

For a wall of four to nine panels we usually recommend one spare panel; for larger walls, roughly one per ten, plus a spare media player or controller card if the design depends on one. Spares should be stored in their original boxes, indoors, in a heated and dry room in the same building, labelled with the wall they belong to and the date they were bought. A spare kept in a garage or a cold storage container is a spare you may not be able to use.

We record the serial numbers of the installed panels and the spares on the handover sheet, so that a replacement next year goes in as a like-for-like swap.

Can one wall show the dispatch map and a training slide deck side by side in a fire district classroom?

Yes. A video-wall controller or processor can split the wall into windows from different sources: a computer showing a map, a laptop with a presentation, a camera feed, a news channel. Layouts are saved as presets, so an instructor picks “training” or “incident” from a panel instead of rearranging windows by hand. The limit is the number of inputs the controller has, which we size to the sources you list at the survey plus one or two spare.

Streamed Meetings in Town Halls and Historic Rooms

Our board meets in a room built for court proceedings. Can displays go in without changing the historic interior?

Usually, yes, by not attaching anything to the parts of the room that matter. Displays can stand on floor-supported frames or mobile stands that can be wheeled aside, cables can run in removable raceway along existing trim lines or under the floor, and the electronics can sit in a cabinet in an adjoining office. In the Putnam Historic Courthouse the county’s own minutes note that work needs Office of Court Administration approval and must respect the building’s historic status, so the drawings we prepare for such a room are written to be submitted for review.

Will a wall of screens behind the board spoil the livestream picture?

It can, if it is planned without the cameras. A bright wall behind the dais makes cameras darken faces, and fine screen patterns can shimmer on video. The fix is placement and settings: put displays on the side walls facing the audience rather than behind the speakers, or lower the wall’s brightness during meetings; aim cameras so screens are out of frame or only at the edge; and send the presentation to the stream directly, so remote viewers see the slides crisp rather than filmed off a screen. We test with the room’s actual cameras before handover.

Can the screens in the room show exactly what remote viewers see on YouTube?

Yes. The cleanest arrangement is a small switcher or encoder that takes the presenter’s laptop, the meeting cameras and any caption feed, and sends one programme both to the room displays and to the streaming platform. Then residents in the room and residents at home see the same slide at the same time. Where a town already has an operator-run system, like the six-camera arrangement the Town of Carmel described to the county in 2021, we connect the new displays to it rather than replacing it.

Schools, Libraries and Congregations

Can a cafetorium wall be a lunch-menu board by day and an auditorium screen at night?

Yes, and that is the right use for a room like Brewster’s new cafetorium, which the district describes as a space for a full grade at lunch or an audience for performances. A scheduled media player shows menus and announcements during school hours, and a wall plate at the stage edge takes a laptop or the auditorium camera for evening events. A locked control panel with three or four labelled buttons keeps students from changing it, and the schedule returns the wall to the daytime content on its own the next morning.

Can library staff with no AV training change what the program-room wall shows?

They should be able to, or the wall will sit unused. We set up a short list of sources — the lectern laptop input, a wireless presentation receiver, the library’s own media player and a streaming stick for film nights — and put them on a single labelled panel. Content for the player is changed by dragging files into a shared folder or a web page. Training is twenty minutes with whoever runs programmes, and a laminated card stays by the panel. With Putnam’s eight libraries running between 467 and 1,839 programmes each in 2025, the wall has to be something any staff member can start at 6:55 p.m.

Can the sanctuary wall switch itself on before Sunday service and off afterwards?

Yes. Commercial displays and controllers accept schedules, so the wall can wake at 8:30 a.m. on Sundays with the welcome slide, switch to the presentation computer when it is turned on, and power down after the last service. Holy days and special services are added to the schedule from a laptop. Scheduling also protects the panels, which last longer when they are not left on all week by accident.

Power, Storms and Restart

What should the wall do when the power drops for two seconds and comes back?

Nothing visible, ideally. Short dips and flickers are common on long overhead lines, and they are what most often leaves a wall dark: the screens come back but the media player or controller does not, or comes back on the wrong input. We put the player, controller and network switch on a battery backup so they ride through short dips, and we set every display to return to its last state and input when power is restored. Then we test it by switching the circuit off and on while you watch.

Is a battery backup worth it for the screens themselves, or only for the controller?

For most Putnam walls, back up the brains and protect the screens. A battery unit sized to carry a whole tiled wall for any length of time is large and expensive, while one sized for the controller, player and switch is small and inexpensive. The screens get surge protection and a clean restart setting. The exception is a dispatch or emergency-operations room, where the building’s generator and the agency’s own power plan decide what stays lit, and we fit the wall into that plan with the agency’s electrician.

Our building switches to a generator during outages. Will the wall survive the changeover?

It should, if the generator and transfer switch are healthy and the electronics have a battery bridge. The moment of transfer causes a brief interruption; without a battery unit, controllers and media players restart, which is the most common cause of a “no signal” wall after an outage. Some generators also produce less stable power than the utility; electronics on a battery backup with conditioning are shielded from that. If a wall flickers whenever the generator runs, we ask the electrician to check the generator’s output while we test the display side.

Licensing and Who Does What in Putnam

Does Putnam County require a licence to install video wall cabling?

Putnam regulates it at county level. Chapter 145 of the county code defines a low-voltage/limited-data communications technician as someone who installs or maintains cabling systems for networking, telecommunications, cable television, intercom, audio/video, fiber optics and video surveillance operating at 90 volts or less and five amperes, starting from an outlet or connection installed by a licensed electrician, and it says no one may carry on that trade in the county unless licensed or registered. A master electrician may also do that work. The county publishes its lists of licensed master electricians and low-voltage technicians on the Consumer Affairs page.

Our New York State licence is #12000287431, and we are glad to show how we meet the county’s requirements on any Putnam job; check the county list for any installer you hire.

Who installs the new outlet for the wall, and who files for the electrical permit?

A Putnam-licensed electrician installs the circuit and outlets; we install everything that plugs into them and the low-voltage cabling between. The county’s Consumer Affairs page says electrical permit applications for locations in the Towns of Carmel, Patterson and Putnam Valley are filed directly with those towns’ building departments; the electrician knows the routine in the other towns and villages. We give the electrician the wall’s power draw, the box locations and heights, and the schedule, so the rough-in happens once.

Running the Wall Day to Day

Who should hold the administrator login for the wall’s content system in a town or district office?

The organisation, not a person and not the installer. We create the administrator account under a shared office address the town, district or library controls, give day-to-day staff their own accounts with permission only to change content, and hand the administrator password over at the end of the job for the client to change. When a clerk or librarian moves on, the account is disabled and the wall keeps running. If the client asks us to keep emergency access, that is written down; by default we do not.

The same goes for the controller’s web page and the media player: every login is recorded on the handover sheet, and default passwords are changed before we leave.

Can a lobby wall in a public building carry storm and closure notices?

Yes. A notice zone can be added to the layout, or the whole wall can switch to a notice slide that staff publish from a phone or laptop. Residents can sign up for county messages through Putnam Alert, which the county links from its website, and for state messages through NY-Alert; a lobby wall is a good place to repeat what those services send: closures, warming and cooling locations, road conditions. Keep the wording identical to the official message and keep the official source on the slide, so the wall never contradicts the county.

For buildings that open as warm or cool places for the public — the county’s libraries describe offering cool spaces in summer and warm spaces in winter — we make sure the wall and its player are on the circuits the building keeps alive.

How do volunteers get their slides onto the wall without plugging in a laptop each time?

Through a shared folder or a web dashboard tied to the media player. A fire company, a parent association or a church office drops images or short videos into the folder, and the player picks them up on its next check and adds them to the rotation. Items can carry start and end dates so last month’s fundraiser does not linger. For live presentations, a wireless presentation receiver lets a guest speaker share a laptop screen with one click, while the wall’s own schedule stays untouched.

Where should displays go so wheelchair users and people in the front row can see them comfortably?

Not too high and not too close. In a meeting room or library hall the front row and the wheelchair spaces are usually nearest the wall, so a wall hung high for the back rows forces them to look sharply upward for an hour. We set the height from the actual seating plan, leave wheelchair positions where the screen is in a natural line of sight, and in long rooms add a second, smaller display partway back rather than raising the main wall. Cables and floor boxes are kept out of the accessible route to the front.

Homes: Media Rooms and Lake Houses

Is a video wall sensible for a basement media room, or is one large TV better?

For most basements, one large display is better: no seams, lower cost, simpler control. A tiled LCD wall makes sense when the room needs several pictures at once — four games on a Sunday, a gaming setup beside a film — and a direct-view LED wall when the owner wants a seamless picture larger than any television sold, and has the budget for it. We walk through the room’s seating distance, ceiling height and window light, and recommend the simplest system that does what the family will actually use.

Can a wall in a seasonal lake house be left unpowered and unheated all winter?

Check the panels’ storage-temperature rating first; it is published on the manufacturer’s specification sheet and differs from model to model. If the house drops below that rating in January, keep the room heated to a minimum or choose panels rated for it. Condensation is the other risk: when a cold house is opened and heated quickly, let the screens warm up with the room for several hours before switching them on. We leave these steps on the handover sheet for owners who close up for the season.

Before the Survey

What to Gather Before a Putnam Survey

A survey takes an hour or two. It goes faster, and the proposal is more accurate, when a few things are ready. None of them requires technical knowledge.

  • How the room is used. A typical week: meetings, classes, services, events, and who runs them. A wall for a weekly board meeting and a wall for a dispatch floor are different purchases.
  • What has to be on screen. Laptops, a building computer, cameras, a streaming feed, cable TV, a media player with announcements. List them, including the ones you might add next year.
  • Who approves. A town board resolution, a district’s facilities office, a library board, a parish council, a co-owner. We write the proposal for that reader.
  • Any drawings. Floor plans or old renovation drawings help locate studs, conduit and electrical panels in older public buildings.
  • The electrician. If the building has an electrician it trusts, their name and number. If not, we work with a Putnam-licensed electrician who knows the town’s permit routine.

Sizing

How Big Should a Putnam Room’s Wall Be?

Most rooms we survey in Putnam are smaller than the photographs in display catalogues, and the right wall is the smallest one that does the job. Three measurements decide it: how far the back row sits from the wall, how much text has to be read from there, and how many different things must be on screen at the same time. Size follows from those, not the other way round.

Viewing distance and text. Slides in a library room or a board meeting are mostly text: agendas, budgets, maps, captions. The test we use at the survey is simple and visible: we put a typical slide on a laptop or a temporary display at the proposed size and ask someone to read it from the last seat. If they cannot, the wall is too small or the content must change. If they can read it comfortably from halfway, the wall may be bigger than needed.

One picture or many. A single large display shows one thing well. A tiled LCD wall exists to show several things at once, or one picture larger than any single display, at the cost of thin seams between tiles. Direct-view LED removes the seams but is priced by area and pixel pitch. A Putnam meeting room that only ever shows one presentation is often better served by two matched displays on the side walls than by a tiled wall behind the board.

Pixel pitch for LED. Pitch is the distance between LED pixels; finer pitch is sharper up close and costs more. Our Putnam table lists 3.9mm, 2.5mm, 1.9mm and 1.5mm options. A sanctuary or cafetorium viewed from well back rarely needs the finest pitch; a media room with a sofa close to the wall may. We show the same content on sample pitches before anyone chooses.

LCD or LED over the years. The two technologies age differently, which matters to a board planning a ten-year asset. A tiled LCD wall is a set of separate displays: when one fails, it is swapped as a unit, and the swap is quick if a matching spare exists. A direct-view LED wall is built from cabinets and small modules: a failed module is replaced from a stock of spares, but modules from a later production run may need recalibration to match. Either way, the spares plan is written into the proposal, and the maintenance line in our Putnam table covers periodic calibration so tiles that drift are brought back in line before anyone notices.

Brightness and the room. A library room with blinds and a church with stained glass need very different brightness. Too bright is a problem as well as too dim: a wall turned up for a sunny afternoon is tiring in an evening meeting and makes cameras struggle. Commercial panels can follow a brightness schedule or a light sensor, and we set both at the survey hour and at the evening hour the room is used most.

Height. In rooms where people sit, the bottom of the wall usually sits just above head height of the seated audience in front, so the back rows see over the front. In standing rooms, such as lobbies, it goes higher. In every public building we check that the mount does not project into a walkway.

What AI Gets Wrong About Video Wall Installation in Putnam County

What follows tests seven claims that appear in widely read cost guides and forums — the material AI summaries and aggregator sites like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to repeat — against what Putnam’s own records show.

“Most installed walls run about $500 to $2,500 per square foot”

That benchmark comes from an LED-wall price guide, and it describes direct-view LED priced by area. It is a fair way to price a sanctuary-sized LED wall. It is the wrong frame for most Putnam projects, which are tiled LCD walls priced per panel, plus mounting, controller and labour.

A 2x2 wall of 55-inch LCD panels in our Putnam table runs $11,000 to $16,500 installed. Divided by its area, that number means little; what matters is the panel count, the controller and the wall it hangs on.

Ask any quote, ours included, to state which kind of wall it prices, and to break panels, mounting, processing, power coordination and labour into separate lines.

“A 10-by-5-foot P2.9 LED kit costs $11,000 to $15,000 online”

One display company’s 2026 guide lists online LED kits at roughly that price and points out, correctly, that they are hardware only: event-grade cabinets, a basic sending card and a simple frame.

What the kit leaves out is what a Putnam room needs most: a mount designed for that wall, a circuit run by a licensed electrician, calibration so the cabinets match, and someone who will come back when a module fails.

A kit can be the right answer for a portable event screen. For a dispatch room or a church wall that has to work every week, the missing lines are the expensive part.

“Installation adds 15 to 30 percent on top of hardware”

Several guides use a percentage like this. In a Putnam public room the cost that percentage misses is coordination: the meeting cameras, the livestream, the historic-room approvals, the school calendar, the electrician’s circuit.

The county’s own figures make the point. In 2021 the IT director’s quotes for the Historic Courthouse were $10,000 for video and $11,000 for audio, separate from any displays, and Carmel’s livestream system had cost about $180,000.

We price those items as their own lines, so a board can see what the wall costs and what the room around it costs.

“Any TV will do for a video wall”

Forum threads in the commercial AV community answer this one bluntly: consumer televisions may not fail at once, but used as a wall they can burn in, lose colour and brightness, and show wide seams; commercial displays are rated for far longer daily operation.

We agree for public rooms. A 911 floor or a library that runs its wall all day needs panels rated for that duty and sold with commercial warranty terms. The county’s own 2026 replacement shows that tiles do fail in service.

In a home media room used a few evenings a week, a single good consumer television is often the better buy than a cheap tiled wall.

“The wall can range from $15,000 to $200,000”

A commercial AV installer on Reddit gave roughly that range, adding that mounting structure alone could run tens of thousands on some jobs. It is honest for large corporate and venue walls.

Putnam’s scale is usually smaller. Replacing two failed tiles on the county’s 911 wall was budgeted at about $10,000, and our Putnam table starts at $11,000 for a complete 2x2 wall.

The useful question is not the range but which line items your room actually needs, and that takes a survey.

“New York has no statewide contractor licence, so anyone can install it”

It is true that New York does not license most trades statewide; one contractor-licensing guide is headlined exactly that. But Putnam licenses at county level.

Chapter 145 of the Putnam County Code requires a licence or registration for low-voltage/limited-data communications work, and names audio/video cabling among the systems covered. New circuits are master-electrician work, also licensed by the county.

So in Putnam the right question is not “does the state license this?” but “is this installer on the county’s list, or working with someone who is?”

“Keep voltage within ±5 percent and the wall will be fine”

An LED price guide lists stable voltage, within five percent, as an installation must. Good advice, but it assumes the power is there. In Putnam the bigger risk is the power disappearing: Isaias left 34,150 of NYSEG’s 38,693 Putnam customers without power the next morning.

What protects a Putnam wall is a battery backup on the controller, player and switch, surge protection on the screens, and displays set to restore themselves.

Every Putnam handover includes a power-off test: we cut the circuit, restore it, and watch the wall come back to the right picture without anyone touching it.

Storm Season

A Storm Plan for a Putnam Video Wall

Putnam’s storms arrive with warning more often than not, which gives a facilities manager, a pastor or a homeowner a day to prepare the wall. This is the short plan we leave with every Putnam installation.

Before the storm

  • Check the battery backup’s status light and press its self-test if it has one; a battery that failed quietly protects nothing.
  • Confirm the media player has the content it needs stored locally, in case the internet goes down with the power.
  • In buildings that will open as warming or cooling spaces, put the closure and shelter slide in the schedule ready to publish.
  • At a lake or seasonal house, decide whether to shut the wall down and unplug it until the storm passes.

During an outage

  • Leave the screens off if the battery unit is carrying only the controller and player; that is what it was sized for.
  • If the building runs on a generator, expect a brief restart of anything not on battery when power transfers.
  • Do not reset or reconfigure the controller during flickers; wait until power is steady.

When power returns

  • Watch the wall come back. If every screen wakes on the right picture, nothing more is needed.
  • If one tile stays dark, power-cycle that tile alone at its outlet or breaker before anything else.
  • If the whole wall shows “no signal”, check that the player or controller restarted; the label on the rack says which button to press.
  • If anything still looks wrong, send a photo to (845) 640-3835; most post-storm faults are settings, not hardware.

Storm damage from a surge can reach a wall through the power line or through a long data cable from another building. Surge protection on both, installed at the start, is cheaper than the panels it protects.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Spare Tile and the Power-Off Test

Two things end every Putnam wall job, whatever its size. The first is the spare. If the client bought one, it goes on a shelf in a heated room in the same building, in its box, with a label naming the wall, the position it fits, the date and the serial number, and the same details go on the handover sheet. The county’s own budget transfer for two broken 911 monitors in 2026 is the reason we insist on it: a tile will fail one day, and the cost of that day depends on whether its twin is already in the building.

The second is the power-off test. With the person who will run the wall standing beside us, we switch off the circuit feeding the wall and its equipment, wait, and switch it back on. The screens should wake, the controller should restore its layout and the player should resume its schedule, with no one touching a remote. If anything does not come back, we fix the setting and test again. In a county where a single storm can leave most of a town without power, a wall that cannot recover by itself is only half installed.

Neither step takes more than half an hour. Both are written into the proposal so that a board or district can see them before it votes.

FAQ

Putnam County Video Wall Installation FAQ

Can we see a full-size layout on the wall before anything is drilled?
+

Yes. Before a bracket goes up we tape the outline of every screen on the wall at real size, with the bezel lines marked, and stand where the audience will sit. In a town meeting room we also check the outline through the meeting camera, and in a library program room from the back row. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a mounting rail costs a day. Most layout changes happen at this step, usually lowering the wall or shifting it away from a window.

Will the wall’s fans be audible during a quiet library program?
+

It depends on the hardware, which is why we ask about quiet rooms at the survey. Many commercial LCD video-wall panels are cooled without fans, so the screens themselves are close to silent. Direct-view LED walls and their processors usually do have fans, and a media player or switch in the same room can hum. In a reading room or a sanctuary we put the rack-mounted gear in a closet or a ventilated cabinet on the other side of the wall and run the signal through, so the only thing in the room is the picture.

Can we start with four screens and add more later?
+

Yes, if the first wall is planned for it. That means choosing a panel model that is likely to stay available, a mounting rail longer than the first four screens need, a controller with spare outputs, and power and data boxes already roughed in for the next column. Even then, panels bought years apart can differ slightly in colour, so we calibrate the old and new screens together when the wall grows. If expansion is likely, we tell you what to buy now to keep it possible.

Does the wall need the internet to keep showing content?
+

No. A wall driven by a local media player or a computer in the room keeps playing what is stored on it when the internet drops, and Putnam’s rural lines do drop. Internet only matters for updating content remotely, pulling live web dashboards or streaming. We set the player to fall back to stored content when a live source fails, so a lost connection shows the last schedule rather than an error screen.

Do you remove the old projector, screen and ceiling mount?
+

Yes. When a wall replaces a projector we take down the projector, its ceiling mount and the pull-down or fixed screen, cap and label any cable we leave in the ceiling, and patch the mounting holes enough to paint. Old projectors and screens leave with us unless the owner wants to keep them; a school or town may have its own surplus or disposal procedure, and we follow it.

Can the wall show live captions during a public meeting?
+

It can display them, but the captions have to come from somewhere. If the meeting is streamed through a platform that produces automatic captions, or a captioning service is booked, the caption feed can be placed on one zone of the wall while the presentation fills the rest. We set up the zone and the routing; the choice of captioning service and its accuracy belong to the board or district. We point this out early, because a caption zone changes how much of the wall is left for slides.

Can you work from a municipal purchase order or a school bid specification?
+

Yes. Towns, fire districts, libraries and school districts in Putnam buy through purchase orders, bids and board resolutions, and we quote to the line items a specification asks for. Where a specification names a brand or model, we price it as written and can list an equivalent separately. We send insurance certificates and our NYS license number with the proposal, and we can attend a pre-bid walkthrough if the district holds one.

What should staff check on the wall each month?
+

Five minutes is enough. Turn the wall on from the control panel and confirm every screen wakes; look for a tile that is darker, tinted or late to start; check that the scheduled content changed when it should have; glance at the battery backup’s status light; and dust the vents on any rack gear. Anything odd gets a photo sent to us. A tile that is starting to fail usually shows it weeks before it goes dark.

Who answers if the wall goes dark the evening before a meeting?
+

Call or text the Putnam line, (845) 640-3835. A photo of the wall and the control panel usually tells us whether it is a power or input problem that staff can fix on the phone in a few minutes, or a visit. Visits are billed at the service rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum ($585). Agencies with a maintenance plan get priority scheduling written into it.

Will a wall work in a room with big windows facing a lake?
+

Yes, with planning. Lake and river light in Putnam comes in low and bright in winter and reflects off water, so a wall opposite a window can wash out at certain hours. The answers are placement first — side walls rather than the wall facing the glass — then a brighter panel model, a matte or anti-glare surface, and shades on the worst windows. We visit at the time of day the room is used, or ask for photos taken then.
