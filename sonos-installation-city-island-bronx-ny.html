The Neighborhood

Why City Island Homes Need Sonos Installation

City Island is the only part of the Bronx where every address is close to open water. Wikipedia describes the island as roughly 1.5 miles long and half a mile wide, set at the western end of Long Island Sound with Pelham Bay Park to the north and Eastchester Bay to the west, and counts 4,417 residents in the 2020 census. Benjamin Palmer bought it in 1761 meaning to build a seaport to rival New York, which is where the name came from; the port never happened, and the island became a place of oystermen, boatyards and, later, seafood restaurants. It joined New York City by a narrow vote in 1895, in exchange for a bridge, and one bridge is still the only road on or off. Writing for Forgotten New York, Kevin Walsh compared it to "a New England seaside town transplanted into the five boroughs." For a Sonos installer, that comparison is useful: the houses, the weather and the risks are coastal-village ones, not big-city ones.

The first thing that shapes the job is water at street level. In 2021 City Limits reported that on some City Island blocks the front of a house "becomes a lake" several times a month, whenever there is a new moon or more than a drizzle, and quoted John Doyle of the community group City Island Rising: "This is not a once-a-season problem, this is a Tuesday problem." Superstorm Sandy in 2012 flooded living rooms and tore the neon lobster off the Lobster House at the southern end of the island. This September News 12 Bronx filmed waterfront businesses stacking tables and covering chairs ahead of a nor'easter, with the Lobster House's manager recalling how Sandy's water ran into the parking lot and the street "and then there was no way for the water to go." In a house like that, the place the internet provider drops the router — often a basement or a ground-floor wall near the meter — is exactly where the most expensive Sonos parts should not live.

The second thing is the houses themselves. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's research staff, writing about the island's newest individual landmarks in 2017, described City Island's housing as "predominantly Italianate and Second Empire," and three of the houses the Commission has designated individually on the island show the range: the William H. Schofield House on Schofield Street, a transitional Italianate farmhouse of about 1860 sheathed in wood clapboard; the Booth House on Centre Street, a two-and-a-half-storey Stick-Style Queen Anne cottage of the late 1880s or 1890s; and the Stafford House on Pell Place, a 1930 Craftsman bungalow bought as a kit from Sears Roebuck. Wikipedia notes larger Victorian houses "mostly on the Sound side, complete with tall pointy spires and gables." Almost all of it is timber framing — good news for anyone who wants ceiling speakers, because wood joists and open attics are the easiest structure in the city to wire through.

The third thing is the air. Spray off the Sound, wind across Eastchester Bay and a long boating season mean that anything left outside on a porch or deck lives in conditions closer to a marina than a backyard. Sonos's own outdoor speaker pair is described by the company as "engineered for humidity, water, salt spray, heat, UV rays, and freezing temperatures," with an IP66 rating — but the portable speakers people carry out to the porch are rated for splashes and dunks, not for a summer of salt settling on the grille. Matching each product to where it will actually sit is most of the outdoor work we do here.

And then there is the neighbour on the next island. High Island, connected to the north end of City Island by a private bridge, carries the transmitter towers for two of the region's big AM stations, WFAN on 660 and the 880 station long known as WCBS. Strong nearby radio signals are one of the classic causes of buzzing in audio equipment, and a long run of unshielded speaker wire can behave like an antenna. It is not a problem in most houses, but it is a City Island question no installer on the mainland has to think about, and we cover it below.

City Island facts that shape a Sonos plan

  • 1.5 miles by 0.5 mile, 4,417 people: the island's size and 2020 population (Wikipedia), small enough that every house is a few blocks from the shoreline.
  • "A Tuesday problem": how City Island Rising described street flooding to City Limits in 2021 — several times a month on the lowest blocks, not once a season.
  • Italianate and Second Empire: the predominant house styles on the island according to the Landmarks Preservation Commission's research staff (CityLand, December 2017) — wood-framed houses with attics, porches and clapboard walls.
  • October 29, 2017: the day the New City Island Causeway Bridge opened, replacing the 950-foot steel bridge of 1901 (Wikipedia). Every installer, every delivery and every evacuation still crosses it.
  • 660 and 880 AM: the two radio stations whose transmitters stand on High Island, just off the north end of City Island, in operation there since 1963 (Wikipedia).
  • 43 units in 2015: the On the Sound condominium, which Wikipedia, citing the New York Times, calls the first major residential project on the island since around 2000 — new construction is rare here.

Put together, the numbers describe a small, old, wooden, low-lying community that lives with the weather. None of that makes a good Sonos system harder to own. It changes where the parts go, which products face the outside, and how the network is built so that it comes back by itself after the power and the water have gone.

Most City Island households pair a Sonos visit with a TV move or a network fix, since the soundbar, the screen and the router are usually all on the ground floor and all due for a rethink. Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for City Island's Architecture

Six kinds of property make up almost every City Island call. Most are wood-framed; what separates them is age, height, how close they sit to the water and who else, if anyone, has a say.

Italianate and Second Empire Houses

The island's characteristic nineteenth-century houses: square plans, tall windows, bracketed eaves, porches and verandas, and in the Second Empire examples a mansard top floor. Clapboard over timber framing, with attics and joist bays that take speaker cable readily.

Sound-Side Victorians

The larger Victorian houses Wikipedia places mostly on the Sound side, with spires, gables and wraparound porches facing the water. Big rooms, several floors, long distances from the router, and outdoor spaces that take the full force of the weather.

Queen Anne and Stick-Style Cottages

Asymmetric two-and-a-half-storey houses like the landmarked Booth House on Centre Street: gables, deep eaves, bay windows and irregular rooms where speaker placement is decided by measurement rather than symmetry.

Bungalows and Small Cottages

One-storey and storey-and-a-half houses, including Craftsman bungalows like the 1930 Sears kit house on Pell Place. Low roofs, shallow attics and living space close to the ground — which puts flood height at the centre of the plan.

Waterfront Condominiums

Newer buildings such as the 43-unit On the Sound complex of 2015, with big windows onto the water, modern partitions and a condominium board whose rules cover anything on balconies and exterior walls.

Apartments On and Off the Avenue

Flats over shops on City Island Avenue, units in the six-storey Pickwick Terrace of 1963 (which Forgotten New York, writing in 2000, called the island's tallest building) and apartments carved from older houses — renters first, so free-standing systems are the default.

Two methods matter more on City Island than in the rest of the borough. The first is the direction of the wiring. On the mainland we often start in a basement because that is where the internet arrives and where there is room. Here we try to start at the top: we go up into the attic or the mansard space, run cable down into the bedrooms and upper rooms that want ceiling speakers, and bring a single network cable down to the provider's equipment. The basement becomes a place the cable passes through on its way up, not a place where anything powered is left behind.

The second is the outside wall. Clapboard houses lose heat and let in drafts around every hole, and on the Sound side a hole in the wrong place lets in water driven by the wind. Where cable has to pass through an exterior wall to reach a porch or deck speaker, we drill on a slight downward slope toward the outside, use a sealed entry, form a drip loop in the cable and seal the penetration, then check it from both sides. Where we can, we avoid exterior penetrations altogether by bringing porch cable down through the porch ceiling from an upstairs room.

An open attic is a good moment for the rest of the low-voltage list. Island owners regularly add a porch camera or a new door intercom on the same cable routes. Above the Waterline

Where Sonos Equipment Should Live on a Flood-Prone Island

Every Sonos system has a few parts that matter more than the speakers: whatever connects it to the internet, whatever powers the passive speakers, and whatever bridges it to an older stereo. On City Island those parts — the router, the Amp, the Port, and any switch or access point that ties floors together — are the ones we move first, because they are the ones most often sitting where the water arrives.

Internet providers install their equipment where the line comes into the building, and on an older island house that is commonly a cellar wall, a crawlspace entry or a ground-floor room by the front door. Nobody asks whether that spot took water during Sandy or floods at the new moon. The City Limits report on island flooding describes residents carrying out the contents of whole living rooms after Sandy, and blocks where water collects "even after the slightest weather events." An Amp on the cellar floor in one of those houses is a matter of when, not whether.

Three heights we plan around

The water height. We ask what the street and the house have actually done: did the ground floor take water in 2012, does the cellar get wet in a heavy rain, does the front walk flood at the new moon? Owners usually know to the inch. That line, plus a margin, is the floor below which nothing powered and permanent goes.

The listening height. Speakers belong where they sound right and where the household spends time: soundbars under the TV, ceiling speakers over the kitchen island or the bed, outdoor speakers under the porch roof. A speaker on a ground floor that sometimes floods is acceptable if it can be lifted or replaced; the Amp feeding it should not be on that floor.

The equipment height. The Amp, Port, switch and, where possible, the main router move up — to a hall closet on the first floor of a raised house, a landing cupboard, or a ventilated shelf on the second floor. One network cable runs back down to the provider's box, which stays where it is because the provider owns it. Sonos's guidance asks for open space around the Amp — about three inches — and a working range of 32–104°F, which also rules out unheated porches and uninsulated attics in January and August.

Moving the brains up does more than protect them. A router in the middle of the first or second floor reaches more of a tall Victorian than one in a stone cellar, and the cable that connects it back down becomes the start of a wired backbone that Sonos uses happily. On a house where the cellar is dry and always has been, none of this is necessary — we say so and leave things where they are.

After a storm, in the right order

When water has been in the house, the order of work matters more than speed. The CDC's guidance on re-entering a flooded home is plain: have an electrician check the electrical system before the power goes back on, and "all electrical equipment and appliances must be completely dry before returning them to service." We do not plug in or test any Sonos product that sat in floodwater until the electrician has cleared the house, and we do not close up any wall or ceiling cavity that is still drying. Once it is safe, we re-join what survived, replace what did not, and use the visit to move the survivors upstairs.

Salt, Wind and Spray

Outdoor Sonos Speakers for Porches and Decks Facing the Sound

A wraparound porch with a view across Eastchester Bay, a deck at the end of a Sound-side lot, a back yard that runs down to a seawall: City Island's outdoor spaces are the reason many people live here, and they are the hardest places on the island for electronics. Three things work against a speaker outdoors here — salt carried on the wind, driving rain off the water, and the plain force of the wind on anything mounted high.

Choosing the product for the spot

For a permanent outdoor zone we use Sonos's outdoor speakers, powered by an Amp kept indoors. Sonos describes them as "engineered for humidity, water, salt spray, heat, UV rays, and freezing temperatures," rates them IP66 and gives an operating range of −13°F to 149°F. They are passive speakers: the only thing outside is the speaker and its cable, while the Amp sits in a dry room. That split is exactly right for the island.

Portables are different. The Move 2 is rated IP56 and Sonos says its charging base is for indoor use only; the Roam 2 is rated IP67, which covers a dunk in fresh water for a short time. Neither rating is a promise about months of salt air, so we treat portables as things that go out for the evening and come back in, not as fixtures on a porch rail.

Fixings and cable that last near the water

Brackets go into framing, not just into clapboard or trim, because a speaker that shifts in one winter storm pulls its fixings loose. Fixings are corrosion-resistant. Cable is outdoor-rated, runs inside the porch structure wherever possible, and enters the house through a sealed, downward-sloping penetration with a drip loop. Where a cable has to run along a surface, it goes in a channel on the sheltered side of a post or beam rather than on the face that takes the weather.

We also think about where the sound goes. Water carries sound, and on a calm summer evening music from a deck facing the bay can travel across to the boats on their moorings and to the houses on the other side of the harbour. Outdoor speakers are aimed down at the seating, not out at the horizon, and we set volume limits on outdoor zones at handover so the level that sounds right on the deck is not the level heard across the water.

Winter on the Sound

Outdoor speakers rated for the cold can stay up all year; the Amp indoors is what needs a heated room. Before a big storm we suggest owners bring in any portable, and before the season ends we check fixings and seals on porch and deck speakers in the same visit as any other work. Salt that has dried on a grille comes off with a cloth dampened in fresh water; we show owners how at handover.

Two active sail lofts and several commercial marinas operate on the island (Wikipedia). Large open lofts and marina offices need sound that carries across a big floor without filling the yard outside.

Small shops along the Avenue where music should sit quietly under conversation, start when the door opens for the day and stop at closing.

Community Venues

Church halls, the City Island Theater Group's productions and the meeting rooms of the island's many civic and boating organisations — places that need simple controls volunteers can use.

Compliance notes for business music on City Island

  • The house behind the restaurant decides the volume. According to the city's noise code fact sheet, commercial music "shall not exceed 42 dBA as measured in any dwelling unit receiving the noise." On an island where houses sit behind and above the restaurants, we set Sonos volume limits per room and check them with you before we leave.
  • Nothing aimed at the street or the water to draw customers. New York City Administrative Code §24-244 prohibits using a sound reproduction device outside, or through an opening onto a public street, to attract attention for commercial purposes. We aim restaurant and shop speakers into the room and onto the seating.
  • A business music service. Personal streaming accounts are not meant for dining rooms: Spotify's help centre states it is "only for personal, non-commercial use" and rules out playing it publicly in bars and restaurants. We set up whichever licensed business service you choose on the Sonos system; we do not sell licences.
  • Outdoor dining. The city's Dining Out rules state there should be "no musical instruments or amplified sound" in outdoor dining areas on the sidewalk or roadway unless authorised by a Street Activity Permit. Waterside decks on private property are a different question, and the 42 dBA limit at neighbouring homes still applies.
  • Outlets are not low-voltage work. If the Amp cabinet behind the bar has no receptacle, a licensed electrician adds one; we show them exactly where and fit our work around their visit.

A summer room and a winter room

A dining room that seats a full house on a July Saturday and a dozen regulars on a February Tuesday needs two different volumes, and staff rarely remember to change them. Sonos alarms can start music at a chosen time, in a chosen room, at a chosen volume, and volume limits stop anyone pushing the level past what the neighbours will accept. We set up a quiet-season level, a peak-season level and the limit above both, and show the manager the two controls staff are allowed to touch.

Water, salt and the weather off the Sound

Our front room windows face the Sound and stay open all summer — does the salty air harm indoor Sonos speakers?

Not quickly, but it is worth placing them with the air in mind. Sonos does not publish a salt rating for its indoor speakers; it does name the Era 100, Era 300, Move 2 and Roam 2 as products built to cope with humidity, which is why we choose those for rooms that are open to the water for months at a time. What actually harms an indoor speaker by an open window is spray and condensation landing on it, and salt residue building up on the grille and the connections at the back.

So we keep speakers back from the sill rather than on it, out of the path of rain that blows in, and away from the corner where a window air conditioner drips. At handover we show you how to wipe the grille with a cloth dampened in fresh water at the end of the season. If a room is effectively a screened porch for half the year, we treat it as one and plan it with the outdoor section above in mind.

Water came into the ground floor in the last storm — can the Sonos speakers that sat in it be dried out and used again?

Do not plug them in to find out. Floodwater on City Island is seawater mixed with whatever it picked up on the way, and electronics that have been under it can be damaged in ways that only show once power is applied. The CDC's guidance for flooded homes says the house's electrical system should be checked by an electrician before power is turned back on, and that all electrical equipment and appliances "must be completely dry before returning them to service." In practice, a speaker or Amp that was submerged in salt water is usually a replacement, not a repair.

What can be saved is the system itself. Once the house is cleared, we re-join any products that stayed dry upstairs, set up the replacements in the same rooms with the same names and groups, and use the visit to move the Amp, Port and router above the level the water reached. If your insurer needs a written description of what was installed and where, we can provide one from our records of the original job.

Old wood-frame houses

When the Sub plays, the old sash windows and the china cabinet in our house rattle — can that be stopped without losing the bass?

Usually, yes. A wood-frame house is a light structure compared with a masonry building, and old sash windows, loose glass panes, cabinet doors and glassware on shelves all have their own resonant frequencies. When a Sub happens to hit one of them, the house buzzes along. The fix starts with where the Sub sits: moving it a few feet along a wall, out of a corner or off a hollow floor section often changes which notes are reinforced and stops the rattle completely.

After placement, we adjust the Sub's level and use Trueplay on the soundbar so the room is not over-driven in the first place, and we put the Sub on a dense pad where it stands on old floorboards. If one window still buzzes on a particular note, the problem is the sash, not the Sonos — a small weatherstrip or a turn of the sash lock usually ends it. We find the culprit with you in the room before we leave.

Our Victorian has a round tower room under one of the spires — where do speakers go in a room with curved walls?

Not in the middle of the curve. A round or many-sided room tends to focus sound toward its centre, so a speaker placed facing straight across the room can create a spot where everything sounds louder and boomier than elsewhere. In a tower room we usually place a single Era 100 or Era 300 to one side of the curve, firing across the room at an angle rather than through its centre, or at the flat wall where the tower joins the main house if there is one.

Ceiling speakers are possible if there is a flat section of ceiling below the spire and a way into it from the attic, but conical or domed ceilings rarely suit them. Whichever product we use, Trueplay is run with it in its final spot, because a curved room is exactly the kind of space where automatic tuning earns its keep. We also check the windows: towers often have more glass than wall, which leaves fewer places to mount anything.

The porch speaker keeps dropping off the system — could our aluminum storm windows be the reason?

Quite possibly. Sonos's guidance on wireless interference rates metal as a very high obstruction for Wi-Fi, higher than plaster or brick, and a run of aluminum storm windows and a metal storm door between the router and the porch can cut the signal sharply. Foil-faced insulation and metal porch screens have the same effect. A speaker on the far side of that barrier may work when the house is quiet and drop out when the network is busy.

The cure is to stop asking Wi-Fi to pass through the metal. Porch speakers on an indoor Amp do not need Wi-Fi outside at all: the Amp sits inside and the porch gets speaker cable. For a wireless speaker on a screened porch, we put a wired access point or a wired Sonos product on the inside of the wall closest to the porch. Range extenders are not the answer; Sonos lists them among the setups it does not support.

Living next to the radio towers

We live at the north end, near the WFAN and 880 towers on High Island — can their signal make our speakers buzz?

It can, though most houses never notice it. The ARRL, the national association for amateur radio, explains that interference happens when audio equipment picks up radio signals it was never meant to receive, and that a strong nearby signal can overload equipment "even if a nearby radio signal is being transmitted on its assigned frequency." The FCC's interference handbook, as the ARRL summarises it, treats audio devices that pick up radio signals as improperly acting as receivers, with the cure being filtering applied to the affected device. High Island has carried the transmitters for 660 and 880 AM since 1963, a short distance across the water from the north end of City Island.

The symptom is usually a faint voice, music or buzz in passive speakers, an older receiver connected through a Port, or a turntable preamp. Long runs of speaker wire are the most likely pickup. We keep runs short, twist pairs, route speaker cable away from network and power cables, ground equipment properly and, where needed, fit ferrite chokes or filters at the affected component. Sonos's own wireless speakers, with no long cable runs, are the least likely part of a system to be affected.

Will the towers on High Island interfere with the Sonos Wi-Fi itself?

No; they work in a completely different part of the radio spectrum. WFAN broadcasts on 660 kHz and the 880 station on 880 kHz, while Sonos runs on Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz and above — thousands of times higher in frequency. A dropout on a City Island Sonos system is almost always caused by the same things that cause dropouts everywhere: distance, walls, metal, crowded Wi-Fi channels and products that are not where the network expects them.

If your speakers drop out, the useful questions are where the router is, what is between it and the speaker, and whether anything has changed on the network recently. Sonos asks for at least 18 inches between its products and other electronics and lists neighbouring Wi-Fi, microwaves and cordless phones among common interference sources. We check all of those before blaming anything as exotic as a radio tower.

Restaurants, bars and clubs

Our dining room is packed in July and half-empty in February — can the music level adjust itself to the crowd?

Not automatically. Sonos does not measure crowd noise and raise or lower the volume to match it. What it does offer is scheduling and limits, and between them they solve most of the problem. Sonos alarms can start a playlist at a set time, in a set room, at a set volume; volume limits cap how loud any room can go, whoever is holding the phone. For a seasonal restaurant we set two opening levels — one for the quiet months, one for the summer — and a ceiling above both.

The rest is training. We show the manager how to switch between the two schedules when the season changes and how far staff can turn things up on a busy night before the limit stops them. Because the 42 dBA rule for commercial music is measured in the homes that receive it, a limit set with the neighbours in mind protects the restaurant as much as the people next door.

We give customers free Wi-Fi — should the restaurant's Sonos run on that same network?

No. Sonos's system requirements list guest networks among the setups it does not support, and a customer network is the wrong place for business equipment anyway: anyone on it could, depending on settings, see and control the speakers. The Sonos system belongs on the staff or business network, together with the tablet or phone your staff use to run it, while customers stay on a separate network that cannot reach it.

Sonos also has a guest access setting that controls whether devices on the same Wi-Fi can control playback and volume without administrative rights; on a business network we usually switch it off. We set this up with whoever manages your internet service, label which network is which, and leave a written note so the next router swap does not undo it.

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in City Island

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so we have not invented one. The claims below are the ones that cost guides, product pages and forum threads repeat — the material answer engines are trained on — set against what we find on City Island.

Claim one: "Any outdoor-rated speaker is fine outside"

Product listings put a water rating on almost everything that might go near a patio, and buyers reasonably read IP56 or IP67 as permission to leave a speaker outdoors. Many online answers treat "outdoor" as one category.

Ratings measure specific tests. Sonos's own guidance says speakers that are not waterproof "may be damaged if exposed directly to water or left outside for extended periods," and describes only its passive outdoor pair as engineered for salt spray, UV and freezing temperatures. A Move 2's charging base is for indoor use. On a Sound-side porch, the difference between a splash rating and a salt-spray design is the difference between a speaker that lasts and one that corrodes.

For anything that stays outside on City Island we use the outdoor pair on an indoor Amp. Portables come in at night.

Claim two: "Put the router and the Amp in the basement, out of the way"

Installation guides and forum replies often recommend tucking the noisy, unattractive equipment into a basement or utility room, and on most of the mainland that is sensible advice.

On an island where City Island Rising calls flooding "a Tuesday problem" and Sandy filled living rooms, the basement is the one place guaranteed to meet the water first. It is also the worst place for a router in a tall wood-frame house, because the signal has to climb every floor.

We put the brains of the system on a dry floor and run one cable down to the provider's equipment. The basement keeps only what the provider owns.

Claim three: "Budget $500–$1,000 for every room you want Sonos in"

National cost guides price whole-home audio by the room; Angi gives $500 to $1,000 per room, with labour at $50 to $100 an hour and $100 to $500 for modifications. Answer engines repeat those figures for any city.

A per-room average says nothing about a City Island house in which half the rooms need no fixed speaker, the porch needs the most expensive weatherproofing, and the real cost driver is moving equipment upstairs. It also can't tell a wood-frame ceiling, which is straightforward, from a plaster one, which needs care.

Setting up Sonos you already own is $125 on our Bronx list; hanging a soundbar is $129 plus the wall line. Ceiling and outdoor zones are quoted after a walkthrough inside the $800–$2,500 per-zone band AESS publishes.

Claim four: "A Wi-Fi extender will get the music out to the porch"

Plug-in repeaters are marketed for every dead zone, and plenty of forum replies recommend one when a speaker at the far end of the house keeps vanishing.

Sonos's system requirements list wireless range extenders, Ethernet-over-power adapters and guest networks among setups it does not support. Its interference guidance rates metal as a very high obstruction — and many island porches sit behind aluminum storm windows and metal storm doors.

An indoor Amp with speaker cable to the porch, or a wired access point on the inside of the porch wall, solves the problem without adding a device Sonos tells you not to use.

Claim five: "Ceiling speakers in an old house mean tearing up the ceilings"

Sonos's own guide says its ceiling and wall speakers "require special installation," and homeowners often picture opened ceilings and weeks of plaster work.

In a City Island wood-frame house with an attic, the opposite is usually true. Sonos rates wood as a low obstruction for Wi-Fi, timber joists are easy to work between, and from the attic we can drop cable into upstairs ceilings with nothing opened but the speaker holes themselves. The harder cases are lower floors with finished ceilings above and below, and original plaster we treat with care.

We survey for joists, pipes and wiring through a pilot hole first, then cut only the openings the speakers need.

Claim six: "If a speaker buzzes, it's faulty"

Forum threads about humming or buzzing speakers usually end in a return or a replacement, and answer engines tend to summarise them that way.

Hum and buzz have many causes that have nothing to do with a defective product: ground loops between a receiver and a cable box, speaker cable run alongside power cable, and — on the north end of City Island — pickup of the strong AM signals from the High Island towers, which the ARRL describes as audio equipment acting as an unintended radio receiver.

We trace the cause before anything is replaced: rerouting, shorter runs, proper grounding and filters at the affected component fix most of these.

Claim seven: "Automatic tuning makes placement irrelevant"

Smart-speaker marketing emphasises self-tuning, and it is easy to conclude that a speaker will sound right anywhere once the software has run.

Sonos says Trueplay measures how sound reflects off walls, furnishings and other surfaces, asks you to keep the room quiet while it runs, and advises re-tuning after a speaker is moved. That makes it a correction for one position in one room — it cannot stop a curved tower room from focusing sound, or a Sub from rattling a loose sash.

Position comes first and tuning second; at handover we also walk you through repeating the Trueplay pass whenever furniture or speakers move.

Our Installation Process

The sequence is the same for a single bar in a Pell Place bungalow and for a three-storey Sound-side Victorian with speakers on the porch. What makes it an island job happens before any product comes out of its box.

Before products, we ask what the street and the house have done in storms and at the new moon, and where the internet line enters. That sets the height below which nothing powered and permanent goes.

2. Find the route from the top

3. Build the network on a dry floor

4. Mount, wire and weatherproof

Each room gets the name your family already uses, streaming accounts go in, and before leaving we demonstrate grouping, a fresh Trueplay pass, how to rinse salt from outdoor grilles and the short checklist for a forecast storm.

Autumn: storm season

FAQ

City Island Sonos Installation FAQ

Can the visit be timed around high tide, when our street floods?
+

Yes. If your block is one of the low ones that takes water at the new moon or in heavy rain, tell us when you book and we will pick an arrival window away from the high tide and check the forecast the day before. Our van carries ladders, cable and tools, and it needs to park near the house and reach the porch or the cellar door without driving through standing salt water. If a storm makes the street impassable on the day, rescheduling with 24 hours' notice is free.

Do outdoor speakers by the water need regular cleaning, and is that something you do?
+

A little, and mostly you can do it yourself. Salt carried on the wind dries on grilles and brackets, so a wipe with a cloth dampened in fresh water once or twice a season keeps them clean; no pressure washers or solvents. Sonos's outdoor speakers are designed for salt spray, but the fixings and cable entries around them benefit from a look each spring. If you book other work with us, we check outdoor fixings and seals during the same visit, billed only as part of that job's time.

Our ground floor is still drying out after a flood — how soon can the Sonos work go ahead?
+

Upstairs work can usually go ahead as soon as an electrician has cleared the house's electrical system; work on the flooded floor should wait. The CDC advises drying a flooded home out as soon as possible to prevent mold, and we will not close up a wall or ceiling cavity that is still wet or run cable through damp framing. In the meantime we can set up free-standing products upstairs, move the Amp and router to a dry floor, and come back for the ground floor once the walls are dry and repaired.

When the power goes out in a storm, can we still play music on any of our Sonos speakers?
+

Only on a battery portable. Sonos's mains-powered speakers, soundbars and Amps stop when the power does. A Roam 2 or Move 2 keeps playing on its battery, and Sonos says the Roam 2 can stream from a phone over Bluetooth when Wi-Fi is unavailable, which is what you will have if the router is down too. Keep a portable charged when a storm is forecast. When the power returns, the rest of the system reconnects to the network on its own once the router is back up.
