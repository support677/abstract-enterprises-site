The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Lefferts Gardens Properties Need Access Control

Prospect Lefferts Gardens packs three building eras into a small rectangle. Between Empire Boulevard and Clarkson Avenue, and from Ocean Avenue and the park east to New York Avenue, there are the one-family brick and stone houses of Lefferts Manor, the six-story elevator apartment houses that went up in the 1920s and 1930s, a handful of mid-century developments, and since the mid-2010s a run of new towers along Flatbush Avenue. Each era has its own kind of front door, its own kind of owner and its own rules, and that is what makes a door system here harder to plan than the sales sheets suggest. A keypad that suits a Manor stoop is wrong for a ninety-apartment lobby, and a fob plan that works in a new tower can break the law in a rent-stabilized walk-up.

The houses come with a promise written into their deeds. When James Lefferts divided his family's estate in 1893, he split it into 600 lots and required each one to carry a single-family brick or stone house at least two stories high; that covenant still stands, and the Lefferts Manor Association, founded in 1919, enforces it. In 1979 the Landmarks Preservation Commission made the area a historic district, and the association's own page puts the consequence plainly: "any proposed alterations to the outside of homes must receive approval from the Commission before work begins." So a family that wants a keypad or a video station on its stoop is dealing with one household's keys on the inside and a city agency's design rules on the outside, and both have to be planned before anything is drilled.

The apartment houses have a different problem, and it is mostly about people and paper. Buildings such as 115 Ocean Avenue, which StreetEasy lists as a six-story, 90-unit rental built in 1925, were wired for brass keys and an audio buzzer. Many of their tenants are rent-stabilized, which means that when an owner replaces street-door keys with fobs, the state housing agency, DHCR, has treated it as a change in a required service and attached conditions: free fobs for every lawful occupant, a cap on replacement fees, a metal key that still opens the door in an emergency, a battery that keeps the door working through an outage. A 2021 city law, the Tenant Data Privacy Law, adds its own rules about consent, what data a fob system may collect and how quickly it must be deleted. A door system in one of these buildings is as much a set of written policies as it is hardware.

The towers are the third case. The Parkline at 626 Flatbush Avenue, which StreetEasy lists as a 23-story, 254-unit building finished in 2015, has a doorman, a concierge, a package room, a garage, a bike room and storage lockers. A building like that rarely has trouble at its front door, because someone is sitting behind it. Its exposure is the doors the desk cannot see: the garage elevator lobby, the amenity floors, the bike and storage rooms, the loading door and the stairs. Smaller new buildings have the opposite profile. CityRealty lists 185 Ocean Avenue as a 20-unit condominium from 2008 with an elevator, private storage and a package room and no doorman, and Hello Lenox at 271 Lenox Road as 56 apartments over 16 floors with "private keyed elevator access" to each apartment. In both, the owners run the doors themselves.

Then there are the shops. Flatbush Avenue forms the western spine of the neighborhood, and Nostrand and Rogers Avenues, Lincoln Road and Empire Boulevard carry stores, restaurants, salons, offices and churches, many with apartments above them. Over three weeks in January and February 2016, DNAinfo reported, burglars hit four Flatbush Avenue businesses at the neighborhood's southern edge, from 650 Flatbush Avenue down to Parkside Avenue, two of them through back doors after closing, and at a pharmacy near Parkside they took the store's hard drive with its camera footage. In February 2026, News 12 reported that the state shut a smoke shop at 645 Rogers Avenue that drew late-night traffic beside a building housing a day care center. The back doors, stockrooms and shared side entrances of buildings like these are where access control earns its keep.

What the materials mean for the install

The Manor houses and the Chester Court and Ocean on the Park rows were built with heavy wood doors in wood frames set into brick, brownstone or limestone. A wood frame takes a strike pocket well, as long as it has not been bored and patched for three generations of locks; the hard part is getting cable to it without chasing plaster in a parlor hallway or putting conduit on a landmarked front. We look first for the route the old bell wire took, the space under the stoop, and the cellar ceiling below the front hall. The Commission's guidebook asks that conduit run through plain masonry behind an installation where possible, and allows a short visible run, about ten inches, painted to match, when nothing else works.

The prewar apartment houses are a different trade. Their street doors are often steel-clad or aluminum-and-glass replacements hung in frames that have been kicked, wedged and re-screwed for decades. A strike is only as strong as the frame it sits in, and tenants at 501 Lefferts Avenue, a 60-unit building Patch places just over the line in Crown Heights, described the problem exactly in 2022: "They might change the lock, but the frame, they can kick it [open]." Before we wire a reader to a door like that, we price whatever the frame needs, from a steel reinforcement plate to a new hollow-metal frame grouted into the opening, because a fob on a door that can be shouldered open is a log entry and nothing more. The towers are the easiest physically: steel frames, drop ceilings, and riser closets with room for cable.

One Family, a Ring of Keys and a Landmarked Stoop: Door Systems for Lefferts Manor Houses

A Lefferts Manor house is, by deed, a one-family house, and it is easy to assume a one-family house has no use for access control. Count the keys and the picture changes. A typical household here has parents, children old enough to come home alone, a grandparent, a sitter or a cleaner, a dog walker, a neighbor who waters the plants, and a contractor who has had a key since the kitchen job. Every one of those keys opens the same stoop door, none of them leaves a record, and the only way to take one back is to change the lock and hand out a new ring. That is the problem a door system solves, and it is as real on Rutland Road as in any lobby.

The doors themselves are specific to the Manor. Most houses have the parlor-floor door at the top of a high stoop, which guests use; an areaway door under the stoop at garden level, which is how many families actually come and go with strollers, groceries and bikes; and a garden door at the back. In many houses the areaway door sits behind an iron gate at the sidewalk. We usually put the family's everyday credential on the door they use most, not the one that looks most like a front door, and keep the others on good mechanical locks with contacts that report when they open. A reader on the stoop door is often the last one a family adds, not the first.

Inside, the system is small. A controller and battery the size of a shoebox goes in a closet or the cellar, wired to each controlled door. Each person gets a personal code, a fob or a phone key, and each helper gets one that works only on the days and hours they come. When the cleaner changes or the contractor finishes, one credential is deleted and nothing else changes. The household can see which door opened and when, and can open a door from a phone for a delivery or a relative who forgot their code. The doors still open from inside with the handle, as they always did.

What the Landmarks Commission looks at on a Manor front

Because the 1979 district covers the exteriors, anything visible on the front of a Manor house needs Commission approval before work begins. The Commission's Permit Guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment lets staff approve intercom panels, doorbells and light fixtures when they are installed "within or adjacent to a door opening," allows an intercom on "a fence or wall in front of the entranceway," limits each door opening to one intercom panel, and asks that equipment be "as small as possible and painted to blend" and attached at mortar joints or plain masonry rather than through decorative stone or cast iron. The chapter does not mention card readers or keypads by name, so we treat a reader the way the rules treat a panel, keep it inside or beside the door opening, and confirm with Commission staff when a design falls outside that pattern.

In practice that points to a few patterns that usually pass. A single combined station with a camera, a call button and a reader, mounted on the masonry beside the stoop door or recessed into the door frame, in a dark or bronze finish. A keypad inside the areaway gate rather than on the facade. A reader that reads through the glass of a vestibule door so nothing shows outside at all. What rarely works is a row of separate boxes: a doorbell camera, a keypad and a reader side by side on a brownstone pier. The application needs color photos of the whole building, close-ups of the location, drawings of existing and proposed conditions and the manufacturer's cut sheets; we prepare the technical parts and the owner files.

Where the Lefferts Manor Association fits

The association enforces the single-family covenant, and its landmark page explains the Commission's role, but the rules about what goes on a facade come from the Commission, not from the association. A keypad on a Manor door does not change how the house is used, so it does not touch the covenant. What does touch it, and what we hear about, is a door system designed as if the house were a rental, with a directory and separate apartment buttons. If a household has a relative living upstairs or a long-term live-in helper, we give them their own credentials without making the front look like a multiple dwelling.

From Brass Keys to Fobs in Prewar Prospect Lefferts Gardens Rentals: What DHCR Has Required

The apartment houses of the 1920s and 1930s are where most of this neighborhood's tenants live, and where a fob conversion most often goes wrong. The hardware is not the hard part. The hard part is that a building with rent-stabilized apartments cannot simply swap its keys for fobs on the owner's schedule. DHCR, the state's housing agency, has treated a change from keys to an electronic entry system as a change in a required building service, and owners have applied for permission to make it. The decisions that came out of those applications, reported over the years by Landlord v. Tenant, read like a checklist for how to do the job properly.

In a July 2016 administrative review, DHCR let an owner replace entry keys with fobs on conditions that included keeping a metal key that still opens the building in an emergency, recording entries but not departures, accommodating tenants who keep the Sabbath and cannot use electronic devices, a 48-hour battery backup, free fobs for every occupant including children plus up to four more for guests or employees, replacements at no more than $25, no recording of identification, and no name, address or photo on the fob. In a September 2017 decision approving a magnetic lock and key-card system at a main entrance, the agency let tenants redact personal information from the ID they showed and barred the owner from requiring guests' arrival and departure dates. And in a July 2022 decision, a tenant reported two blackouts of more than 24 hours each, with no backup battery or generator, during which the door had to be propped open; DHCR said that was grounds for a service-reduction complaint against the owner.

We build to those conditions whether or not an owner has applied yet, because they describe a system tenants can live with. Every lawful occupant gets a fob without charge, the controller keeps doors working on battery for days rather than hours, a restricted mechanical key still opens the street door, the reader logs entries and keeps them only as long as the owner's written policy says, and no fob carries a name or an apartment number. Where an owner has not yet received approval, we tell them so in writing before we start, and we do not switch off the old locks until the application process is finished. Whether an application is needed for a particular building is a question for the owner's attorney; how to build a system that meets the conditions is ours.

The 2021 privacy law on top of DHCR

The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies to every multiple dwelling that uses smart access, which HPD defines to include key fobs, RFID cards, phone apps and biometrics, whether or not the apartments are regulated. Owners must get tenants' express consent in writing or through an app, give them a data retention and privacy policy, collect only a short list of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell it. HPD's summary adds that "No information may be used to harass or evict a tenant," and owners may not require smart access for a tenant to enter their own apartment. A fob on the street door is fine; a fob on each apartment door with no key option is not.

The concern is not theoretical in Brooklyn. In 2019 amNY reported on tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in East New York fighting their landlord's plan to replace keys with a facial-scanning system, and quoted the NYCLU's Lee Rowland: "It's fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data." We do not install face or fingerprint readers on residential street doors in rent-stabilized buildings, and on any residential system we set the shortest log retention the owner will accept.

Fix the door first, then add the fob

The same 2022 Patch report from 501 Lefferts Avenue counted 87 open HPD violations there, including broken locks, and quoted a management employee saying "There's only so much a landlord can do." A fob system does not cure violations, and on a street door that will not self-close and self-lock it can make an inspection worse, because it adds a powered lock to a door that is already failing. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires entrance doors in covered buildings to close and lock by themselves and, in buildings with eight or more apartments, an intercom with voice and a remote release. We start every apartment-house job by making the door, closer and frame meet that standard, then put the reader on it, and we never take out a working intercom because fobs are going in.

The Doors Behind the Desk: Access Control in the New Towers on Flatbush Avenue

The towers that rose along the park side of Flatbush Avenue in the last decade changed what access control means here. A building like The Parkline, which StreetEasy lists with a doorman, a concierge, a package room, assigned garage parking, a bike room, cold storage and storage lockers, a gym, a media room, a children's playroom and a roof deck across 23 stories and 254 units, has a staffed front door and a dozen other doors that nobody watches full time. Residents expect a fob to open the ones they pay for and nothing else, management expects a record when something goes missing from a storage cage, and the desk expects not to be the only thing standing between the sidewalk and the elevators.

The weak point in most towers is the second way in. Residents who park in the garage often ride the elevator up from a garage-level lobby without ever passing the desk, and so do their guests. Loading docks and service doors open for movers and contractors who are on site for a day. Fire stairs reach every floor. Amenity floors hold expensive equipment and are used at all hours. Each of those doors needs its own decision: who may open it, at what hours, whether it should alarm when it stays open, and what the desk should see when it does. A reader on the garage elevator lobby door that only residents with a parking assignment can open, and a camera beside it that feeds the desk, closes the gap most towers have.

Elevators deserve particular care. Hello Lenox advertises "private keyed elevator access" to its apartments, meaning a key decides which floor a car will stop at. Moving that to fobs means a reader in each cab and an interface to the elevator controller, which is the elevator contractor's equipment and must stay under its control and its inspections. We supply the access side and coordinate the interface with the elevator company the building already uses, so that firefighter service and the elevator's own safety circuits are never touched by our wiring. In a building that size the elevator work is often the longest lead item, and we say so when we price it.

Package rooms, bike rooms and storage cages

The rooms where residents keep property are where a tower's access log does the most good. A package room on its own reader, with couriers given a code that opens that room and the door between it and the street and nothing else, keeps deliveries off the lobby floor. A bike room or a cage room on fobs that only the residents who rent a space can open turns an argument about a missing bicycle into a list of who entered and when. We set those rooms up so the log answers a specific question and is then deleted on the building's schedule, not kept forever, which is what the city's privacy law expects of residential data.

Small new condos without a desk

The smaller buildings of the 2000s and 2010s have the same rooms and no staff. At a 20-unit condo like 185 Ocean Avenue, the owners themselves decide who gets a fob, who may change the list, and what happens when someone sells. The usual plan is a reader on the street door and the package room, a code-only lock on the roof and the cellar for the managing agent and contractors, a controller that keeps every credential on site so the door works when the internet is down, and two administrators chosen by the board, never one. The board's bylaws decide who may approve the system; we give them a one-page description of the work to vote on.

Manor houses and the landmarked rows

The covenant keeps our Lefferts Manor house a one-family home, yet two grown children, a grandparent and a Thursday cleaner all carry keys. Is a door system really worth it for one household?

Usually, yes, and for a reason that has nothing to do with the size of the house. The question is not how many families live behind the door but how many keys are out and whether you can take one back. A household like yours typically has six to ten keys in circulation, a few of them with people who no longer need them, and the only fix today is a new lock and a new ring for everyone. With a small controller and one reader or keypad on the door you use most, each person has their own code, fob or phone key, the cleaner's works only on Thursdays, and a lost fob is deleted in a minute while everyone else carries on.

It does not have to be the whole house. Many Manor families start with the areaway door under the stoop, which is the one they use with groceries and strollers, and leave the parlor door on its original lock with a contact that reports when it opens. Does the Lefferts Manor Association have to sign off on a keypad, or is the Landmarks Preservation Commission the only approval we need?

For what goes on the outside of the house, the approval that counts is the Commission's. The 1979 historic district designation covers the exteriors, and the association's own landmark page says that "any proposed alterations to the outside of homes must receive approval from the Commission before work begins." The association's role is the single-family covenant, which a keypad does not affect because it does not change how the house is used. Neighbors on a Manor block do care about how fronts look, so it costs nothing to show the design to the people next door.

Where a design could look like a conversion, for example a station with several buttons and a directory, it is worth avoiding for both reasons. A single-button station or a reader without a visible directory keeps the front looking like what it is, and it is also the kind of small, single device the Commission's staff rules are written around. Anything mounted inside the vestibule, where it cannot be seen from the street, usually needs no Commission review at all; we confirm that with staff when it is close.

Chester Court and Ocean on the Park were landmarked on their own, years after the 1979 district. Does a reader on one of those houses go through a different review?

The process is the same Commission permit process, applied to a different designation. On Chester Court's Tudor-revival fronts, with their arched doors and recessed entries, that usually means a slim reader or station inside the arch, finished dark, with cable brought through the masonry behind it. On the set-back limestone and brick townhouses of Ocean on the Park, the entrance is far enough from the sidewalk that a small device beside the door is barely visible from the street. In both places we suggest that owners on the row agree on one device and one position, which makes approval simpler and keeps the row consistent.

Apartment houses, old and new

Tenants in our prewar building say the lock on the street door keeps getting changed while it is the frame that gives way. Will an electric strike and a fob reader fix that?

Not by themselves. A strike is screwed into the frame, so if the frame is split, loose in the masonry or bent at the latch, the door can be forced just as easily with a fob reader beside it as without. The tenants at 501 Lefferts Avenue said it in 2022: "They might change the lock, but the frame, they can kick it [open]." That is a description of a frame problem, and the fix is in the frame.

So the first part of our survey on a building like yours is the opening itself. We check whether the frame is solid in the wall, whether the door sits square and closes by itself, and whether the latch side has been chewed up by years of changes. Depending on what we find, the price includes a steel reinforcement at the strike, a new strike plate with longer screws into the structure, a latch guard, a new closer, or, where the frame is beyond saving, a new hollow-metal frame. Only then does the reader go on. A reader on a door that closes and locks every time is a real improvement; a reader on a door that does not is decoration.

Our rent-stabilized building has dozens of open violations and a long list of litigation. If the owner adds fobs now, will the next HPD inspection go better or worse?

It depends entirely on the door. Fobs do not clear violations, and inspectors are not looking for readers; they are looking at whether the entrance closes and locks by itself, whether the intercom works, and whether locks and doors are in good repair. A new fob system on a door that still drifts open adds a powered lock to a failing door, and a reader that dies in the first blackout becomes one more complaint. Done in the right order, with the door, closer, lock and intercom repaired first, the same job can remove door violations rather than add to them.

Buildings with long violation histories are common here; openigloo's list of Prospect Lefferts Gardens buildings with doormen alone includes one with 45 open violations and 12 litigation cases. Our tenants' association wants a say before the landlord picks a fob system. What is reasonable to ask for in writing?

Ask for the things DHCR and the city have already said matter, so the request is grounded rather than open-ended. A copy of the data retention and privacy policy the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires, before anyone is asked to consent. Free fobs for every lawful occupant, including children, and a stated, capped price for replacements. A metal key that still opens the street door. A battery backup sized for a long outage, since DHCR treated a door propped open through two 24-hour blackouts as grounds for a service-reduction complaint. A promise that the system records entries only, not departures, if that is what the owner intends, and that no identification is copied and kept.

It is also fair to ask what happens to the old intercom, who holds the administrator login, how long logs are kept, and who outside the building can see them. A landlord who has thought the job through can answer all of that on one page. When we install in a building with an active tenants' association, we are glad to walk through the hardware with its representatives, because a system residents understand is a system they do not prop open.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Close the Door Twenty Times Before Choosing a Reader

This describes how we start on a Prospect Lefferts Gardens apartment house, not a particular customer. Before we talk about fobs, we stand at the street door and let it close by itself, twenty times, from fully open, from half open and from a gentle push. On a prewar door we usually find at least one of three things: a closer that has lost its oil and lets the door stop an inch short of the latch, a latch that catches only when someone pulls the door shut, or a frame that has been kicked or re-screwed so often that the strike plate moves when we press on it. Each of those turns a fob reader into a light that blinks beside an unlocked door.

Then we go to the inner vestibule door and do the same, because a building with a good outer door and a loose inner door has only moved the problem a few feet. Then the cellar, the yard door and the roof bulkhead, which must close by itself but never lock against someone leaving. We write down how many keys the super thinks are out, and how many the tenants think are out, and we note that the two numbers almost never match. Only after that do we choose the reader. By then the owner usually knows that the first money goes into the door, and that the fobs are the easy part.

Can a reader be mounted on the iron railing or newel post at the bottom of a Manor stoop instead of beside the door?
+

Sometimes. The Landmarks Commission's staff rules allow an intercom on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway, so a small reader or station on a sidewalk fence or gate post can be acceptable, while drilling into original cast iron or carved stone is not. A post-mounted reader also needs a concealed cable path to the house, usually under the areaway. We look at the specific railing before suggesting it.

Can the access control and the building's cameras be installed as one job?
+

Yes, and it usually costs less than doing them separately, because the cable for both runs on the same visit and the camera at the door can be tied to the reader's events. We quote each part on its own line so an owner or board can see what each costs, and the same controller and recorder can live in one locked closet or cabinet.

Will a fob reader work for a resident who uses a wheelchair and cannot pull a heavy prewar street door?
+

Yes, with a power door operator. The reader releases the lock and signals the operator, which opens the door and holds it while the resident passes, then closes it so it latches. The reader and the operator's push plates are mounted at a height a seated person can reach. The operator needs its own power, which a licensed electrician provides, and we coordinate with the operator supplier.

Do the old metal street-door keys stop working once the fobs go live?
+

The old keys usually do, because the cylinder is changed, but a metal key does not disappear. We fit a restricted-keyway cylinder that still opens the door in an emergency, as DHCR has required in rent-stabilized conversions, and its keys go only to the people the owner names. In a Manor house that is usually two family members and a trusted neighbor.

What is a door position switch, and why does every controlled door get one?
+

It is a small magnetic contact in the door and frame that tells the controller whether the door is actually shut. Without it, the system knows only that someone presented a fob, not whether the door closed afterward. With it, the system can warn the super when the street door is held open, report a forced door, and show in the log whether a door latched after each entry.

Can residents open the street door with their phone instead of carrying a fob?
+

Yes. Most of the readers we install accept phone keys as well as fobs, so residents who want to use a phone can, and those who prefer a fob or a code keep that option. In rental buildings we never make the phone the only way in, because the city's privacy law bars owners from requiring smart access for a tenant's own apartment and many residents do not want an app.

Does AESS keep a copy of our building's entry logs?
+

No. The system and its records belong to the owner, board or business, and the logs stay on the building's controller or its own cloud account. We need access only while we install and when the owner asks us to service the system. At handover we set retention to the owner's written policy and show the administrators how to export or delete records.

Can the reader's finish match the dark or bronze hardware on an old Prospect Lefferts Gardens door?
+

Usually. Readers and small stations come in black, dark grey, silver and some bronze-toned finishes, and the Landmarks Commission's rules ask for equipment painted to blend with the building. Where no factory finish suits a door, we use a dark housing or a cover plate that can be painted. We show the owner samples against the door before ordering.

Can a reader sit inside the vestibule and read a fob through the glass of the outer door?
+

Often, yes, when the door has a plain glass panel and no metal mesh or tinted film in the way. Some readers are designed to read through glass, so nothing shows on the outside of the building. It needs a quick test with the actual reader and fob on the actual door, because thick or wired glass can cut the range. Where it works, it suits landmarked fronts well.

What is the difference between fail-safe and fail-secure on a street door?
+

A fail-safe lock unlocks when it loses power and a fail-secure lock stays locked from outside. Both always open from inside. Most street doors get fail-secure hardware with a battery and a metal key override, so a blackout does not leave the building open. Magnetic locks and some exit doors are fail-safe by design and need listed release hardware under the Building Code.

Can the system lock every controlled door at once if something happens on the block?
+

Yes. A controller can switch every controlled door to locked mode from one button, the app or the desk, so scheduled unlocks are cancelled and only chosen credentials still work. Exits keep opening from inside the whole time. We set who may trigger it, usually the super, the managing agent and a board member, and how the building returns to normal afterward.
