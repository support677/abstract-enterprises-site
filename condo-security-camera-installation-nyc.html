Why Condos Are Different

Condo Security Cameras Are a Board Decision, Not a Landlord's Purchase

In a rental building one owner buys the cameras, decides where they point and keeps the recordings. A condominium works the other way around. Every unit belongs to someone different, and the lobby, corridors, elevators, garage and amenity rooms usually belong to all of them together. New York's Condominium Act names those shared spaces in its definition of common elements: the "halls, corridors, lobbies, stairs, stairways, fire escapes, and entrances and exits of the building," the "basements, cellars, yards, gardens, recreational or community facilities, parking areas and storage spaces," and "all apparatus and installations existing for common use" (Real Property Law §339-e). Hold that list up against a camera layout and it covers nearly every place a condo security camera goes. That is why condo surveillance cameras are bought, approved and governed by the board of managers on behalf of all the owners, and why the questions a condo board asks us are different from the ones a landlord asks.

The board's authority is not unlimited, either. The Condominium Act requires the bylaws to cover the powers and duties of the board, whether it may hire a managing agent, and how rules for the use of the common elements are adopted (§339-v). In practice that means a condo camera project moves through a predictable chain: a board member or the managing agent spots a problem, the agent gathers proposals, the treasurer checks the operating budget or the reserve fund, the board votes the way its bylaws require, and owners are told. We write our condo security camera proposals for that chain. Each one names the camera locations, what each camera is meant to see, who will be able to view and export recordings, what the system costs to buy and what it costs to keep, so the people voting can see the whole decision on two pages.

Ownership patterns in New York City condos make the camera system matter more than it would elsewhere. The NYC Comptroller's March 2024 spotlight on the homeowner market, drawn from the 2023 Housing and Vacancy Survey, counted 318,000 occupied condominium apartments in the city and found that 18 percent of condo units are held by LLCs, 21 percent in Manhattan. Plenty of condo apartments are rented out by their owners, some are empty for months at a time, and some change hands every few years. A condo building video surveillance system is often the only consistent record of who came through the door when the people living upstairs keep changing, and the board is often the only party with a reason to keep it running.

What a condo board is really deciding when it votes on cameras

  • Coverage: which common elements get condo surveillance cameras, and which stay camera-free because owners expect privacy there or because nothing happens there that a recording would help with.
  • Access: who in the building may watch live video and who may pull and export a clip, and under what written policy.
  • Retention: how many days of recordings the recorder keeps before it overwrites, which drives how much storage we install.
  • Budget: whether the system is paid from operating funds, from reserves, or by an assessment, and what it will cost to maintain over its life.
  • Notice: how unit owners, and through them their tenants, learn about the cameras before they go live.

Every one of those decisions is the board's, made with its own managing agent and attorney. Our part is to make each decision easy to understand: what the hardware can do, what it costs, where it physically can and cannot go in your building, and what we have seen work in other condominiums around the five boroughs. If your condo also needs intercom, fob or elevator work, the broader picture lives on our condo security systems page for New York City; this page stays on cameras.

Camera Placement

Where Condo Building Security Cameras Go, Space by Space

A condominium has more kinds of shared space than most rental buildings, and each one has its own reason for a camera. Below is how we think about the common elements in a typical New York City condo, from the sidewalk inward. No building needs every item on the list. A 12-unit boutique condo in a converted walk-up may be well served by four or five cameras; a 200-unit tower with a garage, pool and roof deck may need forty or more. The point of walking through each space is to choose cameras for a purpose, not to cover walls.

Sidewalk, entrance and vestibule

The entrance camera is the one owners care about most, because it answers the most common question after an incident: who came in, and did someone let them in? We like two views here. One camera outside or in the vestibule catches faces as people approach the door, mounted low enough to see under hat brims and high enough to stay out of reach. A second, wider camera inside the lobby shows the door from behind, so tailgating is visible. Where the facade belongs to a landmark or a historic district, exterior mounting has rules of its own, covered further down.

Lobby and concierge desk

In staffed condo buildings the concierge desk usually gets a monitor showing the entrance, the package room and the garage, and the lobby camera itself covers the desk. That camera protects the staff as much as the building: a recorded view of the desk settles disputes about deliveries and keys quickly. Condo boards should know that camera views of employees raise their own questions under the building's employment arrangements, which is one reason we keep workplace coverage to open lobby areas and never to staff rooms.

Mailroom and package room

Package theft is the single most frequent reason condo boards call us. A good package room camera is mounted on the ceiling opposite the door, looking at the shelves and the door at once, with enough resolution to read a box label at a few feet. Where the package room is a locked space opened by fob, the camera recording plus the access log together answer almost every "my box disappeared" complaint. Many of those complaints, building staff tell us, turn out to be packages that never arrived at all, and a clear camera view is how the managing agent proves it.

Elevators and elevator landings

Cameras in elevator cabs are popular with condo boards and tricky to install. The camera in the cab is easy; getting its video out of a moving car is not. We use a wireless link between the cab and the hoistway that carries the video to the recorder, and we coordinate with the building's elevator contractor for access. Anything that touches the elevator controller, the car-top wiring or the traveling cable is the elevator contractor's work, not ours. Where a wireless link is not practical, a camera at each elevator landing on key floors, or one in the lobby facing the elevator doors, is a solid alternative.

Garage and parking areas

Condo garages need cameras that handle headlight glare and dim corners. We place one camera at the vehicle entrance to record cars entering and leaving, wide cameras along the drive aisles, and a camera at every door from the garage into the building, because those doors are where people slip in behind a car. Garage ceilings are also where low-light and full-color cameras earn their price.

Stairwells, roof doors and service entrances

The doors nobody watches are the ones that get propped open. A camera at the service entrance, the trash room door, the roof bulkhead and the cellar stair door costs little and closes the gap between the front door, which everyone watches, and the back door, which nobody does. In older converted condos, the rear yard and the side alley often matter more than the street.

Amenity spaces: gym, pool, roof deck, lounge, bike room and storage

Amenity cameras answer questions about damage, guests and after-hours use. In a gym a single ceiling camera near the entrance usually shows who came in and when, which is all most boards want. At a pool or roof deck, cameras should show the entrances and the open deck. Bike rooms and storage cages get a camera at the door and one down the main aisle. Changing rooms, restrooms and showers inside those amenity suites get no cameras at all; the law section below explains why.

Residential corridors

Hallway cameras are where condo owners disagree most. Some want a camera on every floor; others see a camera outside their door as an intrusion. The deciding detail is aim. A corridor camera at the end of a hall, angled down its length, records movement without looking into any apartment when doors open. A camera placed directly across from a unit door does the opposite, and an attorney quoted below explains why that can become a privacy problem. We mark every corridor camera on the plan with its field of view before the board votes on it.

Places a condo camera should never point

  • Inside any unit, or through an open unit doorway, window or terrace.
  • Restrooms, changing rooms, showers and locker areas in the gym, pool or staff spaces.
  • Staff break rooms and any area where employees change clothes.
  • Neighboring buildings' windows and private yards beyond your property line.

We will not install a camera in those locations, even when a board or an owner asks. The reasons are legal as well as practical, and they are set out with sources in the privacy section of this page.

Equipment

Condo Security Camera Systems: Cameras, Recorders and Software

Every condo security camera system has three parts: the cameras, the recorder that stores their video, and the software owners and staff use to view it. Choices in each part decide how sharp the picture is, how many days of recording the building keeps, and how easy it is for the managing agent to pull a clip on a Monday morning. Our default is our own house-brand system, a non-branded line of cameras and recorders we install across the city, because it gives condo boards dependable image quality at the prices on our price pages. If a board prefers a named brand, we install those too; we are a partner with Lorex, Luminys and Reolink, and we put in other major lines on request.

Resolution gets most of the attention and matters less than placement. A 4-megapixel turret mounted at the right height and distance will identify a face at a lobby door better than an 8-megapixel camera on a ceiling twenty feet away. We pick resolution camera by camera: 4K for wide spaces like garages and plazas where you will zoom into recordings, lower resolutions in tight corridors where the subject is always close.

DVR or NVR for a condominium?

Older condo buildings often have coaxial cable already running from the cameras to a recorder in a closet. A modern high-definition DVR can reuse that coax and still deliver sharp video, which saves the building the cost and disruption of pulling new cable through finished corridors. In new construction, or where we are running fresh cable anyway, an NVR with network cameras powered over Ethernet is our usual choice. It supports higher resolutions, cleaner audio-free analytics and simpler expansion. Many condo security camera upgrades are a mix: keep the coax and a new DVR for the cameras that already work well, and add network cameras where coverage is missing.

Storage and retention

The board decides how long recordings should be kept; we size the hard drives to match. Retention depends on the number of cameras, their resolution, whether they record continuously or on motion, and how busy each view is. A busy lobby camera fills a drive much faster than a quiet roof door. We will show the board two or three retention options with the drive capacity each needs, so the policy and the hardware agree from day one.

Viewing apps and user accounts

The software part is where condo systems most often go wrong after installation. A recorder with one shared password that every super, agent and board member has used for six years is a liability. We set up named accounts with different permissions: live view only for the concierge desk, playback and export for the managing agent, full administration held by the board through the agent. Every account belongs to the condominium, not to us and not to any individual, so control stays with the association when people move on.

Brands

House-Brand Systems First, Named Brands When a Condo Board Asks

Most condo boards ask the same thing first: what brand are these? Our answer is usually our own non-branded house system. We buy those cameras and recorders through our wholesaler, we have installed them in buildings across the five boroughs, and their prices are the ones published on our price pages. A single supplier for every camera in a building also means one viewing app, one set of spare parts and one support path, which matters more to a condo over ten years than any logo.

When a board has a reason to want a particular manufacturer, we price that instead. Lorex, Luminys and Reolink are the brands we hold partner relationships with. We also install Uniview, Hikvision, Dahua, Hanwha, Axis, Ubiquiti UniFi Protect and others when a building specifies them, for example to match an existing recorder or an access system that integrates with one camera platform. A named brand can make sense when the condo already owns a large investment in that platform, when its access control or intercom vendor requires it, or when the board's IT advisor prefers a particular cloud dashboard.

Two cautions apply to every brand. First, some cloud-managed camera platforms carry a per-camera subscription to keep remote viewing and recording features turned on, and those fees belong in the board's long-term budget alongside the purchase price. Second, any camera that connects to the internet should sit on its own network segment with its default passwords changed. We set both up as part of a condo security camera installation, regardless of which manufacturer is on the label.

Privacy & New York Law

Condo Camera Privacy Rules in New York: What the Sources Say

We are a licensed low-voltage contractor, not a law firm, and nothing here is legal advice. Every condominium should have its own attorney review a camera plan against its declaration, bylaws and house rules. What we can do is show boards the published sources we work from, so the questions they bring to counsel are the right ones.

Video in common areas versus audio anywhere

Video cameras in lobbies, hallways and other shared areas are widely used in New York condominiums. A Newman Ferrara attorney writing in CooperatorNews put the general principle plainly: "New York does not recognize a common law right of privacy in public or common areas" (CooperatorNews, Q&A: Camera Placement). Sound is different. New York's eavesdropping statute treats recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party as "mechanical overhearing of a conversation" (Penal Law §250.00), and eavesdropping is a class E felony under §250.05. A lobby microphone records conversations among people who are not party to the recording at all. For that reason, we install condo common-area cameras with audio recording switched off unless the board's counsel has approved otherwise in writing. Many of the cameras we sell have microphones built in; turning them off at the recorder is part of our setup.

Bathrooms, changing rooms and staff spaces

New York's unlawful surveillance statute makes it a class E felony to use a recording device for no legitimate purpose to view or record a person, without their knowledge or consent, "in a bedroom, changing room, fitting room, restroom, toilet, bathroom, washroom, shower" (Penal Law §250.45). FirstService Residential's guidance for New York condos and co-ops also points to Labor Law §203-c, which bars employers from video recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms or rooms where they change clothes (FirstService Residential, Sept. 23, 2024). A condo with a staffed lobby is an employer. We plan every amenity suite and staff area with those rooms left out entirely.

Cameras that look into a unit

The same CooperatorNews piece warns that a camera whose "vantage point is more expansive, and peers into a neighboring unit" invites a claim that it "interferes with a neighbor's privacy rights," because "the law recognizes the interior of a neighbor's unit as subject to considerable privacy safeguards." FirstService gives the matching practical rule: hallway cameras "must be carefully placed to avoid capturing footage that shows the inside of any residential unit." That is the reason we draw each corridor camera's field of view on the plan and, after installation, check each view with a unit door standing open.

The board's authority comes from its documents

A condo board can usually install cameras in the common elements, but the source of that power matters. In a 2023 Habitat magazine Q&A about cameras in a Midtown condo, attorney Andrew Lieb said: "A house rule that is unauthorized by these foundational documents is unenforceable," adding that if cameras rested only on such a rule, "you have a great case to get them taken down" (Habitat, Aug. 28, 2023). Before a vote, the board's counsel should confirm the declaration and bylaws support the plan and whether owners need a vote or only notice.

Owners who put up their own cameras

Video doorbells and small cameras mounted by owners outside their own doors are a growing source of condo disputes. CooperatorNews notes that condominiums "typically prohibit unit owners from unilaterally altering" the common areas, so owner-installed cameras without the board's consent "may have violated the governing bylaws," and it advises owners that "a request for board consent is advisable." Some boards respond by banning owner cameras in hallways; others approve a standard model and mounting position. Either way, the decision is the board's, and we will only install an owner's hallway camera once the owner shows us the board's written approval.

Landmark facades

Many Manhattan and Brooklyn condos sit in historic districts or in individually designated landmarks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook allows staff-level approval for security cameras that meet its criteria, including keeping equipment off the primary facade where another location works, spacing cameras "to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and limiting how many cameras go on each stretch of street frontage (LPC Permit Guidebook, ch. 11). When a condo's exterior is landmarked, we design for those criteria and the building applies for the permit before exterior cameras go up; interior cameras are not affected.

Face recognition and the city's smart access law

NYC's Tenant Data Privacy law, Local Law 63 of 2021, sets rules for "smart access" systems, meaning entry systems that use fobs, apps or biometrics, in class A multiple dwellings, including a written privacy policy and limits on how long authentication data is kept (intro.nyc, Local Law 63 of 2021). Ordinary recording cameras are not entry systems. But if a condo board wants face recognition that unlocks a door, that becomes a smart access question, and how the law applies to owner-occupied condo units is one for the board's attorney. We do not install face-recognition entry on a condo without counsel's sign-off.

Who may legally install the system

New York requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security systems, and the Department's rules include closed-circuit and video surveillance systems used to detect or monitor intrusion (NYS DOS, 19 NYCRR Part 195). Our license number is #12000287431. Ask any condo security camera installation company you are considering for its number and check it.

Footage Policy

Who Sees the Recordings: Footage Access in a Condo Building

The most common fight about condo video surveillance is not about cameras at all. It is about recordings: an owner wants the clip of their car being scratched, a board member wants to watch the lobby from home, a police detective calls the managing agent. A building that decides these questions before the first incident avoids most of the arguments. FirstService Residential's advice for New York condos summarizes common practice well: footage "is generally stored for 30 to 90 days depending on the building size"; access usually goes to the property manager or a security provider rather than individual board members; "the property manager or security provider should maintain a log of requests and a list of anyone who accessed footage"; and "residents should not have access to footage or copies of footage without prior approval from the board and legal counsel."

We do not write the policy, but we build the system so whatever policy the board adopts can be followed. That includes:

  • Named user accounts for the managing agent, resident manager and concierge desk, each with only the permissions the policy allows, so the recorder's own log shows who logged in and when.
  • Export permissions limited to one or two people, so clips leave the system only through the person the policy names.
  • Retention matched to the policy: if the board chooses 30 days, the drives hold 30 days; if it chooses 60, we size for 60.
  • A clean export routine that saves a clip with its time stamp and camera name intact, so a recording handed to an insurer or the police is usable.
  • Remote viewing through the manufacturer's app for the people the policy authorizes, with board members' access set at whatever level the board itself decides.

Requests from police, insurers and attorneys should go to the managing agent and, where the board's policy says so, to its counsel. Whether to release a clip voluntarily or wait for a subpoena or court order is a legal decision for the condominium, not for the camera installer, and we never release a building's recordings ourselves. Our role is technical: help the agent find the clip quickly and export it properly.

Signs and notices

Many condos post signs at entrances stating that common areas are recorded. A sign tells visitors and residents that cameras are there, discourages some trouble before it starts, and matches the notice the board gives owners. The wording and the decision to post signs are the board's; we can install signage alongside the cameras if the board orders it.

Board Approval

Getting a Condo Camera Proposal Through the Board and Out to Owners

We have watched good condo camera projects stall for months for reasons that had nothing to do with cameras. A board member was not at the meeting where the plan was explained. An owner on the second floor heard "cameras in the hallways" and pictured one aimed at their door. The treasurer could not tell whether the price included storage. Each of those stalls is avoidable, and our proposal format is built to avoid them.

What goes into the board packet

  1. A floor-by-floor camera plan with every camera numbered and its field of view drawn, so nobody has to imagine where a camera points.
  2. A purpose line for each camera: "package room door and shelves," "garage ramp, plates entering," "roof bulkhead door." Cameras without a purpose come off the plan.
  3. Two or three options, for example entrances and package room only; entrances, garage and elevators; and full common-area coverage. Each option is priced on its own.
  4. The recording plan: retention in days, drive capacity, where the recorder lives, and how it is powered through a short outage.
  5. Ongoing costs: any software subscriptions, the warranty period and what an extended warranty would cost, and the expected lifespan of cameras and drives.
  6. A neutral summary of privacy design choices: audio off, no cameras in restrooms or changing rooms, corridor views checked against open unit doors.

Paying for it: operating budget, reserves or assessment

How a condo pays for cameras is a board decision under its own bylaws and financial plan. Smaller additions, such as a package room camera or a replacement recorder, often fit in the operating budget. A building-wide replacement is the kind of capital project many boards plan through the reserve fund or a special assessment. We can split a large plan into phases, entrances and package room this year and garage next year, so the system grows without a single large bill, and every phase is designed to plug into the same recorder. Financing is available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Telling owners and tenants

Owners should hear about cameras from the board before they see a technician on a ladder. We supply the managing agent with a plain-language summary they can adapt for an owner notice: which spaces will be recorded, that audio is off, who can view recordings, how long recordings are kept, and the dates work will happen on each floor. Owners who rent out their units can pass it on to their tenants. When work requires access to a unit, which it rarely does for camera projects, we schedule it through the agent with notice.

Scheduling around a condo's house rules

Every condo has its own working hours, elevator reservation rules and insurance requirements for contractors. We follow them. The certificate of insurance naming the condominium and its managing agent is issued once the contract is awarded, and we work inside the building's permitted hours, early in the day where we can. Our crews book within 2 hours of a request, seven days a week, and jobs go on the calendar first come, first served once the deposit is paid.

What moves a condo camera quote up or down

  • Concealed versus exposed cable. Fishing cable inside finished walls and ceilings costs more than running it in a garage or cellar. Concealed wiring is quoted as an extra, and we tell you before you order which runs will be concealed.
  • Reusable cable. Existing coax or network cable that tests good can cut the labor sharply. If a run might fail its test, we say so before the order, along with what replacing it would cost.
  • Elevator cameras. A wireless cab link adds equipment and coordination time with the elevator contractor.
  • Recorder size and retention. More cameras and longer retention mean more drive capacity.
  • Height and access. Double-height lobbies and garage ramps may need a lift.
  • Brand. A named brand or a cloud platform with subscriptions changes both the price and the long-term cost.

Customer-supplied cameras

Some associations buy their own hardware first, often through a board member or a previous vendor. In that case our number is installation labor alone, worked out from the camera list and the floor plan you send, and given over the phone or by text message. The work is scheduled once the deposit clears. We skip the survey visit for owner-purchased gear, and any defect in it goes back to the seller rather than to us.

Warranty and terms

On warranty, condo boards should read three layers separately. Our labor and the installed system carry the 30-day warranty posted on our site, starting the day the cameras go live. Installation Conditions

Lighting, Weather and Mounting in New York City Condo Buildings

Image quality in a condo depends as much on conditions in the building as on the camera itself. These are the conditions that most often decide which camera we specify and where it goes.

Glass lobbies and backlight

A camera inside a glass lobby looking toward the street sees bright daylight behind every visitor and records silhouettes. We counter that with cameras that handle wide dynamic range, and by mounting the face-capture camera so it looks across or slightly away from the glass rather than straight into it. Often the best face shot in a glass lobby comes from a camera beside the inner door, looking back toward the elevators as people walk in.

Dim garages and cellars

Garages and cellars are dark and full of reflective surfaces. Infrared cameras work, but their light bounces off nearby walls and pipes and washes out the image. Full-color low-light cameras, or infrared cameras positioned away from close surfaces, solve most of it. Headlights at the garage entrance call for a camera aimed inward rather than out at oncoming cars.

Exterior walls, salt air and winter

Outdoor cameras on New York condos face freeze-thaw cycles, wind-driven rain, and on waterfront buildings, salt air. We use weather-rated housings, seal every cable entry, and choose mounts that will not rust streaks down a facade. On masonry we anchor into the brick or stone itself rather than the mortar joints that crumble over time, and we keep exterior penetrations to a minimum.

Finished interiors

Condo lobbies and corridors are often finished in plaster, stone, wood paneling or wallcovering that owners paid for and do not want marred. We plan cable paths through existing ceilings, chases and closets wherever we can, and where cable must show, we run it in paintable wire mold or surface raceway along trim lines so it disappears. Which runs will be concealed, which will use raceway and which will be exposed in utility spaces is spelled out in the proposal, so nobody is surprised on installation day.

Power and outages

Most of the network cameras we install draw power through their data cable from a switch or recorder, so individual camera locations do not need outlets. The recorder and switch do need power, and we put them on a battery backup so a short outage or a tripped breaker does not leave a gap in the recordings at the very moment something may be happening.

Network & Cybersecurity

Keeping a Condo Camera System Off the Internet's Radar

A camera system that the managing agent can view from a phone is also, in principle, a system someone else could try to reach. Condo boards rarely ask about this, and they should. The steps that protect a building are not exotic, but they have to be done on purpose.

  • Default passwords changed on every camera, the recorder and the switch, and stored in a document the condominium holds.
  • A separate network segment for cameras, so the camera system does not share a network with the building's office computers, the gym's smart TVs or residents' devices.
  • No open ports on the building's router pointing at the recorder. Remote viewing goes through the manufacturer's secure app connection instead.
  • Firmware updates applied at handover and checked on maintenance visits, because manufacturers fix security flaws through updates.
  • Named accounts and prompt removal: when a super leaves or an agent changes, their access ends that day.

Where a condo's management company or IT consultant has its own network standards, we follow them and coordinate before installation. A condo security camera system integration with a building's existing network is far smoother when the person who manages that network is in the conversation early.

Plain English

Camera Terms Condo Board Members Will See in Proposals

Camera proposals are full of jargon. Here is what the terms mean in plain language, so board members can compare bids on substance.

  • NVR (network video recorder): the box that stores video from network cameras. It sits in a closet or rack.
  • DVR (digital video recorder): the equivalent box for cameras on coaxial cable. Modern DVRs record in high definition on older coax.
  • PoE (power over Ethernet): one cable carries both video and power to a network camera, so no outlet is needed at the camera.
  • Channels: the number of cameras a recorder can handle. A 16-channel recorder takes up to 16 cameras.
  • Megapixels (MP) and 4K: image resolution. 4K is about 8 megapixels. Higher resolution means more detail and more storage.
  • Field of view: how wide an area a camera sees. Wide lenses see more but show each person smaller.
  • Varifocal lens: a lens the installer can zoom and focus on site to frame a view exactly.
  • WDR (wide dynamic range): a camera's ability to show faces against bright windows or doorways.
  • Retention: the number of days recordings are kept before the oldest are overwritten.
  • Analytics: software that detects events, such as a person crossing a line or a door left open, and sends alerts.
  • Cloud subscription: a monthly or yearly fee some platforms charge for remote access, cloud storage or features.
  • UPS: a battery backup that keeps the recorder and switch running through a short power loss.
After an Incident

The First Hour After Something Happens on a Condo Camera

The value of a condo camera system shows up on the morning after a break-in, a damaged car in the garage or a stolen package. What happens in the first hour decides whether the recording helps. These steps are practical, not legal; anything involving police, insurers or a dispute between owners should go through the managing agent and, where needed, the board's attorney.

  1. Note the time window and the place. "Between 1 and 3 a.m., garage level two, near space 14" lets the agent find a clip in minutes instead of hours.
  2. Lock the clip before it overwrites. Recorders overwrite the oldest video automatically. The person authorized to export should save the relevant clip promptly, especially if retention is short.
  3. Export with the time stamp and camera name. A clip that shows when and where it was recorded is far more useful to an insurer or detective than a phone video of a monitor.
  4. Log the request. Record who asked for the footage, why, and what was exported, as the board's policy requires.
  5. Look at the gaps. If the incident happened somewhere no camera covers, or a camera was dirty, dark or knocked out of aim, write it down. That list is the best possible input for the next camera decision.

If the agent cannot find or export a clip, or discovers the recorder was not recording, call us. We can help retrieve what is on the drives and, on a service visit, fix whatever stopped the system from doing its job. We do not release recordings to anyone ourselves; the condominium decides who receives them.

Owner Devices

Video Doorbells and Owner Cameras: Policy Options for Condo Boards

Unit owners increasingly install their own video doorbells or small cameras outside their apartment doors. In a condo, the hallway outside the door is usually a common element, so those devices touch board territory. Boards we work with tend to land on one of three approaches, each a choice for the board and its counsel:

Not permitted

No owner devices in common hallways; the building's own cameras cover corridors where the board has decided coverage is needed. Simple to enforce, and it avoids neighbor disputes about where a doorbell camera points.

Permitted with approval

Owners apply to the board, which approves a specific device, mounting position and field of view that does not look into a neighbor's door. Recordings stay the owner's responsibility.

Building-standard device

The board picks one model and mounting detail, and owners who want one have it installed to that standard. Hallways stay uniform and every device is aimed the same approved way.

Whatever the board decides, we follow it. We install owner hallway devices only with the board's written approval in hand, aimed exactly as approved, and never wired into the building's own camera system without the board's agreement. Inside their own units, owners can do what their declaration and the law allow; inside-the-unit cameras are a separate residential job and do not need the building's recorder at all.

Real Questions

Questions Condo Boards and Owners Ask Us About Cameras

Our condo has cameras, but nobody can log in to the recorder. Do we have to replace it?

Usually not. Most recorders can be reset and re-registered, though some reset procedures erase stored recordings and settings, so we confirm with the board before starting. Once access is back, we create named accounts in the condominium's name, check the drives and cameras, and tell you honestly whether the system is worth keeping.

An owner wants a copy of a clip from the garage. What should the managing agent do?

Follow the board's footage policy. If the building has none, that is the moment to write one with counsel. Technically, the agent can find the clip by time and camera and export it with the time stamp intact in a few minutes; whether to hand it to the owner is a decision for the board and its attorney.

Can the board watch the lobby from their phones?

The software can do it. Whether it should is a governance question. Many boards keep live view with the managing agent and the concierge desk and give board members playback only when the policy calls for it, which avoids board members becoming the building's informal security staff.

Can the cameras tell us when the package room door is propped open?

Yes. Most current recorders can flag a door left open, a person loitering after hours or a line crossed in a garage, and send an alert to the agent or staff. We set these rules sparingly, because a system that sends forty alerts a day trains everyone to ignore it.

We have a part-time super and no doorman. Who watches the cameras?

In most buildings, nobody watches live, and that is normal. Cameras in a condo without staff are a record to review after something happens, plus a deterrent. Remote viewing for the managing agent and alerts for a few high-value events cover the rest. We do not provide a live video watching service.

Will hallway cameras bother owners who work from home or have home businesses?

Corridor cameras record movement in the corridor, not inside units, and with audio off they capture nothing said behind a door. The best answer to owner concerns is the plan itself: show each camera's field of view in the owner notice so nobody has to guess.

Our elevators are old. Can a camera still go in the cab?

Usually yes, using a wireless link from the cab to the hoistway so nothing is added to the elevator's own wiring. The elevator contractor provides access and any work on the car or controller. If the hoistway layout rules out a wireless link, landing cameras do the job instead.

Can cameras help with dog waste and damage in the courtyard?

A courtyard camera with a wide view and enough resolution to identify people at a distance helps with exactly that. Expect it to show who and when; it will not stop the problem by itself, but it gives the board something concrete for its house rules process.

How long does installation take in an occupied condo?

A four- to eight-camera system with exposed or reusable cable is often a one-day job. Larger buildings with concealed cable, elevators and garages take several days, scheduled floor by floor inside the building's permitted hours so owners are not inconvenienced for long on any one floor.

Our board wants to add cameras in phases. Will the first phase work with the second?

It will if the recorder is sized for the full plan from the start. We usually recommend buying a recorder with spare channels in phase one, so later cameras plug straight in without replacing anything.

Do condo security cameras need a separate internet line?

Not necessarily. The recorder needs an internet connection for remote viewing and alerts, but it can share the building's existing service on its own isolated network segment. Buildings with heavy remote viewing sometimes choose a dedicated connection, which the board orders from its provider.

Can a commercial unit owner see the shared garage cameras?

If the board allows it, we can give a commercial owner a view-only account limited to the cameras that serve their space, such as the loading door. Everything else stays with the board's permissions.

Reality Check

Common Claims About Condo Cameras, Checked

Boards research before they call, and much of what comes back from search engines and chatbots is either generic or written for single-family homes. Here are claims we hear repeated, with what actually applies in a New York City condominium.

"Wireless cameras are cheaper and just as good for a condo"

Battery or Wi-Fi cameras are designed for houses. In a concrete condo building, Wi-Fi signal drops between floors, batteries need replacing on dozens of cameras, and footage often lives in individual cloud accounts rather than on one recorder the board controls. Wired cameras on a recorder cost more up front and far less to live with across a building.

"You need a camera on every floor"

Coverage should follow risk. Entrances, the package room, the garage and the back doors account for most incidents in condos we work in. Corridor cameras on every floor add cost, add owner objections and rarely show anything the lobby and elevator cameras did not.

"Recording audio makes the system more useful"

In common areas, audio exposes the building to New York's eavesdropping law, cited above, for little benefit. Video with good placement answers nearly every question a board has. We leave audio off unless counsel approves.

"The higher the megapixels, the better the camera"

Placement, lens choice and lighting matter more. A properly aimed 4-megapixel camera at a lobby door beats a 4K camera mounted too high and too far away. Higher resolution also consumes more storage, which shortens retention unless drives are added.

"A camera system is a one-time purchase"

Drives wear out, cameras get knocked, firmware needs updating and cloud platforms may charge subscriptions. Boards should plan for periodic maintenance and the eventual replacement of drives and cameras, and should ask any installer what the system costs to keep, not only to buy.

"The installer keeps the admin password for support"

That arrangement is how buildings end up locked out of their own systems. The condominium should hold every admin login, with installer access granted when needed and removed afterward.

From the Field

Field Notes From Condo Camera Work Across New York City

Our company has been installing security systems in New York since 2002, and condo buildings make up a steady share of that work. A few patterns repeat often enough that we now plan for them on every condo security camera installation.

The package room camera is always too far from the shelves. Original installers tend to mount it over the door, looking into the room, which shows the backs of people taking boxes. Moving it to the far corner, looking back at the door and the shelves at once, shows faces and labels. It is a small change that answers most package complaints.

Garage door cameras face the wrong way. A camera pointed out the garage entrance sees headlights and little else at night. Pointing it inward at cars after they pass the door, with a second camera at the pedestrian door, shows plates and people.

The recorder lives somewhere it cannot survive. We find recorders on top of boilers, in damp cellars and in closets with no ventilation. Heat kills hard drives. A short move to a ventilated closet with a battery backup is often worth more than a new camera.

Nobody knew it had stopped recording. The most common failure we see in condo systems is not a broken camera; it is a recorder that stopped writing to its drive weeks before anyone needed a clip. We configure health alerts so the agent hears about a failed drive the day it fails.

Owner notice prevents most objections. In buildings where the board circulated a plan showing each camera's view before installation, we rarely hear owner complaints afterward. Where cameras appeared without notice, the same equipment draws objections.
