The Neighborhood

Why Todt Hill Homes Need Sonos Installation

Todt Hill is a ridge before it is a neighbourhood. Wikipedia describes it as a 401-foot hill "formed of serpentine rock" and "the highest natural point in the five boroughs of New York City"; the Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report for the Gillett-Tyler House on Circle Road puts the summit at 409.8 feet and repeats the long-standing claim that it is the highest natural point on the eastern seaboard between Maine and Florida, and a geology guide published by MiraCosta College calls it "the highest point along the Atlantic Seaboard south of Maine." The figures differ by a few feet depending on who surveyed it, but every source agrees on the thing that matters for an installer: almost nothing here is flat. Houses step down their lots, driveways climb, and the back lawn is often a full storey or two below the front door.

The houses that sit on that slope are, for the most part, large. When SILive featured 935 Todt Hill Road as its Home of the Week in August 2026, the listing described 5,550 square feet on more than 17,000 square feet of land, a two-storey foyer with 22-foot ceilings, golf course views, a finished lower level with a billiards lounge, wine refrigerator and fitness room, "surround sound throughout," smart-home audio and intercom, nine heating zones and five air-conditioning zones. A listing for 177 Benedict Road, carried on Zillow in 2026, describes about 1.6 acres, a rooftop level with a ballroom, movie theater and indoor heated pool, and "a commercial-grade 12-person elevator." Those are the upper end, but they show the shape of the problem. A Sonos system in a house like this is not a speaker in the kitchen and a bar under the TV. It is ten or twenty listening spaces, spread across several levels, some indoors and some out, a long way from wherever the internet comes in.

The ground itself adds a second layer. The City's Zoning Resolution places Emerson Hill, Dongan Hills, Todt Hill and Lighthouse Hill in Special Natural Area District-1, describing "the central, serpentine, hilly spine of Staten Island" as "richly endowed with steep slopes, rock outcrops, erratic boulders and ponds, lakes, swamps, creeks and many trees." Inside the district, a "site alteration" is defined to include excavating, removal of topsoil and modification of natural features "whether or not a permit is required from the Department of Buildings." A trench for garden speakers is, in plain words, excavating. Most small jobs fall under the district's exemptions, but the rules on trees of six-inch caliper and their critical root zones apply on any lot, and they change how we get cable to a pool or a lower terrace. We explain the practical effect further down this page; it is one of the main reasons a Todt Hill outdoor system is planned differently from a yard in New Dorp.

The third layer is history. Ernest Flagg, the architect of the Singer Building and the Naval Academy in Annapolis, bought the property he called Stone Court in 1898 and spent four decades building on it. The Historic Districts Council's survey of Todt Hill–Dongan Hills describes the 32-room fieldstone mansion at 209 Flagg Place, the stable at 79 Flagg Court, a fieldstone water tower, and the small stone cottages Flagg built from serpentine quarried on his own grounds — Bowcot on West Entry Road, Wallcot on Flagg Place, the McCall's Demonstration House on Richmond Road — most of them now private homes and several of them individual landmarks. In September 2026 SILive reported the converted Flagg stable at 79 Flagg Court on the market at just under $3.5 million. Thick stone walls and landmark rules shape where speakers and access points can go.

Finally, the hill is quiet in its own way. A resident on r/statenisland described Todt Hill Road as "a large and steep literal hill that is a narrow street with high traffic and no sidewalks," and Brick Underground noted that most streets here have neither sidewalks nor street lights. Neighbours are often below you rather than beside you, so outdoor sound has to be aimed so it does not roll downhill.

Todt Hill facts that shape a Sonos plan

  • 401 to 409.8 feet: the summit height given by Wikipedia and by the LPC designation report respectively — the reason so many houses here have walk-out levels and terraces stacked down the slope.
  • Special Natural Area District-1: the zoning overlay covering Todt Hill and its neighbouring hills; steep slopes are defined as 25 percent or greater, and a tree's critical root zone is one radial foot per caliper inch, from 4 to 22 feet (Zoning Resolution, Article X, Chapter 5).
  • Nine heating zones and five cooling zones: the count in the 935 Todt Hill Road listing (SILive, August 2026) — a good proxy for how many separately controlled spaces a large house on the hill really has.
  • 32 rooms: the size of Flagg's fieldstone mansion at Stone Court (Historic Districts Council), designated a city landmark in 1967; the estate's other buildings followed in 1983 and 1987.
  • 10,623 households: in the census area that combines Todt Hill, Emerson Hill, Lighthouse Hill and Manor Heights (2020 Census via Wikipedia); 32.7 percent have children under 18, and the largest age group is 55 to 74.
  • 500 apartments: at NYCHA's Todt Hill Houses in Manor Heights, completed in 1950 and in the middle of a $150 million renovation due to finish in mid-2027 (NY1, December 2025) — the other end of the hill, with a very different brief.

Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for Todt Hill's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every call on the hill. They differ less in style than in three things that decide a Sonos plan: how far apart the rooms are, what the walls are made of, and how much of the house is below grade on one side and open to the garden on the other.

Large Post-War and Contemporary Estates

Brick, stone and stucco houses of 5,000 square feet and up on large lots, typically three or four levels with a walk-out lower floor, a double-height entry and a separate wing for bedrooms. Distance and level count drive the design; the walls are usually framed and fishable.

Flagg Stone Cottages and Estate Buildings

Bowcot, Wallcot, the McCall's Demonstration House and the converted outbuildings of Stone Court: mosaic-rubble walls of serpentine with concrete behind, retaining walls that form part of the house, and in several cases individual landmark status. Wireless reach between rooms is the main problem.

Early Twentieth-Century Country Houses

Frame and stone houses from the first decades of the 1900s on the East and West Loop and Entry Roads, often subdivided lots now, with plaster interiors, deep porches and later additions. Mixed construction means mixed Wi-Fi.

Relocated and Historic Landmarks

Houses such as the Gillett-Tyler House at 103 Circle Road, built around 1846 in Massachusetts and re-erected here in 1931 (LPC). Interiors can usually take free-standing Sonos; anything that would change the exterior is a Landmarks question first.

Hillside Houses Built Into the Rock

Newer houses cut into the slope, with a lower level that is underground on the uphill side and full glass on the downhill side, concrete foundation walls and terraces cantilevered or stepped below. Cable has to climb, and outdoor runs start one or two levels down.

Todt Hill Houses Apartments

NYCHA's seven six-storey buildings in Manor Heights, being modernised floor by floor. One- and two-bedroom apartments where the right system is a soundbar and a speaker or two, with nothing fixed until the resident has the housing authority's permission.

Two methods matter more on Todt Hill than elsewhere in the borough. The first is how we treat stone. Before a single hole goes into a fieldstone or mosaic-rubble wall, we check whether we are drilling into stone, mortar joint or the concrete behind it, because each takes a different anchor and some walls are part of a designated landmark. Usually the better answer is not to drill the stone at all.

The second is how we treat the slope. A house that drops three levels front to back has cable paths that look short on a floor plan and turn out long in practice, because the cable has to go down to the mechanical room, along under the floor, and back up. We measure routes, not rooms, and we write the length of each run on the zone map before anything is ordered, so the gauge of speaker wire and the choice between Wi-Fi and cable are settled on paper.

Distance Planning

Wiring a House That Climbs: Distance, Levels and the Head-End

Every Sonos product has to reach the same network, and every passive speaker has to reach an Amp. In a two-storey colonial both are easy. In a Todt Hill house that runs 150 feet end to end and drops three levels to the pool, both become design questions, and they are the questions we answer first. The section drawing below shows the pattern we see most often: equipment on the lowest interior level, rooms stacked above it, and outdoor spaces stepping down the hill beyond the glass.

One head-end, chosen for the cable, not for convenience

The equipment location — the head-end — is where the internet connection, the main switch, any Sonos Amps and the router or firewall live together. It is tempting to put it wherever the provider dropped the line, often the garage at the top of the drive, which leaves the pool and the far wing at the end of very long runs. We look for the room that minimises the longest run rather than the average one: usually a mechanical room or storage space on the lower level, near the middle of the house's footprint, with a straight path to the outside wall that faces the terraces. Extending the provider's line to that room is one short job; a dozen long runs to a badly placed rack are a dozen compromises.

The head-end needs three things besides space. Ventilation: Sonos publishes an operating range of 32 to 104°F for the Amp and asks for clearance around it, and Amp Multi is designed around a 2U rack mount that leaves air above and below each unit. Power: a dedicated outlet or two, installed by a licensed electrician if they are not already there. And a written label on every cable, because in a house with twenty runs nobody remembers which pair goes to the guest suite.

Speaker wire over long runs

Sonos publishes a speaker-wire table for its Amps, and on the hill we use all three rows of it. Under 80 feet from Amp to speaker, 16-gauge is enough. From 80 to 200 feet, Sonos recommends 14-gauge; from 200 to 300 feet, 12-gauge; and Sonos says runs over 300 feet "are not recommended." Most indoor zones in a large house sit in the first or second row. Terrace and pool zones on a sloping lot are the ones that reach the third, because the cable leaves the house below grade and follows walls and steps rather than a straight line. When a run approaches the limit, we move an Amp closer instead — into a pool house, a cabana or a weather-protected closet near the terrace — and feed that second location with network cable.

Network cable, Wi-Fi and the stone between rooms

The speaker-wire limit is generous. The network limit is tighter: a copper Ethernet channel is limited to 100 metres, about 328 feet, end to end under the TIA cabling standards our structured-cabling crews certify to. That is usually plenty inside one house. For an outbuilding beyond that distance, or a run that would share a route with power, fiber becomes the sensible medium, and our fiber optic installers on Staten Island handle that part.

Inside the house, the question is how many Sonos products depend on Wi-Fi and how far their signal has to travel. Sonos's interference guidance rates brick and marble as a medium obstruction, plaster and concrete as high and metal as very high; it does not list fieldstone, but a foot of serpentine rubble with concrete behind it behaves like the worst of those. Its system requirements also list wireless range extenders and Ethernet-over-power adapters among setups Sonos does not support, and say that in a mesh network with a wired Sonos setup, the Sonos product must be wired to the primary node. In practice that means wiring the products that matter most — Amps, the main soundbar, anything at the end of a long run — and placing wired access points so the free-standing speakers on Wi-Fi are never more than one wall from one.

Households wiring a backbone for Sonos usually wire the cameras and the gate on the same runs. Our network installation team on Staten Island designs the switch and access-point layout, and the Staten Island Cat6 installers certify every drop.

Rock and the Natural Area

Terraces, Pools and the Special Natural Area District

Outdoor music on Todt Hill is mostly a cable problem. Speakers for a terrace or a pool deck are straightforward; getting a pair of conductors from the head-end to them is not, because between the house and the pool there is often a retaining wall, a set of stone steps, a slope of 25 percent or more, exposed serpentine ledge and two or three big oaks. The plan below shows a typical terrace layout and the three routes we consider, in the order we prefer them.

What the zoning text actually says

The Special Natural Area District rules sit in Article X, Chapter 5 of the City's Zoning Resolution. They apply to "any development, enlargement or site alteration," and the definition of site alteration in that chapter includes "land contour work, topographic modifications, removal of topsoil, vegetation, excavating, filling, dumping, changes in existing drainage systems ... relocation of erratic boulders or modification of any other natural features, whether or not a permit is required from the Department of Buildings." Section 105-021 then exempts two kinds of work from the district's special review: small, nearly flat lots with no significant natural features, and work on a lot with a pre-existing house "involving a structure that does not require a permit from the Department of Buildings," provided cut or fill stays within two feet and "no tree of six-inch caliper or greater shall be removed and the critical root zone of such tree shall be an area of no disturbance."

We are installers, not zoning lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice; whether a particular project needs City Planning review is a question for your architect or for the Department of City Planning. But the practical reading for low-voltage work is clear enough to plan around. A shallow trench across an open lawn on an existing house lot is usually the kind of minor work the exemptions describe. A trench through the root zone of a protected tree is not, on any lot. And anything that cuts into a rock outcrop or a steep slope is a modification of a natural feature, which is exactly what the district exists to control. So we design outdoor runs that avoid those three things, and when a route cannot avoid them, we stop and say so before work starts.

Trees: the critical root zone in numbers

The Zoning Resolution measures a tree's caliper 4 feet 6 inches above the ground and sets its critical root zone at "one radial foot for every caliper inch of the tree, with a required minimum of four radial feet and maximum of 22 radial feet." A 14-inch oak, which is an ordinary tree on a wooded Todt Hill lot, therefore has a 14-foot no-disturbance radius around its trunk. Two or three of those between the house and the pool can rule out the straight-line trench entirely. We measure the trunks during the walkthrough, draw the circles on the site sketch, and route around them — along a wall, through a sleeve under a path, or up and over on the house itself.

Rock: why we follow walls instead of digging

Serpentinite is the rock the hill is made of, and on many lots it is a few inches under the lawn or standing out of it. Cutting a channel in it is slow, noisy and, in the natural area, a change to a protected feature. The routes that work are the ones that never touch it: low-voltage cable in a conduit fixed along the base of a retaining wall or the riser of a stone stair, inside a planting bed's edging where there is soil, or across a paved area in a saw-cut that the paving contractor reinstates. Where nothing else works, a speaker that needs no cable at all — a Sonos Move 2 carried out from its indoor charging base — is an honest answer for a remote corner.

Sound that rolls downhill

On a ridge, the nearest neighbour may be forty feet below your terrace rather than beside it. Speakers aimed outward and level from a high terrace throw sound over the slope toward houses lower down. We mount terrace speakers higher and angle them down into the seating area, use more speakers at lower volume rather than two loud ones, and set a volume limit on every outdoor zone in the Sonos app. The target is a zone that is pleasant on the terrace and inaudible at the bottom of the lawn.

Owners lighting a terrace often run the landscape lighting and the speakers on the same visit, although we keep the two in separate conduits. The Todt Hill soundbar team covers the indoor TV rooms that the outdoor zones are grouped with.

Compliance notes for business music around Todt Hill

  • 42 dBA in the flat upstairs. DEP's noise code summary caps commercial music at 42 dBA "as measured in any dwelling unit receiving the noise." Plenty of storefronts on Victory Boulevard and Richmond Road have tenants above, so each room gets a Sonos volume ceiling that we confirm with the owner before leaving.
  • Music stays indoors. Under §24-244(b) of the city's Administrative Code a business may not use a sound device to draw the attention of people outside or play it through a door or window opening onto the street. We point every shop and office speaker inward.
  • Commercial playback rights. A household Spotify or Apple Music plan does not cover a waiting room or a club bar; the business needs a service sold for commercial use. We connect whichever one you subscribe to, but licensing is between you and the provider.
  • Campus and club rules. Schools and clubs often have their own facilities and IT policies, including separate networks for staff and guests. Sonos needs the app and every product on the same network, so we agree the network plan with whoever runs it before installing.
  • Electrical work goes to an electrician. A fresh receptacle beside the rack, a separate circuit for a club’s Amps, or any change at the breaker box is handed to a licensed electrician; our part is marking where each one should go and timing our visit around theirs.

Rock, slope and the Natural Area

There's bare rock a few inches under our back lawn — how do outdoor speakers get wired without a trench?

By following the hard surfaces instead of the soft ones. Where serpentine ledge sits just under the turf, the realistic cable routes are the ones that never need to cut it: a UV-rated conduit fixed along the foot of a retaining wall or the side of a stone stair, a run inside the edging of a planting bed where there is real soil, a saw-cut across a paved path that the paving contractor reinstates, or a route that leaves the house high and drops down a wall or a post close to the speakers.

If the only route to a far corner is across exposed rock, we say so and offer two honest alternatives: move the speakers to where a route exists — often the near edge of the terrace, angled outward — or use a portable for that corner. A Move 2 carries Sonos's IP56 rating and up to 24 hours of battery, so it can spend an evening on the far side of the lawn and go back to its indoor charging base afterward. What we will not do is chip a channel through ledge for a speaker cable; it is slow, it rarely lasts, and on Todt Hill it is a change to a protected natural feature.

Does the Special Natural Area District affect digging a cable trench across our lot?

It can. The Zoning Resolution's definition of a "site alteration" in the district includes excavating and removing topsoil, whether or not the Department of Buildings would require a permit, and the district's rules apply to any site alteration. Section 105-021 exempts the kind of minor work most outdoor audio involves — work on a lot with an existing house, with no more than two feet of cut or fill — but only if no tree of six-inch caliper or more is removed and every such tree's critical root zone is left undisturbed. Larger or steeper lots, rock outcrops and steep slopes bring further rules into play.

So for most existing houses a narrow, shallow trench across open lawn is the kind of work the exemption describes, while a trench through a tree's root zone, across a steep slope or into an outcrop is not. We are not your zoning adviser and do not give legal opinions; when a route is close to any of those lines, we recommend you ask your architect or the Department of City Planning before we dig. Our part is to design routes that stay well clear of the protected features in the first place, and to put the route on paper so whoever you ask can see exactly what is proposed.

We're planning an addition — do conduits for future outdoor speakers belong on the site plan our architect files for the Natural Area review?

If the addition is going through review anyway, yes, it is the cheapest moment to include them. The district's application requirements ask for a site plan showing existing and proposed patios, decks, pools, walkways and driveways, steep slopes and the staging area, and the location, caliper and species of every tree of six-inch caliper or more. An empty conduit from the new foundation to the future terrace, drawn on that plan and laid while the ground is already open for the approved work, is part of the approved disturbance. Adding a trench later, after the landscape has been restored, means a second disturbance and possibly a second conversation with the City.

What we give your architect is simple: the start and end point of each conduit, its size, the depth we would lay it at, and the speakers and Amp location it serves. One or two spare conduits — one for speakers, one for network or a future camera — cost little in an open trench. We do not prepare or file zoning applications; we supply the low-voltage information the people who do will need.

Can cable run inside or along our dry-laid stone retaining wall to the lower terrace?

Along it, usually; inside it, rarely. A dry-laid wall holds itself up by the weight and fit of its stones, and pulling stones to bury a conduit in the core weakens the wall and voids whatever the mason intended. A mortared wall can sometimes take a conduit in a raked joint, but only with the owner's mason involved. The method we use most is a small conduit fixed at the base of the wall on the uphill or downhill face, where it reads as part of the wall's drainage detail, or tucked under the coping on the top course where a cap stone overhangs.

Two cautions specific to the hill. Walls here are often part of the natural-feature story — the Flagg houses use retaining walls as building walls — so a wall that belongs to a landmark is not one we fix anything to without a Landmarks answer. And water runs along the foot of every retaining wall on a slope, so the conduit is sealed at both ends and the speaker connection is made above grade in a weatherproof box, never in the mud at the bottom.

Stone walls, elevators and big-house networks

Will Sonos Wi-Fi pass through the mosaic-rubble walls of a Flagg cottage?

Often not well enough to rely on. The landmark reports and the Historic Districts Council describe Flagg's mosaic rubble as flat-faced serpentine set against forms with concrete poured behind it, so an interior wall can be stone, concrete and plaster in one thickness. Sonos rates concrete as a high obstruction to wireless signals, and a Sonos product two rooms away on the far side of that construction can join the system and then drop out every evening when the neighbours' networks wake up. We test signal in the actual rooms before recommending anything, because two walls that look the same can behave very differently.

The fixes, in order: wire the product nearest the router so Sonos can use its own dedicated wireless network; put a wired access point in the room on the far side of the thickest wall, with its cable run inside a closet or along a baseboard rather than through the stone; and keep the speakers in those far rooms free-standing so nothing has to be fixed to the masonry. In a landmarked cottage it also keeps the work reversible.

Our house has an elevator — can the cab have music, and can the shaft carry speaker cable between floors?

The cab can have music; the shaft cannot carry our cable. Anything inside an elevator cab, including a speaker, is connected through the elevator's travelling cable, and work on the elevator, its cab and its hoistway belongs to the elevator company that maintains it. We do not run speaker or network cable in a hoistway, and you should be wary of anyone who offers to. If you want music in the cab, ask your elevator contractor whether the travelling cable has spare pairs and whether they will connect a cab speaker; we can then deliver an amplified feed to the point they nominate in the machine room or the top landing.

Most families decide they would rather have music in the landings and the hall outside each stop, which we can do with a ceiling speaker or a free-standing Era 100 on each level. For cable between floors, stacked closets and mechanical chases are better routes anyway.

Is Sonos's new Amp Multi worth it over several standard Amps for a house this size?

It depends on how many zones you have and where they are. Sonos announced Amp Multi in January 2026 with eight amplified outputs of 125 watts each at 8 ohms, configurable as up to four independent zones, each output able to drive up to three Sonos Architectural speakers; it uses a fanless design with a 1.5U chassis and a separate 2U rack mount, and Sonos says it is available through its installation partners from August 25, 2026. A standard Amp drives one zone. So a house with eight or ten passive-speaker zones all served from one rack is where Amp Multi earns its place: fewer boxes, fewer network connections, and per-output tuning tools for awkward rooms.

It is the wrong tool where zones are far apart. If the pool terrace is 250 feet from the rack, the better design is often a standard Amp near the terrace, fed by network cable, with the rack's Amp Multi covering the indoor zones close to it. Amp Multi is sold through Sonos's installation partners, and AESS does not claim to be one; if you buy one through a partner, we can wire the speakers and the rack and set the system up.

Our IT consultant wants Sonos on its own VLAN, away from the cameras — will the app still find the speakers?

Only if the network is built to let it. Sonos's own documentation says the app and every Sonos product must be on the same network and subnet, because the app finds speakers by discovery traffic that does not cross between networks on its own. Separating Sonos onto a VLAN is common in large houses with cameras, door stations and staff devices, and it can work, but the router or firewall then has to pass the discovery traffic between the VLANs — network engineers describe reflecting mDNS on UDP port 5353 and SSDP on UDP port 1900, and handling multicast with IGMP — and the phones that control Sonos have to be on a network that can see it.

The simpler design, which we usually recommend, is to keep Sonos and the household's phones and tablets together on the main network, and to put the cameras, the alarm and the gate equipment on their own segment instead. That isolates the devices that most need isolating without asking the music system to work across a boundary. Either way, we agree the plan with your consultant first.

Offices at the foot of the hill

Can the waiting room have music while the exam rooms next to it stay silent and private?

Yes, and the music can help with privacy rather than hurt it. Each Sonos room is its own zone, so a waiting-room pair can play all day while speakers in the treatment rooms, if there are any, stay off or play only when someone chooses. The practical work is in placement: ceiling speakers in reception aimed away from the shared wall with the exam rooms, a volume limit set so conversation at the front desk stays easy, and nothing mounted on the partition between the two. A low, steady level of music in the waiting room also makes it harder to overhear speech through a door, which many practices on Richmond Road ask for.

We set the waiting-room zone on an alarm so it starts and stops with the office day, limit its maximum volume, and leave control with one staff device so patients' phones never see the system. If the practice uses a commercial music service, we sign it in; if it does not yet have one, we explain why a personal account is the wrong choice for a business before we set anything up.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation in Todt Hill

This page was built without access to Google's AI Overview, so nothing below claims to reproduce it. What we test instead are seven statements that recur across Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr cost articles, manufacturer marketing and forum threads — the sources AI summaries lean on — checked against estate houses on a serpentine ridge.

Claim one: "Whole-home audio costs about $500 to $1,000 per room"

Ask an answer engine what whole-house audio costs and the number is usually Angi's: $500 to $1,000 for each additional room or zone, $50 to $100 an hour for labour, and $100 to $500 if walls or ceilings need modifying.

Per-room pricing breaks down on Todt Hill because the expensive part is not the room. A bedroom 30 feet from the rack and a terrace 250 feet away down a slope are both "one room," but one needs 16-gauge wire through a basement ceiling and the other needs 12-gauge in conduit along a retaining wall, or a second Amp location fed by network cable. The distance and the route set the labour.

That is why our written quotes itemise runs as well as rooms,. Our published guidance for whole-home audio is $800 to $2,500 per zone installed, and the walkthrough decides where each zone falls.

Claim two: "A good mesh Wi-Fi system covers any house"

Router makers advertise whole-home mesh as the end of dead zones, and the usual forum reply to a Sonos speaker that keeps vanishing is "buy a mesh kit."

Sonos's requirements are more specific: with a wired Sonos setup on a mesh, the wired Sonos product belongs on the primary node, and range extenders and powerline adapters are on its unsupported list. Every wireless hop between satellites costs capacity, and a hop through serpentine rubble or a concrete foundation wall costs a lot — usually right where the bedroom wing or the media room is.

Satellites cabled back to the switch behave very differently from satellites talking to each other through stone. On the hill, the wiring behind the access points matters far more than the logo on the router.

Claim three: "Outdoor speakers just need a trench to the house"

DIY guides describe burying direct-burial speaker cable a few inches down across the lawn as the standard method.

On a lot in Special Natural Area District-1, excavating is part of the definition of a site alteration, trees of six-inch caliper or more have protected root zones of up to 22 feet, and the ground under the turf is often serpentine ledge. A trench that is routine in a flat South Shore yard can be impossible or inappropriate here.

We plan outdoor cable along walls, steps and paving, through conduit laid during approved site work, or by moving an Amp closer to the terrace. The speakers are the same; the route is the job.

Claim four: "Sonos is plug-and-play, so installation is optional"

Unboxing an Era 100 and adding it in the app really is a five-minute job, which is what the adverts show. Yet the company's ceiling-speaker guide states plainly that in-ceiling models "require special installation," and adds that its architectural range often calls for a professional.

A Todt Hill house with an equipment rack, passive speakers in a dozen ceilings, outdoor zones and a network spread across four levels is a low-voltage installation project, with cable to certify, gauges to choose and zoning to respect. The Sonos app is the easy part at the end.

Free-standing speakers still need no installer, and we will say which of yours are in that category.

Claim five: "Any speaker wire will do"

Many guides treat speaker wire as an afterthought, and home-centre advice often recommends one gauge for every job.

Sonos's own table gives 16-gauge under 80 feet, 14-gauge from 80 to 200 feet, 12-gauge from 200 to 300 feet, and recommends against runs over 300 feet. On a sloping estate lot, outdoor zones routinely land in the bottom rows of that table, and a single gauge chosen for the kitchen is the wrong choice for the pool.

We measure each route and write the gauge beside it on the zone map. Where a run would pass 300 feet, we change the design rather than the wire.

Claim six: "Historic houses can't have modern audio"

Forum answers about landmarked and old stone houses often assume that any installation will damage them or need years of approvals.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's published guidance is that interior work needs an LPC permit when it requires a Department of Buildings permit, affects the exterior, or involves a designated interior landmark. Free-standing Sonos speakers, a soundbar on furniture and a wired access point inside a closet do none of those things, and the Commission's staff will confirm for a specific job.

In Flagg's stone cottages and the hill's other landmarks, we keep exterior speakers off the facade unless the owner has the Commission's answer, and we keep interior work reversible. Good sound and preservation are not in conflict.

Claim seven: "More access points always mean better Wi-Fi"

Big-house network advice often amounts to "add another access point" whenever something drops.

Sonos asks for at least 18 inches between its products and other electronics and lists Wi-Fi routers, extenders and access points among interference sources. Access points placed carelessly on top of speakers, in the media cabinet beside the soundbar, or on the same channels as their neighbours, can make Sonos worse.

We place fewer access points with more care: wired, spaced and on planned channels.

Our Installation Process

From a single terrace pair to a rack serving four floors, the sequence has seven stages. On the hill, steps two and three take longer than anywhere else in the borough, and they save the most later.

1. Walk the grounds, then the house

We start at the lowest terrace or the pool and work up: levels, retaining walls, stone steps, exposed rock, and the trunks of trees big enough to carry protected root zones under the Natural Area rules. Distances are paced and trunks measured before we go indoors.

2. Choose the head-end

We find where the internet enters and walk down to the room that minimises the longest run — usually a lower-level mechanical or storage room near the middle of the footprint — and check its ventilation, power and cable paths to every level.

3. Draw the zone map

Every listening zone goes on paper with its route, its measured length, the speaker-wire gauge Sonos recommends for that length or the network cable that feeds it, and whether it is served by the main rack or a second Amp location. The written price follows the map.

4. Pull and certify the backbone

Network cable goes in first, from the head-end to each level, wing and access point, and every drop is tested before ceilings are closed or terraces finished. Outdoor conduit is laid along walls, steps and paving, never through ledge or a root zone.

5. Run speaker cable and mount

Speaker wire of the specified gauge goes to each zone; ceiling, wall and outdoor speakers are fitted, soundbars mounted on fixings chosen for that wall, and free-standing speakers placed where stone or landmark status rules out fixing anything.

6. Set up, group and tune

All products join one Sonos system; zones are named by level and wing the way the household speaks; Trueplay runs in every supported room; outdoor zones get volume limits aimed at the lawn below, not just the terrace.

7. Hand over the map

We walk the household through grouping, limits and re-running Trueplay, test discovery from every phone, and leave a one-page map showing which cable feeds which zone, so the next electrician, landscaper or installer knows what is where.

Zone Map

Counting Zones in a House With Nine Heating Zones

The 935 Todt Hill Road listing counted nine heating zones and five cooling zones. That is a useful clue, because the people who designed the heating already divided the house into spaces that are used at different times. Sonos zones follow a similar logic, but not the same one: a heating zone is about temperature, a listening zone is about who hears what at the same moment. The matrix below is the worksheet we fill in with you during the walkthrough.

Rooms that always share

Spaces open to each other — a kitchen, breakfast area and family room in one long room; a foyer and the gallery above it; a terrace and the pool deck beside it — should play together, because two different songs in one open space sound like an argument. These become one zone, even if they cross a heating zone boundary, and in a passive-speaker design they share one Amp channel or one Amp.

Rooms that never share

Bedrooms, a home office, a gym and a guest suite each get their own zone, because someone will want silence in one while music plays in the next. In a passive design that means an Amp output each; in a lighter design, a free-standing Era 100 or a Ray under a bedroom TV does the same job without any cable to the rack.

Rooms that look like zones but are not

Corridors, back stairs, powder rooms and mud rooms rarely need their own speakers. In a large house they are where people pass, not where they listen, and a hall speaker grouped with the nearest living space covers them. Leaving them off saves Amp channels and ceiling holes.

Households mapping Sonos zones often add a TV to one of them on the same visit, since the soundbar becomes the zone's speaker. Our Staten Island TV installation crews and the AV and sound team for Staten Island can plan the screens and the audio together.

Existing Wiring

When the House Already Has Speaker Wire in the Walls

Many larger Todt Hill houses were built or renovated with audio already in mind. The 935 Todt Hill Road listing advertised "surround sound throughout" the lower level and smart-home audio and intercom on the main floors; plenty of houses on the hill have ceiling speakers, wall volume knobs and a closet full of older amplifiers from a system installed fifteen or twenty years ago. That wiring is often valuable. It is also often unlabelled, partly broken, or wired in ways a Sonos Amp cannot use directly.

What we test before we promise anything

Each pair of speaker wire is traced from the closet to its speaker, tested for continuity and for shorts, and labelled. Each speaker is checked for its impedance and its condition; surrounds on older drivers perish, and a speaker that rattles at moderate volume is a speaker to replace rather than reconnect. Wall volume controls are identified, because impedance-matching knobs from older whole-house systems sit between the amplifier and the speakers and change what the Amp sees. Sonos's guidance is that the Amp can drive two 8-ohm pairs in parallel and nothing below 4 ohms; we design the reconnection around that.

The common outcomes

In most houses, most of the old speaker wire is reusable. Ceiling speakers in good condition can stay, driven from a Sonos Amp or one output of an Amp Multi; dead ones are replaced with Sonos architectural speakers in the same openings where the sizes allow. Wall knobs are usually bypassed and the plates blanked or replaced, since the Sonos app does the job better.

Owners reusing old audio wiring often find old network and camera cable in the same closet. Our structured cabling crews on Staten Island can test and re-terminate it during the same visit.

Field Notes: How a Todt Hill Walkthrough Runs

Below is our standing checklist for a first visit on the ridge — the sequence, not a tale from one house.

We start outside, at the lowest point of the property we can reach, and look back up at the house. From the pool or the bottom terrace you can see how many levels the system has to climb, where the retaining walls and steps are, where rock shows through the lawn and which trees are big enough to have protected root zones; we pace the distances and measure the trunks. Then we find where the internet enters — often the garage at the top of the drive — and walk down through the house to the room that would make the best head-end, counting floors and noting closets that stack. On each level we ask how the household uses the rooms, which spaces are open to each other, and where people want silence. We look for old speaker wire and wall knobs, and if there is an existing equipment closet we open it. In stone houses we test Wi-Fi in each room with a meter before saying anything about products. Only at the end, at the kitchen table, do we draw the zone map, and by then the conversation is about routes and zones, not about which speaker looks nicest.

FAQ

Todt Hill Sonos Installation FAQ

Can speaker cable share a trench or a conduit with the landscape lighting cable?
+

We keep them apart. Landscape lighting runs from its own transformer and is installed by the lighting contractor, and sharing a conduit with it means anyone servicing one system has to disturb the other. More practically, speaker cable that runs alongside lighting cable for a long distance can pick up hum. Where both have to follow the same route along a wall or under a path, we lay two conduits side by side in one excavation, label both ends, and record the route on the run map. On a Todt Hill lot that also means the ground is opened once rather than twice.

Can a speaker live in the wine cellar, where the room is kept cool and damp?
+

Usually, with care over placement. Sonos publishes an operating range of 32 to 104°F for products such as the Amp, and a cellar held in the fifties is inside that. The risk in a cellar is condensation, not cold: a speaker carried in from a warm room, or mounted on an outside wall that sweats, can collect moisture. We fit a passive ceiling speaker on the cellar's interior side and keep the Amp that drives it out in the dry equipment room, so the only thing in the cool, humid air is the speaker itself. Free-standing products are better kept out of a working cellar.

Are there speakers made to sit in the planting beds instead of on the house?
+

Yes. Landscape speakers are passive weatherproof satellites designed to be staked into beds, often with a buried or semi-buried bass unit, and they are driven from an Amp like any other passive speaker. Sonos's own outdoor speakers mount on walls and eaves, so a landscape system uses third-party speakers on a Sonos Amp. On the hill they suit terraces with no wall nearby and planting beds edged with real soil, but every stake and cable has to stay outside the critical root zones of protected trees, and we do not set them into rock. We draw the bed positions on the site sketch before anything is ordered.

The only route to the pool crosses our paved driveway — can the cable cross it without tearing it up?
+

Usually yes. The tidy method is a narrow saw-cut across the paving, a small conduit laid in it and the cut sealed or reinstated, which your paving contractor can finish to match. Pavers can often be lifted along one joint, the conduit bedded beneath, and the pavers relaid. A driveway already crossed by an empty sleeve, which many newer houses have, is the easiest of all, and we look for one first. What we avoid is running cable across the surface under a mat or in a ramp cover on a driveway that carries cars and a snow plough every winter.

Will the Sonos app still work if the house uses two internet providers with automatic failover?
+

Yes, as long as both connections feed the same router or firewall and the internal network stays the same when it switches. Sonos needs the app and every product on the same local network; it does not care which provider carries the traffic to the internet. Streaming will pause for a moment when the failover happens and then resume. Problems appear only when the backup line brings its own router with its own Wi-Fi network, so that phones and speakers end up on different networks. We set the failover up behind one router, or check that your IT consultant has, before we install.
