TERMINOLOGY

New York Commercial Security Terms, Translated

Most bad quotes hide inside vocabulary. This is the working glossary for reading any NYC commercial security proposal.

PoE / PoE+. Power over Ethernet — one Cat6 run carries both data and power to a camera, reader or intercom. The commercial default, because it centralizes power and backup.

NVR vs DVR vs VMS. DVRs record analog cameras, NVRs record IP cameras, and a VMS is the software layer that manages many recorders and sites. If your quote says DVR in 2026, ask why.

ONVIF. The interoperability standard that lets cameras and recorders from different brands work together — the difference between owning a system and being locked to a vendor.

OSDP. The current secure protocol between readers and access panels, replacing legacy Wiegand wiring that can be skimmed at the door. New installs should be OSDP-ready.

Fail-safe vs fail-secure. What a locked door does when power dies: fail-safe unlocks, fail-secure stays locked. Egress doors and fire code decide this, not preference.

Electric strike vs maglock. Two ways to electrify a door. Strikes work with the existing latch; maglocks hold the door with magnetic force and carry strict egress rules in NYC.

REX. Request-to-exit — the sensor or bar that lets people leave freely while the door stays locked from outside. Miss it and the door either alarms all day or traps people.

Door position switch. The contact that tells the system a door is actually closed — the difference between “locked” and “propped open since lunch.”

Mobile credential. Your phone as the key. Harder to lend out than a fob, revocable in seconds, and increasingly what commercial tenants expect.

Dual-path communicator. An alarm panel that reports over both cellular and internet, so a cut line or an ISP outage does not silence monitoring.

Video verification. A short clip attached to an alarm signal so the monitoring center dispatches on evidence, not guesswork — the single best false-alarm reducer.

Retention. How many days of footage the recorder holds before overwriting. Insurers and claims timelines, not gut feel, should set this number.

Edge analytics. Detection running on the camera itself — person, vehicle, line-cross, loiter — so alerts arrive in real time instead of after a scrub.

NDAA-compliant. Hardware permitted on federally connected projects. If your business bids government work, this one word belongs in your camera spec.

Asked on Bing: Commercial Security in NYC

Straight answers to the questions Bing surfaces for this market — each expanded elsewhere on this page.

What is the average cost of a business security system in NYC?

Across the five boroughs, most single-location businesses land between $5,000 and $25,000 installed once cameras, a monitored alarm and a door or two of access control are combined. Storefront-only packages start around $3,000; warehouse and multi-tenant builds run well past $50,000. The spread is doors, risers and building rules more than camera count — which is why the number that holds comes from a free survey, not a table.

Do commercial security systems lower insurance premiums in NYC?

Often, yes — many commercial carriers discount for monitored intrusion alarms with recognized certificates, and some for camera coverage of specific risks. The discount depends on your carrier and policy, so the working order is: ask your broker what earns credit, then install to that written spec. Monitored systems with open/close reporting are the most commonly credited item we see on NYC policies.

Are security cameras tax deductible for a NYC business?

Security equipment is generally a deductible business expense, and installed systems commonly qualify for accelerated treatment such as Section 179 expensing in the year of purchase. The specifics depend on your entity and situation, so confirm with your accountant — but bring it up, because owners routinely leave this on the table when comparing quotes on sticker price alone.

How long does a commercial camera system last?

Plan on seven to ten years for quality cameras and five to seven for recorders, with hard drives the first consumable — they are the component that dies quietly. Door hardware and panels commonly run a decade or more with maintenance. The practical lifespan question is support: open-platform ONVIF equipment stays serviceable across vendors, while proprietary gear ages exactly as fast as its manufacturer’s interest.

Do businesses have to post camera signage in New York?

Camera notice signage is not broadly mandated for ordinary video-only surveillance of commercial premises in New York, but posting it is common practice — it deters, and it heads off disputes. The sharper rules involve audio recording and cameras in private areas such as restrooms and changing rooms, which are off-limits. For workplace-specific recording policies, a short conversation with counsel is money well spent.

Can a commercial security system work without internet?

Yes — and the core of it should. Cameras record to an on-site NVR, door controllers enforce cached credentials, and the alarm reports over cellular, all with zero internet. What requires connectivity is remote viewing, cloud backup and push alerts. The design rule for any NYC building: internet adds convenience on top of a system that functions without it, never the reverse.

Which is better for a small NYC business: local installer or national chain?

Match the model to the portfolio. One to a few locations usually does better with a licensed local integrator: owned equipment, month-to-month monitoring, a published service rate and the same crew twice. Dozens of sites across states is where national scale and single-contract consistency earn their trade-offs — multi-year terms, leased hardware, branch-routed service. Read the exit clause either way before comparing prices.

How much is commercial alarm monitoring per month in New York?

Commercial central-station monitoring typically runs $40–$120 a month depending on zones, cellular reporting and open/close supervision. Live remote video monitoring of lots and entries is a separate tier, roughly $800–$2,500 monthly. The number to check harder than price is the term: month-to-month exists industry-wide, so a multi-year commitment is a choice someone is making for you.

Who is allowed to install security systems in New York State?

Businesses that install or service security and fire alarm systems are licensed under General Business Law Article 6-D, verifiable in a minute on the Department of State’s public search — our number is 12000287431. NYC layers building rules on top, and owners working on their own property sit outside the license requirement. The one-minute lookup before hiring anyone is the cheapest security measure on this page.

What should a 24/7 business look for in a security system?

Round-the-clock operations invert the usual design: there is no armed-empty state, so the system leans on access control with full audit trails, cameras sized for continuous retention, duress buttons at cash points, and health monitoring that flags failures immediately because there is no quiet window to absorb them. Arming schedules matter less; who-was-where evidence matters more. Design for shifts, not closing time.

YOUR QUESTIONS ANSWERED

Real Questions from New York Business Owners

How much does a commercial security system cost for a business in NYC?

Real New York numbers: a camera-plus-monitored-alarm storefront package typically lands in the low four figures; a small office with cameras and one or two controlled doors runs mid four figures to low five; an integrated mid-size system — sixteen to twenty-four cameras, four to six access doors, monitored intrusion — commonly falls in the $15,000–$45,000 class; multi-site and enterprise work starts around $50,000. The spread between two quotes for the same address is almost never the cameras — it is doors, cabling conditions, retention and what got silently excluded. A fair quote itemizes all four.

Are cloud security cameras worth the subscription for a small business?

Sometimes — and the math should be done out loud. Per-camera and per-door cloud licensing looks small on a brochure and compounds: a twenty-four-camera, six-door cloud stack can quietly run hundreds of dollars every month, forever. Cloud earns its keep for multi-site owners with no IT staff who want one dashboard and zero servers. Single-location businesses usually come out ahead owning an on-premises NVR with optional off-site backup. We price both at a three-to-five-year horizon in every proposal, and hybrid — local recording, cloud access — is the most common winner.

What is a fair per-door price for commercial access control in New York?

Most standard commercial doors land between roughly $1,500 and $3,000 installed — reader, electrified hardware, door contact, REX and programming. Budget hardware on an easy door can touch $500–$1,000; biometric or enterprise doors reach $6,000–$8,000. What moves the number in NYC is the door itself: fire-rated assemblies, fresh cable runs at $250–$600 per door, herculite glass storefront doors and elevator integration all cost real money. Anyone quoting one flat per-door price before seeing your doors is guessing.

How do I vet a commercial security installer in NYC before signing?

Four checks. First, licensure — any commercial security company you shortlist should hold it, because New York State licenses security and fire alarm system businesses under Article 6-D, and the Department of State lets you verify a licensee. Second, a certificate of insurance naming your building or landlord as certificate holder — in managed New York buildings this is what decides whether a crew badges in at all. Third, whether they will install to your insurance carrier’s written spec when the policy names brands or retention. Fourth, a real on-site survey before any number. Skip any shop that quotes a building it has never walked.

Should a small office use a national security company or a local integrator?

Read the contract term before you read the brochure. The single most common complaint New York owners trade about the national chains is multi-year monitoring agreements with automatic renewal and equipment you never actually own. Nationals bring footprint and financing; a local licensed integrator brings open hardware you own outright, a published service rate and someone accountable by name. For a security system for a small business — the twelve-person office, the two-register storefront — the practical answer is usually local install, optional monitoring month to month, and no contract you need a lawyer to exit.

Is it normal for a security installer to ask for full payment upfront?

No. Deposit-and-balance is the industry norm, and our own standard is a fifty percent deposit with the balance at completion. One hundred percent upfront from a contractor you have not worked with before is a walk-away signal — you lose every point of leverage on punch-list items, and the pattern shows up constantly in stories about installers who collected and disappeared. A written scope, a deposit structure and a named warranty are the baseline of a professional commercial proposal.

Can I install my own security cameras and access control at my business in New York?

Legally, on property you own, yes — Article 6-D’s licensing requirement covers businesses installing for others, and owners working on their own premises fall outside it. Practically, the line arrives fast: monitored alarm certificates your insurer will accept, electrified hardware on egress and fire-rated doors, and anything touching a life-safety system all belong with licensed hands — fire alarm work in the five boroughs carries its own Master Electrician and FDNY chain entirely. Plenty of owners self-install a camera or two; whole-building systems are where DIY quietly gets expensive.

Can an installer reuse the wiring from our old security system?

Often, and a survey answers it in under an hour. Existing coax can frequently carry IP video over converters, sound Cat5e regularly survives for cameras, and conduit pathways are reusable even when the cable inside is not. Access control is the usual exception — door hardware wants dedicated home runs, and legacy reader wiring is often the skimmable old protocol worth retiring anyway. We test what exists, keep what passes, and the proposal says which is which, because ripping out working cable is billing you twice for one building.

Do commercial buildings need wired or wireless security systems?

Wired PoE is the commercial default — one cable per device carrying power and data, centralized battery backup, no radio congestion and no fleet of batteries to manage. Wireless earns specific spots: gates and yards where trenching is absurd, landmarked facades that cannot take conduit, and no-drill lease situations. The honest answer for most NYC businesses is a wired backbone with wireless as a tactical exception, chosen door by door and camera by camera at the survey rather than as a philosophy.

How many days of camera footage should a NYC business keep?

Thirty days is the working floor for commercial systems. Slip-and-fall claims, insurance investigations and NYPD follow-ups routinely reach back weeks, not days, and finding out your recorder overwrote the incident is the most expensive storage lesson there is. Cash-heavy, regulated and higher-liability businesses — dispensaries, medical, nightlife — commonly hold sixty to ninety. Retention is a hard-drive math problem, so we spec it as a number in the proposal, not a shrug after the fact.

Does cloud access control stop working when the internet goes down?

Properly specified, no — and this is the question that separates real platforms from cheap ones. Serious cloud access systems cache credentials and schedules on the local door controller, so doors keep granting and denying exactly as programmed through an outage; what pauses is remote administration and event sync, which catch up on reconnect. Pair that with battery-backed panels and the fail-safe-versus-fail-secure hardware decision made deliberately per door, and an ISP outage becomes a non-event instead of a lockout.

What can a commercial tenant install if the landlord will not allow drilling?

More than most tenants assume. Surface raceway carries clean cable runs without opening walls, wireless sensors and cameras cover no-penetration zones, and suite-side access control can mount to the tenant’s own door hardware. The paperwork matters as much as the hardware: a licensed installer’s COI, landlord sign-off in writing, and restore-on-exit language in the lease rider keep the system from becoming a move-out dispute. We do this dance in managed buildings weekly — bring the alteration rules to the survey and we design inside them.

How do property managers stop tenants from propping doors and sharing fobs?

With data instead of memos. Door-prop alarms escalate when a door sits open past a set time; audit trails end the guessing about whose credential opened what and when; mobile credentials cut the fob-lending economy because nobody hands a stranger their phone; and a camera tied to the worst-abused door settles disputes in thirty seconds. Do I need a licensed installer for a commercial security system in New York?

If you hire it out, yes — New York licenses the business of installing and servicing security and fire alarm systems under General Business Law Article 6-D, and you can verify any company with the Department of State. Owners installing on their own property are exempt, though insurers and landlords usually require licensed work anyway, and fire alarm scope in NYC additionally demands the Master Electrician and FDNY chain.
