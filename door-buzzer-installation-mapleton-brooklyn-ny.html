The Neighborhood

Why Mapleton Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Mapleton is a small neighborhood in southwest Brooklyn, usually drawn from 16th or 17th Avenue east to Dahill Road and from 57th Street south to 65th Street, though a 2012 New York Times profile let it reach as far north as 47th Street and many residents north of 60th Street simply say they live in Borough Park. It belongs to Community Board 11 with Bensonhurst, Bath Beach and Gravesend, and its mail goes to Brooklyn 11204. The land was farms until about 1910. William Sugarman's Alco Building Company began selling "Mapleton Park" lots from 1908, and when the Sea Beach line opened through its open cut in June 1915, with stations at 18th Avenue, 20th Avenue and Bay Parkway, the side streets filled quickly with one- and two-family houses of brick or clapboard, many of them with front porches.

For a buzzer installer, that history explains almost everything about the job. Those houses were built for one household, and the door was answered by whoever was home walking to it. Brownstoner's 2012 summary of the Times profile counted about 27,000 residents, including Chinese and other Asian families, Orthodox Jewish families, Italian Americans, Poles, Latinos, Pakistanis and Russians, and many of the houses now hold more than one household: an owner on the first floor and a tenant upstairs, grown children over parents, or two related families sharing a front door. The bell that came with the house rings a chime in the downstairs hall. Nobody upstairs can open the door without walking down a flight, and the visitor waiting on the porch often cannot tell which household they have reached.

The commercial blocks add a second kind of building. Along 18th Avenue, listing records show three-story, three-apartment buildings from 1931 at 5001 and 6417 18th Avenue, the familiar pattern of a store across the ground floor and a narrow residential door beside it leading straight to the stair. Those apartments need a buzzer that calls each one and releases a street door the store never touches. And the newest buildings bring a third: Marketproof's permit reports show an eight-unit, three-story building filed in September 2025 at 1835 51st Street, a twelve-unit mixed-use building filed in 2021 at 5606 18th Avenue, and three-unit buildings filed at 986 Dahill Road in May 2025 and at 2001 53rd Street in March 2026, each replacing an older house or low-rise. Some of those new buildings are large enough that New York law, not the owner's preference, decides what the entry system must do.

Six facts that shape a Mapleton buzzer installation

  • Houses from the Sea Beach years. The Sea Beach line opened on June 22, 1915, and by then dozens of houses had gone up near its stations. Most Mapleton side streets are one- and two-family houses of that era, so the typical job is a first buzzer for two or three households, not a lobby panel.
  • Shop-and-apartment buildings from about 1931. Listing data dates 5001 and 6417 18th Avenue to 1931, each with three apartments. Their residential doors sit beside storefronts and need a panel that calls only the apartments.
  • New buildings on both sides of the legal line. 1835 51st Street was filed for eight units and 5606 18th Avenue for twelve; 986 Dahill Road and 2001 53rd Street for three each. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a's talk-and-release requirement applies to class A buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968.
  • A working intercom is treated as essential. Habitat magazine reported in February 2024 that the housing maintenance code requires a functioning intercom and that courts have found a failure to maintain one breaches the warranty of habitability.
  • A landlord's exposure is real. Brick Underground, citing the New York Attorney General's tenants' rights guide, reported that a landlord could be held liable if a crime occurs because a broken buzzer left the street door unlocked.
  • Small systems go in quickly once planned. Brick Underground's sources estimated about one day to replace the entry system in a five-apartment building and about two days for a hundred units, so in a Mapleton two- or three-family the planning and the cable route take more thought than the installation itself.

Mapleton calls that start with "we need a buzzer" sort into three groups once we have seen the house. Nothing in it can talk to a visitor or release the door, so the job is a first entry system, even though there is wire in the walls. The third is a building with a system that works but no longer fits: a second household added upstairs, a basement made into a home office, a store whose back door now needs its own station.

Building Specialists

Door Buzzer Installation for Mapleton's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every buzzer we plan in Mapleton. They differ in how many households share a door, which doors those households actually use, how cable can travel from the street to each apartment, and who signs for the work. Each one has a mistake we see when the job is rushed.

One-family houses from the 1910s and 1920s

Brick or clapboard houses near the three Sea Beach stations, many with a front porch, still lived in by one family across two floors.

Two-family houses

The most common Mapleton house: an owner or one household on the first floor, a second household upstairs, sharing a front door or using a separate side door.

Three- and four-family houses

Houses that gained an attic apartment, a rear unit or a legal basement apartment, with three or more households and often two entrances.

1931 shop-and-apartment buildings

Three-story buildings on 18th Avenue such as 5001 and 6417 18th Avenue, with three apartments above a store and a narrow street door beside the shopfront.

New infill buildings

Three-story buildings filed since 2021, from three units at 986 Dahill Road and 2001 53rd Street to eight at 1835 51st Street and twelve at 5606 18th Avenue.

Small apartment buildings and co-ops

The walk-ups and small co-ops found on some Mapleton blocks among the houses, with a shared vestibule, a stair and an old intercom riser.

One-family houses: one button, and answering where the family actually is

A Mapleton house kept as one home needs only one button, so owners often think of the job as a doorbell. The useful part is upstairs. A family in a back kitchen cannot always hear a chime in the front hall, and a parent upstairs should not have to come down to find out who rang.

Our usual plan is one station by the kitchen, one on the second-floor landing, and phone answering for anyone who wants it. The porch adds a decision most rowhouse neighborhoods never face: whether the door station belongs at the top of the porch steps, at the front door behind the porch, or on a front gate if the yard is fenced. The release goes on whichever door actually locks.

Two-family houses: two buttons, two releases, and the owner's door kept separate

The most common Mapleton buzzer job is a two-family house where the owner's household lives on one floor and a tenant or relatives live on the other. Each household needs its own button at the door, its own answering station, and the ability to talk to its own visitors and release the street door without involving the other. That sounds simple, and it is, once one question is settled: which door does the upstairs household come through?

In some houses both households share the front door and a small hall, with the upstairs apartment's door at the top of the stair. Then one two-button station at the front door and a strike on that door does the job, and the owner's own apartment door inside the hall stays on a key. In others, the upstairs household has its own door at the side of the house or up a rear stair, and the front door belongs to the owner alone. Then each entrance gets its own station, and the cable from the side door has to reach the upstairs apartment without passing through the owner's rooms, usually through the cellar and up an outside wall or a closet.

The mistake we see most is a battery video doorbell for each household, bought separately and stuck beside the same door. Each household then sees a picture on its phone, but neither can release the door, so someone walks down anyway and the door ends up on the latch for deliveries. A proper two-button system costs less than many owners expect, and it is the thing that actually lets the upstairs household answer its own door.

Three- and four-family houses: the count changes over the years

Mapleton's larger houses have often gained households over time: an apartment made in the attic, a rear unit, a basement converted into a legal apartment. By the time an owner calls us, the house may have three or four households and two or three doors, and the old bell buttons on the front no longer match anyone. The right panel has one live button for each household living there now, and each button rings that household only.

1931 shop-and-apartment buildings: the residential door beside the store

On 18th Avenue, a typical building from around 1931 has a store across the ground floor and a narrow door to one side leading straight up a stair to the apartments. Listing records describe 5001 18th Avenue and 6417 18th Avenue this way, each with three apartments. The apartments need a panel at that narrow door that calls each of them and releases it, and the panel has to be visible and clearly labeled beside a storefront that draws far more visitors than the apartments do.

The store may need its own buzzer too, usually at a back or side door for deliveries, and the two systems must never be able to release each other's doors. We keep them as separate systems, each on its own power supply, with labels in the cellar saying which is which.

New infill buildings: the walls are still open

Mapleton's newest buildings are three stories tall and, from the permit reports, range from three units to twelve. For an entry system, the best moment in a building's life is while the walls are open, and the worst is the week before the certificate of occupancy, when the developer discovers the panel was never ordered. We work with the developer, the architect or the general contractor to decide the system early, mark the station locations in each apartment, pull cable to each one during rough-in, and leave a spare pair for the future.

Small apartment buildings and co-ops: a vestibule, a stair and an old riser

Where a Mapleton block has a small apartment building or co-op instead of houses, it usually has a proper vestibule, a stair hall and an apartment door on each landing. Buildings like these usually have an audio system already, with a metal button plate at the door and handsets in the apartments, and they are often the best candidates for reusing old wire. The pairs run up the stair hall in a single path, and a modern two-wire system can often use them once each pair is tested.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Find the Door Each Household Really Uses, Then the Chime, Then the Meter

These notes describe how our technicians approach a buzzer installation in a Mapleton house or small building. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Find the door each household really uses. The first walk is outside. Many Mapleton houses have a front door behind a porch, a side door off a driveway or a path, and sometimes a rear stair. The technician asks each household which one its visitors come to, which one couriers use, and which one the household itself uses when it comes home. If the upstairs family's guests always go to the side door, that is where their button belongs.

Then find the chime. Inside, the technician looks for the existing doorbell chime, usually high on a wall in the front hall, and follows its wire. The chime tells us where the porch button's pair goes, and the transformer in the cellar tells us whether it is a plain doorbell circuit or part of an older intercom. A plain doorbell pair cannot carry an intercom's audio as it is, but if it is a sound pair running from the porch to the cellar, it may be reusable for a two-wire door station once tested. The technician also checks the porch door and the front door: does each one close and latch on its own, is the frame wood or steel, and is there glass beside the lock?

Then find the meter. Last, the technician notes where the new power supply can go and which electric meter feeds that outlet. In a two-family house the owner and the tenant often have separate meters. A buzzer powered from the tenant's side goes dead when the tenant's power is shut off or a breaker trips upstairs, so the supply goes on the owner's or the house's circuit, and if no suitable outlet exists, the written price says a licensed electrician will add one.

Everything from that walk goes on the written price: the doors that get stations, the households each button calls, the strike, the closer, the pairs we will reuse and the ones we will replace, the power source and the route each new cable takes. Nothing is discovered on installation day.

New Buildings

Three Units, Eight Units, Twelve Units: Where Mapleton's New Buildings Fall Against the Eight-Apartment Line

Mapleton has been rebuilding one lot at a time. Marketproof's reports on new-building permits give a picture of the scale: three-story buildings, about 30 to 35 feet tall, replacing a house or a one-story store. What matters for an entry system is the unit count, because New York's Multiple Dwelling Law draws a line at eight apartments, and Mapleton's new buildings land on both sides of it.

Some of these addresses sit north of 57th Street, in the wider Mapleton that Marketproof uses. The permit reports do not say whether or when each building is finished, and nothing here is a statement about any of those owners; the table simply shows the range of new buildings being filed.

At eight apartments and above, the law decides the basics. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets residents talk with a visitor at that entrance and release the door from their apartments. ButterflyMX's guide to the New York City rule describes it the same way: the system must "let residents communicate with visitors at the main entrance and release the entrance door remotely." A new eight-unit or twelve-unit Mapleton building is squarely inside that rule. The developer cannot treat the intercom as an optional finish, and a building whose system is missing or dead when tenants move in starts life with a reportable condition.

Below eight, the owner chooses, and should still choose well. The same guide says class A buildings with fewer than eight units are "Not generally under this rule." A three-unit building on Dahill Road or 53rd Street is not required by §50-a to have talk and release. But a three-unit building with no way to answer the door is a building where tenants prop the street door for deliveries and walk down to let in guests, and Habitat magazine's February 2024 article noted that courts treat a failure to maintain an intercom as a breach of the warranty of habitability. Once a system is there, keeping it working is part of running the building.

What we ask a developer for before the walls close

  • The unit count and the entrances. How many apartments, which door is the main entrance, whether there is a rear or parking-side door that residents will use, and whether a ground-floor commercial space needs its own door station.
  • A location for the head-end. A spot in a meter room, cellar or utility closet for the power supply and controller, on a house circuit rather than any one apartment's panel, with the electrician providing a dedicated outlet if needed.
  • A station location in each apartment. Usually near the apartment's entry door at a height residents can reach, marked on the plans so the electrician and the drywall crew leave it clear.
  • The door hardware schedule. The main entrance door, its frame and its lock need to be chosen with the release in mind. A steel door and frame ordered with a strike prep save a day of cutting later.
  • A decision on answering. Wired stations in every apartment, phone answering, or both. If an app service is chosen, who owns the account when the building is sold or the apartments are rented, and who pays any per-unit monthly fee.
  • A handover plan. Who receives the administrator login, the wiring record and the directory, and on what date. On a new building that is often the difference between a system the owner can manage and one nobody can program a year later.

A new twelve-unit building with stores on the ground floor, like the one filed at 5606 18th Avenue, also needs the commercial and residential doors kept separate from day one. The residential panel should call only apartments; any store or office station should call only that business and release only its door; and the power supplies should be on the owner's circuit, not on a commercial tenant's meter that may be shut off when the space is between tenants.

First Buzzers

The Key Out the Window, the Walk Downstairs, or a Release: Choosing a First Buzzer for a Mapleton House

Most Mapleton houses have never had a buzzer that opens the door. They have a doorbell, and the household walks to the door. In a house where one family lives across two floors, that is often fine. In a house with a second household upstairs, it means the tenant either comes down every time a guest arrives, or leaves the street door unlocked, or does what Brooklyn renters have done for generations and drops a key from the window. Brownstoner's 2007 thread on buzzers for brownstone double doors captured all three habits. One resident asked, "You don't want to just toss the key out the window?" and noted it was common where they lived. Another said, "We chose not to do the buzzer option for safety reasons," preferring that residents open the door in person.

Both are reasonable instincts, and neither is a reason to skip a proper system in a two-family house. A key thrown from a window can be picked up by anyone, a door left on the latch is open to every visitor, and walking down only works while the person walking down is able and at home. A door station with two-way voice or video lets each household see or hear who is there before deciding anything, and the release opens the door for the length of time it takes to push it, then the door locks again behind the visitor.

The safety question is really about how the system is used, and we plan for that. Each household gets its own button, so nobody is buzzing in a stranger who rang the wrong floor. Video lets a household see the porch, and an audio station still lets them speak to the caller first. The release time is short, so the door is not left unlatched for long. And the closer is set so that the door shuts and latches on its own after every visitor, because a release is pointless on a door that does not relatch.

Porch, front door or gate: where the station goes on a Mapleton house

The porches that came with so many Mapleton houses change the placement decision. On an open porch, the front door is the door that locks, and the station goes beside it, out of the rain, at a height a visitor faces naturally. On a porch that was closed in with windows long ago, the porch door has become the outer door, and the station usually belongs at the porch door, with the release on it, while the original front door behind it stays on a key for the owner's household. If the house has a fenced yard and a gate that stays locked, the station may need to go at the gate, which brings its own questions of cable, power and weather.

The old doorbell pair: what it can and cannot do

Almost every Mapleton house has a doorbell pair from the porch to a chime and a transformer. It was never meant for voice or video, and a plain chime circuit cannot be turned into an intercom by changing the button. What the pair can sometimes do is carry a modern two-wire system, which puts voice, video, power and the release signal on one pair. Aiphone describes its GT Series as working for "new builds and retrofit projects" with the advantage of "using existing wire and infrastructure," and Aiphone's upgrade guidance puts it simply: "If the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused." So the pair gets tested end to end before anyone promises it will work, and any replacement cable is named on the price.

Installation Day

Eleven Apartments Done, One Door Closed: How We Run an Installation When Not Every Tenant Is Ready

A system that needs a station in every apartment needs entry to every apartment, and in any building with tenants that is where schedules slip. A Brownstoner thread from September 2009 described what can happen. A Park Slope landlord had replaced the intercoms in 11 of a building's 12 apartments, but the twelfth tenant refused to let the electrician in. Three months later the job was still unfinished, and tenants had started propping the lobby door open for deliveries, which the poster called "not safe." Asked whether the electrician could simply work around the missing unit, another forum member replied, "Yes, the one station can just be bypassed."

That reply is correct about the wiring, and it points to how we plan the work in a Mapleton three- or four-family or a small building. The common parts go in first: the door station, the strike, the closer, the power supply and the cable to each apartment's door. Each apartment station is then connected and tested as the tenant is home. A unit that cannot be reached on the day is left out of the circuit cleanly, so the rest of the building has a working system and the street door locks normally, and the missing station is installed on a second visit. The Rules

What New York Expects of a Mapleton Entry Door, From a Two-Family House to a Twelve-Unit Building

Mapleton's buildings run from single-family houses that no entry rule touches to new buildings that the intercom law covers in full, so the rules land differently from door to door. The eight-apartment rule in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a is set out in the new-buildings section above; older buildings of that size must also install talk and release when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask in writing, with work started within 90 days and finished within six months. The rest applies more widely. None of it is legal advice, and an owner with a violation or a tenant dispute should speak to a lawyer or the city.

A working intercom is part of habitability. Habitat magazine's February 2024 article, drawing on a New York Times "Ask Real Estate" column, reported that the housing maintenance code requires a functioning intercom and that courts have found failing to maintain one breaches the warranty of habitability. Nancy Kourland of the Lasser Law Group put it plainly in that piece: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The article listed the steps a resident can take: a written demand, a complaint to 311 and an HP proceeding in Housing Court.

An unlocked door can become the landlord's problem. Brick Underground reported that the New York Attorney General's tenants' rights guide says a landlord could be held liable if a crime occurs because a broken buzzer left the door unlocked. For a Mapleton owner renting the upstairs of a two-family, that is a reason to fix or replace a failed release quickly, not a reason to remove it.

Everyone must be able to leave. Any door with a buzzer still has to open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. With an electric strike that is automatic; Allegion's guide explains that with a strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. Magnetic locks hold the whole door shut, so the building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT" 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, release on loss of power or fire alarm activation, and UL 294 listed hardware. On a Mapleton house or small building we use strikes almost without exception.

Buttons within reach. On public and commercial doors, buttons, keypads and readers stay within the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range: no higher than 48 inches and no lower than 15 inches above the floor for a forward reach. We use the same range on houses, because a station an older resident or a child cannot reach is a station that does not get used.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Mapleton: Questions Answered

Questions Mapleton owners, developers, tenants and businesses bring to us, together with ones raised in Brownstoner forum threads, Brick Underground and Habitat magazine, answered for the houses and buildings here.

Houses built for one household

Our porch was closed in with windows long before we bought the house, so visitors now knock at the porch door and the old front door is inside. Which door should the new buzzer release?

Usually the porch door, because it is now the house's outer door and the one a visitor reaches first. The station goes beside the porch door, the strike goes in the porch door's frame, and the original front door behind it stays on a key. That keeps the release on the door a stranger would try, and it leaves the owner's household with a second locked door between the porch and the rooms.

If the porch door is a light storm door whose thin frame will not hold a strike, the release stays on the original front door instead. We look at both frames before deciding.

The upstairs tenant in our two-family has her own electric meter. Whose power should the new buzzer run on?

The owner's or the house's, never the tenant's. A buzzer whose power supply is plugged into the tenant's outlet goes dead when her breaker trips, when she is between leases and the account is closed, or when she is away and switches things off. The supply belongs on a circuit the owner controls, usually in the cellar near the owner's panel.

New buildings on old lots

We are putting up eight apartments where a single townhouse used to stand. Does reaching eight units really put our building under the intercom law, and what does the system have to do?

On our reading, yes. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies to class A multiple dwellings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968, and a new building is built after that date. The main entrance door must be self-locking, and the system must let residents talk with a visitor at that door and release it from their apartments. Your architect or expediter should confirm how the building is classified, and we work from that.

The law sets a minimum, not a design. It does not require video or phone answering. It does mean every apartment needs an answering point that can talk and release, so the cable or the network to each apartment has to be planned during construction, not after the tenants arrive.

Our new building has only three apartments, so the law does not make us install an intercom. What is the least we should put in so tenants do not end up propping the door?

A three-button audio station at the main door, an answering station with a release key in each apartment, a fail-secure strike in the frame, a closer that shuts and latches the door, and a battery-backed power supply on the house circuit. That is the system that lets each household let in its own guests, and it sits at the low end of our Brooklyn list for a small building.

If the budget allows, run cable to each apartment that can carry video later, even if you install audio now. The cable costs little with the walls open, and it lets a future owner upgrade without opening finished walls.

Our general contractor says the electrician will "take care of the intercom." What should we check before we accept that?

Ask four things in writing: which system will be installed, who supplies and programs it, who tests every apartment from the street door, and who receives the wiring record and any administrator login at the end. An electrician can pull cable very well and still leave a building with a panel nobody has programmed or a strike that was never wired. If the answers are vague, the entry system will be the last unfinished item on the punch list.

Owners, tenants and timing

We are renting out our second floor for the first time. Does the new tenant need a buzzer before moving in, or can that wait?

A two-family house is far below the eight-apartment line in §50-a, so no part of that law requires one, and we are not aware of a rule that makes an owner add a talk-and-release system to a two-family. The practical case is strong anyway. Without one, every guest and delivery means the tenant walking down or the door being left unlocked, and Brick Underground reported that a landlord could be held liable if a crime occurs because a broken buzzer left a door unlocked.

Our upstairs tenant gives notice next month. Is the gap before the next tenant the right time to put in the buzzer, and does it change the price?

It is usually the best time. With the apartment empty, the technician can route cable, mount the station and test it without arranging access around someone's work schedule, and any cable that has to run through the upstairs rooms can go in before the new tenant's furniture does. The owner's floor still needs one visit for its own station.

Stores and mixed buildings

Our store rents the ground floor of a one-story building on 18th Avenue, and there is no cellar we can reach from our space. How would a back-door buzzer be powered and wired?

From inside your own space. The power supply mounts on a wall or in a ceiling near the back door, on an outlet on your store's circuit, and the cable runs along the wall or above a hung ceiling to the station at the door and to the answering point at your counter. No cellar is needed. If there is no outlet near the back door, an electrician adds one, which is on the written price.

The ground-floor commercial space in our new mixed-use building has no tenant yet. Should its door be prepared for a buzzer now?

Yes, prepare it, but do not install the station. Run a conduit with a pull string from a ceiling or utility space to the commercial door's frame, order the door and frame with a strike preparation, and leave a labeled outlet location for a power supply on the commercial meter. When a tenant arrives, their buzzer goes in in a few hours without cutting into a finished storefront.

Do not connect that door to the residential panel, even temporarily.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Mapleton

Search for buzzer installation and you get confident summaries assembled from national cost guides, vendor blogs and old forum threads. Some of it does not fit Mapleton. Below are seven common claims, where they come from and what the field shows. We have not quoted any AI-generated answer word for word; the claims come from the published guides and threads those answers draw on.

"Every New York apartment building has to have an intercom"

Summaries of the New York City rule often drop its limits. Brick Underground's guide and ButterflyMX's guide both tie the requirement to buildings of at least eight apartments, and Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a applies it to class A buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968, with older ones required to install when a majority of tenants ask in writing.

In Mapleton that limit decides most cases. The one- and two-family houses that fill the side streets, the three-apartment buildings on 18th Avenue and new three-unit buildings like those filed at 986 Dahill Road and 2001 53rd Street are all below eight. A new eight-unit building like the one filed at 1835 51st Street, or the twelve-unit building at 5606 18th Avenue, is above it.

Below the line, an entry system is still the practical answer to propped doors and keys thrown from windows; it just is not a legal requirement.

"Video intercoms run about $500 per apartment"

Brick Underground's 2014 guide repeated a New York Times figure of around $500 per apartment for video intercoms, and in the same piece a security company representative estimated repairs or installation at $2,000 to $50,000 depending on the work.

The Times figure was about large buildings, where the cost of a lobby panel is spread over many apartments. A Mapleton two-family has one door station, one strike, one closer and one power supply shared by two households, so a per-apartment number from a large building says little about it. On our Brooklyn list, a video door station with monitors or phone answering for a house or small building falls in the $700 to $1,800 range.

The useful question is what the door needs, not the cost per apartment.

"Budget $1,000 to $5,000 for each entry door"

Swiftlane's cost guide gives "$1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door for modern IP or video systems," and HomeAdvisor's guide puts the national average installation at about $2,500 within a range of $1,500 to $15,000.

Those figures describe networked lobby panels and whole-house systems across the country. An audio buzzer with a new release on a Mapleton one- or two-family house sits at $400 to $900 on our Brooklyn list, well below that per-door figure. The building range, from about $2,500 to past $10,000, is where a new eight- or twelve-unit building with a directory panel and a station or app account in every apartment usually lands.

National averages also miss what moves a Mapleton price: the old doorbell pair, a separate side door, and whether the walls are still open.

"App intercoms cost nothing after installation"

Some vendor material leads with hardware prices and mentions subscriptions later, if at all.

Swiftlane's own guide says software subscriptions "commonly add $5 to $15 per unit per month, or $30 to $100 per door per month." On a new twelve-unit Mapleton building at $5 to $15 a unit, that is $60 to $180 a month, every month, for as long as the service runs. On a two-family house it is smaller but still permanent.

A wired audio or two-wire video system has no monthly fee at all, and that is the default we quote. Where an owner wants app answering, we name any subscription, who pays it and who owns the account on the written price.

"A Wi-Fi relay makes any old buzzer smart"

A Brownstoner reply to a three-family owner explained that Wi-Fi relays can be added to existing systems at the latch contacts, matched to the system's voltage, and warned that "the hard part will be programming the system to your smart phones."

The bigger problem in Mapleton comes first: a relay can only trigger a release that exists. Most of the houses here have a doorbell and a chime, not a door release, so there is nothing for a relay to operate. A relay also adds no button for a second household, no talk-back and no closer.

Where a house has only a doorbell, a relay is the last piece of a real system, not a substitute for one.

"Anyone can install a hardwired buzzer system"

Kits are sold online, and forum threads swap wiring diagrams. The same Brownstoner thread drew the opposite answer from a home inspector: "Hardwired systems must be installed by a low voltage electrician."

The truth sits between the two. A battery doorbell that only rings a chime is a homeowner project. A system that releases a street door shared by two or more households, draws power from a house circuit and puts cable through a cellar and walls is low-voltage work for a licensed installer, and any new 120-volt outlet is work for a licensed electrician. We do the low-voltage part under NYS license #12000287431, and the price tells you if an electrician is needed.

Brick Underground's repair guide gives the same advice from the other side: do not work on the electrical wiring yourself.

"A small building's intercom is replaced in a day"

Brick Underground quoted Daniel Vasquez estimating that a five-apartment building takes about a day, and Pete Carmello estimating about two days for 100 units.

The installation day is often that short. What takes longer is everything around it: entry to every apartment, a landlord's approval, an electrician's outlet, or a new building's walls and doors. FAQ

Mapleton Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Our 1920s house still has its original wooden front door. Can it take an electric strike, or does the door have to be replaced?
+

Usually it can stay. The strike goes into the door frame on the latch side, not into the door, so what matters is whether the frame is solid enough to cut and whether the latch lines up with it. We check both at the walkthrough. If the frame is split or the door has sagged, the hinges and frame are repaired or reinforced first, and that is named on the written price.

Can the upstairs household's answering station go on the landing outside their apartment door instead of inside the apartment?
+

It can, but we rarely recommend it. A station on a shared landing can be used by anyone on the stair, and the household cannot answer it from inside. The usual place is just inside the apartment's entry door, where the household can answer without opening its own door. If cable is hard to get inside, phone answering with the wired station inside is the better compromise.

We only want the front door done now, but the tenant may want a station at the side door next year. What should be done today to make that easy?
+

Choose a system that can take a second door station, and while the cellar is open for the front-door cable, pull a spare cable to a point near the side door and label it. That adds little to today's job. Next year the side station, its strike and its connection go in without new cable through the house.

If the old porch doorbell pair fails its test, how does a new cable get from the front door down to the cellar?
+

Usually straight down through the floor beside the door frame into the cellar below, or along the foundation from the side of the porch if the porch sits on its own footings. Inside the cellar it runs along the joists to the power supply and then up to each household. We choose the route at the walkthrough and put it on the written price.

How far ahead of the first tenants moving in should a developer bring us in on a new Mapleton building?
+

Before the walls close, which on a three-story building usually means at framing or electrical rough-in. That is when station locations are marked, cable is pulled to each apartment and the entrance door is ordered with a strike preparation.
