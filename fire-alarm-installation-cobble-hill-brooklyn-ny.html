The Neighborhood

Why Cobble Hill Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Cobble Hill is about forty blocks between Atlantic Avenue, Court Street, Degraw Street and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, and almost all of it is old. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Cobble Hill Historic District in December 1969 and extended it in June 1988. The district counts 796 contributing buildings, mostly homes, put up between the 1830s and the 1920s in the Greek Revival, Italianate and Queen Anne styles, with a run of early twentieth-century apartment buildings among them. Those dates matter for fire protection. Not one of these houses was built with a wired smoke alarm, a carbon monoxide alarm or a gas detector, so every device in the neighborhood was added later, by someone, with varying care. In houses this age it is normal to find several generations of alarms side by side: an old unit by the parlor stair, a newer combination unit screwed into a hallway ceiling, and a sealed ten-year alarm still in its box in a kitchen drawer, none of them linked to the others.

The second reason is the way fire behaves in these buildings. Row houses here share party walls, have wood joists under plaster ceilings, and often have hollow spaces between floors, behind lath and above the top-floor ceiling. Two fires near the neighborhood show how that hides a fire from the room it threatens. In June 2017 a fire on Court Street at Baltic Street burned inside the wall cavities of a burger restaurant, and an FDNY spokesman told Patch, "There are voids inside the walls — you have insulation and wires in there." In March 2022 a five-alarm fire at 96 Boerum Place, at the neighborhood's eastern edge, started on a second floor and filled the space between the roof and the top floor; the acting chief of department said of the foam insulation under the roof, "Once that starts burning, it's hard to extinguish." A detection plan for an old Cobble Hill building has to account for smoke that shows up late, from the wrong direction, through a stair well or a ceiling crack rather than from the room where it began.

The third reason is the rulebook, which changes on January 1, 2027. HPD already requires smoke detectors in every apartment of a multiple dwelling, within fifteen feet of each bedroom door, and carbon monoxide alarms wherever a fossil-fuel appliance is in the building. From the first day of 2027, owners of multiple dwellings and of rented one- and two-family homes with gas piping must also provide natural gas detectors in the area of each gas-burning appliance. Cobble Hill is full of exactly the buildings this reaches: brownstones with a garden rental, three-family houses with a gas range on every floor, walk-ups with a boiler in the cellar. In April 2023 News 12 reported tenants in a Cobble Hill building saying they had experienced high levels of carbon monoxide inside their apartments for months and that their calls had gone unanswered. Detection is what turns a vague complaint into a dated alarm event an owner can act on.

The last reason is renovation. Cobble Hill houses change hands and get opened up, and every gut renovation is a chance to put linked, hardwired alarms where the code expects them, inside each bedroom, outside each sleeping area and on every story, before the plaster goes back. It is also when owners discover that drawings can call for more linked alarms than a single product line can connect, or that a careful plan for a parlor floor runs straight into a ceiling medallion. An open-wall phase is also the cheapest moment to pair alarm work with an intercom or access-control upgrade at the street door, since both run cable along the same stair.

Six Cobble Hill facts that come up when we plan detection

  • Old by design. The historic district's 796 contributing buildings date from the 1830s to the 1920s, so every alarm, detector and panel in them is a retrofit into plaster, brick and wood framing.
  • Two filings, not one, for some buildings. The FDNY's own checklist for fire alarm plan approval lists "landmark certification where required" among the documents a filing can need, which is why we ask early whether exterior work is involved.
  • Courtyard housing. The Home Buildings (1876–77) and the Tower Buildings with their cottages (1878–79) along Hicks Street held 218 rental apartments when built and 188 after the 1978–1986 restoration, reached by exterior stairs and balconies around large courtyards rather than interior halls.
  • Conversions. St. Peter's church and school on Hicks Street became condominiums, and the Strong Place Baptist Church became apartments in 2010. Former sanctuaries mean very tall ceilings over people's bedrooms.
  • A new mixed building. On the former Long Island College Hospital campus, 354 Hicks Street is planned at 20 stories, with 34 condominiums above five floors of medical space and an attended garage, completion anticipated in 2027.
  • A firehouse next door, not inside. The closest FDNY company, Engine 224, works out of a 1903 firehouse at 274 Hicks Street between Joralemon and State Streets, in Brooklyn Heights just north of Atlantic Avenue.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Cobble Hill's Architecture

Six building types make up nearly all of Cobble Hill, and each one changes where alarms can go, how cable reaches them, and which rules apply.

One-family row houses

Greek Revival and Italianate houses from the 1830s onward, three or four stories over a garden floor and a cellar, with plaster cornices, medallions and a single stair rising through the middle. Linked alarms are the whole job, and the route for cable is the whole problem.

Two- and three-family brownstones

The same houses split into an owner's duplex and one or two rentals, or rented floor by floor. HPD's rules for tenants apply, carbon monoxide follows the gas appliances, and from 2027 so do natural gas detectors.

Early apartment buildings

Low-rise walk-ups from before the 1920s with several apartments off one stair, a boiler in the cellar and a mix of detector ages from decades of turnovers. Hall and stair detection, in-unit compliance and records are the usual scope.

Courtyard housing

The Home and Tower Buildings on Hicks Street, with open stairs and balconies instead of interior halls, and the 34 brick cottages of the gated Warren Place mews. Detection has to work where there is no corridor to put it in.

Church and school conversions

Condominiums made from St. Peter's church and school on Hicks Street and apartments made from the Strong Place Baptist Church. Sanctuary volumes, thick masonry and unusual stairs mean the alarm plan cannot be copied from an ordinary apartment.

New buildings on the hospital campus

Condominiums and townhouses replacing Long Island College Hospital, from the Polhemus Residences at 100 Amity Street to 354 Hicks Street, with building-wide systems designed and filed by others and in-unit protection that owners still have to look after.

One-family row houses: plaster ornament, one stair, and nowhere obvious to hide a wire

The code language owners hear from their architects is simple. As quoted by a contributor on a Brownstoner forum thread about a single-family brownstone remodel, NYC section 907.2.11.1 calls for smoke alarms outside sleeping rooms, inside each sleeping room and on each story. In a Cobble Hill row house that usually means an alarm in every bedroom, one in each hall that serves bedrooms, one on the parlor floor, one on the garden floor and one in the cellar, with carbon monoxide alarms near the sleeping areas and near the boiler. The count climbs fast in a house with five or six bedrooms. The harder question is the route. A one-family house here typically has one main stair running from the garden floor to the top, plaster on wood lath, and ceilings with cornices that cannot be cut.

We solve the route from the top down when we can. The top-floor ceiling often has an accessible space above it, reached from a roof hatch, which lets cable drop into each top-floor room through a small hole centered in a plain stretch of ceiling. Below that, closets stacked floor over floor give a vertical chase that never shows. Old dumbwaiter shafts, abandoned gas-light pipes and the cavity beside the chimney breast sometimes help too, but we check each one before using it, because a chimney cavity can be hot and a gas pipe may still be live. Where none of these exist, a wireless-linked set of alarms is the honest answer: each unit is mounted on a plain area of ceiling, the units talk to each other by radio, and nothing in the plaster is opened at all. We tell owners which floors can go hardwired and which should stay wireless, and we price both.

Ornament decides placement inside a room. Smoke rises to the ceiling and spreads, and an alarm tucked into the corner where a deep cornice meets the ceiling sits in air that moves slowly. We keep alarms on the flat field of the ceiling, clear of the cornice and of the medallion, and away from the paths of ceiling fans and supply registers, following the product's own spacing instructions. If the only flat area is the medallion itself, we look for another spot in the room rather than drill it.

Two- and three-family brownstones: an owner downstairs, tenants upstairs, gas on every floor

When a brownstone holds more than one household, the rules that apply depend on how many. HPD's detector page treats buildings of three or more apartments as multiple dwellings: each unit needs at least one approved smoke detector within fifteen feet of the main entrance to each sleeping room, carbon monoxide detection in every unit when the building has a fossil-fuel appliance, notices posted in the entrance, and records of install and expiration dates. A rented one- or two-family home that the owner does not live in needs carbon monoxide detection wherever fuel burns and, from January 1, 2027, natural gas detection wherever gas piping serves an appliance. A two-family where the owner lives on the parlor and garden floors and rents the top is the common Cobble Hill case, and it sits between the categories, which is why we ask about occupancy before we count devices.

In practice a three-family brownstone with a gas range in each kitchen and a gas boiler in the cellar ends up with smoke alarms in each apartment's bedrooms and halls, carbon monoxide alarms in each unit and near the boiler, gas detectors in each kitchen and the boiler room after 2027, and a decision about linking. Linking every unit can turn one tenant's burnt toast into a building-wide alarm, so we usually link alarms within each household and give the shared stair its own detection, rather than tie three apartments into one noisy chain. Every apartment gets a dated record and testing instructions, since HPD expects tenants to test monthly and never paint over a detector.

Early apartment buildings: one stair, many households, decades of mixed detectors

Wikipedia's history of the neighborhood notes that Cobble Hill's low-rise apartment buildings mostly predate the 1920s, and the historic district's designation covers early twentieth-century apartment houses alongside the row houses. These buildings usually have one stair serving every apartment, a cellar with the boiler and meters, and smoke detectors installed apartment by apartment as tenants turned over. The result is a building where no two apartments have the same alarm, some are well past their life, and the owner has no record of which is which. That record is exactly what HPD asks owners to keep: the date each device was installed, by unit, and its manufacturer's expiration date.

Our scope in these buildings usually has three parts. Inside each apartment we bring the alarms up to the rule and record them. In the stair and halls we add detection that gives every household early warning of smoke climbing the one way out, sized so it does not turn into a filed building system unless the owner wants or needs one. In the cellar we place carbon monoxide detection away from the boiler's immediate exhaust, since the FDNY notes that a CO detector placed too close to a fuel-burning appliance is a common cause of unwarranted alarms. Where the building already has a panel, we test it first and report what works before recommending anything new.

Courtyard housing: no hallway, so the alarms have to go where people actually walk

Alfred Tredway White's Home Buildings at Hicks and Baltic Streets, completed in 1876 and 1877, and the Tower Buildings and cottages that followed in 1878 and 1879, were built with exterior staircases and balconies around large courtyards, according to the neighborhood history site cobblehill.nyc. After a 1975 purchase and a restoration that ran from 1978 to 1986, the original 218 rental apartments became 188, and a condominium conversion began in 2008. Every apartment opens onto air rather than a corridor, so the usual idea of "an alarm in the hall outside the bedrooms" has to be translated into the apartment's own entry area, and the open stairs cannot hold a smoke detector at all.

The Warren Place mews, 34 brick cottages from the 1870s facing a private courtyard between Warren and Baltic Streets behind one gate, raises a different issue: small two-story homes, built close together, where the main risk is that nobody notices a fire in a cottage whose owner is away. Here the work is mostly linked alarms inside each cottage and, for owners who want it, central-station monitoring so that an alarm in an empty house reaches someone. In both places, anything that would mount on the outside of a building inside the historic district is a Landmarks question first, and we plan to keep everything indoors.

Church and school conversions: thirty-foot ceilings over bedrooms and walls a drill bit dreads

St. Peter's church on Hicks Street, built in 1860 with its school, was turned into condominiums, and the Strong Place Baptist Church, built in 1851 and 1852 to a design by Minard Lafever, became apartments in 2010. In conversions like these, some apartments sit under the old sanctuary roof, with ceilings far higher than any row house and lofts or mezzanines open to the space below. A smoke alarm mounted at the top of a tall, peaked ceiling can sit above a layer of warm air that keeps smoke from reaching it early, while one mounted low on a wall may sit below the smoke layer. We follow the product's instructions for sloped and high ceilings, add alarms at the open loft edge where people sleep, and treat each level of a duplex as its own story.

The structure is the other challenge. Church walls are often solid masonry several feet thick, and the original timber trusses may be exposed and finished as a feature. Cable cannot be fished through solid stone, and it should not be stapled to a beam the owners paid to restore. In these apartments we lean on wireless-linked alarms, route any cable through the new partitions the developer built, and keep each device on a surface that is not part of the original fabric. Where the building has a shared fire alarm system that belongs to the condominium, we coordinate with the board or managing agent before touching anything tied to it.

New buildings on the hospital campus: the building's system and the owner's alarms are two different things

Long Island College Hospital closed in 2014, and its campus around Henry, Hicks, Amity and Pacific Streets is being rebuilt as condominiums. 6sqft reported in 2018 on the first phase, the Polhemus Residences at 100 Amity Street with townhouses at 88 to 98 Amity Street, and on 5 River Park at 347 Henry Street with 25 units. CityRealty reported in 2025 that 354 Hicks Street, bought from the original developer in 2022, is planned at 20 stories with 34 condominiums above five floors of medical community-facility space and an attended garage. Buildings of this size have engineered fire alarm systems designed by a registered design professional, filed with the FDNY and maintained by the building.

That leaves owners with a narrower but real set of questions: what in the apartment belongs to them, whether in-unit smoke and CO alarms are tied to the building's system or stand alone, and what happens when a renovation moves a bedroom wall. We do not alter a building's filed system without the board's or managing agent's written approval and the building's own fire alarm company in the loop. What we can do is test the alarms inside an apartment, replace stand-alone units that are past their life, add alarms when a renovation creates a new sleeping room, and give the owner a record that matches what is on the ceiling.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Ceiling Before You Read the Drawings

The first thing we look at in an old Cobble Hill house is not the floor plan. It is the ceiling of every room, from the cellar up. Drawings tell you where the bedrooms are. Ceilings tell you where an alarm can actually go and how cable could reach it. On the top floor we check for a roof hatch and whether the space above the ceiling is reachable. On each floor below we look for a plain run of ceiling away from cornices, medallions, fans and registers, and we note which closets stack on top of each other, because a stack of closets is the cleanest cable chase in a row house.

Then we stand at the top of the stair and ask a simple question: if a fire started in the cellar tonight, what would the people in the top bedrooms hear, with their doors shut? Most of the decisions follow from that answer. Which alarms must be linked. Whether the garden-floor tenant's alarms should join the owner's chain. Where carbon monoxide belongs relative to the boiler. Whether a skylight over the stair changes where the top-floor alarm should sit.

January 1, 2027

Gas Detectors in Cobble Hill Rentals From January 1, 2027: Who Needs Them and Where They Go

HPD's June 2026 bulletin to property owners states the rule plainly: under Local Law 102 of 2025, owners of multiple dwellings and of one- and two-family homes that the owner does not live in must provide, "by January 1, 2027, at least one approved and operational natural gas detector" where the building has gas piping. HPD's detector page adds where they go: in the area of each gas-burning appliance, and within fifteen feet of a sleeping room only if a gas appliance is in that room. Buildings with no gas piping at all are exempt from the gas detector and its notice. The same bulletin reminds owners that "Gas leaks are extremely dangerous and can create fires and explosions," and that the separate Suspected Gas Leaks Notice must be posted with the utilities' emergency numbers.

For Cobble Hill that rule reaches far more buildings than people expect. A brownstone where the owner lives on the bottom two floors and rents the top two is a two-family with an owner in residence, but a brownstone rented whole to one family while the owners live elsewhere is not, and neither is a three-family. In most of these houses the gas appliances are a range in each kitchen, a boiler and often a water heater in the cellar, and sometimes a gas dryer. Each of those areas needs a detector. A combination device that detects smoke, carbon monoxide and gas in one housing is allowed, as long as each of its alarms still sits where the rules want it, which in a narrow row-house kitchen is not always the same spot.

The money side is also set by HPD. In a Class A multiple dwelling, an owner may collect $25 from the occupant for each single-function smoke, CO or gas detector, $50 for a combined two-function device and $75 for a combined three-function device, and the occupant has a year to pay. In a rented one- or two-family house the $25 charge applies to CO and gas detectors. Owners must give at least one adult in each unit written notice about testing and maintenance, post the required notices, and keep the dates of installation and expiration. We leave each of these in writing at the end of the job, apartment by apartment.

  • What to send us now: a photo of each gas appliance, the number of apartments, whether the owner lives in the building, and a photo of one existing alarm label.
  • What we bring back: a device count by apartment and room, a written price on our Brooklyn list, and a date we can install before January 1.
  • What stays with you: the notice text, the install and expiration dates for every device, and testing instructions for each household.
What Local Fires Show

Court Street, Boerum Place, Warren Street, Baltic Street: What Fires Near Cobble Hill Teach a Detection Plan

The fires below were reported in or at the edge of Cobble Hill. We cite them for what they show about where smoke and heat travel in these buildings, not to judge anyone's alarms. None of the reports we cite say whether alarms sounded, so we do not claim that they did or did not.

Inside the walls on Court Street, June 2017. Patch reported that a fire broke out inside the wall cavities of a burger restaurant at Court and Baltic Streets around 2:30 in the afternoon, drew at least 60 firefighters and paramedics, and was under control in about 45 minutes with no injuries. An FDNY spokesman said an electrical wire inside the wall could have sparked it: "There are voids inside the walls — you have insulation and wires in there." The owner told Patch afterward that the damage was limited to the wall adjoining the hallway. The lesson for an old building is that a fire in a wall cavity can grow out of sight of a ceiling alarm in the room. Detection in the spaces that cavity connects to, the hall, the floor above, the top of the stair, is what gives earlier warning.

Above the top floor on Boerum Place, March 2022. 1010 WINS reported a five-alarm fire at 96 Boerum Place near Pacific Street, a two-story mixed-use building with a gym in the former New York Sports Club space and a tennis court on its roof. Twelve people were hurt, nine of them firefighters, and more than 200 firefighters and EMS workers responded. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that the fire began on the second floor and moved into the space between the roof and the top floor, that foam insulation under the roof made it hard to put out, and that water pressure dropped early until the Department of Environmental Protection raised it. Apartment residents were evacuated. For a building with a hung ceiling below the roof, the question is whether anything watches that hidden space; heat detection there is a design decision, not an afterthought.

A roof on Warren Street, August 2009. Brownstoner readers reported a morning fire on the roof of new townhouses on Warren Street between Smith and Court Streets, a roof furnished with tables, chairs, umbrellas, awnings and grills. No indoor smoke alarm sees a fire on a roof deck until smoke finds its way down through a hatch or bulkhead door, which is a reason to keep a working alarm at the top of the stair below any roof access.

A fourth floor on Baltic Street, August 2023. News 12 reported a fire that started around 1 a.m. on the fourth floor of an apartment building on Baltic Street, injuring two firefighters and one resident; the report did not give the address or the cause. A fire at that hour, on an upper floor, is the case linked alarms exist for: everyone else in the building is asleep behind closed doors, and the alarm in the room of origin has to be heard floors away.

Carbon monoxide complaints, April 2023. News 12 reported that tenants of a Cobble Hill building said they had experienced high levels of carbon monoxide in their apartments for months and that their calls had gone unanswered. Carbon monoxide alarms do not fix a boiler or a flue, but they do create a dated record of each event, and an alarm with a digital display shows the level that set it off. That record is what an owner, a managing agent or HPD needs to find the source.

Commercial Fire Alarm Installation

Business Fire Alarm Installation in Cobble Hill

Business work in Cobble Hill is small-scale and old-building: a shop with apartments above it, a restaurant in a ground floor that was once a parlor, an office over a storefront, a congregation in a sanctuary older than the Civil War. The corridors below are where it happens.

Court Street

The neighborhood's main shopping street, where Wikipedia describes older Italian meat markets and barber shops sitting beside newer shops and cafés, and where the 1922 South Brooklyn Savings Institution building at 130 Court Street now holds a grocery store.

Smith Street

The other main commercial street, whose northern blocks became a "Restaurant Row" in the late 1990s and 2000s and added bars late in that decade. Kitchens, late hours and apartments upstairs set the terms here.

Atlantic Avenue

The northern edge of the neighborhood, home to one of the city's largest collections of Middle Eastern shops, many of them food businesses with cooking or roasting in back rooms and cellars.

Hicks Street

Stores along the Hicks Street side of the Home and Tower Buildings, and the medical and residential mix coming to the former hospital campus at 354 Hicks Street.

Henry, Amity and Pacific Streets

The old Long Island College Hospital blocks, where NYU's freestanding Cobble Hill emergency department opened in October 2014 after the hospital closed, and where new condominium buildings now rise.

Congregations and schools

Christ Church, Old St. Paul's, the Kane Street Synagogue in the former Middle Dutch Reformed Church, the South Brooklyn Seventh-Day Adventist Church and the schools around them, each a place of assembly inside the historic district.

The common thread is mixed use. A typical Court or Smith Street building has a business on the ground floor and in the cellar, and one to three apartments above. The business may have a fire alarm system, a set of household-style alarms or nothing at all, while the apartments upstairs fall under HPD's detector rules. Those two worlds have to work together: the family on the third floor needs to know when a fire starts in a closed restaurant at 2 a.m., and the restaurant does not want its kitchen to set off the apartments every dinner rush. We plan the two halves together and write down which devices belong to the tenant business and which belong to the owner.

Kitchens and food shops call for a different device than a bedroom. A heat detector, which reacts to temperature rather than smoke particles, belongs near a fryer, a roaster or a bread oven, where a photoelectric smoke detector would alarm every hour. Cellars used for stock need detection that tolerates dust and cold. Hood suppression systems over commercial cooking lines are a separate trade, installed and serviced by a suppression contractor, and we do not service them; when a kitchen has one, we coordinate with that contractor so that their system and the building's detection are wired and tested the way the design calls for.

Compliance notes for Cobble Hill businesses and mixed-use buildings

  • Filed systems start with a design professional. The FDNY's guide to fire alarm plan approval says a required system must be designed and certified by a New York State registered Professional Engineer or Registered Architect, who files the plans and a TM-1 online; installation is then done by a New York City licensed electrician or an FDNY-approved fire alarm company.
  • Plan on months, not weeks. The same guide says initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks once everything is received, and that the whole process from submission to final inspection takes approximately twenty to twenty-four weeks.
  • Landmark paperwork can be part of the filing. The FDNY's document list for a fire alarm filing includes "landmark certification where required." For a building inside the Cobble Hill Historic District, exterior work belongs in the plan from the start.
  • Detectors are not alarms. The FDNY explains that self-contained alarms sound on their own, while "The detectors are NOT stand-alone units" and report to a panel. A business required to have detectors is prohibited from swapping them for plug-in smoke or CO alarms.
  • Carbon monoxide in commercial spaces. Since July 1, 2021, NYC requires CO detectors in listed occupancies protected by a fire alarm system that contain fuel-burning equipment, an enclosed garage or a loading dock. Placement follows the FDNY's rule or a design professional's plan.
  • False alarms cost. The FDNY states it is unlawful for a monitored system to "transmit 2 or more unnecessary or unwarranted alarms in any three-month period," which is why device choice near kitchens and roasters matters.
  • Inspection is a production. The FDNY's acceptance inspection for a filed system takes place with a certified S-97 or S-98 technician, a licensed plumber for waterflow tests where sprinklers exist, an elevator mechanic if there is an elevator, and staff who can open every space, after central-station monitoring is active.
Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Cobble Hill: Questions Answered

These are the situations Cobble Hill owners, tenants, boards and shopkeepers bring to us, grouped by the kind of building they come from.

Row houses under the 1969 district

Our parlor ceiling has a plaster medallion and a deep cornice. How close to them can a smoke alarm go and still work?

Keep the alarm on the flat part of the ceiling, away from both. The corner where a cornice meets the ceiling is a pocket of slow-moving air that smoke reaches late, and the medallion is ornament you do not want drilled. We follow the spacing in the alarm maker's instructions, which set a minimum distance from walls and corners, and we also keep the unit clear of ceiling fans and heating registers that would push smoke away from it. In a typical Cobble Hill parlor there is a plain field of ceiling between the cornice and the medallion that satisfies all of that. If a room has ornament everywhere, a wireless alarm on the most neutral spot is better than cutting into plaster that cannot be matched.

Our architect's drawings for the renovation show fifteen linked smoke and CO alarms, but we read that only twelve can be wired together. How is that handled?

That exact problem came up on a Brownstoner forum thread about a single-family brownstone remodel. Each alarm product line has a published maximum number of units that can be interconnected, and a large house can exceed it. The usual answers are a relay module that lets one group of alarms trigger a second group, a product line whose wireless or hardwired interconnect accepts more units, or a small control panel with addressable detectors for a very large house. We check the count on the drawings against the specific product's limit before anything is bought, and we test the whole chain with every bedroom door closed so we know an alarm in the cellar sounds on the top floor.

Our stair rises four floors to a skylight. Where does the top-floor alarm belong?

On the ceiling of the top-floor hall that serves the bedrooms, near the head of the stair, not up inside the skylight well. A stair is a chimney for smoke in a fire, which is exactly why the top of it needs an alarm, but the shaft under a skylight can trap hot air and the glass is no place to mount anything. We place the alarm on the flat ceiling just outside the well where smoke rolling up the stair will reach it, and we make sure it is linked to the alarms below. If the skylight has a hatch to the roof, that spot also covers smoke coming down from a roof deck.

Our certificate of occupancy says three families, but we use the house as one home. Which detector rules do we plan for?

Plan for the way the house is lived in, and settle the paperwork separately. Fire safety follows the people: alarms inside every bedroom, outside each sleeping area and on every story, linked, with carbon monoxide wherever fuel burns. HPD's rules for multiple dwellings and rentals turn on how a building is occupied and rented, and a future sale or conversion may raise the question of the legal occupancy. We note on your device list how the house was being used when we installed, and if you intend to rent a floor again, we plan the alarms so each apartment can stand on its own.

Fires you cannot see from the room

The Court Street restaurant fire burned inside the wall voids. Would a smoke alarm in the room have caught a fire hidden inside a wall?

Not necessarily early. A fire inside a wall cavity grows behind plaster until smoke finds a gap, which may be an outlet box, a ceiling crack or the floor above rather than the room in front of it. That is why the FDNY spokesman's remark about voids full of insulation and wires is useful for old Cobble Hill buildings. Good coverage is layered: alarms in each room, in the hall and at the top of the stair, linked so smoke surfacing anywhere sounds everywhere, and heat detection in hidden spaces that matter, such as the space above a top-floor ceiling. No alarm makes a wall fire visible, but linked coverage shortens the time until someone hears about it.

Apartments, condos and conversions

Our condo is in a converted church, and the bedroom loft is open to a ceiling about thirty feet up. Where do the smoke alarms go?

At the loft and at each sleeping level, not only at the peak. In a very tall space, warm air can collect high and hold smoke below it early in a fire, so an alarm at the top of the ceiling may be the last to know. We mount alarms on the ceiling or wall surfaces nearest where people sleep, following the product's rules for sloped and high ceilings, add one at the open edge of the loft, and treat the floor below as its own story. Because church walls are solid masonry, these units are often wireless-linked rather than wired. If the condominium has a building system, we check with the board before connecting anything to it.

Our apartment in the Home Buildings opens onto an outdoor stair and balcony. Where does the alarm outside the bedrooms go when there is no hallway?

Inside the apartment, in the entry or circulation space that connects the bedrooms to the door. The rule's purpose is an alarm between the sleeping rooms and the rest of the home, so we find the space inside the unit that plays that role. The open-air stair and balcony cannot hold a smoke alarm; weather, wind and cold make outdoor smoke detection unreliable. Each bedroom still gets its own alarm, carbon monoxide goes where the unit's fuel appliances are, and all of it is linked within the apartment. If the condominium has rules about work in units, we follow the board's process.

Tenants keep reporting carbon monoxide alarms in our building. As the new managing agent, what should we check first?

Start with the people and the appliances, then the alarms. Any alarm that is sounding with symptoms is a 911 call and a call to the gas utility. Once the building is safe, a licensed plumber or heating contractor should inspect the boiler, flue and gas appliances. Our part is the detection: which alarms reported, how old they are, whether any sit so close to a fuel-burning appliance that they false-alarm, which the FDNY names as a common cause, and whether units with a digital display or a peak-level memory would give the plumber better evidence. We record each alarm's location and date so that complaints can be matched to a specific apartment and time.

Shops and congregations

Our Atlantic Avenue shop roasts nuts and coffee in the back room. What detection will not trip every time the roaster runs?

Heat detection in the roasting area, with smoke detection kept in the parts of the shop where it belongs. A roaster puts out fine smoke and hot air as part of its normal work, which a photoelectric smoke detector reads as a fire. A fixed-temperature or rate-of-rise heat detector ignores that and reacts to a real flame-up. We place it to the side of the roaster's exhaust path, keep smoke detectors in the sales floor, office and storage, and, if the shop has monitoring, tune the plan so routine roasting never sends the fire department. The FDNY treats repeated unwarranted alarms from a monitored system as a violation, so this is worth getting right.

Our congregation meets in an 1850s sanctuary with a balcony and a wooden ceiling high overhead. What detection reaches up there?

Usually a combination of devices designed for tall spaces. Projected-beam smoke detectors, which send a beam across the room and react when smoke blocks it, can cover a high ceiling without a ladder to every point. Detection under the balcony and in the stairs to it protects the people seated there. Heat or smoke detection in the attic over a wooden roof, where accessible, covers the space most at risk and least visited. A sanctuary is a place of assembly, so a system here is normally designed by a registered design professional and filed with the FDNY, and inside the historic district anything outside the walls also needs Landmarks review.

Filing, timing and Landmarks

How long does it take from hiring an engineer to the FDNY's final sign-off on a small filed system?

Plan for about half a year. The FDNY's guide says initial plan review can take up to eight weeks after all forms and documents arrive, and that the whole path from submission to final inspection and approval runs approximately twenty to twenty-four weeks. Before that comes the design time, and objections add a resubmission; the guide notes no new filing fee is due if corrected plans go back within six months of the objection. Installation itself is often the shortest part. For a Court Street shop or a sanctuary, we tell owners to start the design well before any opening date.

There is no way inside to get cable from our cellar to the top floor. Can it run up the back of the house instead?

Inside the historic district, exterior work needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission's approval, even on a rear wall that nobody sees from the street. The LPC Permit Guidebook's rules for health, safety and utility equipment ask that conduit be concealed in plain masonry where possible, that visible conduit be kept to about ten inches, and that any surface conduit be painted to match and secured in mortar joints, never on decorative surfaces or through cast iron. In practice we nearly always find an interior route first, through closets, an old dumbwaiter shaft or a chase beside the chimney, or use wireless-linked alarms on the floors cable cannot reach.

Cobble Hill Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

How can we tell whether the hardwired alarms the last owner left are actually linked to each other?
+

Press and hold the test button on one alarm and have someone stand by each of the others. If they all sound, they are interconnected; if only the one you pressed sounds, they are not, or the link is broken somewhere. In an old house we also check that every unit is the same family of product, because different brands usually cannot talk to each other, and we note each alarm's manufacture date, since a linked set that is past its life should be replaced together.

We rent our whole townhouse to one family while we live abroad. Does the 2027 natural gas detector rule apply to us?
+

Yes, if the house has gas piping. HPD's rule covers one- and two-family homes that the owner does not occupy, as well as multiple dwellings, starting January 1, 2027. A detector goes in the area of each gas-burning appliance, typically the kitchen range and the cellar boiler, with the required notice and records. Carbon monoxide alarms are already required in a rented house with fuel-burning appliances. We can schedule around your tenants and send you the records by email.

Our architect is drawing our renovation now. Can you give them an alarm layout to put on the plans?
+

Yes. We walk the house, mark each smoke, carbon monoxide and gas device by floor and room with its power source, interconnect and cable route, and send it as a marked-up plan the architect can transfer to the drawings. Doing this before demolition lets the electrician set boxes in the right places and lets the plasterer know where each opening goes, and it catches cases where the device count exceeds what one product line can link.

We split part of our parlor floor with a partition to make a nursery. Does the new room need its own alarm?
+

Yes. Any room used for sleeping should have a smoke alarm inside it, and the space outside it that serves as the hall needs one too. A new partition also changes how smoke moves on that floor, so an alarm that used to cover the whole parlor may now sit on the wrong side of the wall. We add an alarm in the nursery, link it to the rest of the house and, where there is a fuel appliance nearby, add carbon monoxide protection near the room.

We have a Lutron lighting system. Can our smoke alarms switch the lights on when they sound?
+

Often, yes, in a house with household alarms. Some hardwired alarm product lines offer a relay module that closes a contact when the alarms sound, and a lighting or home automation system can use that contact to turn on stair and hall lights. We check that the relay is listed for use with your alarms and that the alarms keep working on their own if the lighting system fails. In a building with a filed fire alarm system, any such connection has to be part of the approved design.

Yes. Low-rise apartment buildings from before the 1920s are a big part of the neighborhood, and they are where HPD's multiple dwelling rules apply most directly: a smoke detector within fifteen feet of each bedroom door in every apartment, carbon monoxide detection where there is a fuel-burning appliance, gas detection from 2027, notices and records. +

Yes. Carbon monoxide alarms with a digital display show the current level in parts per million, and many also store the peak level since the last reset. That helps when tenants report alarms and the owner needs to know whether it was a brief spike or a sustained problem worth calling a heating contractor about. They cost a little more than plain alarms, and we place them near sleeping areas and set back from fuel-burning appliances.

Do the alarms you install sound different for a fire and for carbon monoxide?
+

Yes. Smoke alarms sound a repeating pattern of three beeps, and carbon monoxide alarms a repeating pattern of four, and many combination units also announce which danger they detect in a spoken message. We can sound both patterns at the end of the job so everyone in the house knows the difference, since the right response differs: a fire means getting out by the safest way, and a carbon monoxide alarm means getting everyone out into fresh air and calling 911.

What do you do with the old alarms you take down?
+

We take them with us unless you want to keep them, and we dispose of them following the manufacturers' instructions. Before removing anything we photograph and record each old unit's location and date on your device list, so you have a record of what was replaced and when. If an old alarm was part of a building system or a tenant's apartment, we leave the paperwork the owner or managing agent needs to show the change.
