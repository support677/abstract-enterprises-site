Outdoor surveillance cameras built for Manhattan winters

Outdoor cameras in Manhattan live hard lives. They sit under awnings that drip salt slush, on facades hit by wind funneling down the avenues, and on roof bulkheads that bake in July and freeze in January. The two ratings that matter on an outdoor camera are the ingress rating and the operating temperature range. IP66 means the housing is dust-tight and protected against powerful water jets; IP67 adds protection against temporary immersion. Several of our house-brand kits carry IP66 or IP67 ratings, and we list which on each package below. A camera without a stated rating stays indoors.

Weatherproof security cameras for winter also need heaters or a wide cold-rated range when they sit fully exposed, such as on a rooftop or a fire escape landing. Under an awning or a building overhang, a standard outdoor camera is usually fine. We look at the actual spot before recommending one over the other, because a rooftop camera that fogs every January morning is worse than no camera.

Indoor surveillance cameras for lobbies, halls and stores

Indoor cameras are where Manhattan buyers save money, because most indoor views do not need weather sealing or long infrared reach. A lobby, a hallway or a deli aisle is lit most of the day, and a 2MP or 5MP dome on a drop-tile ceiling will identify faces at the distances those spaces allow. Spend the money at the doors instead: the camera that faces the entrance has to handle bright daylight behind a dark figure, which is where wide dynamic range earns its keep.

Hallway, stairwell and elevator cameras

Hallway security cameras in a Manhattan building usually go at the end of the corridor, aimed back toward the elevator or stair door, so they catch faces walking toward them. A camera over a door frame looking down the hall works too, as long as it does not see into any apartment when a door is open. Stairwell security cameras belong at landings where people enter and leave, not halfway up a flight. Elevator security cameras inside the car are a job for the building's elevator contractor and its inspector, so we supply the camera and recorder side and coordinate with them.

Front door, package and lobby cameras

Front door security cameras and package theft prevention cameras are the most requested items on the island. In a doorman building the lobby camera should cover the desk, the street door and the package shelf behind the desk as three separate views; one wide lens across the lobby shows people as dots. In a walk-up, the vestibule between the street door and the inner door is the one place every person entering has to stand still, and a small dome there does more than any camera on the facade.

Doorbell security cameras are a separate decision. They are popular, but in a co-op or condo hallway they raise rules questions we cover in the apartment section below. For brownstones and townhouses with their own stoop, a doorbell camera at the door and a wired camera covering the stoop and areaway is the usual pair.

Rooftop, basement, garage and backyard cameras

Rooftop security cameras cover the bulkhead door, the roof hatch and the adjoining roofs on an attached row, which is how many walk-up break-ins begin. Basement security cameras watch the boiler room door, storage cages, the bike room and the sidewalk cellar hatch. Garage security cameras and parking garage security cameras cover the ramp, the pay station and the elevator lobby at garage level, where residents come and go without passing the doorman. Backyard and driveway security cameras matter mostly on townhouses, the few Manhattan houses with a garden, and the rear yards behind restaurants.

Resolution is the spec most buyers ask about first, and the one that is easiest to overspend on. Megapixels decide how much detail lands on a face or a hand at a given distance. When 4K security cameras are worth it

4K CCTV cameras earn their place on wide views where you may need to zoom in later: a storefront that faces both an avenue and a side street, a double-height lobby, a garage entrance, a long restaurant dining room. On a narrow walk-up hallway the extra pixels mostly show more wallpaper. Buying 8MP security cameras for every position also pushes up the recorder and drive size, because each 4K stream takes roughly twice the storage of a 2K stream at similar settings.

Night vision: infrared, color and starlight

Night vision security cameras use infrared LEDs to light the scene in black and white; the range quoted on our house-brand kits runs from 60 feet on the entry-level dome package to 120 feet on the 4K eyeball kits. Color night vision cameras, sometimes sold as full color or starlight cameras, use more sensitive sensors and, on our full-color packages, a dusk-to-dawn spotlight, so clothing and car colors stay visible after dark. Manhattan is rarely truly dark; street lighting means color night vision works on more facades here than in the suburbs, while infrared is still the better choice for a pitch-black cellar or bike room.

Low light, glare and backlit doors

Many lobby and storefront cameras in Manhattan face a glass door with daylight behind it. A camera without real wide dynamic range shows a silhouette. Low light security cameras and cameras with true WDR cost a little more and are the right purchase for those few positions; we flag them on the quote so you are not paying for the feature on every camera.

Choosing an NVR for a Manhattan building

Three numbers decide the recorder: how many cameras now, how many you might add, and how many days of video you must keep. Buy one size up on channels when there is any chance of adding cameras; a co-op that starts with eight cameras in the lobby and halls almost always adds the roof and cellar within two years. Then check that the recorder can decode the resolution you are buying on every channel at once, and that it has enough drive bays for the storage target. A PoE NVR with built-in ports is tidy for four to eight cameras in one closet; larger buildings do better with a separate PoE switch per riser closet feeding one recorder.

How to calculate CCTV storage: a worked estimate

Storage questions come up on almost every call, so here is the method we use, labeled clearly as an estimate. Real bitrates depend on the camera, compression (H.264, H.265 or H.265+), frame rate and how much moves in the scene, and they vary hour by hour, as the screen above shows.

  • Step 1, bitrate: take the camera's recording bitrate. For this example, assume a 4K camera recording continuously at 8 megabits per second.
  • Step 2, per hour: 8 Mb/s × 3,600 seconds = 28,800 megabits, divided by 8 = 3,600 megabytes, or about 3.6 GB per hour.
  • Step 3, per day: 3.6 GB × 24 hours = about 86 GB per camera per day.
  • Step 4, per retention period: 86 GB × 14 days = about 1.2 TB per camera for two weeks; × 30 days = about 2.6 TB for one month.
  • Step 5, whole system: four such cameras for two weeks = about 4.8 TB, so a 6TB or 8TB surveillance drive; for one month, about 10.4 TB, so 12TB to 16TB of storage.

Halve the bitrate for a 2K camera at similar settings and the numbers roughly halve. Motion-only recording, smart codecs and lower frame rates stretch it further, at the cost of gaps in the record. Security cameras with two weeks recording are the baseline we build to; security cameras with one month recording are common for co-ops and condos whose house rules set a retention period. What Is the Difference Between CCTV and IP Cameras?

CCTV, closed-circuit television, is the older umbrella term for any private camera system that records to its own recorder rather than broadcasting. In everyday use it means analog or HD-analog cameras on coax feeding a DVR. IP cameras are digital network cameras: each one is a small computer that compresses its own video and sends it over network cable to an NVR. In practice the line has blurred, and many people say CCTV for both. For a Manhattan buyer the difference is mostly about the wiring already in the walls. A prewar co-op with coax running to every hall camera can upgrade to sharp HD-analog cameras on a new DVR without opening walls; a building that is rewiring anyway is better off with IP cameras and Cat6. Apartments & Buildings

Apartment, Co-op and Condo Security Cameras in Manhattan

Most Manhattan camera buyers live in an apartment, and that shapes every choice. The first question is not which camera but whose wall it goes on. Inside your own unit you decide. In the hallway, the lobby, the roof and the cellar, the owner of the building decides: the landlord in a rental, the board in a co-op or condo, usually through its managing agent. Getting that right first saves buying a camera you are not allowed to mount.

What Security Cameras Are Best for Apartments?

For the inside of a Manhattan apartment, the best security cameras are small indoor cameras that record locally, view on a phone and come off the wall without damage when you move. A compact turret or dome on a shelf or a light-duty mount aimed at the apartment door covers the one entrance every apartment has. A second camera covering the main room catches what a door camera misses, such as a dog walker or a contractor wandering beyond the kitchen. Wired cameras are steadier, but in a rental the short cable run from a camera to a small recorder or a PoE switch is usually tucked along a baseboard rather than through the wall.

Owners of co-op and condo apartments have more freedom inside the unit and can wire properly during a renovation, when walls are open. That is the cheapest moment to run cable for cameras at the entry, a terrace door or a home office, and it avoids the stick-on look that renters live with.

Renters: inside your apartment versus the common areas

A camera inside your own rented apartment, pointed at your own space, is generally your business; check your lease for rules about drilling and restore the walls when you leave. The common areas are different. Hallways, lobbies, roofs and cellars belong to the building, and a tenant who mounts a camera there without permission can be told to take it down. In a long-running r/nyc thread from a renter whose building had rampant package theft, the practical advice was to ask the landlord first, especially before drilling in the hallway (r/nyc). A peephole-style door camera that sees only the hall immediately outside your door is the usual compromise, and even that is something to clear with management in writing.

Renters also learn the hard way that a building's cameras are not their cameras. In one r/NYCapartments post, a tenant whose package was stolen from the lobby of a building they had just moved into was told the security camera system was down (r/NYCapartments). If the building's system is unreliable, the useful move is to ask management, in writing, to repair or replace it, and to offer a written plan they can approve.

Co-op and condo boards: who decides and what they ask

Boards make the rules for common areas. Brick Underground put the question of hallway doorbell cameras to attorney Niki Khindri of Adam Leitman Bailey P.C., who said, “In some circumstances it could be illegal for owners to have video doorbells recording footage in the hallway,” and the article notes a board can adopt a ban as a house rule; it also notes a camera should never point into a neighbor's apartment (Brick Underground, June 23, 2022). So before buying a doorbell camera for a co-op hallway, read the house rules or ask the managing agent.

For building-wide systems, boards usually ask three things: who can see the footage, how long it is kept, and whether any camera sees into a unit. Rules

Security Camera Laws and Privacy Rules for Manhattan Buyers

Buying a camera is legal; where you point it and what it records are where the rules apply. Below are the sourced rules our Manhattan customers ask about most. This is general information, not legal advice; a building attorney or your own lawyer is the right person for your specific situation.

  • Audio recording. New York's eavesdropping law defines mechanical overhearing of a conversation as the intentional overhearing or recording of a conversation without the consent of at least one party, by a person not present (NYS Penal Law §250.00); §250.05 makes eavesdropping a crime. A camera microphone that records customers or neighbors talking when you are not part of the conversation is the risky case. Audio recording security cameras and two way audio security cameras leave us with audio recording turned off unless you ask otherwise.
  • Employees. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording employees in restrooms, locker rooms and rooms designated for changing clothes, as FirstService Residential's guidance notes. That applies to staff areas in stores, restaurants, gyms, offices and residential buildings alike.
  • Biometrics. Retail stores, food and drink establishments and places of entertainment in New York City that collect biometric identifier information must post signs at customer entrances (NYC Administrative Code Title 22, Chapter 12, covered above).
  • Common areas. In co-ops and condos, the board's house rules govern cameras in hallways and lobbies, and boards can restrict video doorbells (Brick Underground, cited above).
  • Neighbors' windows. Whatever the rules, aim cameras at your own door, store or property, not into a neighbor's window or apartment. It is the simplest way to stay on the right side of both the law and the building.

CCTV regulations for businesses in Manhattan are mostly about these same points: no hidden recording where people expect privacy, no audio without care, signs where biometric tools are used, and footage handled by a small number of named people. A clear, visible camera with a posted notice is both the lawful choice and the better deterrent.

Cybersecurity & Phone Viewing

Cybersecure Cameras, Hacking Prevention and Viewing on Your Phone

Every network camera is a small computer on the internet, and in a city where a single apartment building can carry hundreds of connected devices, security cameras need the same care as a laptop. Most camera breaches are not clever. They come from default passwords, old firmware and recorders opened straight to the internet with a port forward.

How Do You Prevent Security Camera Hacking?

  • Change every default password on the recorder, every camera and the app, and use a different one for each viewer.
  • Give each person their own account with only the rights they need: live view for the doorman desk, playback for the managing agent, full control for the owner.
  • Keep firmware current on the recorder and cameras; we check and update it at installation and when a system is serviced.
  • Do not open ports on the router for remote viewing; use the manufacturer's encrypted app connection instead.
  • Put the cameras on their own network where possible, separate from the office or apartment WiFi, so a compromised laptop cannot reach the recorder and the other way round.
  • Turn on two-factor sign-in in the app where the brand supports it, and remove accounts when staff or residents leave.

Encrypted security cameras and cybersecure security cameras are worth buying, but only on the strength of what the spec sheet says, not the box. We point out which models document encrypted streams or encrypted cloud connections when we send the quote.

NDAA compliant security cameras

Section 889 of the 2019 National Defense Authorization Act, implemented in FAR 52.204-25, bars federal contractors from using video surveillance equipment produced by Hytera, Hikvision or Dahua, including their subsidiaries and affiliates (FAR 52.204-25). Private Manhattan buyers are not bound by that rule, but federal contractors, some institutions and some corporate offices are, and others simply prefer NDAA compliant CCTV systems. Compliance is a property of a specific model and its components, so we only call a product NDAA compliant when its manufacturer's spec sheet says so, and we will send that sheet with the quote.

Viewing security cameras on a phone

Every system we sell supports security cameras with smartphone viewing through the manufacturer's app. The recorder connects to your internet router; the app on your phone finds the recorder by scanning a code or signing into an account; you then see live view, play back recordings and get motion alerts. When we install, we set up the app on the phones you name and show each person how to find a clip and export it. When you buy equipment-only, setup instructions come with the recorder and we answer the phone if the app will not connect. Remote video surveillance on a phone needs internet at the building; recording does not.

Smart detection and analytics

AI security cameras with human and vehicle detection send fewer false alerts than plain motion detection, which matters on a Manhattan block where buses, cabs and pedestrians pass the camera all day. Smart search lets you ask the recorder for every person who passed the package room door between 2 and 4 p.m. instead of scrubbing through hours of video. Smart motion detection cameras, people counting cameras and object detection security cameras are all available on specific models; we only list the feature for a product when its spec sheet includes it.

Reality Check

Where Generic Camera Buying Advice Misses in Manhattan

Most camera buying guides online, and most AI summaries built from them, are written for a detached house with a driveway and a yard. Manhattan has a few of those, but most buyers here live or work in something very different. These are the points where the generic advice most often steers Manhattan buyers wrong.

“Just buy wireless, it is easier”

Wireless is easier to mount and harder to live with in Manhattan. Prewar walls of plaster on brick, steel-framed floors and dozens of neighboring WiFi networks on the same channels all cut into signal. A wireless camera that streamed perfectly during setup can drop frames every evening when the building's residents come home and start streaming video themselves. Wired cameras avoid all of it. Wireless still has a place, on a terrace or in a rental where nothing can be drilled, but it is the exception, not the default.

“Battery cameras are fine outdoors”

Battery cameras sell well because they need no wiring. On a Manhattan rooftop, fire escape or rear yard in January, cold drains batteries faster, and a camera that has to be taken down and charged every few weeks tends to end up in a drawer. For any outdoor spot that has to work through the winter, a wired camera with power on the cable is the safer buy.

“The camera on the facade covers the lobby too”

A camera mounted outside, above a building's door, sees the tops of heads and the sidewalk. It rarely shows a face clearly, and it sees nothing once the person is inside. In Manhattan the most useful entrance camera is usually inside the vestibule or lobby, aimed back at the door at head height, where everyone entering has to face it and the light is controlled.

“A doorbell camera is a doorbell camera”

On a house, a doorbell camera is a simple purchase. On an apartment door in a co-op or condo hallway it can break the house rules, and on a rental door it needs the landlord's permission. Brownstone and townhouse owners have the freedom that the generic advice assumes; apartment residents usually do not, which is why we ask about the building before recommending one.

“Buy the system with the most cameras for the money”

Bundles with eight or sixteen cameras look like value until you check the recorder and the drive. A large bundle with a small drive keeps only a few days of video, and a camera count that does not match your actual doors and halls means some cameras end up watching nothing important. In a Manhattan store or building, fewer cameras in the right places, on a recorder with enough storage, beat a bigger box every time.

“The building's cameras will cover you”

Tenants of stores and offices often assume the landlord's cameras protect them. Base-building cameras cover the lobby and the building's doors, not the inside of your suite or the register in your shop, and getting footage from a landlord can take days. A tenant who needs evidence of what happens inside their own space needs their own cameras on their own recorder.

Can cameras record audio at a Manhattan bar or restaurant counter?
+

Be careful. New York Penal Law section 250.00 defines mechanical overhearing as recording a conversation without the consent of at least one party by someone not present, and eavesdropping is a crime under section 250.05. Recording customers talking at a bar is the risky case. Our systems are set up with audio recording off unless the owner has a lawful reason, posted notice and, ideally, advice from a lawyer.
