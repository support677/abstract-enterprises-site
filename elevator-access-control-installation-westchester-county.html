Westchester by the Numbers

Westchester's Elevator Buildings Are Getting Taller, and the Lobby Door No Longer Covers Them

For most of the last century, an elevator in Westchester County meant one of a short list of buildings: a postwar co-op near a Metro-North station, a hospital, a corporate campus off the Cross Westchester Expressway, or a handful of downtown office blocks. That list has changed. The four cities in the southern half of the county, Yonkers, New Rochelle, White Plains and Mount Vernon, have spent the last ten years approving towers that would have been unthinkable in a suburban downtown a generation ago, and most of them are rentals with garages, amenity floors and roof decks stacked above the apartments. Each of those features is a reason to control who the elevator will carry, and to which floor. These are the published figures we plan Westchester elevator access control work from.

The towers get the headlines, but they are not the whole Westchester elevator picture. Real estate listings for Hartsdale co-ops still advertise the elevator as a selling point, and residents on the local subreddit point out that once you rule out single-family houses, most of lower Westchester's for-sale housing is co-ops. Those buildings are small, self-governed and careful with money, and their elevator access control question is different from a 25-story Yonkers rental: usually one reader, one car and a board that wants to know exactly what it is paying for.

Then there is the institutional layer. The office parks of the I-287 corridor, from Tarrytown through White Plains, Harrison and Purchase, were built for one corporate tenant and are now often split among several, which turns a single open elevator bank into a shared hallway between companies that do not know each other. All three layers end up asking the same thing of an elevator access control installer in Westchester: make the car answer only to the people who belong, and only for the floors they belong on.

How It Works

How an Elevator Access Control System Decides Who Rides in a Westchester High-Rise

An elevator access control system, which some managers call an elevator entry system and British suppliers call lift access control, is a small set of parts that sits beside the elevator, not inside its machinery. A reader or keypad goes in the car, at a hall call station, or both. A cable carries the reader's signal from the moving car down the hoistway to a controller panel, usually mounted in the machine room or a nearby electrical closet. That panel checks each credential against a list the building maintains, and when the credential is good it closes a relay or sends a message to an interface board. The elevator contractor wires that relay or board into the elevator controller, so the elevator treats the signal as permission. Everything else, the motor, the brakes, the door operator, the leveling, the safety circuits, belongs to the elevator and stays exactly as it was.

In a 19- or 25-story Westchester tower, that basic chain shows up in one of three designs, and choosing among them is the first real decision of the job.

Car enable: one tap unlocks the buttons

The simplest design treats the whole car as locked. A rider taps an elevator key fob or enters a code on the elevator keypad, and for a few seconds every floor button works. It is fast to install, it costs the least, and it stops the most common problem in a rental tower, a stranger who walks in behind a resident and rides up to wander the hallways. What it does not do is tell floors apart. A resident of the 9th floor can ride to the 22nd. For a mid-rise Hartsdale co-op where everyone is a shareholder, that is usually fine. For a Yonkers tower with a fitness floor, a roof deck and a garage, it rarely is.

Floor lockout: each credential carries its own floors

Floor lockout gives every floor button its own relay. When a resident taps in, the panel releases only the floors that resident is allowed: their own floor, the lobby, the garage if they rent a space, the amenity levels during the hours the building chooses. Everyone else's buttons stay dead. This is what management companies usually mean when they ask for elevator floor restriction, and it is the design most Westchester high-rise rentals end up with: an elevator access control system for multiple floors, with each floor treated as its own door. It takes more wiring, because the number of relays grows with floors and cars, and more planning, because someone has to decide who belongs where before a single relay is installed.

Destination dispatch: the panel talks to the dispatcher

Some newer towers in White Plains and Yonkers have destination dispatch: riders choose a floor at a kiosk in the lobby and the system assigns them a car, and there may be no floor buttons in the car at all. In those buildings, access control works through the dispatch system itself. The reader at the kiosk identifies the rider, the access software passes their allowed floors to the dispatcher, and the kiosk only offers those floors. That integration depends on the elevator manufacturer's interface, so it is planned with the elevator contractor from the first meeting and priced after the survey.

Whichever design fits, two things are true in every Westchester building we work in. The elevator does not lose any safety behavior because of our equipment, and the elevator contractor, not our crew, makes the connection inside the controller. The rest of this page explains how we decide which design a building needs, what each part costs, and how the work fits around the rules a Westchester building already lives under.

The Downtown Towers

Elevator Access Control for High-Rise Buildings in Yonkers, New Rochelle, White Plains and Mount Vernon

Westchester's four southern cities each built their towers for different reasons, and the elevator security problems they create are not identical. We plan each downtown on its own terms.

Yonkers: the waterfront and the garage problem

The Yonkers towers rising along the Hudson near the Metro-North station are long rental buildings on tight lots, and almost all of them solve parking by stacking it inside the building. Miroza Tower at 44 Hudson Street is the clearest example: 25 stories, 250 apartments and a garage sized for 222 cars. When a garage shares cars with the apartments, the garage landing becomes a second front door that no doorman watches. Anyone who walks into the garage behind a car can call the elevator and, without access control, ride to any floor. For a Yonkers waterfront tower, the core of the design is usually a reader at the garage hall-call station plus floor lockout in the cars, so a garage visitor can reach only the lobby until a resident or the front desk releases a floor. Older Yonkers rentals further from the river, such as the six-story, 60-unit Park Vista building at Croton Heights that the Yonkers Industrial Development Agency lists among its completed projects, are smaller and simpler: one or two cars, one reader each, and a lobby intercom that should be able to release a visitor's floor.

New Rochelle: a downtown rebuilt in ten years

New Rochelle changed its downtown zoning in late 2015, and the Pew Charitable Trusts later found that the city added housing at more than double the national rate from 2017 through 2021. The result is a cluster of new elevator buildings within a few blocks of the train, most of them less than a decade old. That age is good news for an elevator access control installation in New Rochelle: these buildings usually have microprocessor elevator controllers, spare conductors in the traveling cable and a cloud door system at the lobby that can be extended into the cars. The challenge is mixed use. Highgarden Tower, the 19-story building the state celebrated in 2024, holds 219 affordable apartments, 77 supportive units, two community lounges and a gym. Residents, social service staff, home aides and visitors all need different floors at different hours, and the lounges and program rooms want schedules rather than open access. That is floor-by-floor work with time-based permissions, and it is where careful programming matters more than the hardware.

White Plains: twin towers and a business district

Downtown White Plains combines the county seat's office and courthouse district with a deep stock of residential towers, from the condominium tower at City Center to the two 16-story buildings at 55 Bank Street that together hold 561 apartments. Twin-tower sites raise a management question first: one credential database for both buildings, with each resident limited to their own tower's cars, or two separate systems. We almost always recommend one platform with separate access groups, so the management office issues one fob per resident and can see both buildings' activity in one place. White Plains office buildings near the train station add a different requirement: tenant floors that open for employees during business hours, a lobby that stays public, and after-hours access that only works with a credential and leaves an audit trail.

Mount Vernon: new construction beside older walk-up blocks

Mount Vernon is in the middle of its own building wave. State housing officials announced in August 2026 that construction was underway on QWest Towers, two buildings with 229 apartments in the Mount Vernon West transit-oriented zone, including 12 units designed for residents with mobility disabilities and 7 for residents with sensory disabilities. Not far away, 115 South MacQuesten Parkway adds 315 units in two connected buildings. These projects are being designed now, which is the best moment to get elevator access control right: conduit to the machine room, conductors reserved in the traveling cable and readers placed at a height and with audio and visual feedback that work for every resident, before the walls close. Mount Vernon's older six-story apartment houses are the opposite case. In 2021 the Journal News reported on residents of one six-story Mount Vernon building who went more than five weeks without their only elevator. In a one-car building like that, the access control design has to protect the car without making a fragile elevator any harder to keep running, so we keep our equipment small, serviceable and easy for the elevator mechanic to isolate.

Building Types

Westchester Building Types and the Elevator Security System Each One Usually Needs

Westchester is not one elevator market. A design that is right for a 25-story Yonkers rental is overbuilt for a four-story Bronxville co-op, and a system that is fine for that co-op would leave a medical office building in Hawthorne exposed. These are the building types we see most often across the county and the starting design for each.

Postwar co-ops near the train in Hartsdale, Scarsdale and Tarrytown

These buildings, usually four to seven stories with one car, sometimes two, are the backbone of residential elevator access control in central Westchester, and most of our elevator access control for condominium buildings and co-op apartment buildings starts here. Many still rely on the lobby door lock and nothing else, or on a brass key switch in the car that opened the basement storage floor decades ago and whose keys are now in unknown hands. The usual starting point is a single Level 1 reader in each car, with the storage, laundry or roof level restricted to the residents and staff who need it. A co-op board here cares about three things above all: what shareholders will notice, what it costs, and how the superintendent will manage fobs when a unit sells. We plan around all three before we write a proposal.

Prewar elevator co-ops and condos in Bronxville, Pelham and Larchmont

The older Tudor and Colonial Revival apartment buildings near the Sound Shore and Bronxville stations often have small cabs, hand-finished wood interiors and elevator controllers that have been modernized at least once. Mounting a reader in a paneled cab without damaging it is a real design decision: we use surface-mount readers sized to fit an existing panel cutout where possible, run cable through the car's existing paths, and match faceplate finishes to the cab. Where a board would rather not touch the car at all, a reader at the lobby call button controls who can summon the car, which is a weaker protection but sometimes the right trade.

Downtown rental towers

The Yonkers, New Rochelle and White Plains towers described above nearly always want floor lockout in every car, a reader at the garage landing, schedules on amenity floors and a link between the lobby intercom and the elevator so a visitor can be released to one floor. They also want management software the leasing office can run without calling us, plus phone credentials for residents who would rather not carry a fob.

Affordable, supportive and senior buildings

Buildings like Highgarden Tower and the new Mount Vernon developments mix residents, staff and services on shared cars. Staff groups reach every floor, residents reach home and the shared spaces, and the front desk can release a floor for a signed-in guest. Readers must be usable from a seated position and give clear sound and light feedback. Biometrics are generally avoided for residents in these buildings unless the operator has a documented consent process, because a fob that can be replaced in five minutes is kinder than a face template that cannot.

Medical office buildings around Valhalla and Hawthorne

The medical office buildings near Westchester Medical Center's Valhalla campus and along Skyline Drive and Bradhurst Avenue in Hawthorne have public floors where patients arrive, clinical floors that should stay open only during practice hours, and records, pharmacy or equipment floors that should be elevator access control for authorized personnel only. Most are best served by floor lockout tied to time schedules, with patient floors open during posted hours and locked to staff credentials afterward. We do the low-voltage work around clinical operations and coordinate the hours with the building's property manager, not with individual practices, so the schedules stay consistent.

Office parks on the I-287 corridor

Many corporate buildings from Tarrytown to Purchase were designed for a single company and now hold several tenants. The elevator bank that once served one employer now connects strangers, and a tenant on the third floor has no reason to reach the sixth. Commercial elevator access control for office buildings here means floor-by-floor control by tenant group, with each company's office manager able to add and remove its own employees, is the usual design. Audit trails matter here too, because a tenant's security team will eventually ask who rode to their floor on a Saturday.

Village downtowns and the river towns

Ossining, Peekskill, Port Chester, Mamaroneck and Sleepy Hollow each have a handful of elevator buildings, often mixed-use with stores below and apartments above. The stores must never get a path into the residential car, so the reader usually sits at the street-level call station, and deliveries for the stores are kept on the ground floor.

Floor Restriction

Designing Elevator Floor Restriction for a Multi-Floor Westchester Building

Floor restriction is where most of the thinking in a Westchester elevator floor restriction system installation happens. The hardware is familiar; deciding who gets which floor, when, and how a guest gets upstairs is the part that takes time, and it is far cheaper to settle on paper than after the relays are wired.

Count the floors, cars and stations before anything else

The size of a floor restriction system follows a simple multiplication: the floors you want to control, times the cars that serve them, plus any hall call stations that get their own reader. A two-car building with 16 controlled floors needs 32 floor outputs before counting garage or lobby readers. A 25-story Yonkers tower with three cars and a two-level garage can need close to a hundred. That number drives the size of the panel, the relay or interface boards, the conductors needed in the traveling cable and the hours of elevator contractor time to land the connections. It is also why we never quote floor restriction for a Westchester building over the phone: we count it in the machine room.

Decide which floors stay open

Not every floor should be locked. The lobby nearly always stays open so anyone in the car can get out. Some buildings keep a public floor open during business hours, such as a leasing office, a doctor's waiting room or a building management office. Every floor you leave open is a floor that does not need a relay, which lowers the cost and simplifies the programming.

Give amenity floors a schedule

Rental towers in Westchester sell their amenities: fitness floors, lounges, co-working rooms, roof terraces, children's playrooms. This is elevator access control with time-based floor permissions: each of those can be released by a schedule, for example 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. for the gym and sunrise to 10 p.m. for the roof, so residents can use them without a staff member present and nobody is up there at 3 a.m. Schedules live in the access software, so management can change them in a season without a service call.

Treat the garage as its own front door

In most downtown towers, the garage is the least supervised entrance. A reader at the garage hall-call station limits who can call the car there, and a garage restriction inside the car keeps residents without parking from riding down. A garage visitor without a credential can reach the lobby, where the front desk or intercom takes over.

Plan the exceptions before they happen

Every building eventually needs a move-in elevator, a contractor who must reach one apartment for three days, a home aide who comes on weekday mornings, and a resident who loses a fob at midnight. We write those cases into the access plan, with staff credentials for the superintendent and a key-switch override at the lobby for the elevator contractor and the fire department, so the system never traps a building in its own rules.

Elevator access control in Westchester is always a two-contractor job, and buildings save the most time when both are clear from the start about who owns which part.

What your licensed elevator contractor does

The elevator contractor connects our relay outputs or interface board to the elevator controller, confirms that fire service, independent service and inspection operation override the access system, confirms that spare conductors in the traveling cable can be used or replaces them, and handles anything the local authority requires for an alteration to the elevator. We do not open elevator controllers, change elevator wiring or perform elevator mechanical work, and we would be wary of any installer who offered to.

How Westchester's local rules come into it

Westchester has no single elevator authority. Building and elevator rules are administered under the New York State Uniform Fire Prevention and Building Code by each city, town and village building department, and some have their own elevator chapters. The Town of Greenburgh, whose unincorporated area includes Hartsdale and a large share of central Westchester's co-op stock, is a good example. Its Chapter 170 says no elevator may be materially altered without an elevator permit approved by the town, that the permit is issued only to a holder of an installer's permit for that work, that elevators are inspected twice a year, and that a certificate of operation must be displayed in each car. Whether adding a reader counts as a material alteration in a given town is a question for the elevator contractor and the local building department, and we build the time for that answer into the schedule. At the state level, the New York Department of State's 2025 code notices reference ASME A17.1-2022, the national elevator safety code, as the standard the state codes adopt for elevators, and that code is the reason access equipment must never interfere with fire service operation.

One more distinction matters in Westchester. The New York City rules that New York City buildings follow, such as the Department of Buildings' elevator filings and the city's Local Law 63 rules on tenant data in smart access systems, do not apply in Yonkers, Scarsdale or Peekskill. Westchester buildings follow their own local building department and state law. We still bring the good habits of those city rules north with us, especially written privacy terms for any system that stores resident data.

Safety

Fire Service, Power Loss and Emergency Rides in Westchester Elevator Buildings

A good elevator access control design is built around the moments when it must step aside. These are the three we plan for in every Westchester building.

Fire service: the elevator stops asking us

When a fire alarm recalls the elevators or a firefighter takes control with the fire service key, the elevator must ignore every access restriction. The elevator contractor wires the access interface so that fire service operation always overrides it, and we test that behavior with them before we leave. In practice this means the access panel never gets a vote during an emergency, which is exactly how it should be. If your Westchester building's fire alarm panel is also being replaced or tied to the door system, we coordinate the sequence with the fire alarm contractor so that recall, door release and elevator override all agree.

Power loss: decide fail-safe or fail-secure in advance

Westchester has its share of storm outages, and many elevator buildings have generators that pick up at least one car. What should happen to floor restriction when the access panel loses power? In most residential buildings the answer is that the floor buttons should work, so residents are never stuck in a car that will not take them home. In some office and medical buildings, the answer is that restricted floors stay locked and the car serves only the lobby until power returns. Both are reasonable; what matters is choosing on purpose. We supply battery backup for the access panel sized to ride through short interruptions and set the long-outage behavior the board or owner chose in writing.

Medical emergencies and accessibility

An ambulance crew coming to the 14th floor of a New Rochelle tower cannot wait for a resident to find a fob. Front desks, superintendents and, where the building chooses, emergency responders get credentials or a key-switch override that releases every floor. Readers are mounted where a seated rider can reach them and give a tone and a light, not only a screen message, so riders with low vision or hearing loss know the car accepted them.

A Day in the Building

One Day in a Westchester Rental Tower With Elevator Floor Control

Specifications only go so far. Here is what a well-designed system looks like to the people who use it, in a composite of the downtown rental towers we plan for in Yonkers, New Rochelle and White Plains. The building and its residents are illustrative, not a specific customer.

6:10 a.m. A resident on the 17th floor heads to the fitness floor. She taps her fob in the car; the gym schedule opened at 6:00, so the gym button lights along with her own floor and the lobby. At 5:50 it would not have.

7:45 a.m. A commuter rides down to the garage. His fob belongs to the parking group, so the garage button works for him. His neighbor without a parking space presses the same button and nothing happens, which is exactly what the building decided.

9:30 a.m. A home health aide arrives for a resident on the 11th floor. Her temporary fob, issued by the management office, works on weekdays between 9:00 and noon and only for the 11th floor and the lobby. When the aide's agency changes staff next month, the office disables one fob and issues another in a minute.

1:15 p.m. A courier with a cart of packages comes in through the side entrance. The package room is on the ground floor and the residential buttons stay dark, so the packages stay where the building wants them.

4:00 p.m. A moving crew has booked the service car. The superintendent puts that car on independent service with his key, which the elevator contractor wired to override the access system, and the crew uses it all afternoon while the other cars keep their restrictions.

7:20 p.m. A dinner guest presses the apartment number on the lobby video intercom. The resident answers on her phone, opens the door, and the intercom releases her floor for the guest's ride. The guest sees only one working button.

11:05 p.m. The roof terrace closes on its schedule. A resident who taps in at 11:10 can reach home but not the roof, and the audit log records the attempt.

2:40 a.m. A fire alarm on a lower floor recalls the cars. The access system has no say: the elevators return to the designated landing and the fire department takes control with its key. When the alarm is cleared and the elevators return to normal service, the restrictions resume.

Nothing in that day required a staff member to stand at the elevator, and nothing in it slowed a resident down by more than a tap. That is the standard we design to.

Records

Audit Trails, Incident Reports and Insurance Questions for Westchester Elevator Systems

An elevator access control system with audit trail logs every ride it allows or refuses: the credential, the car, the floor and the time. Most of the time nobody looks. The log becomes valuable on the day a resident reports a stolen package, a tenant's security team asks who was on their floor over a holiday weekend, or a board wants to know whether a shareholder's renovation contractor really stopped coming at 5 p.m.

We set up three habits in each Westchester building. First, the log is kept for a period the board or owner chooses and states in its policy, long enough to investigate an incident but not forever. Second, the management office knows how to export a report filtered by floor, by credential or by time, without calling us. Third, if the building also has cameras, the elevator events and the camera recording use the same clock, so a log entry at 2:14 p.m. can be matched to the video in seconds.

Visitors

Visitor Management for Westchester Elevators: Guests, Aides, Deliveries and Movers

Restricting the elevator is easy. Restricting it without making every guest a problem is the real work. An elevator access control system with visitor management handles guests in four ways in Westchester, and most buildings use all of them.

Intercom floor release

The visitor calls the apartment from the lobby video intercom. When the resident opens the door, the intercom also tells the access panel to release that resident's floor for the next ride. The visitor walks in, steps into the car, and only the resident's floor button works. This is the single most requested feature in downtown Westchester rentals, and it depends on an intercom that can talk to the access system, which most current cloud intercoms can.

Front desk release

Buildings with a concierge or doorman can release a floor for a signed-in guest from a desk screen or a button panel. The guest's ride is logged against the release, which gives management an audit trail without asking guests for anything.

Temporary credentials

Home health aides, dog walkers, cleaners and contractors get credentials limited to one floor, set days and set hours, which expire automatically. A Scarsdale co-op whose shareholders rely on daily home care can issue those fobs from the managing agent's office and see when each aide arrived.

Deliveries and move days

Package volume is the reason many Westchester towers added access control in the first place. Couriers who once rode to every floor now leave packages in a lobby room or locker. For move-ins, the superintendent puts one car on independent service with a key, which the elevator contractor confirms overrides the access system, and the other cars keep their restrictions.

Upgrades & Repair

Elevator Access Control Upgrade, Repair and Replacement in Westchester

A large share of our Westchester calls are elevator access control repair, maintenance and elevator access control system replacement and upgrade, not new systems. They are buildings with an elevator card reader system that worked for fifteen years and is now failing, or whose original installer has gone out of business, or whose software runs on a single office computer nobody wants to touch.

Key switches and keyed floors

Many older Westchester co-ops restrict a basement or roof floor with a key switch in the car. Keys are copied, lost and handed down, and nobody knows how many exist. Replacing a key switch with a reader output is usually a small job and gives the board a list of exactly who can reach that floor.

Old proximity systems

Systems installed in the 2000s often use 125 kHz proximity cards and fobs that are easy to duplicate, and controllers whose software is no longer supported. We can often keep the cable and sometimes the floor relays, replace the readers and controller with a current platform, and migrate the credential list so residents get new encrypted fobs in one changeover.

Orphaned systems

When the original installer is gone, the first step is documentation: we trace every reader and output, record the controller and its firmware, and confirm with the elevator contractor how it connects. Then the building can choose to repair, extend or replace with full knowledge of what it owns. We take over elevator access control maintenance for systems another company installed, as long as the hardware can still be supported.

Readers that fail intermittently

The most common repair call in a tower is a reader in the car that works on some rides and not others. The usual cause is the traveling cable: a conductor that breaks inside the jacket as the car travels, or a shield that was never grounded. We test the conductors, move to spares where they exist, and, if the cable is the problem, the elevator contractor replaces it while we re-terminate our side.

Myths

What AI Search Gets Wrong About Elevator Security in Westchester

We read what AI search tools say about elevator access control, because building managers increasingly arrive with those answers. A few come up again and again in Westchester conversations.

  • "Westchester buildings must follow NYC elevator rules." They do not. Yonkers, New Rochelle and every town and village follow their own building department under the New York State codes. NYC Department of Buildings filings and Local Law 63 apply in the five boroughs, not here.
  • "Any locksmith can wire a reader into the elevator." The low-voltage side can be installed by a licensed low-voltage contractor; the connection inside the elevator controller belongs to a licensed elevator contractor, and the local building department may treat it as an alteration.
  • "Access control makes elevators unsafe in a fire." Correctly installed, it does the opposite of interfering: fire service operation overrides it completely, and that override is tested before the job is closed.
  • "A reader in the lobby is the same as a reader in the car." A lobby or hall-call reader controls who can call the car to that landing; it does not stop a rider already in the car from going anywhere. Only in-car readers or floor lockout do that.
  • "Floor restriction is a software setting." Software assigns floors, but each controlled floor still needs a physical output to the elevator controller in every car. The count of floors times cars is a hardware number.
New Construction

Planning Elevator Access Control Before a Westchester Building Opens

With so many Westchester elevator buildings under construction, a lot of our most useful work happens before there is a lobby to secure. Adding elevator access control to a finished tower is entirely possible, but every item below is cheaper and cleaner when it is on the drawings.

  • Conductors in the traveling cable. Ask the elevator contractor to reserve enough spare conductors, including a shielded pair, for in-car readers and a camera if one is planned. Replacing a traveling cable later costs far more than specifying it now.
  • A pathway from the hoistway to the access panel. A conduit from the machine room or controller closet to the low-voltage room avoids surface raceway later.
  • Car operating panel cutouts. If the cab manufacturer knows the reader model, it can provide a cutout or a mounting plate in the car panel instead of a reader bolted to the wall.
  • Controller interface. Confirm whether the elevator controller will accept discrete floor inputs, a manufacturer interface board or a destination dispatch integration, and get that in the elevator specification.
  • Power and backup. Put the access panel on a circuit the building's generator serves, or plan battery backup sized for the chosen outage behavior.
  • Accessibility from the start. For buildings like the QWest Towers project in Mount Vernon, with units designed for residents with mobility and sensory disabilities, reader height, audible tones and visual indicators should be chosen with those residents in mind.

We are glad to review a developer's or architect's elevator and low-voltage drawings for a Westchester project and mark up what the access system will need, so the elevator contractor, the electrician and our crew are all working from the same plan.

After Installation

Running a Westchester Elevator Access Control System After Installation

An elevator access control system is only as good as the credential list behind it. These are the habits that keep a Westchester building's system working years after we leave.

  • Remove credentials the day someone leaves. When a co-op apartment closes or a tenant's lease ends, deactivate their fobs before handing keys to the new resident.
  • Review the access groups twice a year. Staff turn over, aides change and contractors finish. A ten-minute review with the audit log catches credentials that should have expired.
  • Keep spare fobs in the system, not in a drawer. Unassigned spares should be enrolled but disabled, so a lost one cannot open anything.
  • Tell us before the elevator is modernized. When your elevator contractor replaces a controller, the access interface has to be reconnected and retested. We coordinate with them so the readers work on the first day of the new controller.
  • Book periodic maintenance. A yearly visit to test every reader, every floor and the power-loss behavior, and to update firmware, costs far less than a failed reader in a car full of residents.
  • Coordinate with the elevator inspections. In towns that inspect elevators twice a year, let the elevator contractor know how to put the access system aside during testing, so inspections are never delayed.
