The Neighborhood

Why Fulton Ferry Buildings Need Fire Alarms Planned Around Park Leases and Landmark Walls

Fulton Ferry buildings need commercial fire alarms planned around park leases and landmark walls because almost nothing here is a plain private building on a plain private lot. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fulton Ferry Historic District on June 28, 1977, and its designation report calls the landing "the place where Brooklyn began." The district is tiny: the stretch of Old Fulton Street running down to the East River, the north side of Water Street out to Main Street, a few blocks of Front Street, and Furman and Doughty Streets along the bluff. Inside those lines are an 1834 insurance office, two rows of 1830s store buildings, an 1860s railroad office with a cast-iron ground floor, an 1890s storage warehouse turned into apartments, a fireboat station, and the two largest buildings on the waterfront, the Empire Stores and the Tobacco Warehouse.

Those last two explain the ownership puzzle. Manhattan builders Nesmith & Sons finished the Empire Stores in 1869, a year after fire destroyed the storage warehouse that stood on the same stretch; the New York Times of the day wrote that "the losses by the fire at the Empire Stores" were hard to pin down. The warehouses held coffee, sugar and molasses for decades, emptied out in the 1960s and sat as a ruin until New York State's parks office bought them in 1978. In 2010 the state passed the site to the Brooklyn Bridge Park Corporation, and in 2013 the park picked Midtown Equities and its partners to rebuild it under a 99-year lease whose rent goes to park maintenance. Brooklyn Bridge Park describes the finished complex as seven historic warehouses with about 300,000 square feet of offices and 80,000 square feet of restaurants, shops and event space.

Next door, the 1850s Tobacco Warehouse stood roofless for years as an open-air picnic spot until St. Ann's Warehouse built a 25,000-square-foot theater inside its brick walls, opening on October 6, 2015. Brownstoner reported that the project needed state legislation, National Park Service approval and an agreement with preservationists after a 2011 court ruling held that the land was part of a public park. Architect Jonathan Marvel summed up the site in one line at the ribbon cutting: "The space between the bridges is magical." Across Old Fulton Street, Pier 1 carries another park development site, the 1 Hotel Brooklyn Bridge and the Pierhouse condominiums, which opened in February 2017 with 194 guest rooms, 106 homes and event rooms that hold up to 1,000 guests.

For a fire alarm, that history turns into practical questions. Who signs as owner on the FDNY filing when the building belongs to a park and the system belongs to a ground tenant? Which preservation reviewer looks at a horn strobe on an exterior wall when the lot is park land rather than a private parcel? When the Empire Stores rooftop was debated in 2013, The Real Deal reported that the LPC held only "an advisory role" and that the State Historic Preservation Office would make the final ruling; CityLand reported the same advisory route for the Tobacco Warehouse theater. Private buildings a few steps away on Old Fulton Street answer to the LPC directly. A design that ignores those differences can lose a season waiting for the wrong approval.

Fire is also part of the landing's own story. The Brooklyn Public Library's history blog records that the 1871 Fulton Ferry House at the foot of Old Fulton Street had a small fire in 1910, a much larger one in 1919, and a fire on October 8, 1925, that led to its demolition in March 1926. The city built a fireboat station on the same spot that year, and Brownstoner notes that Fireboat Engine 77 moved in by 1931 and stayed until the house closed in 1970. The building at 1 Water Street later went to the River Café's owner on a city lease, and the Brooklyn Ice Cream Factory took over that lease in 2001.

What is a commercial fire alarm system in a district like this? It is the control panel and its power supplies, the smoke and heat detectors, pull stations, horns, strobes and speakers, and the modules that watch sprinkler valves, release doors, recall elevators, stop fans and report to an FDNY-approved central station, all laid out by a design professional and approved by the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? Every initiating device reports to the panel over supervised wiring; the panel separates a real alarm from a broken wire, runs the programmed sequence and calls for help even when the theater is dark or the office floors are empty. When does a building need a fire alarm system? The NYC Building Code answers from occupancy and size: places of assembly from an occupant load of 300, business occupancies above set head counts, hotels, and apartment buildings above set heights or unit counts. Hotel fire alarm installation, hospital fire alarm installation, nursing home fire alarm installation and assisted living fire alarm installation each carry their own triggers, and every one of these commercial fire detection systems is NFPA 72 fire alarm installation under the city's amendments, which a design professional reads for your building.

If a fire alarm panel in your Fulton Ferry building is beeping, dark or showing a trouble light, call (347) 934-8335 and send a photo of the display; we book an emergency fire alarm service visit within 2 hours of your request. The first questions are the same in a warehouse office and a hotel: can every floor still be warned, do signals still reach the central station, and does the FDNY need a fire watch until the system is back.

Why is my fire alarm panel showing trouble? In this neighborhood the answer often starts with water. The River Café sat under chest-high water during Superstorm Sandy, the Brooklyn Eagle reported, and did not return to full dinner service until February 7, 2014, roughly fifteen months later. Cellars and ground floors near the landing can take in water from storms and leaking walls, and a wet junction box is the classic start of fire alarm ground fault troubleshooting. What causes a fire alarm ground fault? Any place a conductor touches a grounded path: moisture in a box, a jacket crushed behind a new cabinet, a screw through a cable in a tenant's millwork. Fire alarm ground fault repair means isolating circuits at the panel, splitting the faulted one until the fault moves, and replacing or rerouting the damaged run.

The other common causes follow the building type. In the warehouse offices, a tenant's contractor moves a ceiling or a wall and leaves a device covered, unplugged or wired to the wrong loop, which shows up as fire alarm open circuit repair work or a fire alarm device fault repair on the floor that was just finished. In the hotel and the condominiums, standby batteries age out and produce fire alarm battery trouble repair calls, then fire alarm battery replacement. Where an older panel still reports over copper phone lines, a carrier change leaves the fire alarm system not communicating, and the visit becomes fire alarm communication failure repair. Each of those is a fire alarm system trouble condition, and none is fixed by silencing the buzzer.

Theaters and restaurants bring their own patterns. Stage haze, steam from a dish room and dust from a renovation can all reach smoke detectors; fire alarm false alarm troubleshooting begins with the panel history and then compares each device type with the room it sits in. A sprinkler valve left part-way closed after a contractor drains a zone shows up as a fire alarm supervisory trouble. A trouble that comes and goes with the tide, the weather or the overnight marine work at the landing is fire alarm intermittent trouble, and the event log is where it gets solved. When the history shows one detector in the wrong place for its room, the cure is a fire alarm device replacement drawn and filed as a change, never a cover taped over the head.

How a Fulton Ferry trouble call runs

Fire alarm troubleshooting follows a fixed order. A fire alarm panel trouble light only says something is off-normal, so fire alarm fault diagnosis starts with the panel's own fire alarm system diagnostics: the active list, the history and each point's status. Commercial fire alarm troubleshooting in a building shared by a park landlord, a ground tenant and a dozen space tenants then sorts out whose devices are involved, so a fault on one tenant's floor is not chased through the whole complex.

On an addressable system, addressable fire alarm troubleshooting is mostly fire alarm SLC troubleshooting: the signaling line circuit tells you which addresses dropped off, and fire alarm signaling line circuit repair follows the loop to the open, short or doubled address. That is why addressable fire alarm system repair is usually quicker; the panel names the device. On an older conventional system in a small Front Street building, fire alarm circuit troubleshooting and smoke detector circuit troubleshooting mean measuring each zone against its end-of-line value and halving it until the fault appears, which makes conventional fire alarm repair fast on short zones and slow on long ones. Fire alarm short circuit troubleshooting, fire alarm notification circuit repair on horn and strobe circuits and fire alarm wiring repair follow the same discipline. If the panel itself is failing, fire alarm control panel troubleshooting, fire alarm control panel repair and FACP repair come before any talk of replacement.

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for Fulton Ferry's Warehouses, Theater, Pier Hotel and Old Rows

Commercial fire alarm systems in Fulton Ferry fall into five building families, and each one asks a different first question. The design professional decides what the code requires; this is how the families usually differ when we survey them.

The Empire Stores: one landmark, many tenants

The Empire Stores are seven connected warehouses with thick brick walls, timber and iron inside, a newer rooftop level and an open-air courtyard, filled with offices, shops, restaurants and event space. A building like this usually runs one base-building fire alarm owned by the ground tenant, with every office or shop fit-out shown as a modification of that system. Our first question is always for the landlord's team: who programs the panel, and what does the tenant work letter say about devices inside a suite? Sorting that out before a lease is signed saves months. Warehouse fire alarm installation in a landmark of this size is less about pulling cable than about agreeing who owns each device.

St. Ann's Warehouse: a theater inside a ruin

St. Ann's built a new roof, real floors, catwalks and soundproof doors inside the Tobacco Warehouse's 1850s walls. A theater of this kind is an assembly occupancy, where the design turns on the crowd, the stage, rigging and haze, and the voice message that has to cut through a performance. Devices high in a catwalk ceiling need a service plan the stage crew can live with, and testing has to fit between productions rather than inside them.

Pier 1: hotel rooms, ballrooms and homes on park land

The hotel and the Pierhouse condominiums share a park development site, but a hotel and a condominium are different occupancies with different owners. Guest rooms, a ground-floor event space for up to 1,000 people and a rooftop with a pool and bar for up to 350 each bring their own detection and notification questions, and the condominium board needs to know exactly which devices and records are its own.

Eagle Warehouse and 8 Old Fulton Street: homes in old commercial shells

Frank Freeman's Eagle Warehouse was built in 1893–94 around the old Brooklyn Eagle pressroom; Brownstoner notes that Brooklyn Law School used it from 1904 to 1928, and architect Bernard Rothzeid converted it to apartments in 1980. Today it has 85 units around a central atrium that brings light down through the building. Across the street, the Brooklyn City Railroad Company Building at 8 Old Fulton Street went up in 1860–61 with a cast-iron ground floor and brick above; David Morton converted it in the 1970s, and it is now an 11-apartment co-op. In both, the fire alarm is the board's, and every renovation in a unit can touch it.

The 1830s rows on Old Fulton and Front Streets

The LPC report lists Commercial Row at 7–23 Old Fulton Street (1836–39), Franklin House at 1–5 (completed 1839), the Long Island Safe Deposit Company at 25–27 (1868–69) and the Long Island Insurance Company at 5–7 Front Street (1834), which the Historic Districts Council calls possibly the oldest surviving office building in the city. These are small buildings with restaurants and shops at street level and apartments or offices above. Whether a building fire alarm is required depends on its use and size; when one is, the cellar, the stair and the storefront ceiling are where the work concentrates, and exterior devices on a landmark facade are rarely the answer.

Material and method notes

  • Brick bearing walls. Old masonry is drilled at mortar joints, not through face brick; conduit runs inside wherever the building allows.
  • Heavy timber and iron. Exposed beams in the warehouses look like an invitation for surface conduit; the drawings should decide routing, not the installer's convenience.
  • Atriums and courtyards. Open centers like the Eagle Warehouse atrium change how smoke moves, which the design professional considers when placing detection.
  • Cast-iron fronts. Cast iron is not drilled for devices; the 8 Old Fulton Street storefront is a reminder to keep exterior work off historic metal.
  • Low-lying floors near the river. Panels, power supplies and batteries are placed with flooding in mind, and the cellar is not automatically the right room.

Business Fire Alarm Installation on Old Fulton, Water, Front and Furman Streets

Business fire alarm installation in Fulton Ferry follows four short streets, and each one carries a different kind of business. Business fire detection systems here are shaped as much by who owns the ground as by what the business sells.

Old Fulton Street. The street that once led to the ferry slip is now the main approach to the landing and the park, lined with the 1830s rows, the railroad office and the Eagle Warehouse. Pizzerias, cafés and shops sit at street level, and lines on summer weekends stretch along the sidewalk. The LPC approved a one-story red-brick retail infill at 20 Old Fulton Street between the Eagle Warehouse and the co-op at 7 Everit Street, Brownstoner reported, and the lot at 9 Old Fulton Street, left by a fire years earlier, drew an LPC-approved plan for a new four-story building in 2010. Every new storefront on this street is a new fire alarm conversation.

Water Street. The Empire Stores and the Tobacco Warehouse face Water Street, with offices, shops, restaurants and a museum gallery behind those walls; the Brooklyn Historical Society opened a gallery in the Empire Stores in 2017. Here the landlord's base-building system is the starting point for every tenant.

Front Street. Front Street holds the Long Island Insurance Company building and smaller commercial buildings that lead toward Dumbo. Small offices and restaurants in old buildings bring kitchen and cellar questions more than tower questions.

Furman Street and the landing. Furman Street runs along the foot of the bluff past Pier 1. Brooklyn Bridge Park is renovating 334 Furman Street as park offices and a future education center, due in Spring 2028, and is doing overnight maintenance on the piles and bulkheads at Fulton Ferry Landing from August through December 2026 with a work barge in the former Bargemusic slip. NYC Ferry boats still call at the landing.

Approvals that sit beside the FDNY filing

  • FDNY. The plans are filed by a NYS registered design professional on a TM-1, examined, and installed only after approval; the FDNY's Fire Alarm Inspection Unit at 9 MetroTech Center witnesses the acceptance test. The FDNY says plan submission to final approval averages about 20 to 24 weeks, and the first plan review can take up to 8 weeks.
  • Landmarks on private lots. LPC says any interior work that needs a Buildings Department permit inside a historic district needs an LPC permit too, and so does exterior work, even when the Buildings side can be self-certified.
  • Park land. For the Empire Stores and the Tobacco Warehouse, published reports describe the LPC in an advisory role with the State Historic Preservation Office making the final ruling. Ask the park landlord and your design professional which review applies before any exterior device or conduit is drawn.
  • The landlord. Ground leases and work letters often require the landlord's approval of drawings and its own vendor for panel programming. That is a lease term, not a code requirement, and it belongs in the schedule.
  • Accessibility. Visible notification in public rooms, restrooms and guest rooms follows the code's accessibility rules; the design professional lays out strobes and their synchronization.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Three Signatures Before One Strobe

A pattern we see in park-owned buildings, told as a composite rather than a single job: a design studio signs for a suite in a converted waterfront warehouse and wants two meeting rooms and a pantry built in eight weeks. The architect draws the rooms; nobody draws the fire alarm. When we walk the suite, the base-building system is addressable and in good shape, but the new rooms need a smoke detector, two strobes and a speaker each, and the pantry needs a different detector type than the one in the ceiling.

The installation is the easy part. The ground tenant's engineer has to accept the drawings, the landlord's panel vendor has to schedule programming, and the tenant's design professional has to file the change with the FDNY. On top of that sits the question of whether anything shows on the outside of a landmark wall, which in a park building is not the same conversation it would be on a private lot. The meeting rooms can be framed in eight weeks; the fire alarm part cannot be approved in eight weeks.

What works is boring: one call in week one with the tenant, the landlord's property manager, the base-building vendor and the design professional, a one-page list of who signs what, and a calendar that starts with the filing date rather than the move-in date. We have watched that single call save a season. The tenant still moves in on time when the fit-out is phased and protection is kept in place; what slips is any plan that treats the fire alarm as the last trade in.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Questions From Fulton Ferry Ground Tenants, Boards, Venues and Shopkeepers, Answered

These are questions owners and tenants in Fulton Ferry bring to a first survey. The answers are general; the design professional on your job applies the code to your building.

Our building sits on Brooklyn Bridge Park land under a long lease. Whose name goes on the FDNY fire alarm filing as owner?

Usually the party that controls and operates the building under the lease, but the lease itself decides it, and the park may need to consent. Have your attorney or property manager confirm the owner of record and the signing authority before the design professional files, because a filing signed by the wrong party is sent back.

The park landlord says it must approve our fire alarm drawings before we file. Does that add time to the FDNY review?

It adds time before the FDNY clock starts, not during it. Landlord review happens first, then the TM-1 filing and the FDNY's own review, which the FDNY says averages about 20 to 24 weeks to final approval. Put the landlord's turnaround in the schedule as its own line.

Does a horn strobe on the outside wall of a park-owned warehouse go through the same landmarks review as one on Old Fulton Street?

Not necessarily. Private lots in the district answer to the LPC through its permit process. For park-owned warehouses, the published accounts of the Empire Stores and Tobacco Warehouse projects describe an advisory LPC role and a final ruling by the State Historic Preservation Office. Ask the park and your design professional which review applies, and avoid exterior devices where an interior location meets the code.

Our restaurant on Old Fulton Street has a line on the sidewalk every summer weekend. Does a busy dining room change what the fire alarm must do?

It can. If the room's occupant load reaches the assembly threshold, generally 300 under the NYC Building Code for new work, a fire alarm may be required, and the design must cover the dining room and kitchen. Your posted occupancy and the design professional's calculation decide it; a crowd outside the door does not count toward the load, but every seat inside does.

We are a theater company inside historic walls. How do we keep catwalk-level detectors serviceable without stopping a production?

Plan the access when the system is designed: where a lift can reach, which devices can be tested from a catwalk, and which rooms need detection that is serviced from below. Then fix the test and maintenance dates between productions in the service contract, so a dirty detector never becomes a show-night problem.

Our hotel ballroom is booked for a 900-guest gala. Does the event company's lighting and fog need fire alarm sign-off?

Any plan to change, bypass or cover devices for an event needs the building's approved procedure and the people responsible for the system, never the event crew alone. Haze or fog in a room with smoke detection must be handled through the building's fire safety procedures and the design professional's guidance, with the central station and the FDNY requirements respected.

Our condominium shares a park site with a hotel. Which fire alarm devices belong to the condo board?

The approved drawings and the property's declaration and agreements answer that, not the floor plan alone. Ask for the as-built drawings and a written list of panels, devices and interfaces each party owns, tests and pays for. Shared items such as a connection between buildings should be named in that list too.

The Eagle Warehouse atrium runs through every floor. Does an open center change where smoke detectors go in a renovation?

It can. An open shaft or atrium changes how smoke travels, and the code has specific rules for atriums. When a unit renovation touches walls or ceilings near the open center, the design professional should check the device layout before work starts, not after the ceiling is closed.

A new restaurant with an open kitchen is leasing the ground floor of our 1830s Old Fulton Street building. What should we ask its designer about the fire alarm?

Ask four things before the lease is signed: whether the new seating changes the occupant load enough to require or alter a fire alarm, which detectors suit an open kitchen, how the hood suppression system will report if the building has a fire alarm, and who files any change. Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms in the apartments above stay required whatever the answer.

A fire in the building next door damaged our shared wall on Old Fulton Street. Should our fire alarm be checked before we reopen?

Yes. Heat, smoke and firefighting water can reach devices, boxes and cable on your side of a shared wall even when your rooms look untouched. Have the circuits near that wall tested, replace anything smoke-stained or wet, confirm the central station link, and keep the report with your building records.

A tenant fit-out in our warehouse left one floor's devices on the old programming. Who fixes it, the tenant or the base-building vendor?

Whoever the lease and the approved filing put in charge of panel programming, which in most multi-tenant buildings is the landlord's vendor, billed to the tenant under the work letter. The fix is the same either way: program the panel to the approved drawings and retest the floor.

Our gallery sits inside a historic warehouse and borrows artwork from lenders. Can we use a different detector type to protect the work?

Sometimes. Space tenants should keep copies of the approvals and test results for their own suites so a future landlord or buyer can trace every change.

Can a Fulton Ferry building keep an old fire bell or gong from its warehouse days as a display piece?
+

Usually yes, as long as it is disconnected, clearly not part of the fire alarm, and placed where nobody could mistake it for a working device. It must not sound, look like a pull station, or block a real device. If you want an old bell wired back into service, that is a design and filing question, and most old devices are not listed for use today.

Who explains the fire alarm signal to hotel guests and condominium residents sharing the Pier 1 site?
+

Each operator explains it to its own people: the hotel through its front desk, room information and staff training, and the condominium board through notices to residents. Both should agree on what each tone and voice message means, because people from the two buildings share the same pier, entrances and streets during an emergency.

How should an Old Fulton Street building schedule fire alarm work around the summer crowds heading to the park?
+

Plan noisy work, lifts on the sidewalk and tests for weekday mornings or the off-season, and give the central station and tenants notice of every test window. Summer weekends bring the heaviest foot traffic down Old Fulton Street, so testing then annoys neighbors and raises the chance a real alarm is ignored.

Does changing the recorded voice message in a Fulton Ferry theater or ballroom need FDNY involvement?
+

Changes to voice messages, their sequence or which areas hear them are changes to an approved fire alarm design. They should be drawn by the design professional, programmed by the certified company and tested, with filing as the FDNY requires. Do not let a sound or event crew edit fire alarm messages.

What happens when part of a Fulton Ferry building's fire alarm has to be shut down for work?
+

The building follows an impairment procedure: notify the central station and the people responsible, protect the affected area, and arrange a fire watch when the FDNY rules call for one. Keep the shutdown as short as possible, restore and test the devices, and log the start and end times.

Can one tenant's fire alarm devices in a Fulton Ferry warehouse be tested without sounding the alarm for the whole building?
+

Often, yes. With the building's procedure, the panel operator can test a tenant area while notification elsewhere stays quiet, provided the central station and the people responsible are told and protection is restored the moment testing ends. Whether that is possible depends on how the system was designed and programmed.

Do all guest rooms in a Fulton Ferry hotel need strobes?
+

Not every room. The code requires visible notification in public and common areas and in a set share of accessible guest rooms, and the design professional decides which rooms and how they are equipped. Rooms for guests who are deaf or hard of hearing must have visible alarms that work with the building system.

Can event sets, drapes or stage scenery in a Fulton Ferry venue cover strobes or detectors?
+

They should not. Pipe and drape, banners and scenery that block a strobe, speaker or detector can leave part of the room unprotected. Plan the layout with the venue's fire safety staff so devices stay visible and clear, and walk the room before doors open to check.
