Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Fulton Ferry's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every fire alarm call in Fulton Ferry. They differ in what they were built for, how tall their ceilings run, who controls the system and how much of their outside can be touched, and those differences decide the plan long before a brand or a model number comes into it.

The warehouse converted to apartments

The Eagle Warehouse is the landmark example: eight stories built for storage in 1893 and 1894, with cellar vaults, converted to a co-op in 1980.

The office building converted to lofts

The Brooklyn City Railroad Company building of 1860 and 1861, with cast-iron piers, turned into about ten lofts between 1972 and 1975.

The two- to four-story brick commercial building

Stores with lodgings or offices above, like the Commercial Row on Cadman Plaza West from 1836 to 1839, and the 1834 Long Island Insurance Company building on Front Street.

The giant warehouse turned into offices and shops

Empire Stores on Water Street, about 500,000 square feet of offices, retail and restaurants around an open courtyard and under a public rooftop.

The theater and the small public building

St. Ann's Warehouse inside the old Tobacco Warehouse walls, and the two-story Fireboat House of 1926 at 1 Water Street.

The new building on an old lot

Infill such as the one-story red-brick retail building the LPC approved at 20 Old Fulton Street, and the newer condominiums on Furman Street.

The warehouse converted to apartments: storage floors, vaults below and a building full of homes

A warehouse built to hold furniture and silverware was designed around heavy loads, thick walls and floors that could carry stacked goods, not around corridors, bedrooms and kitchens. When the Eagle Warehouse and buildings like it became apartments, the conversion added halls, stairs, kitchens and bathrooms inside that frame, and usually a building fire alarm system of its era. Forty-plus years later, that system may still be running on its original panel and cable, with additions from later renovations layered on top. Our first job in a building like this is to find out what is actually there. We trace the circuits from the panel through the risers, match each device to a location, check the age and condition of the detectors and the batteries, and compare what we find with any FDNY paperwork the board can locate. ASM Integrators' summary of the rules explains the line that matters most: replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is treated as a repair, while moving or replacing the control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY.

The office building converted to lofts: high ceilings, beams and rooms without walls

Loft apartments in a building like the old Brooklyn City Railroad Company offices, or in the industrial conversion at 7 Everit Street, tend to share a set of features that make standard advice misleading. Ceilings are high. Beams or joists cross them and create pockets where smoke can gather before it spreads. Sleeping areas may be partly open to the living space or set on a platform. Kitchens are open to everything. A single detector placed by habit in the middle of the main ceiling can end up shielded by a beam, too far from the bed, and too close to the stove. We plan around those realities: devices placed where smoke from the likely fire starting points will reach them, in the beam pocket or on the beam as the manufacturer's instructions and the ceiling layout allow, close enough to the sleeping area that the alarm wakes someone behind a partial wall, and far enough from the cooktop that normal cooking does not trigger it.

The small brick commercial building: two halves of one building that have to talk

The two- to four-story buildings that make up most of the National Register district were built as stores, offices, a hotel and lodgings. The Commercial Row on Cadman Plaza West was built between 1836 and 1839 as stores with lodgings above. The Long Island Insurance Company building of 1834 on Front Street was an office. The cast-iron Long Island Safe Deposit Company building at 1 Front Street dates from 1868 and 1869. Today many such buildings hold a restaurant, café or shop at street level and offices or apartments above, with separate leases, separate keys and separate hours. A fire in the ground-floor kitchen at night threatens whoever is upstairs, and a fire upstairs during business hours threatens the customers below. The plan has to make each half warn the other, which means agreeing in writing who owns each device, who maintains it, who receives a signal, and who can unlock the store for a technician or a firefighter.

The giant warehouse turned into offices and shops: one system, many tenants

A building on the scale of Empire Stores is a different kind of job entirely. With hundreds of thousands of square feet of offices and retail, restaurants, an open courtyard rising four stories and a rooftop terrace open to the public, a building like this runs on a large building fire alarm system that belongs to the landlord, and each tenant's fit-out connects to it. The sources we read do not describe Empire Stores' system, and we make no claims about it. What we can describe is how tenant work in a building of this kind generally goes: the landlord's fire alarm vendor or engineer sets the rules, the design professional for the fit-out prepares the drawings, the FDNY reviews them, and the installer works to the approved plan, often in coordination with the base-building contractor who must add the new devices to the panel's program.

The theater and the small public building: audiences and a 1926 shingled station

Assembly spaces are where fire alarm rules are strictest, because many people who do not know the building gather in one room at once. St. Ann's Warehouse, set inside the walls of the old Tobacco Warehouse, is the district's best-known example; we do not describe its system and do not present it as a customer. In assembly spaces generally, the questions are about notification that every seat and every corner can see and hear, detection that copes with stage lighting, haze and high open volumes, and coordination with the people who run performances so a test never happens during a show. Those systems are designed by a registered design professional and filed with the FDNY; our work is installation, testing and support for the inspection.

The new building on an old lot: designed in, not added on

The lot at 20 Old Fulton Street, between the Eagle Warehouse and the 7 Everit Street co-op, sat empty for decades, since well before the 1977 designation, until the LPC unanimously approved a one-story red-brick building with two retail spaces, one planned as a coffee shop. Infill like that, and the newer condominiums along Furman Street, are designed with their fire protection from the first drawing. The NYC Business portal's description of the FDNY fire alarm application lists what such a filing needs: a Certificate of Occupancy or approved Schedule A, floor plans, a riser diagram and an operating sequence matrix, plans approved by a New York State registered professional engineer or architect, and a fire alarm plan involving a licensed Master Electrician and an FDNY S97 certificate holder. Our role is installation to the approved drawings, labeling and pre-testing, and coordination with the designer and the general contractor. We say that at the first meeting, so nobody expects us to be the designer.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Fulton Ferry Building Before We Draw Anything

These notes describe our technicians' routine in buildings like the ones on this page. They are practice, not a story about any one address.

Outside first, with the hands in the pockets. Before going in, the technician stands across the street and looks at the facade: where the entrance is, where the fire department would arrive, whether any old bell, strobe or box is already mounted, and what is ornament versus plain masonry. Nothing gets touched; it just goes on the sheet, so if an exterior device is ever needed, we already know where it could go and what Landmarks will see.

Then the roof and the top floor. Roof doors, bulkheads and top-floor halls tell us how smoke would leave or trap itself in the building. In a converted warehouse, the top floor is often where the most unusual apartments are.

Every floor on the way down. At each level the technician notes ceiling height, beams, open or closed sleeping areas, kitchens, and the condition of every detector, and tests a sounder while someone listens from the farthest bedroom with the door closed.

The ground floor and its tenant. If a café, shop or office is downstairs, we ask who holds the keys, when it is empty, where batteries or e-bikes are charged, and what cooking equipment runs. Firehouse.com reported in 2026 that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent that year, so charging spots go on the survey sheet.

The cellar last, and closely. Vaults, service entries, the panel location, how high water has come in the past, and anything that suggests someone works or sleeps down there. Only after the walk do we compare what we saw with the paperwork.

Compliance notes for Fulton Ferry buildings and businesses

  • Where a system is required. Section 907 of the NYC Building Code sets out which occupancies need a fire alarm system. Built Engineers' summary notes that sprinklers alone do not trigger a full building fire alarm, and that a required system must be monitored by an FDNY-approved central station.
  • Plans before installation. Fire alarm documents go to the Fire Department for review and approval before installation begins.
  • What the application needs. The NYC Business portal lists a Certificate of Occupancy or approved Schedule A, floor plans, a riser diagram, an operating sequence matrix, approval by a New York State registered PE or RA, and a fire alarm plan involving a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 certificate holder.
  • Ready means ready. The system must be 100 percent complete and pre-tested before the FDNY inspection is held.
  • Professional certification has limits. FC 104.2.1 allows professional certification of fire alarm design and installation in place of FDNY plan exam and inspection, but excludes the core building fire alarm system.
  • Kitchens and sprinklers. The 2022 Fire Code requires sprinklers in many occupancies and within 30 feet of commercial cooking equipment, which affects the cafés and restaurants around the landing.
  • Outages are limited. FC 901.7.5.2 sets thresholds for systems out of service 8 or more hours in 24, or 30 or more hours in a month, which govern every retrofit in an occupied building.
  • Firefighter radio is separate. An FDNY permit is required to operate an in-building auxiliary radio communication system, an item that can come up in larger buildings.
  • The facade is reviewed separately. Exterior work in the historic district needs an LPC permit, filed through Portico, and LPC approval does not replace DOB approval.
Inside the Walls, Outside the Walls

Fire Alarms in a Landmark District: What Needs a Landmarks Permit and What Never Does

Owners in Fulton Ferry often assume a historic district makes fire alarm work slow or impossible. It does neither, as long as the interior and exterior parts of the job are separated early. This section explains how we draw that line, based on the LPC's published guidance. We are installers, not preservation consultants or lawyers, and this is not legal advice; it is how we keep a Fulton Ferry fire alarm job from stalling.

Almost all of a fire alarm lives indoors. Detectors, panels, power supplies, notification devices in halls and suites, and the cable between them are interior work. The LPC says interior work in a historic district generally does not require its approval, unless the interior itself is designated, which is rare for private buildings. In most Fulton Ferry buildings, that means the bulk of a fire alarm job can be planned, filed with the FDNY and installed without Landmarks being involved at all. The one discipline we apply indoors is to keep every cable path inside the building envelope, so that an interior job never accidentally becomes an exterior one, for example by running conduit out a window and down the street wall to reach a cellar.

The exterior exceptions are specific. The items that tend to cross the line are an exterior strobe or horn, a box or annunciator outside an entrance, a communicator antenna, and any visible conduit on a facade. The LPC's permit-types page says an exterior project needs a permit even if it needs no DOB permit or can be self-certified at DOB. Its Permit Guidebook asks that equipment stay off the primary facade unless there is no alternative, be small and discreet, be painted to match, and keep visible conduit to a minimum, roughly ten inches. Guidance on exterior fixtures says mounting points should go through mortar joints or plain masonry, not ornamental detail. And an LPC permit from the Prospect Park South district lists removing a fire alarm among the exterior work it approved, a small but useful sign that the Commission does review exterior alarm hardware rather than ignoring it.

Ordinary upkeep is usually simpler. The LPC Permit Guidebook says a permit is not required for ordinary maintenance and repair of safety equipment. Replacing a failed interior detector, changing batteries, repairing a cable fault, or swapping a like-for-like device indoors is maintenance. Moving an exterior device, adding a new one, or changing its size or finish is not. When a building already has an old exterior bell or strobe that nobody uses, removing it is an exterior change and should be treated as one.

Sequence the approvals so they do not collide. The practical problem is timing. An FDNY design may be finished and filed before anyone realizes it includes an exterior device, and then the LPC application starts late. We raise any exterior item at the survey, mark it on the plan, and ask the design professional whether an interior alternative meets the code. If the exterior item is truly needed, the owner or design team files with the LPC through Portico while the interior work proceeds, and the exterior device is installed only after approval.

Check who owns the building. The LPC cannot regulate state or federal property. Empire Stores and the Tobacco Warehouse were state-owned as of a 2013 appraisal prepared for Brooklyn Bridge Park, and we could not confirm their status today. For tenant work in either building, we ask the landlord which approvals apply rather than assuming. For a private co-op, condominium or commercial building in the district, Landmarks applies to the outside, and the National Register listing of 1974 adds no restrictions on a private owner using private funds, according to the National Park Service, unless federal funding or permitting is involved.

The Landing That Burned

A Terminal Lost to Fire, a Fireboat Station in Its Place: What the Landing's History Teaches About Detection

The old Fulton Ferry terminal was a wooden building at the water's edge, handling crowds at its busiest and, after the bridge opened, far fewer. The Brooklyn Public Library's account records a small fire there in 1910, a bigger one in 1919, and the fire of October 8, 1925 after which the building was demolished the following March. The city replaced it in 1926 with the shingled Marine Fire Boat Station, and by 1931 Fireboat Engine 77 was based there. We do not know, and the sources do not say, how any of those fires was discovered. What the story shows, in a neighborhood that still mixes old timber with crowds, is the value of finding a fire while it is small.

A fire in a building that is busy only part of the time. The terminal was crowded at certain hours and quiet at others, and much of Fulton Ferry still works that way. Restaurants and ice cream shops close for the night; offices empty at six; event spaces sit dark between performances. A fire in a space nobody is watching can grow before anyone smells it. Detection in those spaces, linked to a panel that is monitored or to the people who live above, turns a dark room into an early warning instead of a blind spot.

Timber and old construction. The district's buildings are brick on the outside, but many have timber floors, beams and roof framing, and some have concealed spaces between ceilings and the floors above. Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Fulton Ferry: Questions Answered

Each is answered fully here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

The facade, the Landmarks Commission and the order of approvals

The engineer's design for our building shows a strobe on the street wall. Who files with Landmarks, and will that hold up the FDNY filing?

The owner or the design team files the Landmarks application, through the LPC's Portico system, and it runs on its own track from the FDNY filing, so one does not have to stop the other if the sequence is planned. The LPC says exterior work in a historic district needs its permit even when the Department of Buildings requires none, and its Permit Guidebook prefers equipment off the primary facade unless there is no alternative, small, discreet and painted to match. So the first step is to ask the engineer whether the strobe can go somewhere less prominent, on a secondary wall, inside a recessed entrance, or indoors, and still meet the code's purpose. If it truly must go on the street wall, we help prepare what the application needs: a photo of the facade, the exact device, its size and finish, and a mounting detail that uses mortar joints or plain masonry rather than ornament, with the cable entering from behind so no conduit shows. The interior installation can proceed while the LPC reviews the exterior item, and the strobe goes up only after approval.

Our ground floor has a cast-iron front from the 1860s. How does fire alarm cable get from the cellar to the floors above without crossing it?

It goes up inside the building, never across the iron. Cast-iron fronts like the one on the Long Island Safe Deposit Company building at 1 Front Street, built in 1868 and 1869, are among the most protected surfaces in the district, and drilling them is the last thing anyone should do. Inside, these buildings usually offer a few practical routes: a stair enclosure, a utility chase or closet stacked floor over floor, a dumbwaiter or old shaft, or the back corner of each floor where a small surface raceway will not be noticed. We trace the options on the survey, choose the one that disturbs the least original fabric, and seal every floor penetration with fire-rated material so the path we make cannot carry smoke. Where a device is needed near the storefront itself, such as a detector inside the shop window display area, it goes on the ceiling or the inside face of the wall, with its cable coming from above. The street side stays exactly as it is.

Our business leases space in one of the big Water Street warehouses that was state-owned. Does the city's Landmarks Commission review our alarm work there?

Possibly not, but do not assume either way; ask the landlord in writing. The LPC says it cannot regulate state or federal property, only city, private and institutional property, and a 2013 appraisal prepared for Brooklyn Bridge Park listed Empire Stores and the Tobacco Warehouse as state-owned. We could not confirm their ownership or jurisdiction today, and leasing arrangements can change who controls what. In practice, an interior fit-out rarely raises a Landmarks question anyway, because interior work generally does not need LPC approval. What will certainly apply is the city's fire alarm process and the landlord's own rules: an FDNY filing where the work requires it, the base-building vendor's procedures for connecting to the panel, and the landlord's standards for devices and test times. Before we price a suite in one of those buildings, we ask for the landlord's tenant guidelines and the base-building fire alarm contact, because those two documents shape the job far more than ownership does.

Buildings that were warehouses and offices first

Our co-op's fire alarm went in during the conversion decades ago, and nobody on the board knows whether it was ever filed with or accepted by the FDNY. How do we find out?

Start with what the building has, then fill the gaps from the city. Look in the board's files and the managing agent's archive for any FDNY letter of approval, inspection report, certificate or drawings with a filing number; look on or inside the panel door for a sticker or record card; and ask the central station company, if the building has one, what account information they hold. A Certificate of Occupancy or Schedule A matters too, since the FDNY application asks for one. On our survey we document the system as it stands: panel make and condition, every device and its location, the risers, and whether the layout matches any drawings you find. That gives the board a clear picture of whether the system can be maintained and repaired like-for-like, or whether a change, such as moving or replacing the panel, will require new plans filed with the FDNY. If records cannot be found, the design professional on a future filing will work from the as-found survey, and the board will have the paperwork it should have had all along.

Our loft has very high ceilings crossed by heavy timber beams. Where does a smoke detector have to go so it sees smoke early?

It goes where smoke from a likely fire will collect, which in a beamed ceiling is usually not the spot that looks tidiest. Smoke rises and spreads along the ceiling, and deep beams can trap it in the pockets between them, so a detector on the wrong side of a beam may wait while the pocket fills elsewhere. The manufacturer's instructions and the fire alarm standards both address beams, ceiling height and spacing, and we follow them for the specific ceiling in front of us, measuring beam depth and spacing before choosing positions. The other priorities are distance and audibility: a detector close enough to the sleeping area to sound early, and an alarm loud enough to wake someone behind a partial wall or up on a platform. Then maintenance: a device fourteen feet up must be testable, so we favor hardwired alarms with battery backup or sealed long-life units, linked together, and we leave the owner with a note of what ladder reaches them. If the loft is tied into a building system, the board's requirements come first.

Our building was a storage warehouse and still has vaults in the cellar that hold lockers and old equipment. Do the vaults need their own detection?

If they hold storage, wiring or equipment, they should be in the plan. The Eagle Warehouse is the local example of a storage building with cellar vaults, and in converted buildings those spaces often become storage lockers, meter rooms or places where old building equipment sits forgotten. They are enclosed, often dark and rarely visited, so a fire there could develop unseen beneath the whole building. The conditions underground matter for the device choice: vaults can be damp and dusty, which makes ordinary smoke detectors prone to false alarms or contamination, so heat detection is often more dependable in the vault itself, with smoke detection in the corridor or stair outside where smoke would travel. If the vaults extend under the sidewalk, we treat them as part of the cellar in the plan. If the building has a system, the vault devices report to the panel; if it is on the household path, they are linked to alarms the residents can hear. We also tell the board about anything we see down there that adds fuel or ignition risk.

Our board turns over every year and the fire alarm records keep getting lost between treasurers. How do we keep the system from becoming nobody's job?

Give the system a single home and a single page. In small self-run co-ops, the knowledge about the fire alarm tends to live in one person's head or one person's email, and it leaves when they do. At handover we provide the board with a short binder and the same material as files: a floor-by-floor device list that matches the labels on the devices, the panel's make and the meaning of its common displays, the test results, the monitoring company's contact and account details where there is one, and a call list for who to notify when the panel shows trouble. Keep it with the building's other permanent records, not with an individual, and name one board role, rather than one person, as the fire alarm contact. Industry summaries describe NYC testing on a regular cycle, including semi-annual functional tests and a full retest every five years, so put those dates on the board's calendar, and hand the binder to the incoming officer at every election.

Cafés, tenant spaces and public courtyards

Our café is fully sprinklered because of the cooking equipment. Does having sprinklers mean we also need a full fire alarm system?

Not automatically. The 2022 Fire Code requires sprinklers in many occupancies and within 30 feet of commercial cooking equipment, which is why many small food businesses around the landing have them. But Built Engineers' summary of NYC requirements notes that sprinklers alone do not trigger a full building fire alarm; whether a fire alarm system is required depends on the occupancy and the conditions set out in Section 907 of the Building Code. A sprinkler system usually does have its own supervisory and waterflow signals, and in many buildings those report to a panel and a central station, which is not the same as a full detection and notification system throughout. What we check on the survey is the café's occupancy and size, what else is in the building, whether there are apartments or offices above that need warning, and what the existing sprinkler monitoring includes. Then we tell you whether you need smoke and heat detection tied together, an addition to the building's system, or a system with filed plans.

Our suite opens onto a courtyard the public walks through. Does the fire alarm have to reach people who are outside our walls but still inside the building?

The building's fire alarm design answers that, and it is a fair question to ask the landlord before your fit-out is drawn. Empire Stores, for example, has a four-story open-air courtyard and a publicly accessible rooftop terrace, and buildings designed that way have to consider how people in open, shared spaces will learn of an emergency. Those decisions belong to the base-building design, not to an individual tenant. For your suite, our job is to make sure your space's devices are installed where the approved drawings put them and work with the building's notification, so that people in your space hear and see the alarm and the building system knows about a fire that starts with you. If your fit-out changes how people use the edge of the courtyard, for example by adding seating or a door, raise it with the landlord and your design professional early, because changes to how a space is occupied can change what the fire alarm needs to do. We do not change base-building notification on our own authority.

The landlord says our fit-out can be professionally certified instead of going through FDNY plan exam and inspection. When is that allowed?

Sometimes, and the boundary is the building's core system. FDNY rule amendments under FC 104.2.1 allow professional certification of fire alarm design and installation in place of FDNY plan examination and inspection, but they exclude the core building fire alarm system. So a tenant scope that adds or relocates devices within a suite may qualify, while work that changes the building's core system, its panel or its overall sequence of operation does not. The registered design professional preparing your drawings decides which path applies and takes responsibility for the certification. Our part does not change much either way: we install to the approved or certified drawings, label every device to match the panel's program, coordinate with the base-building vendor who adds your devices to the system, and pre-test the work completely. What changes is the paperwork and the timeline, so ask your designer at the outset which path they intend to use and plan the schedule around it.

The building manager mentioned an in-building radio system for firefighters. Is that part of the fire alarm, and is it in your scope?

It is a separate system with its own permit, and it is not part of our fire alarm scope. The FDNY requires a permit to operate an in-building auxiliary radio communication system, which helps firefighters' radios work inside large buildings where masonry, steel and depth block signals. Big buildings and buildings with deep cellars are where it tends to come up. Because it often surfaces in the same meetings as fire alarm work, owners sometimes assume the fire alarm contractor handles it. We do not design, install or certify those radio systems. What we can do is coordinate: keep our cable paths and equipment clear of the radio system's components, avoid disturbing them during a fit-out, and make sure the building's documentation lists both systems and who maintains each. If you are not sure whether your building has one, the building's records or the managing agent should know, and the FDNY permit page explains what is required.

We are putting up a small new retail building on an empty lot inside the district. When does the fire alarm need to be designed?

At the same time as the building, before the drawings are final. On a new building, the LPC reviews the exterior, the Department of Buildings reviews construction, and the fire alarm, if the occupancy requires one, goes to the FDNY with plans approved by a registered engineer or architect, a riser diagram, floor plans and an operating sequence. Designing the fire alarm early lets the architect hide what must be hidden: a panel location indoors, cable paths built into the walls, and no surprise exterior strobe that has to go back to the LPC after the facade is approved. Fulton Ferry Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Is preparing a Landmarks application included in your price?
+

Our price covers the fire alarm installation itself. Most Fulton Ferry jobs are entirely interior and need no Landmarks application at all. If you want us to assemble that package, we list it as a separate line in the written quote so it is clear before you agree.

Our board already hired an engineer for the building. Can you work from their drawings?
+

Yes. On filed work we expect to install to a registered design professional's approved drawings. We can survey the building first and give your engineer the as-found conditions, including panel details, device locations and cable routes, then install, label and pre-test exactly to the approved plan. If we see a conflict between the drawings and the building, we raise it with the engineer rather than improvising in the field.

Can the cable run in surface raceway across exposed brick and timber, or must it be hidden?
+

In many lofts there is no hollow wall to hide it, so neat surface raceway is the honest answer. We route it along beams, corners and edges where it is least visible, choose a finish that suits the room, fasten into mortar joints rather than brick faces where possible, and agree the route with you before starting. Where a wireless linked alarm avoids visible cable altogether and suits the unit, we will tell you.

Our open loft kitchen is only a few steps from the sleeping area. Which detector goes where?
+

We keep smoke detection near the sleeping area but out of the direct path of cooking smoke and steam, and use a heat detector or a model suited to locations near kitchens where the manufacturer allows it. The goal is an alarm that wakes you for a real fire without sounding every time you cook. We measure the distances on site and explain each position before mounting anything.

The new rooftop air handling unit for our building calls for a duct smoke detector. Is that part of the fire alarm job?
+

Usually it is a shared item. When the mechanical design calls for a duct detector, the mechanical contractor typically mounts the housing in the ductwork and the fire alarm side wires it, connects it to the panel where the building has one, and tests that it shuts the unit down as designed. We coordinate with the mechanical contractor on who does what before the roof work starts, so nothing is left unconnected.

Our building's elevator is supposed to return to the lobby when the fire alarm activates. Can you test that?
+

Where the building's design includes elevator recall tied to the fire alarm, testing it means working with your elevator maintenance company, because both systems are involved. We schedule a joint test, trigger the devices that are meant to start recall, confirm the panel sends the right signals, and record the results. Any fault on the elevator side goes to the elevator company in writing.

How do we know when the standby batteries in our building panel should be replaced?
+

Every battery we install is dated, and we test standby batteries during inspections and service visits against the panel manufacturer's requirements. Replace them when they fail a test or reach the age the manufacturer recommends, whichever comes first.
