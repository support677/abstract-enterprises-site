The Neighborhood

Why Boerum Hill Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Boerum Hill is a neighborhood of three-story rowhouses, most of them put up between about 1840 and 1870, set between Schermerhorn Street, Fourth Avenue, Smith Street and Warren or Wyckoff Street depending on whose map you use. The name itself is younger than the houses; it was coined in 1964 for an area people had been calling North Gowanus. None of those houses was built with anywhere for a data cable to go. They have cellars, stairs, closets, chimney breasts and solid brick walls shared with the neighbors, and today's network has to be fitted into whichever of those an owner is willing to touch. In a neighborhood where much of the housing stock is landmarked and much of the interior has been carefully restored, that willingness is often limited, and the route matters more than the cable.

The service at the curb is not the issue. Untapped Cities, ranking Brooklyn neighborhoods from residents' own speed tests, placed Boerum Hill sixth in the borough, with a 90th-percentile average of 205.23 Mbps and a fastest recorded test of 591.46 Mbps, and listed Spectrum, Optimum, Verizon Fios, RCN and several smaller carriers among the providers seen there. Its explanation was blunt: "the speed test readings suggest that residents here are willing to pay well for strong residential connections." People here buy good plans. Then the plan arrives at a terminal on the front wall of the cellar, a router goes on the parlor floor or the cellar shelf, and a household that is paying for a fast line spends its evenings walking around the house looking for a bar of signal.

The houses themselves explain why. Brick party walls on both sides stop a signal dead. Floors are deep, and plaster on wood lath absorbs more than drywall. Several forum posters on Brownstoner, writing about Brooklyn rowhouses, have described a feature many Boerum Hill parlors and cellars share: pressed-tin ceilings, which one owner said "do not let radio waves to penetrate" to the back of his first floor and basement. Rear extensions, added to almost every block over the last century, put the kitchen and family room beyond the back wall of the original house, often with no cellar beneath them and a solid masonry wall between them and the router. Each of those features takes something away from Wi-Fi. The answer that survives all of them is a cable to each floor, ending at a jack or an access point near where people sit.

The second reason the question comes up here is renovation. Boerum Hill houses are worked on constantly, and the best time to add cable is when a wall or ceiling is already open. Brownstoner's "The Insider" series described one landmarked nineteenth-century Boerum Hill townhouse where the owners swapped a gas boiler and radiators for a heat pump with an energy recovery ventilator, restored the original plasterwork, pine floors and trim, and chose not to insulate the front and rear walls because that "would have required extensive demolition." A house like that has had its systems opened up and closed again with real care. A network cable added during that work costs a fraction of one fished through restored rooms afterward, and a cable added carelessly afterward can damage exactly what the owners paid to save.

The reverse happens too. A former owner ran Cat5 to a room that is no longer an office, the cable company drilled a third hole through the front wall, a mesh kit was added, then a second one. We often find three generations of wire in a single Boerum Hill cellar: cloth-covered telephone pairs, coax from providers that have changed names twice, and gray Cat5 that nobody has tested. Some of it is worth keeping. Some of it is the best pull string the house has. Some of it should simply come out. Sorting that out before quoting is part of the job.

Six Boerum Hill facts that shape a cabling plan

  • Fast service is already here. Untapped Cities ranked Boerum Hill sixth in Brooklyn for measured speed. The job is usually moving that speed through the building, not finding a better line.
  • Much of the housing is landmarked. The Boerum Hill Historic District was designated on November 20, 1973, and an extension of about 288 more buildings was designated on June 26, 2018. The Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews most changes to the outside of those buildings, including a new line on a front wall.
  • The houses are old and narrow. Most are three-story rowhouses from roughly 1840 to 1870, Greek Revival and Italianate, with Second Empire and neo-Grec rows added in the 1880s. Side walls are shared masonry, so every vertical route is inside the house.
  • Walk-ups and divided houses are common. Pacific, Dean and Bergen Streets mix one-family houses with houses split into two or three apartments and small apartment buildings, each with one cellar and several households.
  • New buildings are arriving at the edges. 66 Boerum Place and 239 Atlantic Avenue added 75 apartments over a Marshall's and a Planet Fitness; permits for a 16-story, 99-unit building at 546 Pacific Street were filed in August 2026; and HPD's Third Act Apartments will put about 68 affordable homes for older adults on Third Avenue between Bergen and Wyckoff Streets.
  • The commercial strips are busy and narrow. The Atlantic Avenue business improvement district, approved in 2011, counts more than 400 businesses between Fourth Avenue and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, and Smith Street has been a restaurant row for decades.

Structured Cabling for Boerum Hill's Architecture

Almost every Boerum Hill call comes from one of six kinds of building. For a cabling crew, the differences between them have little to do with the style of the front and a lot to do with four practical questions: where the service comes in, which way is up, how much of the interior is original or restored, and who besides the owner decides. The cards give the short version; the sections after them explain how we work in each.

The restored rowhouse

A three- or four-floor house of the 1840s to 1870s kept as one home, with original plaster, trim and floors that the owners have spent years putting back.

The gut-renovated rowhouse

The same house opened to the studs, sometimes behind a landmark-approved front, where the walls are open for a few weeks and then closed for decades.

The house with rentals

An owner on the garden and parlor floors and one or two tenant households above, sharing one cellar and one way in for every provider.

The converted condo

A rowhouse split into two or three condo units, where the cellar, the roof and the stair belong to everyone and the board's rules decide what may be drilled.

The small walk-up

Brick apartment buildings of four to six stories along Pacific, Dean and Bergen Streets, with a cellar the owner controls and a building network separate from residents' service.

The new building

Recent and planned buildings on Atlantic Avenue, Boerum Place, Pacific Street and Third Avenue, with concrete floors and telecom rooms the developer designed.

The restored rowhouse: every route has to respect what the owners saved

In a Boerum Hill rowhouse kept as one home, the interior is often the reason the family bought it. The parlor has its plaster cornice and ceiling medallion, the hall has its original stair rail, the floors are wide pine that someone spent a summer refinishing. The long side walls are brick party walls shared with the neighbors, so no cable goes into them. That leaves a short list of places a cable can climb: a stack of closets one above another, a plumbing chase beside the bathrooms, an old pipe or chimney flue that is no longer in use, the wall under the stair, and occasionally a run of ductwork chase left by a heating or cooling system. On the survey we look for the one that reaches the most floors without passing through a restored room.

Getting across each floor is the second problem. Rowhouse joists usually run from party wall to party wall, so a cable heading from the front of the house to the back has to cross every joist between them. Under a parlor with an open cellar below, that is easy: the cable comes up through the floor exactly where it is needed. On the upper floors, with finished plaster below, the choices are a careful series of small openings that a plasterer closes, a route behind a baseboard that is lifted and reset, a path along a door casing, or a painted surface raceway in a closet or along the top of a baseboard. We show you each option on the walk-through, say which rooms would see a hole, and write the one you choose into the quote.

The gut-renovated rowhouse: the walls are open once, so plan for twenty years

When a Boerum Hill house is taken back to the studs, the cabling question changes from "how do we get through" to "what will this house need for the next twenty years." Brownstoner's forum has a long trail of owners asking it at exactly this moment. One, renovating a two-family with the walls open, had a general contractor willing to pull cable but wanted someone to design the layout and terminate the ports. The advice in that thread is close to what we would recommend: bring every room's cable back to one central home-run panel, run conduit from outside so providers have a ready way in, put at least one cable in every room, and run cable into closets or ceilings for wireless access points. Another owner, writing after his own renovation, said his one regret was putting the network hub in an upstairs office instead of somewhere central like the basement.

We add a few things to that list. Leave an empty conduit, with a pull string, from the cellar to the top floor next to the cables, because something you have not thought of will want to go up the house later. Put access point cables in the ceiling at the middle of each floor rather than in a corner. Run a cable to the place the TV will hang and to every desk location the architect has drawn, plus one spare. Bring the provider's line in at one point, through one sealed opening, with room for a second provider. And test everything before the drywall or plaster goes up, because once it is closed, a bad termination behind a wall is a repair job instead of a five-minute fix.

The house with rentals: one cellar, several households

A great many Boerum Hill rowhouses hold an owner on the lower floors and one or two rental apartments above. There is usually a single service entry in a cellar the owner controls, and each household either has its own provider account or the owner supplies internet as part of the rent. Either way, each apartment needs its own clean cable path from the cellar, its own labeled termination at the panel, and its own equipment, so that one tenant's router never sits on another's network and nobody has to enter an apartment to restart the owner's equipment. We plan the cellar as the meeting point and the stair or a closet stack as the shared riser, with each household's cables clearly marked.

Where tenants' apartments are involved, the work also needs notice and access agreed in advance. We schedule the parts of the job inside a rental for the times the tenant has agreed to, keep the visit inside each apartment as short as possible by doing the cellar and riser work first, and leave each tenant's jack labeled with their unit. Owners sometimes ask us to leave a spare cable to each apartment for the next tenant's provider. It is a good habit; it means the next installer plugs in rather than drills.

The converted condo: the board owns the way up

Some Boerum Hill rowhouses have been divided into two or three condominium units, typically a garden-level duplex and one or two upper units. In those buildings the cellar, the roof, the stair hall and the walls between units are common elements. A cable from the provider's entry in the cellar to an upper unit almost always crosses one of them. Before we drill, the board has to agree in writing to the route, the opening sizes and the work hours, and usually wants a certificate of insurance naming the condominium. In a small building that board may be two or three neighbors around a kitchen table, but the approval still matters, because the next owner of the unit above will want to know how a cable got into their floor.

Inside a unit, the work is the same as in any rowhouse floor, only shorter. The harder piece is usually the shared riser. Where the building has a common chase or a closet stack, we propose a sleeve that each unit can use, labeled by unit, so that three owners do not end up with three separate holes through the same floor. Where it does not, we look at surface raceway in the stair hall painted to match, which the board can approve without anyone opening a wall.

The small walk-up: the owner's network and the residents' service

The brick apartment buildings scattered along Pacific, Dean and Bergen Streets, typically four to six stories with a cellar, a stair and no elevator, have two separate cabling needs. Residents get internet from their own providers through whatever wiring the building offers. The building itself increasingly needs a network of its own: cameras in the vestibule and halls, an entry panel, a laundry card reader, a boiler controller and, sometimes, a superintendent's computer. That building network is the owner's responsibility and belongs in its own locked cabinet in the cellar, on its own equipment, never sharing a router with anyone's apartment.

Getting a cable up a masonry walk-up to a camera on the fourth-floor landing is where honesty matters. If the building has an old riser closet, a pipe chase or a dumbwaiter that has been sealed off, we use it. If it does not, a painted surface raceway up the stair hall is usually the right answer, and we say so on the survey instead of promising a hidden route through solid brick. Every opening between floors is sealed with listed firestop as the work goes, because in a building where several households share one stair, a hole between floors is a smoke path.

The new building: the developer's telecom room and your apartment

The new buildings at the edges of Boerum Hill come with their cabling designed in. CityRealty described the mixed-use building at 66 Boerum Place and 239 Atlantic Avenue as eleven floors with 75 apartments over a Marshall's of 27,000 square feet and a Planet Fitness of about 20,000 square feet. New York YIMBY reported permits filed in August 2026 for 546 Pacific Street, between Third and Fourth Avenues, at 16 stories and 99 apartments. Buildings like these have telecom rooms, a building provider and a jack or two in each unit, and a renter in one rarely needs, or is allowed, new cable through the concrete.

What residents of those buildings ask us for instead is a wired connection from the unit's existing outlet to a desk in the second bedroom, a better spot for the router than the floor of the entry closet, or an access point in the middle of a long apartment. Those jobs are done with surface raceway, door-casing routes and removable mounts that come down at the end of the lease. For developers, the time to bring in a cabling contractor is before the walls close, when conduit to each unit and a properly ventilated telecom closet cost little compared with fixing them later.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Walk a Boerum Hill Rowhouse Before Anyone Picks Up a Drill

The survey in a Boerum Hill rowhouse follows the same order every time, because the order prevents the expensive mistakes. It starts at the front wall of the cellar, where the provider's line comes in, usually under the stoop. We note what is there today, a fiber terminal, a cable modem, an old telephone block or all three, which cables leaving it carry service, and how many holes earlier providers have drilled through the foundation. Then we look up. Open joists mean the cellar can serve as the main hallway of the network. A finished cellar ceiling, which is common in houses where the garden floor has been made into living space, means looking for a soffit, an access panel or a route along the edge where ceiling meets wall.

Next we go up the house, floor by floor, and look for two things at once: a way up, and the places the cable needs to end. Closets are opened. The wall under the stair is tapped and measured. Any old pipe that comes up through a floor is noted and traced; in houses this old that can be an abandoned gas-light line, a heating pipe or a waste stack, and only one of those is ever a candidate. We ask where people work, where the TV is or will be, and where the signal is worst today, and we look up at each ceiling for pressed tin, plaster ornament or a skylight well that rules out an access point in the obvious spot.

Last, we test what is already in the walls. Old Cat5, phone wire and coax are toned to find their far ends and, where they might be reused, checked on a certifier. In our experience the old cable worth keeping in a house this age is a run that tests clean end to end and ends somewhere useful; a run that fails is still valuable if it follows the path the new cable needs, because we can tie the replacement to it and pull it through. By the end of the survey we can sketch each floor, mark every opening, and price the job. That sketch becomes the written quote, and after the work, the port map.

Pathways

From the Cellar Under the Stoop to the Kitchen Behind the Back Wall: How Cable Moves Through a Boerum Hill House

The two hardest runs in most Boerum Hill houses point in opposite directions. One goes up, from the cellar at the front of the house to a top-floor bedroom or study at the back. The other goes back, from the original house into a rear extension where the family actually spends its evenings. A straight line rarely works for either. A good route goes up somewhere hidden and then across somewhere hidden, and choosing those two places is most of the design work on the job.

Going up, the options are, in rough order of preference: an existing conduit or empty pipe that has been confirmed safe to use; a stack of closets; a plumbing or ductwork chase with room to spare; a chimney flue a mason has confirmed is out of service; and the stair wall, entered from below and reached on each landing. Each has a cost. A closet stack gives up a few inches of a closet corner on each floor. A chase means coordinating with whoever opens it next. A flue needs a sleeve and a firestop at every floor. The stair wall means small openings on each landing that a plasterer closes. We tell you which your house offers and which we recommend, and why.

Going back into an extension is a different problem. Most rear extensions were built against the original back wall, which is solid masonry, and many sit on a slab or a shallow crawl space rather than over the cellar. The cable has to pass through that old wall once, at a point where both sides can be finished neatly, and then reach the room on the far side. Sometimes the cellar reaches under the extension and the job is easy. Sometimes the route is up into the parlor floor, through the back wall at baseboard height inside a cabinet or a closet, and down into the extension ceiling. We look at both sides of the wall before choosing the spot, because a hole in the wrong place comes out behind a kitchen cabinet that cannot be moved.

Openings sealed the same day

Every hole between floors is a path for air, smoke, sound and mice. Each one is sealed as soon as the cable is through, with listed firestop where it passes from one floor to the next or from the cellar into the house, and with a bushing where it passes metal. Where a cable has to leave the house, for an access point in the garden or a camera at the back, it goes through one sealed opening with a drip loop on the outside and surge protection where it comes back in. In houses where owners have spent money on air sealing and new windows, that matters twice over: an unsealed cable hole undoes work someone paid for.

Data kept away from power, heat and older systems

Network cable shares a Boerum Hill house with electrical wiring, heating pipes, gas lines, intercom and doorbell wire and, in many houses, the remains of an older alarm. We keep data cable apart from electrical lines where they run side by side, cross them at right angles where they must meet, and do not pull data through the same holes as new power wiring. Cable stays clear of boiler flues, steam risers and hot water pipes. And where an older system already occupies a closet or a chase, we route around it and label our cables, so the next person who opens that closet knows which wires belong to the network.

Service Entry

Fios, Spectrum, Optimum or a Building Provider: Where Boerum Hill Service Arrives and Where the Network Should Start

Before we plan any run, we need to know how internet reaches your building, because that decides where the network starts. In a typical Boerum Hill rowhouse the provider's line comes in at the front of the cellar, under or beside the stoop, either through the foundation wall from the street or down from an aerial drop. Verizon Fios ends at a fiber terminal on the wall; Spectrum and Optimum end at a coax splitter and a modem. In the newer buildings the service reaches each apartment over the building's own wiring from a telecom room, and some buildings have their own provider arrangements. Brooklyn Fiber, for example, lists buildings under an area it labels "Downtown Brooklyn-DUMBO-Boerum Hill," though whether any particular Boerum Hill address is served is something to check with the provider.

The provider's technician puts the terminal where it is quickest to install, which is usually right where the line comes in, and plugs the router in beside it. That is why so many Boerum Hill households have their router on a cellar shelf or in the front parlor, as far as possible from the top-floor back bedroom and the extension kitchen. Structured cabling separates the two jobs. The provider's equipment stays at the entry; one cable carries the service from there to a better central spot; and the house network spreads from that spot to every floor. In many rowhouses the best central spot is not a room at all, but the bottom of the closet stack in the cellar, directly under the route that goes up.

Switching providers is common, and a good plan makes it painless. If every room's cable returns to one panel, moving from coax to fiber, or from one fiber provider to another, changes only the short link between the provider's box and your router. Nothing in the walls moves. We recommend leaving one spare cable, or an empty conduit with a pull string, from the provider's entry to the panel, so the next installer has a ready path instead of drilling another hole through the front of a house that may be landmarked. Owners of divided houses who have watched each tenant's provider make a new opening in the foundation will see the point immediately.

Rules and Licenses

Permits, Landmarks, Boards and Leases: What Governs a Boerum Hill Cabling Job

The electrical permit question. Since December 21, 2025, Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7, added by Local Law 128 of 2024, has said that an electrical permit is not required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. Network cabling falls inside that description. That is not the same as "anything goes." Cable still has to carry the right rating for the space it passes through, openings between floors still need firestop, and fire alarm wiring is a separate discipline with its own licensing and permits. Work on the power side, such as a new outlet beside the equipment panel or a dedicated circuit for a rack, is an electrician's job and is not part of what we install.

Our license. We work under New York State license #12000287431 and carry insurance for low-voltage work. A certificate of insurance goes to the owner, the condo board, the managing agent or the landlord before the crew arrives, naming whoever the building asks us to name.

Landmarks. A large share of Boerum Hill sits in the historic district designated in 1973 or the extension designated in 2018, and the State Street Houses, a row of 23 houses on State Street between Smith and Hoyt, were landmarked on the same day in 1973 as the original district. Interior cabling is generally not something the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews. The outside of the building is. A new provider line run down a front wall, a conduit across a facade, or equipment on a roof that can be seen from the street all fall within the Commission's interest, and its permit guidebook says that where an installation needs conduit, the conduit must be concealed. Check your address on the Commission's map before any exterior work, and we will plan the job so nothing new appears on a landmarked front without the right permit.

Condo boards and managing agents. In converted rowhouse condos and in the newer condo buildings, the board's rules decide which common areas may be opened, when work may happen and what insurance is needed. For most cabling jobs the paperwork is modest: a scope letter describing each opening, a certificate of insurance naming the condominium and the managing agent, and work inside the permitted hours.

Leases. A tenant needs the landlord's written permission before anything is drilled, and we will not drill a rental apartment without it. Owners of two- and three-family houses need to give tenants proper notice before work inside their apartments. Commercial tenants on Atlantic Avenue or Smith Street need written landlord approval for any drilling, and we ask for it before booking the job.

Party walls and neighbors. The side walls of a Boerum Hill rowhouse are shared. We do not chase cable into a party wall, and we never route anything through a neighbor's cellar or across a neighbor's roof without the neighbor's written consent. If the only practical way for a provider's line to reach your house crosses a neighbor's property, that conversation happens between you, the neighbor and the provider before it becomes a job for us.

This section is a plain-language summary of the rules as we understand them for planning a job, not legal advice. For a specific building, the board's documents, the lease and the Commission's staff have the final word.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Boerum Hill: Questions Answered

These are the situations Boerum Hill owners, renters and businesses describe when they call: features of old houses that fight Wi-Fi, renovations and energy retrofits, speeds that never arrive where they are needed, roofs and neighbors, and two kinds of business with particular needs. Each question is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

What an old rowhouse already has inside it

Our parlor floor and cellar both have pressed-tin ceilings, and the Wi-Fi dies at the back of both. Is the metal really the cause, and what gets around it?

Very likely, yes. A pressed-tin ceiling is a continuous sheet of metal, and metal reflects and blocks radio signals far more than plaster or wood. A Brooklyn rowhouse owner on Brownstoner's forum described exactly this, saying his tin ceilings did not let the signal through to the back of the first floor and the basement. The way around it is not a stronger router but a second source of signal on the far side of the metal: a cable to an access point at the back of the parlor floor, mounted on a wall or in a ceiling that is not tin, and another for the cellar if anyone uses it. We do not screw equipment into original tin; we mount beside it, or on a wall, and bring the cable up from below so nothing on the ceiling is disturbed.

An old metal pipe comes up through our cellar ceiling and disappears into the walls of our 1850s house. Could it carry new cable to the upper floors?

Possibly, but only after we know exactly what it is. In houses this old, a pipe running up through the floors may be an abandoned gas-light line, part of an old heating system or something still in service, and we never put a cable into any pipe until its purpose is confirmed and it is proven disconnected at both ends. Brownstoner forum posters who have tried the idea point out the practical problem too: old gas pipe has tight bends at its fittings, which makes pulling cable through it hard or impossible over any distance. Where a pipe is dead, reasonably straight and big enough, we can sometimes use a short section of it as a sleeve through a floor. More often, its value is that it tells us where an earlier builder already found a way up, and the cable follows the same chase beside it.

A home inspector told us our network panel should be grounded. Is that right, and who would do it?

It is reasonable advice, with a qualification. Network cable itself carries low-voltage data and does not need a ground of its own in an ordinary house, but some of what connects to it does: a surge protector where a cable enters from outside, a metal equipment cabinet, a rack, and the provider's own entry equipment. A home inspector writing on Brownstoner's forum described the approach his electrician recommended, a separate low-voltage grounding bus tied to the house's grounding system, so low-voltage technicians never need to open the electrical panel. That is the right division of labor. We specify what needs a bonding connection and where, and the connection to the house's electrical grounding system is made by a licensed electrician.

Renovations and energy retrofits

We replaced our boiler with a heat pump, and the electrician says the house's electrical service is now close to its limit. Will a network cabinet need new power?

Usually not. A household network, a router, a switch that powers a few access points over their cables, and perhaps a small battery backup, draws a small fraction of what a heat pump does, and in most houses it runs from one ordinary outlet near the panel. Brownstoner's feature on a landmarked Boerum Hill townhouse noted that its move to electric heat "had maxed out the house's electrical capacity," which is a real constraint for big appliances, not usually for networking gear. We list the equipment and its power rating on the quote so your electrician can check it against the service. If the best spot for the cabinet has no outlet, the electrician adds one; that is power work, and it is not something we install.

Our walls are open for a gut renovation. Is it worth leaving some fiber in them for the future, even if nothing will use it yet?

In some houses, yes, but think of it as a conduit question first. A former communications designer on Brownstoner's forum made the useful point that unterminated fiber is inexpensive to pull but costly to terminate later. For most Boerum Hill homes, the better insurance is an empty conduit with a pull string from the cellar to the top floor, running beside the copper cables. It costs little while the walls are open, and it lets you pull whatever the future needs, fiber included, without opening a wall again. Fiber inside a house makes most sense where a run is very long or where a building's backbone between floors needs it; we will tell you if yours does.

Our kitchen is in a rear extension with no cellar underneath, and it has the weakest signal in the house. How does a cable even get back there?

Through the old back wall, once, at the right spot. The extension was built against what used to be the outside of the house, so between the router and the kitchen there is usually a thick masonry wall. We look at both sides before choosing where to go through it, because the cable has to come out somewhere that can be finished neatly, not behind a fixed cabinet or a range hood. If the cellar reaches under part of the extension, the cable goes along the cellar and up. If it does not, the usual route is up into the parlor floor and through the back wall inside a closet or cabinet, then into the extension ceiling, where a single access point covers the kitchen and family room. If the extension has a roof deck or a yard door, the same cable path can serve those later.

When the speed never shows up

We pay for a fast plan, but even a laptop plugged in by cable tests far below it. Is the cable in our wall the problem?

It might be, and it is easy to find out. Test first with the laptop plugged straight into the router with a short new cord. If that test is close to your plan, the router and the line are fine, and the problem lies in the run between the router and the wall jack you were using. Old Cat5 with a damaged pair, a termination that was never finished properly or a cheap coupler hidden behind a plate can all hold a connection to a fraction of its speed. If the direct test is also slow, the problem is the plan, the provider's equipment or the laptop, and new cabling will not fix it. We certify the existing run on site, which shows exactly what it can carry, and tell you plainly which case you are in.

We cannot wire every device at once. Which ones in a family house are worth a cable first?

Anything that stays put and moves a lot of data. In order, that is usually one access point per floor, which improves every phone and laptop at once; the desk where someone works or takes video calls; the main TV and whatever streams to it; a game console if anyone plays online, because a cable steadies the connection more than raw speed does; and any camera recorder or home server. Phones, tablets and laptops that move around the house stay on Wi-Fi and simply get a better signal from the nearby access point. If the budget allows only one floor now, cable the access point on the floor where the family spends its evenings, and leave a pull string for the rest.

Roofs, yards and neighbors

We have a roof deck reached through a hatch. Can we get Wi-Fi up there without anything on the roof that can be seen from the street?

Usually, yes. The simplest approach puts an access point on the top floor ceiling near the hatch or a rear window, aimed at the deck, which keeps all the equipment indoors. Where that is not enough, a small outdoor access point can be mounted low on the bulkhead or a rear parapet, below the line of sight from the sidewalk, with its cable coming through a sealed opening from inside. In the historic district, anything added to a roof that can be seen from a public street can fall within the Landmarks Preservation Commission's review, so we keep equipment off the front of the roof and below parapets, and tell you before the work if your address and the location mean a permit question.

The house next door is being gut-renovated, and since their crew started drilling, part of our network has stopped working. What should we do?

Write down when it started, and have the runs tested before anyone argues. Rowhouse cellars share a party wall, and cables, pipes and old wires sometimes run close to it, so a neighbor's demolition can disturb a run on your side, especially where an earlier provider brought a line across from next door. We tone and certify the dead runs, find where each one fails, and photograph what we find. That gives you a factual record to share with your neighbor's contractor. Often the fix is a short re-route away from the shared wall. If the damage turns out to be on your neighbor's side, the record shows it, and the repair becomes a matter between the two households before it becomes a job for us.

Two kinds of Boerum Hill business

Our small media company on Atlantic Avenue moves large video files between editing stations and a shared storage box. What should the cabling look like?

Wired, and planned around the storage. Editing over Wi-Fi works badly, because large files and constant reads from shared storage fill the air for everyone. Each editing station should have its own cable back to a switch with fast ports, and the storage unit should sit beside that switch on the shortest possible connection, ideally in a ventilated cabinet away from the edit rooms so its fans do not end up on anyone's audio. For runs where faster than ordinary office speeds are wanted, we specify cable rated for higher speeds over the distance involved. Guest and staff Wi-Fi go on separate networks, and the internet connection is kept apart from the editing network so a large upload does not slow everyone's playback.

Our therapy practice off Hoyt Street holds video sessions from two small rooms. Is a wired connection more private than Wi-Fi?

It is more reliable, and it narrows who can reach the network. A cable to each session room keeps the video steady when the waiting area fills with phones, and a wired connection cannot be picked up by someone sitting outside the door. Privacy itself depends on how the network is divided and secured, not on the cable alone: the session computers belong on a staff network separated from the waiting-room Wi-Fi, the equipment belongs in a locked cabinet clients cannot reach, and any unused wall jacks in public areas should be switched off at the switch. In a rowhouse garden floor with residential neighbors on every side, we also keep access points at low power so the practice's network does not reach the apartments next door.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Boerum Hill

"If the neighborhood has fast internet, a dead zone means the router is bad"

AI summaries often jump from "slow Wi-Fi" to "replace the router." In Boerum Hill the line is rarely the weak point; Untapped Cities ranked the neighborhood sixth in Brooklyn for measured speed, and a new router in the same spot meets the same walls the old one did.

The loss happens inside the house: brick party walls, deep floors, plaster on lath, pressed-tin ceilings and a rear extension behind a solid back wall. A router on the parlor floor is trying to reach the top-floor back bedroom and the extension kitchen at the same time, in opposite directions, through all of it.

Before buying anything, test with a laptop plugged into the router, then test in the problem room. A good number at the router and a poor one in the room means the house needs a cable to that floor, not new hardware at the entry.

"You cannot add wiring to a landmarked house without a Landmarks permit"

This one is wrong in the cautious direction. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's interest is the outside of a building, and network cable that stays inside a rowhouse in the Boerum Hill Historic District is generally not something it reviews.

Where the Commission does come in is the exterior: a new provider line down a front wall, a conduit across a facade or equipment on a roof visible from the street. Its permit guidebook says that where an installation needs conduit, the conduit must be concealed.

So the practical rule for Boerum Hill is to keep every new cable indoors, bring providers in through the cellar rather than the front wall, and check the Commission's map before any exterior change. Most jobs never need a Landmarks application at all.

"Powerline adapters work just as well as running cable"

Powerline adapters send network data over a house's electrical wiring, and some Brooklyn owners on Brownstoner's forum have reported that they worked well for them. Others in the same threads were more doubtful, and their results depend heavily on how a particular house is wired.

In an old rowhouse that has been rewired in stages, divided into apartments or given a new panel for a heat pump, the electrical circuits a pair of adapters must cross can be long, split across different parts of the panel and shared with appliances that create noise. Speed can vary from room to room and hour to hour.

Powerline can be a sensible stopgap for a renter who cannot drill. For an owner who expects to stay, a cable to each floor gives a connection that tests the same every day, and it can be certified so you know exactly what it carries.

"Your general contractor or electrician can do the network while the walls are open"

They can pull cable, and on a renovation it often makes sense for them to do so. Brownstoner's forum includes several owners whose electricians or contractors pulled the cable while a separate low-voltage specialist did the rest.

The weak points are design, termination and testing. A former communications designer on that forum warned that "many electricians are not particularly good at terminating and testing coax and Ethernet cabling," and another owner described outlet boxes set at the wrong depths and heights that had to be redone.

If your contractor will pull, give them a layout before they start: where every cable ends, which ones go to ceiling access points, where the home-run panel sits, and the conduit to leave empty. Then have every run terminated, labeled and certified before the plaster or drywall goes up.

"Expect a standard price per drop"

National cost guides from sites such as Angi and HomeAdvisor publish averages for Ethernet installation across every kind of house in the country. Those averages describe a typical house with open framing and easy access, not a four-story Brooklyn rowhouse with restored plaster.

UniFi Nerds, writing about wiring homes in New York City in 2026, makes the local point clearly when it compares an open-framed project with a multi-level townhouse that has finished areas and limited pathways: "The second project can cost double even with the same drop count."

That is the Boerum Hill situation exactly. The same number of jacks can be a short job in a gutted house and a much longer one in a restored house with no closet stack. The fair way to price it is a survey of the actual route, and a written quote that lists each run.

"Since the 2025 code change, data cabling has no rules at all"

Some summaries read Local Law 128 of 2024 as removing every requirement. What the law did, through Administrative Code section 28-105.4.7 from December 21, 2025, is remove the need for an electrical permit for wiring under 50 volts used for signaling, communication, alarm and data.

Everything else still applies. Cable must carry the right rating for the space it passes through, openings between floors still need firestop, fire alarm wiring is a separate licensed discipline, and in Boerum Hill the Landmarks Commission still cares about anything that appears on the outside of a building.

The paperwork that usually matters on a Boerum Hill job is not the city's at all. It is the condo board's approval, the landlord's written permission, the tenant's notice and the certificate of insurance the building asks for.

"A second router or another mesh unit will fix the dead zone for good"

Sometimes a second device does help. One Brooklyn owner on Brownstoner's forum, frustrated by tin ceilings, bought a second router, set the first one up as an access point and reported that "the new router works much better," so he never ran the cable he had planned.

The catch is how that second device gets its signal. If it is fed wirelessly from the first, as most mesh satellites are, it relays a signal that has already crossed a floor or a wall, and every additional hop costs more speed. In a four-story rowhouse the top-floor unit can end up relaying a fraction of the original.

The lasting fix is to give each unit a cable back to the main router, which mesh makers call wired backhaul. The same equipment then behaves like a proper network of access points, and it is often the cheapest upgrade a household that already owns mesh can make.

FAQ

Boerum Hill Structured Cabling FAQ

Does a ceiling access point in a Boerum Hill rowhouse need an electrical outlet nearby?
+

No. The access points we install take their power over the same network cable that carries their data, from a switch in the cellar or closet panel. That means a single cable reaches the ceiling and nothing else has to be wired there, which matters in a room with original plaster or a decorative ceiling.

A single wall jack at my desk is not enough for everything I plug in. What is the right fix?
+

A small desktop switch plugged into that jack turns one connection into several and is fine for a computer, a printer and a dock. If the desk will grow into a second workstation, a second cable to the same spot is the better answer, and it is much cheaper to add while the original route is still open.

Does the cable climbing from our cellar to the top floor need a special rating?
+

Cable that runs vertically between floors should be rated for that use, and cable in a ceiling space that moves heating or cooling air must carry a higher rating. We specify the rating for each section of the route on the quote, and every opening the cable passes through between floors is sealed with listed firestop.

We plan to rent out our Boerum Hill house for a few years. Is cabling still worth doing?
+

Often more so. Tenants who work from home look for a dependable connection on every floor, and a house with an access point per floor and a labeled panel in the cellar generates fewer calls about dead zones. Plan the panel where you can reach it without entering the tenants' living space, and keep the port map with your house papers.

We have not settled where the furniture goes yet. How do we decide where to put the jacks?
+

Put access points by the shape of each floor, which does not change, and put jacks where furniture is almost certain to go: the TV wall, the likely desk wall and beside the bed in a room that may become an office. Add a spare cable coiled behind a plate or an empty conduit on the floors you are unsure about, so a jack can be added later without a new route.

What happens to the price if a planned route turns out to be blocked once the work starts?
+

We stop and show you what we found before doing anything else. Old houses sometimes hide a brick fire stop, a beam or a pipe exactly where a route was planned. We explain the alternative, whether it changes the work, and put any change in writing for your approval before continuing. Nothing extra is added without your agreement.

Is a wired network worth it in a one-bedroom apartment in Boerum Hill?
+

Sometimes not. If the router sits in the middle of a small apartment and every room has a good signal, a cable adds little. It becomes worth it when the router is stuck at the front door, when a desk or TV needs a steady connection, or when the apartment sits in a busy building where neighbors' networks crowd the air.

Our parlor ceilings are very high. Does that change where access points go?
+

It can. An access point on a very high ceiling covers the floor above it less directly and may be hard to reach for service. In tall parlors we often mount on a wall at a sensible height, above a door or on a pier between windows, aimed into the room, and bring the cable up from the cellar so the ceiling and its plasterwork are never touched.

With an access point on every floor of a narrow house, will they interfere with each other?
+

Not if they are set up as one system. Wired access points are placed on different channels and at power levels that suit each floor, so they hand phones and laptops from one to the next as people move through the house. Turning the power down also keeps the network from spilling into the houses on either side of a party wall.

Will we be able to see and restart the network ourselves after you leave?
+

Yes. We show you where each piece of equipment is, which one to restart if the internet drops, and how to reach the management app or page for the access points and switch, and we write the steps on the port map. The passwords for your own network stay with you.

What happens to the old routers, modems and mesh units we stop using?
+

We disconnect them and hand them back to you, labeled. Anything rented from your internet provider should go back to the provider so it stops appearing on the bill. Equipment you own can be reused elsewhere, such as a mesh unit in a garden studio, or taken to an electronics recycling drop-off.
