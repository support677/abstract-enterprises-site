The Neighborhood

Why Allerton Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Allerton was built in a hurry, about a century ago, and almost none of it was built with cameras in mind. Local histories trace the land to the Lorillards, a French Huguenot family who bought it in the 1700s, and Wikipedia says the neighborhood takes its name from Daniel Allerton, an early settler who farmed here with his wife Hustace. CornerFacts dates the change to 1914, when the city cut Allerton Avenue across former estate land, and Welcome2TheBronx records the Lorillard descendants selling parts of the estate as building lots in 1917. That same spring, on March 3, 1917, the Allerton Avenue station opened on the IRT White Plains Road Line, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc notes that speculators were already advertising "country homes in the Bronx" along the avenue. What went up next was brick: rows of two-family houses, Art Deco apartment houses on the side streets, tenements that Wikipedia places mostly within five blocks of the El, and on Bronx Park East a cooperative complex that later became a National Historic Landmark.

The age of that housing is the first fact a camera plan has to deal with. CornerFacts, working from city tax-lot records, counts 1,463 residential lots and finds half of them holding buildings from 1945 or earlier, with 46 percent dating from before 1940. Point2 Homes, using a different method, puts the median construction year at 1949, with about 39.6 percent of homes built before the 1940s and another 11.8 percent going up by 1949. Buildings of that era were wired for bells, a few light fixtures and later a buzzer. They do not come with empty conduit in the hall ceiling, a spare chase from the lobby to the roof or a closet set aside for network equipment. One vendor writing about New York intercoms puts it plainly: running new cable through a 1920s pre-war building is not the same as running it through new construction. In practice that means the camera itself is the easy part of an Allerton job, and most of the planning goes into how the cable reaches it: along the cellar ceiling, up the stair hall, through an old dumbwaiter shaft or riser, and across each floor through masonry that has to be drilled cleanly and sealed afterward.

The second fact is who lives here and who decides. NeighborhoodScout reports that most of Allerton's residential real estate is renter occupied, and Homes.com describes the housing as mostly brick mid-rise apartment buildings with more than 80 percent of residents renting. That shapes almost every camera call. A tenant who has had a package taken from a lobby or a bicycle stolen from a hallway usually cannot put a camera in that shared space; one New York building-security vendor notes that tenants generally have no right to install cameras in common hallways, lobbies or other shared areas without the landlord's permission, and that doorbell cameras on apartment doors are a gray area governed by the lease. Brick Underground adds the other half of the problem: a landlord who does have cameras is not obligated to share the footage with a tenant. So the people who actually commission a building camera system in Allerton are owners, managing agents, co-op boards and store owners, and the tenants who call us usually need help putting a clear, specific request in front of one of them. We are glad to help with that, and this page tries to give both sides the facts.

Then there are the co-ops. Along Bronx Park East and the streets near Pelham Parkway, Allerton has elevator buildings that are owned by their shareholders. StreetEasy lists 2166 Bronx Park East, for example, as a 65-unit, six-story elevator cooperative built in 1936, in the 49th Precinct (StreetEasy files it under Pelham Parkway, which shows how loosely the local boundaries are drawn). A building-security vendor notes that building-wide upgrades such as camera replacements may need board approval, and in our experience a board wants a written plan before it votes. Farther north on Bronx Park East stand the United Workers Cooperatives, the "Coops," at 2700 to 2870 Bronx Park East: Tudor Revival buildings put up in two campaigns between 1926 and 1929 by the United Workers' Association, made a National Historic Landmark in 1991 and a New York City landmark in 1992. City PLUTO records list 716 units at 2700 Bronx Park East, built in 1927, and 680 at 2800 Bronx Park East, built in 1929. Lehman College describes the complex as built around a courtyard with several entrances to different sections. Any camera on a landmarked facade raises a question for the Landmarks Preservation Commission, and we treat that review as part of the job there, not an afterthought.

Public and new housing round out the picture. CornerFacts places NYCHA's Parkside development wholly or partly inside Allerton, and Wikipedia lists Boston Road Plaza, Eastchester Gardens, Parkside Houses and Pelham Parkway Houses in or around the neighborhood. Wikipedia also records that in 2014 there were plans to add 375 security cameras at Pelham Parkway Houses, two years after city funding was approved following a murder in the complex. Cameras in NYCHA hallways and grounds are NYCHA's to install and maintain, so we do not take work orders from residents for those buildings. On the new side, the Bronx Times reported a housing lottery at 2951 Wallace Avenue, a six-story building with 36 apartments, 28 of them at market rate. New construction like that usually has a far easier cable path than its 1930s neighbors, which is one reason two buildings on the same block can get very different quotes.

Finally, there is what local reporting shows about how footage gets used here. In January 2025, ABC7 reported a thief breaking into a parking garage at a Thwaites Place co-op and hitting at least 23 vehicles, with surveillance video showing him first lifting the garage door, and police investigating a pattern of burglaries at three other garages in the 49th Precinct. In 2026, Norwood News covered a Bronx District Attorney case of 252 thefts of cars, tires, rims and catalytic converters, 19 of them in the 49th Precinct, with prosecutors saying many of the crimes were captured on surveillance video. After a fatal shooting at 660 Thwaites Place in February 2026, the same paper noted several surveillance cameras on the street, including one on the awning of Steven Restaurant at Boston Road and Thwaites Place. NBC New York reported that the October 9, 2026 crash at Allerton and Holland Avenues was captured on video. News12 Bronx covered an attack outside 690 Allerton Avenue in April 2026 and a robbery near Barker Avenue and Magenta Street in May. An older NYPD 49th Precinct profile describes neighborhood coordination officers reviewing CCTV from a number of local stores to help charge one suspect with six burglaries. We are not claiming any of these buildings or businesses as customers, and we do not say a camera would have prevented anything. What the reports show is that in Allerton, recorded video is routinely what investigators, prosecutors and reporters reach for afterward, which means the quality and placement of that video matters.

We also want to be clear about the gaps in our research. We found no Allerton-specific Department of Buildings, HPD or 311 records about cameras. Where this page describes what we find inside walls and risers, it draws on the age of the buildings in the public record and on what we see in buildings of that age generally, not on a survey of Allerton buildings. The only way to know your cable route is to walk it, which is the first thing we do.

Allerton facts that shape a camera installation

  • Old buildings, few cable paths: half of 1,463 residential lots hold buildings from 1945 or earlier (CornerFacts); Point2 Homes puts the median build year at 1949.
  • Mostly renters: NeighborhoodScout and Homes.com both describe a renter-majority neighborhood, so common-area cameras are an owner's or board's decision.
  • A landmark on the park: the United Workers Cooperatives at 2700–2870 Bronx Park East are a National Historic Landmark (1991) and a New York City landmark (1992).
  • The El as a spine: White Plains Road carries the elevated 2 and 5 trains, and the Allerton Avenue station sits in the shopping district at Allerton Avenue and White Plains Road.
  • Precinct and board: the 49th Precinct covers some or all of Allerton; the neighborhood sits in Community Board 11 and ZIP code 10467.
  • Video already in the record: 2025 and 2026 reports of a garage break-in, car thefts, a shooting and a crash all mention surveillance video.

What buildings usually add after the cameras. Once a walk-up or co-op has cameras on its entrances, the next conversation is almost always about the door itself: who can open it and how. Cameras and entry control share the same cellar, the same cable routes and often the same equipment closet, so if a board is thinking about fobs or a newer intercom later, say so at the survey and we will plan the cable so it does not have to be pulled twice.

Identify, or just observe

The single most useful idea for an Allerton owner is that a camera can do one of two jobs well, rarely both. A wide camera on the corner of a building shows the whole sidewalk, the car pulling away and which direction everyone went. A narrower camera at the front door, mounted low enough to look at faces rather than down on the tops of heads, shows who came in. Reports like the crash at Allerton and Holland Avenues are what a wide view does well. Investigations like the garage break-ins depend on a camera that catches a person close to a door. Most buildings need both kinds, and the commonest mistake we see in older systems is a single wide camera high on a wall, expected to identify a person at the far end of a lobby.

Time stamps that hold up

When footage from several stores or buildings is put together, as the 49th Precinct profile describes, the clock on each recorder matters. A recorder that has drifted twenty minutes, or one still running on a time set years ago, makes a clip harder to match with other evidence. On every system we install, the recorder keeps its time from the internet where the building has a connection, and we show the owner or super where to check it. On older systems we inherit, the clock is one of the first things we look at.

Exporting without fuss

After an incident, the person asked for footage is usually a super, a store manager or a board member, not a technician. If getting a clip out of the recorder means finding a password nobody has, or a cable to a laptop that no longer exists, the footage often gets overwritten before anyone copies it. That is why our handover always includes showing the owner how to find a time on the recorder or in the app and save a clip, and why we leave the steps in writing. We cover the request-from-police side in the FAQ below.

Garages and lots need two angles

Allerton's co-ops and larger apartment buildings sometimes have garages under or behind them, and the January 2025 reports describe a pattern across several 49th Precinct garages. A single camera inside a garage usually watches cars. It does not show the door being opened, and it rarely gets a usable face because the person is moving away from it. Our garage plans normally put one camera on the vehicle door itself, aimed so the opening, the handle and the person are all in frame, and a second watching the pedestrian door or the aisle. Where the lot is open to the street, a camera aimed at the exit catches vehicles leaving at the point where they slow down.

The cellar ceiling is the main road

In almost every older Allerton building, the cellar is where a camera system begins. It is usually the only space where cable can run the length of the building without crossing finished ceilings, and it is close to the entrances that need watching most: the street door, the rear yard door, the trash and service door, and in larger buildings the garage. We hang cable on supports along the joists or beams, keep it clear of hot pipes and of anywhere water collects, and bring it to a recorder location that the super can reach but a visitor cannot. Where the cellar has a dropped section, a boiler enclosure or a storage cage in the way, we route around it rather than through it.

Going up a pre-war building

Getting from the cellar to the landings is the hard part. Some buildings still have an abandoned dumbwaiter shaft, an old chase that carried bell wire, or a riser used by the intercom, and if one of those runs where we need to go and there is room in it, it is usually the cleanest path. Where none exists, the honest choice is a painted raceway up the stair hall, fixed to the masonry and run neatly along the corner, with a short branch to each camera. In some 1930s buildings the stair walls are hard plaster over masonry, in others block, and the fixings change accordingly. Where the cable has to pass through a floor, the hole is core-drilled to size and sealed afterward with firestop material, so the building's floors keep their fire separation. A vendor writing about Bronx apartment-building cameras puts the cost of this kind of job in the cabling from the lobby recorder up through risers to every floor, the rated penetrations and the labor through pre-war masonry, and that matches our experience.

Brick two-families: short runs, careful holes

The two-family houses that line many Allerton blocks are the simplest buildings to wire, but they bring their own care. The face brick is often original, and a hole drilled in the wrong place, through a brick face rather than a mortar joint, leaves a scar that is hard to hide. Where we can, we bring cable out through a mortar joint, a wooden rim, or under an existing fixture, and mount the camera on a junction box that covers the hole. Many of these houses have a front stoop, a side alley and a rear yard with a garage or parking pad, so a typical plan is a doorbell or door camera for the front, one camera on the alley, and one covering the yard and the cars. The owner who lives downstairs and the tenant upstairs often both want to see the front door; we set the app up so each sees what the owner agrees.

Keeping a store's system separate

In mixed buildings, a deli or salon on the ground floor and apartments above, we nearly always recommend two separate systems: one the business owns, covering its door, counter and stockroom, and one the building owner owns, covering the residential entrance, stair and cellar. They answer to different people, they get handed to different police requests, and when the business moves out, its system goes with it without leaving the building blind. They can share the cellar route if the owner agrees, but they should not share a recorder.

Decorative entrances and where a camera can sit

Many of Allerton's 1920s and 1930s apartment houses were built when builders competed on handsome entrances: patterned brick, stone or terra cotta trim, decorative lobby ceilings and tiled vestibule floors. The camera that matters most in those buildings is the one watching the street door from inside the vestibule, and it is also the one most likely to land on something old and hard to repair. Before we choose a spot, we look for a plain surface nearby, a soffit over the inner door, a plain band of plaster beside it, or an existing fixture we can mount next to, so the camera sees faces coming through the door without anyone drilling into ornament. We keep the cable out of sight along existing trim lines where we can and in a painted raceway where we cannot, and we tell the owner before the visit if a particular entrance has no good spot.

Lighting comes before lenses

One building-security vendor makes the point bluntly: a camera pointed at an unlit corridor provides no usable footage. In older Allerton buildings, hallway and stair lighting is often on timers, fitted with weak bulbs, or simply out on one landing. Modern cameras see in very low light, but a face lit from behind by a bright street door, or a landing lit only by a dying bulb, still records poorly. On our surveys we note every doorway, landing and cellar run where the light is too dim or comes from the wrong direction, and we put that list in the quote. Fixing a light is usually cheaper than buying a better camera, and the owner may already be obliged to keep those areas lit.

Courtyards with many entrances

The Coops, and some of the larger apartment buildings near Bronx Park East, are arranged around courtyards with several separate entrances serving different sections. A camera plan for a building like that is really several small plans: each section entrance, each path from the street into the courtyard, each service door. Cable between sections is the main challenge, because the buildings were not designed to share anything but heat and a courtyard. Where an interior route exists through cellars or connecting basements, we use it. Where it does not, a separate recorder per section, linked over the network, can avoid any new cable across a landmarked courtyard at all.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • No camera where people expect privacy. We do not install cameras in restrooms, changing areas or staff break rooms used for changing, and we keep cameras out of views into neighboring apartments.

  • Fire separation stays intact. Any hole we make through a fire-rated wall or floor for camera cable is sealed with firestop material before we leave.
  • Exits stay clear. Monitors and camera brackets never go where they block an exit sign or narrow a path to an exit.
  • Your landlord may have a say. Many commercial leases require the landlord's consent before work on the building's facade or common areas; check before we drill outside.

Field Notes: The Survey Walk in an Allerton Walk-Up

It is the order our technicians follow on a survey in the kind of building Allerton has hundreds of: a five- or six-story brick apartment house from the 1920s or 1930s, one street door, a stair, a cellar and a roof.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Allerton: Questions Answered

Pre-war buildings and their floors

The new camera cable will pass through every floor of our 1930s walk-up. Who seals those holes, and with what?

We do, before we leave each floor. Floors and some walls in an apartment house separate one part of the building from another in a fire, and a hole drilled for a cable breaks that separation unless it is filled properly. We core-drill each opening only as large as the cable needs, pass the cable through, and seal the gap with firestop material made for the purpose, not ordinary caulk or expanding foam. Where we use an existing chase, we seal the top and bottom where our cable enters and leaves. We note the location of every sealed penetration on the handover record, so the next contractor who opens a ceiling knows what is there.

Why is the labor on our quote so much more than the price of the cameras?

Because in an older Allerton building, the camera is the smallest part of the work. A camera takes a few minutes to mount. The cable that feeds it may have to travel along the cellar ceiling, up five floors of stair hall through masonry, across a landing and out to a bracket, with every floor drilled and sealed and every run tested and labeled. A vendor writing about Bronx apartment buildings says the cost lives in exactly that cabling, the rated penetrations and the labor through pre-war masonry, and we agree. The same cameras in a new building with drop ceilings, like the 2020s construction on Wallace Avenue, take far less labor, which is why we quote each building separately.

Garages, lots and evidence

Someone lifted our building's garage door by hand and went through the cars. What does a camera there need to capture for it to be useful to the police?

It needs to show the moment the door is opened, with the person's face or at least clear features, and the time. ABC7's report on the January 2025 Thwaites Place garage break-ins says the video showed the thief first lifting the door, which is exactly the picture that matters. A camera aimed down the length of the garage usually catches backs and parked cars. We put one camera on the vehicle door at a height and angle where the door, the handle and the person are all in frame, a second on the pedestrian door, and where the garage is large a third down the aisle. The recorder keeps accurate time so the footage can be matched with other buildings' cameras.

Our building faces the elevated tracks on White Plains Road. Will the trains shake a camera mounted on the facade?

They can, if the camera is fixed to the wrong thing. A camera bolted to a loose awning frame, a sheet-metal sign or a light bracket will shudder every time a 2 or 5 train passes, and the shaking is worst on a camera zoomed in on a distant point. We mount facade cameras directly to the brick with masonry anchors, avoid long zoom settings on walls nearest the El, and check the live picture while a train goes by before we finish. If a wall is too loose or crumbling to hold a camera steady, we tell you and pick another spot.

Co-ops, landmarks and permissions

Do exterior cameras on the Coops at Bronx Park East need the Landmarks Preservation Commission to look at them first?

Probably, for anything mounted on the outside of the buildings. The United Workers Cooperatives were designated a New York City landmark in 1992, and work on a landmark's exterior generally goes through the Commission. We have not reviewed the Commission's specific rules for cameras, so we do not promise an answer over the phone. What we do is plan interior positions first, inside vestibules and entrances, which may avoid the question entirely, and for any exterior camera we prepare a description, photos and a drawing of the mounting so the cooperative can submit it or ask the Commission how it wants it handled.

Our complex has separate entrances for different sections around one courtyard. Can each section's committee see only its own cameras?

Yes. Modern recorders and their phone apps let us create separate user accounts and give each one access to a chosen set of cameras. A section committee can see its own entrances and courtyard path, the building's management can see everything, and a super can be given live view without the ability to delete footage. We agree the list of accounts and which cameras each can see before we set them up, write it into the handover record, and show the person in charge how to remove an account when a committee member leaves.

Owners, tenants and footage

Does city law make an Allerton landlord put cameras in the lobby?

Not as a general rule. One building-security vendor states plainly that there is no general New York City law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. The rule people often confuse it with is Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, which, according to the Department of Buildings tenants' rights guide, requires self-closing, self-locking doors and a two-way intercom in multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968; another source describes it as applying to buildings with eight or more units. That rule is about doors and intercoms, not cameras. Cameras are a choice an owner or board makes, which is why tenants who want them usually have to ask.

A package disappeared from our building's lobby. Can I make the landlord give me the camera video?

Not necessarily. Brick Underground reports that a landlord isn't obligated to share surveillance footage with a tenant. In practice many owners will look at a clip when asked politely and quickly, before it is recorded over, and will share it with the police if a report is made. The most useful thing a tenant can do is give the super or managing agent the date and a narrow time window right away, and file a police report so investigators can request it. If you are an owner reading this, a simple written policy on who can see footage and when saves arguments later.

The neighbor's new camera on the next two-family seems to point straight into our backyard. Is there anything we can do?

There may be. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a, sometimes called the backyard camera law, lets residential owners or tenants sue over a neighbor's camera that records the private areas of their yard. We are not lawyers and cannot tell you whether a particular camera crosses that line. What we can say is that when we install cameras on Allerton's closely packed two-family blocks, we aim them at our customer's own doors, alley and yard, and use the privacy-masking feature many cameras have to black out a neighbor's windows or yard that falls inside the frame.

As an owner, can I put a hidden camera somewhere in my rental building?

We will not install hidden cameras in apartments or in any place where people expect privacy, and we do not install concealed cameras in residential buildings. In common areas we install visible cameras, because a camera people can see does part of its job by being seen, and because it avoids disputes with tenants. If an owner has a specific reason to want a less obvious camera somewhere in a building, the right first call is to a lawyer, not to us.

Doing it yourself, and public housing

Could I put up the cameras on our brick two-family myself and save the cost?

You can, and some Allerton owners do with a boxed kit. The parts that usually go wrong are the holes in old face brick, the cable route through the basement, and placement: cameras mounted too high, looking down at hats instead of faces, or aimed into a neighbor's yard. Wireless kits avoid cable but still need power at every camera and a strong signal through brick walls. If you do it yourself, drill mortar joints rather than brick faces, mount the front camera low enough to see a face, and test the picture at night before you close up. If you hire us, the price list and the warranty make the difference clear in writing.

We live in Parkside Houses and want cameras in our hallway. Can you put them in?

No. Cameras in NYCHA hallways, lobbies and grounds are installed and maintained by NYCHA, and work on the building is not ours to do. Wikipedia records that NYCHA planned 375 cameras at nearby Pelham Parkway Houses in 2014, two years after city funding was approved, which shows those decisions run through the agency and its funding. Residents usually raise camera requests through their tenant association and NYCHA's own channels. If you want to know whether a camera you can see in your building is working, ask NYCHA through those channels.

Signs

We could not confirm a city law that requires it. One vendor blog says building owners must post notice of common-area cameras under a "Local Law 50," but we were unable to verify that such a requirement exists, so we do not repeat it as fact. We recommend a clear sign at each entrance anyway. What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Allerton

What follows are claims that turn up repeatedly in quick online answers about cameras in New York buildings and about this corner of the Bronx, set against what the public record and our own field practice show. Marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish national cost guides that feed many of those answers; they give a rough idea, but they are not a quote for a 1930s building on Wallace Avenue.

"NYC apartment buildings are required to have security cameras."

Many summaries blur cameras together with the city's entrance rules. According to one New York building-security vendor, there is no general NYC law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. The requirement that does exist, Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, covers self-closing, self-locking doors and a two-way intercom in buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968.

That matters in Allerton, where half the residential lots hold buildings from 1945 or earlier. Many of those buildings predate the 1968 line entirely, and none of them is required by that law to have cameras.

So a camera system here is a decision: an owner's, a board's or a business's. We plan it around what that decision-maker is trying to solve, not around a mandate that does not exist.

"Anyone can install security cameras in New York without a license."

The truthful answer is that the sources disagree. General Business Law §69-m says no one may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. The Department of State's installer application, meanwhile, says installing "passive," non-detecting security devices does not require licensure. A third source claims video surveillance always needs a state license.

An AI answer that states either side confidently is overstating what the record says. Whether a given camera job falls inside or outside the licensing rule may depend on what else is connected to it, such as alarms or access control.

We sidestep the argument: AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and works under it on every job. If you hire someone else, ask to see their license and decide for yourself what it covers.

"Wireless cameras solve the wiring problem in old buildings."

Quick answers often suggest skipping cable altogether in pre-war buildings. Wireless cameras still need power at every position, which in a stair hall or garage usually means an outlet that is not there, or batteries that someone has to keep charged.

They also need a signal. Allerton's apartment houses are brick and masonry with plaster on top, and a router in one apartment or the cellar may struggle to reach the fifth-floor landing or a garage behind the building. A camera that drops out at the moment it matters is worse than no camera, because everyone assumes it was recording.

For building common areas we recommend wired cameras on PoE, which take their power down the same cable. Wireless still has a place: a doorbell camera on a two-family, or a single camera where no cable route exists at all.

"Professional camera installation costs about the national average."

National cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr average jobs from suburban houses with attics and vinyl siding to city buildings, and AI answers repeat those ranges as if they applied everywhere.

In Allerton the camera count matters less than the route. A four-camera job on a two-family with an open basement and a four-camera job on a six-story walk-up that needs cable up the stair hall, floors core-drilled and sealed, are not the same job and should not cost the same.

The harder problem in Allerton is backlight. A camera inside a vestibule looking out at a bright street door, or a store camera under the El's shadow looking at a sunny sidewalk, records a silhouette unless it is positioned and set up for that contrast.
