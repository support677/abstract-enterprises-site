The Neighborhood

Why Claremont Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Claremont is a neighborhood of big buildings on small blocks. Our own reading of the city's PLUTO land-use file, for a study area running from East 167th Street to the Cross Bronx Expressway and from the Grand Concourse to Third Avenue, counts about 677 residential lots holding roughly 14,750 homes. Sixty-six buildings with fifty or more apartments each hold about 9,492 of those homes, close to two thirds of the total. About 299 walk-up lots hold another 4,783 homes, and 56 private elevator buildings hold about 3,944. Only around 524 homes sit in one- and two-family houses. More than 6,000 of the homes are in buildings put up between 1901 and 1929. When a fire alarm question comes from Claremont, then, it is usually a question about a building where dozens of households share one stair, one roof and one cellar, built a century ago, long before anyone imagined a hardwired detector in every bedroom.

The recent fires show where that matters. The January 5, 2026 fire at 1365 Findlay Avenue began in a first-floor deli and extended through all floors of the building, according to NBC New York, and the Department of Buildings issued a full vacate order covering 32 apartments and four ground-floor commercial spaces. The February 2 fire off the Grand Concourse, reported by ABC7, burned through the top floor and into the cockloft, the low void between the top-floor ceiling and the roof. News 12 reported that flames at 1420 Clay Avenue spread between the ceiling and the roof, after a November 2025 fire there that injured four people and that fire marshals later traced to electrical wiring. A four-alarm fire tore through the roof of the six-story building at 1520 Sheridan Avenue, and back in December 2017 a four-alarm top-floor fire at 1686 Clay Avenue displaced 23 families. Reading those reports together, two patterns stand out to us, though no source states them as a pattern: fires that start in a business at street level under apartments, and fires that take hold on the top floor and under the roof of five- and six-story prewar buildings.

There is also a question of whether people heard anything. A tenant of the Findlay Avenue building told News 12 that smoke woke him, not an alarm. Mayor Mamdani, speaking after that fire, said that a landlord breaking the law by not having working smoke detectors in every unit is something within the city's control. City Limits, in an article that appears to be several years old, reported that residents of the nearby fatal fire at 1368 Webster Avenue, which was started by burning incense, said they did not hear smoke alarms, and that tenants of Butler Houses described malfunctioning fire alarms and carbon monoxide detectors as a long-running hazard. None of these reports establishes why any particular alarm did or did not sound, and we make no claim about that. What they do establish is the question every Claremont owner should be able to answer: on any given night, does every apartment in the building have a detector that works, and can you show it?

The law puts the starting duty on owners. HPD's guidance says owners must provide and install at least one approved, operational smoke detector and carbon monoxide detector in each apartment, and tenants are responsible for keeping them in working order. The FDNY's alarm guide places smoke alarms outside each sleeping room, within 15 feet of the bedroom door, with an alarm inside each bedroom as well in newer buildings. That sounds simple until it meets a 55-unit building from 1924 with tenants who have lived there for thirty years, some detectors dating from who knows when, some removed, some painted, and a few apartments where nobody answers the door. Our work in Claremont is mostly about turning the legal minimum into a verified fact: the right detector in the right place in every apartment, tested, dated, and written down. Where a building has, or needs, more than apartment alarms, such as hall and stair detection, a panel, or a filed building system, we plan that as a separate and clearly priced step.

Six Claremont facts that shape a fire alarm plan

  • Most homes are in large buildings. In our PLUTO study area, 66 buildings with fifty or more apartments hold about 9,492 of roughly 14,750 homes. A missed detector in a building that size is one of dozens of apartments sharing a stair, so the record has to be apartment by apartment.
  • The housing is old. More than 6,000 homes in the study area are in buildings from 1901 to 1929, and another 177 date from before 1901. Plaster walls, original stairs, top-floor cocklofts and cellars that have been rearranged several times are the normal conditions.
  • Shops sit under families. 1365 Findlay Avenue had four commercial spaces under 32 apartments. City property records cited by Hoodline list 538 Claremont Parkway as a five-story mixed-use building with ground-floor storefronts. Webster Avenue's storefronts include mosques and travel agents serving West African residents.
  • Some buildings carry long violation records. augrented.com, which compiles city data, lists 139 recorded HPD violations at 1365 Findlay Avenue, 569 at 200 Marcy Place, 734 at 1326 Grand Concourse and 334 at 1410 Morris Avenue, against an average of 93.3 per building in its Claremont Village figures. The records should be re-checked on NYC Open Data before anyone relies on them.
  • Public housing is its own world. The Claremont Village developments, including Butler Houses (six 21-story buildings) and Webster Houses (five 21-story buildings), are run by NYCHA, whose design guidelines call for two dedicated phone lines to a central station or fire command center and elevator recall. Work on NYCHA systems goes through NYCHA, not a private contractor hired by a tenant.
  • Batteries are a rising fire cause. Firehouse.com reported in 2026 that the FDNY said fires caused by batteries were up about 70 percent that year. In walk-ups where e-bikes and scooters are kept in apartments and halls, charging spots go on our survey sheet.

Need Repair in Claremont? Dead Detectors, HPD Deadlines and Buildings Coming Back After a Fire

Urgent calls from Claremont usually fall into one of four kinds. The first is the owner or managing agent holding an HPD notice that lists apartments with missing or inoperable smoke or carbon monoxide detectors, with a date by which the condition has to be corrected. The second is the super who has walked the building and found a floor's worth of detectors that are dead, chirping, removed or past their date, and wants them replaced before the next inspection or the next fire. The third is the store owner on Webster Avenue, Claremont Parkway or Findlay Avenue who has realized the cellar under the shop has no detection at all. The fourth, more common this year than we would like, is the building recovering from a fire: an owner whose tenants are moving back after a vacate order, or a board whose top floor was gutted and whose remaining detectors were soaked by firefighting water. In each case we ask one question first: is anyone sleeping tonight in an apartment without a working detector? If so, those apartments are handled before anything else is diagnosed or priced.

Common smoke alarms, carbon monoxide alarms and combination units travel on the van, so a list of dead apartment detectors can often be cleared on the first visit, floor by floor, with each replacement dated and recorded, and a single fault on a small building panel is frequently found within an hour or two of the technician arriving. Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Claremont's Architecture

Claremont's housing comes in a few distinct forms, and each one sets different rules for where detectors go, how cable can travel, who controls the work and how tenants are reached. The cards below are the types we meet most; the sections after them explain what changes in each.

The prewar walk-up of five or six floors

The most common Claremont building: brick, built in the 1910s and 1920s, one main stair, a cockloft under the roof, and anywhere from twenty to sixty apartments. 1365 Findlay Avenue, 32 units from 1924, is a typical example.

The mixed-use building with storefronts

A walk-up with shops at street level, like 538 Claremont Parkway, five stories over storefronts, or the Findlay Avenue building with its deli and three other businesses.

The prewar and wartime elevator building

Six-story buildings with elevators, such as 200 Marcy Place from 1924 with 55 units, or 1410 Morris Avenue from 1941 with 63 units, often with a lobby panel of some age.

The private tower from the 1960s

A small number of large private towers, including a 17-story, 321-apartment building from 1965, where elevators, compactor rooms and a building panel are part of daily life.

The HDFC and nonprofit-owned building

About 56 lots with roughly 2,257 homes carry HDFC owner names in PLUTO, many in 1920s buildings, alongside buildings run by nonprofits such as Housing Works.

The small house

Late-19th-century houses on College, Teller, Clay and Findlay Avenues, and 1990s two-family houses on Claremont Parkway, Third Avenue, Crotona Place, Washington Avenue and East 171st Street.

The prewar walk-up: one stair, many households and a roof space nobody sees

A Claremont walk-up from the 1920s usually has the same bones: a front entrance with a vestibule, one interior stair rising through every floor, apartments opening onto a hall at each landing, a roof bulkhead at the top, and a cellar with the boiler, meters and storage at the bottom. Above the top-floor ceilings there is usually a cockloft, a shallow space under the roof that runs across the building. For a fire alarm plan, that layout means three things. First, the apartments are the front line: the law expects a working smoke and CO detector in each one, and in most walk-ups that is all the detection there is. Second, the stair is the shared escape, and smoke that reaches it reaches everyone; detection on the landings, where the code and the building allow, buys warning time for people who are behind closed doors. Third, the top of the building deserves particular attention, because several of the recent Claremont fires either began on the top floor or burned into the space under the roof. We survey the top-floor apartments, the top-floor hall and the bulkhead on every walk-up job, and we tell the owner plainly what the roof space contains, what condition its wiring appears to be in from what we can see, and whether heat detection up there is practical.

The mixed-use building: a store at night, families on every floor above

When the January fire on Findlay Avenue started in a ground-floor deli and climbed through the whole building, it showed the central weakness of the mixed-use walk-up: the space most likely to be empty overnight sits directly under the apartments, and its fire can spread upward before anyone upstairs knows. A store often has its own lease, its own keys, its own hours and its own back room and cellar, and the owner of the building may never have been inside those spaces after closing. Our plan for these buildings starts by drawing a line on paper between the residential part and the commercial part and then deciding, with the owner and the store tenant, how each part warns the other. That can be as simple as detection in the store and its cellar linked to a sounder in the residential hall, or as complete as a building system that covers both, depending on what the occupancy requires. Either way, three things are agreed in writing before we start: who owns and maintains the store's devices, who is called if they signal at night, and who can open the gate and the door for a technician or a firefighter.

The prewar elevator building: a lobby panel, a machine room and a long record

Six-story elevator buildings from the 1920s through the early 1940s are the next step up. Several of the Claremont addresses with the longest HPD records, according to the aggregator figures above, are buildings of this kind. These buildings sometimes have a fire alarm panel in the lobby or cellar, installed during some past renovation, with a few devices in the halls, the elevator machine room or the boiler room. Our first visit to a building like this is about finding out exactly what is there and whether it works: tracing circuits from the panel, matching devices to locations, checking batteries and dates, and comparing what we find with any FDNY paperwork the owner holds. ASM Integrators' summary of the city's approach draws the line that governs everything afterward: replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no change in design is treated as repair, while moving or replacing the control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY. We tell the owner which side of that line each proposed task sits on before it is priced.

The private tower: height, compactors and a building system that must stay on

The HDFC and nonprofit building: tight budgets and the need to show the work

Low-income co-ops and buildings owned or managed by nonprofits make up a real share of Claremont's housing. Boards and managers in these buildings tend to share two concerns: the money comes from reserves, grants or rents that are already stretched, and the work has to be documented well enough to satisfy HPD, a lender, a funder or a regulator. We write plans for these buildings in stages that each leave the building better protected than before: apartment detectors first, because that is the legal baseline and the most direct protection for sleeping people; then the top floor, halls and stairs; then the cellar and any commercial space; then any building system work. Each stage gets its own price from our list and its own record. Where a building has staff on site, such as a live-in super or a front desk, we include them in the handover so the people who will answer a detector at night know what they are hearing.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Order We Walk a Claremont Walk-Up

On the sidewalk. The survey starts outside: where the entrance is, whether the store has a roll-down gate and who holds its key, where the hydrant is and whether cars usually crowd it. After the Findlay Avenue fire, the FDNY's Chief of Department said three cars parked on three different hydrants delayed the operation, and after the Grand Concourse fire, ABC7 reported three frozen hydrants. An alarm cannot fix a blocked hydrant, but it is a reminder of why minutes of early warning matter so much here.

Straight to the top. We climb to the top floor before looking at anything else: the bulkhead door, the top-floor hall, and, with the owner's permission, what can be seen of the cockloft. We note any signs of past fire, water damage or wiring that looks improvised, and we look at the top-floor apartments with particular care.

Floor by floor on the way down. At each landing we note the hall, the stair, anything stored on the landings, e-bikes or scooters by doors, and the condition of any common-area devices. In apartments we look at where people sleep, where the detectors are, how old they are, and whether they work, writing each apartment down by number.

The store, with the shopkeeper. At street level we walk the store, its back room and its cellar with whoever runs it, asking about closing time, cooking equipment, refrigeration and where things are charged overnight.

The cellar last. Boiler, meters, storage rooms, any panel, and signs of water. Only after the whole walk do we sit down with the owner's paperwork and the public record and compare.

Compliance notes for Claremont buildings and businesses

  • Apartment detectors are the owner's duty. HPD says owners must provide and install at least one approved operational smoke and carbon monoxide detector in each apartment; tenants maintain them.
  • Placement follows the FDNY guide. Smoke alarms go outside each sleeping room within 15 feet of the door, and inside each sleeping room in newer buildings.
  • Some buildings must report by unit. Where the building code requires dwelling-unit detectors, the Administrative Code requires them to be annunciated by unit at a constantly attended location.
  • Small houses have a code exception. The 2022 Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where units are separated by one-hour partitions and each has its own exit.
  • Filed systems are reviewed first. The FDNY's Fire Code Chapter 9 requires design and installation documents for fire alarm systems to be submitted to the department for review and approval before installation.
  • Repair and alteration are different. Like-for-like replacement of detectors, pull stations or panels is repair; moving or replacing the control panel requires new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Tall buildings carry more. Buildings over 125 feet need one-way voice communication, a rule that matters in the few towers, not the walk-ups.
  • Vacate orders come from the DOB. After the Findlay Avenue fire, the Department of Buildings issued the full vacate order; the owner works with the city to lift it, and fire alarm work is scheduled within that process.
The Fires of 2025 and 2026

Five Claremont Fires and What Each One Says About Detection

The reports below are public news coverage. We were not involved in any of these buildings, we do not know the condition of their alarms beyond what the reports say, and we draw no conclusions about cause or blame. We describe them because they are the clearest local evidence of where fires in Claremont start and how people learn about them, and because each one points at a part of a building that a fire alarm plan should address.

What a Claremont Building's City Record Can and Cannot Tell a Fire Alarm Installer

Size and age come first. PLUTO tells us how many apartments to expect and when the building went up. A 32-unit building from 1924, like 1365 Findlay Avenue, or a 63-unit building from 1941, like 1410 Morris Avenue, tells us the scale of the apartment work, roughly how long the survey will take and what kind of construction we will be working in. It also tells us whether the building is likely to be a walk-up or have an elevator, which affects how we carry ladders and stock.

Violation counts show how a building is maintained, not what is broken today. augrented.com lists 734 recorded HPD violations at 1326 Grand Concourse, a 56-unit, five-story building, with mold, water damage and pest complaints in 2026, and 569 at 200 Marcy Place, a 55-unit building from 1924, against a Claremont Village average of 93.3. A long record does not mean a building's detectors are missing. It does suggest an owner and super with many demands on their time, apartments that may be hard to access, and conditions such as water damage that can affect where detectors can be mounted and how long they last. When a record includes detector violations specifically, we ask the owner for the notice and plan around the named apartments.

Complaint and permit counts show activity. The same aggregator lists 587 311 calls and 42 DOB complaints at 1410 Morris Avenue, against neighborhood averages of 88.3 and 4.9, and an average of 2.7 permits per building over seven years. Recent permits can mean recent renovation, and renovation is when detectors get taken down and not put back. We ask what work has been done in the building in the past few years and look for its traces on the survey.

What the record does not show. We found no Claremont-level summary of DOB fire alarm permits or FDNY filings, and no summary of 311 complaints specifically about fire alarms. The city's own portals and NYC Open Data are the authority; the aggregator figures quoted on this page should be re-checked there before an owner relies on them. Fire Alarm Installation in Claremont: Questions Answered

After a fire on the block

A tenant on Findlay Avenue said it was smoke, not an alarm, that woke him. As an owner, how do I build a record that shows every apartment had a working detector?

Start by making the record the product of the job, not an afterthought. When we install or replace detectors in a Claremont building, each apartment gets its own line: apartment number, the rooms or hall locations where detectors were placed, the type of each device, its manufacture date, whether it is smoke, carbon monoxide or combined, the date of installation, and the result of a test performed in front of the tenant or super. Where a tenant was not home, the line says so, along with the date of the next attempt. Where a detector was found missing or tampered with, that is noted too, with the date. The owner gets the full list on paper and as a file, signed by the technician. That record does not shift the tenant's legal duty to maintain the detector afterward, and it is not a legal opinion on compliance, but it answers the question a tenant, an HPD inspector or an insurer will ask after a fire: what was in that apartment, and did it work when it went up? Repeat the test on a schedule and add each round to the same file, and the record keeps its value.

Fire marshals said electrical wiring caused the Clay Avenue fire. Will your fire alarm survey tell us anything about our own building's wiring?

Only what is visible, and we will be clear about that limit. We are a licensed low-voltage installer. A fire alarm survey is not an electrical inspection, and we do not test or certify a building's power wiring, which is the work of a licensed electrician. What our technicians do see, because they are on every floor, in the halls, the cellar and often the top of the building, are conditions that are worth reporting: extension cords run through walls or under doors, scorched outlets or fixtures, junction boxes left open, wiring in the cockloft or cellar that looks improvised, overloaded power strips feeding e-bike chargers, and fixtures damaged by water. When we see something like that, it goes into the written report as an observation with the location, and we recommend that the owner have an electrician look at it. What the fire alarm can do about electrical risk is find a fire early wherever one is likely to start, which is why we pay particular attention to rooms where the survey turns up electrical concerns and to the top floor and roof space, where several recent fires took hold.

Firefighting water poured down through our building after a top-floor fire. Can any of the soaked detectors, cable or panel be reused?

Assume the devices in the wet areas cannot, and test anything you hope to keep. Detectors are electronic devices with sensing chambers that are not designed to survive being drenched, and a detector that has been soaked, coated in soot or exposed to heat may look fine and still fail to sense smoke properly. In a building that has taken hose water through several floors, as often happens in a top-floor fire, we replace apartment and hall detectors in every area that got wet or smoky, and we treat any detector whose history is uncertain the same way. Cable is different: low-voltage cable that ran through dry areas may be reusable, but cable that sat in water, ran through burned spaces or was cut during overhaul has to be tested end to end, and anything in doubt is replaced. A building panel that took water is checked with the power off and usually needs replacement, which, depending on the change, can mean a filing with the FDNY. The written report separates what was replaced, what was kept after testing, and why, so the owner's insurer and the city see a clear account.

Our building is under a full vacate order after a fire. Can fire alarm work begin before tenants are allowed back?

Often it can, and it usually should, but only within the arrangements the owner has with the city and only in parts of the building that are safe to enter. A vacate order like the one the DOB issued at 1365 Findlay Avenue means nobody may live there until the department is satisfied, and the owner typically has engineers, contractors and inspectors coming through to stabilize and repair the building. Fire alarm work belongs in that sequence: once the structure is safe and the rooms are dry, and once electrical and construction work in an area is far enough along, detectors and any building system can be installed or restored so that protection is ready before tenants come back, rather than being rushed in during the first week of reoccupation. We need the owner or their representative to arrange access, tell us which areas are cleared for work, and share any conditions the city or the owner's engineer has set, including any asbestos or environmental restrictions. We then plan the fire alarm work floor by floor around the other trades and test everything once the work around it is finished.

Walk-ups, towers and the roof

Our five-story walk-up has detectors in the apartments but nothing in the halls or on the stairs. What can be added without designing a full filed system?

Usually something useful, and the plan has to stay honest about what it is. Many Claremont walk-ups have no building fire alarm system and are not required to have one; the legal duty is the detector in each apartment. Beyond that, an owner can choose to add detection to the common spaces, such as smoke alarms on each landing and in the top-floor hall, linked to each other so that smoke on the stair sounds on every floor. That kind of addition can give people behind closed apartment doors early warning that the stair is filling, which matters in a building with one way down. What it is not is a code-required building fire alarm system with a panel, monitoring and an FDNY approval, and we do not describe it that way in writing or to tenants. Before recommending anything for the halls, we check with the owner whether the building has any filed system or past FDNY involvement, because adding devices to an existing filed system is a different matter. If the building's circumstances suggest a full system may actually be required, we say so and recommend a registered design professional.

Our roof is due for replacement next year. Is that the right moment to add heat detection to the space under the roof deck?

Neighbors in a Webster Avenue fire said they never heard an alarm. Should a smoke alarm inside one apartment be loud enough to warn people in the hall?

We own a private elevator building from the 1960s with more than three hundred apartments. Where does a fire alarm survey of a building that size even start?

With the documents and the panel, before the apartments. In a large tower there is almost always a building fire alarm system, and everything in the apartments has to be understood in relation to it. We ask for any FDNY approvals, inspection reports and drawings, the name of the current fire alarm service company and central station, the Certificate of Occupancy, and the building's most recent test records. Then we spend the first part of the survey at the panel and on the building devices: what the panel reports, which floors and devices are connected, how elevator recall and any compactor or machine-room devices are handled, and what the batteries and communicators look like. Only then do we sample apartments floor by floor, checking the detectors that the owner is responsible for under HPD's rules. For a building of that size, the survey is planned with management so tenants are notified and access is arranged in blocks. The report separates building-system findings, which may need the building's design professional and the FDNY, from apartment findings, which we can usually price and address directly.

Shops under apartments

Four shops share the ground floor under our apartments. Should each shop have its own detection, or should all four tie into one arrangement?

Each shop needs detection that covers its own space, back room and cellar, and the building needs a way for a fire in any one of them to reach the people upstairs; how those two needs are met depends on the building. In a walk-up without a building system, a common approach is detection in each store that sounds locally and is linked to a device in the residential hall, so that a fire behind any of the four gates is heard by the families above. Each store's devices are then documented as that store's, with the tenant's responsibilities set out in writing. In a building with a building system, the stores' devices may need to report to the building panel, which can bring in the FDNY and the building's designer. The building we read about on Findlay Avenue had four commercial spaces under 32 apartments, and the fire that began in one of them ended up affecting all of them. Our advice is to plan all four stores at once, even if each tenant pays for their own part, so the arrangement is consistent and nobody's space is the gap.

Our grocery leases the ground floor of a walk-up. Under our lease, who pays for detection in the store, and who pays for the apartments above?

The lease decides, and many small-store leases are vague about it, so read yours and then put the answer in writing with the landlord before work starts. In general terms, the building owner is responsible under HPD's rules for providing detectors in the apartments, and that has nothing to do with the store. Detection inside the store, its back room and its cellar is commonly treated as part of the tenant's fit-out and maintenance, but some leases make the landlord responsible for all life-safety equipment, and some split it by who installed it. Where a device in the store also protects the building, such as a sounder in the residential hall linked to the store's detection, the parties should agree who owns it, who tests it and who pays to fix it. We are not lawyers and do not interpret leases. What we can do is give each party a written price for its own part, label every device by owner, and leave a record that makes the agreed split clear to the next super, the next tenant and the next inspector.

Our shop's storage space is in a cellar shared with the building's meters and boiler. Whose detection covers that room?

That is exactly the kind of space that gets missed, because each party assumes the other has it. If the cellar room is part of the store's lease, detection there is usually the store's responsibility; if it is common space that the store is allowed to use, it is usually the owner's. Either way, the room needs protection, because a fire in a cellar under a walk-up is a fire under every apartment in the building, and stored cartons next to a boiler and electrical meters are an obvious risk. Cellar conditions also affect the choice of device. Dust and damp make ordinary smoke detectors prone to false alarms and contamination, so heat detection is often more reliable in the storage room itself, with smoke detection in the cellar corridor or at the foot of the stair where smoke would travel, and carbon monoxide detection near the boiler. We walk the cellar with both the store tenant and the super, mark on the plan which devices belong to whom, and price the store's share and the building's share separately.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Claremont

"Every New York apartment building has a central fire alarm system"

In Claremont, many of the roughly 299 walk-up lots in our PLUTO study area rely on the detector the owner must provide in each apartment and nothing more. Building systems are more common in elevator buildings and towers, and whether one is required depends on the building's occupancy, size and history under the Building Code.

The practical consequence is that a tenant in a walk-up should not assume a building alarm will wake them. The detector in their own apartment is the one that has to work, and an owner who wants more than that has to add it deliberately.

That source is a contractor's summary, not the code, and we flag it as something to verify. For a Claremont building that has been gutted after a fire or substantially renovated, the answer may differ from a building that has not been touched in decades. "Public housing towers work the same way as private apartment buildings"

FAQ

Claremont Fire Alarm Installation FAQ

Our super keeps asking for spare detectors. Can you leave a few on site so a dead one can be swapped the same day?
+

Yes. On building-wide jobs we can supply a small stock of the same model we installed, so the super can replace a failed apartment detector without waiting for a visit. We show the super how to mount and test it, and we ask that every swap be added to the building's record with the apartment number and date, so the list stays accurate. Spares are priced from our list and shown on the quote.

Our apartments have smoke alarms but no carbon monoxide alarms. Can CO protection be added without replacing everything?
+

Often it can. If the existing smoke alarms are in good condition, within their service life and in the right places, we can add separate carbon monoxide alarms in each apartment and record them alongside the existing devices. If the smoke alarms are old, damaged or badly placed, it is usually more sensible to replace them with combination units at the same time. We check a sample of apartments before recommending either path.

Do you install photoelectric or ionization smoke detectors, and does it matter in a Claremont apartment?
+

We generally install photoelectric smoke alarms in apartments, because they respond well to the slow, smoky fires that start in furniture, bedding and wiring, and they tend to cause fewer nuisance alarms from cooking when placed sensibly. The exact model must be an approved type that meets the requirements for the building, and we confirm that before installation.

A small stove fire left soot on the detector nearest the kitchen. Can it stay up?
+

Replace it. Soot and heat can affect a detector's sensing chamber in ways you cannot see, and a detector that has been through a fire is no longer something you can rely on.
