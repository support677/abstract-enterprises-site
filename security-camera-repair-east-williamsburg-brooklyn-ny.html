The Neighborhood

Why East Williamsburg Properties Need Security Camera Repair

East Williamsburg is the industrial and loft district between the BQE and Flushing Avenue, running east from Bushwick Avenue toward the Queens line at Newtown Creek. One neighborhood guide lists its arteries as Metropolitan Avenue, Grand Street and Johnson Avenue, and its loft and factory streets as Morgan Avenue, Meadow Street, Ingraham Street, Bogart Street, Ten Eyck Street and Boerum Street. The building stock is mixed in a way few Brooklyn neighborhoods are: low-rise brick manufacturing buildings, corrugated-steel warehouses, nineteenth-century factories turned into studios, timber and concrete lofts from the 1920s, small tenements and row houses on the residential blocks near Graham and Bushwick Avenues, and new glass apartment buildings on what used to be empty lots. A camera system here is as likely to watch a roll-up door and a truck yard as a stoop.

That mix decides what a repair looks like, and the recent incident record shows why it matters. At about 1:40 in the morning on October 30, 2025, four men went in through the rear door of a commercial building on Stagg Street while it was empty and took roughly $60,000 in merchandise, according to News 12 Brooklyn. The building's cameras caught them, hooded, some in ski masks, and one looking straight into the lens wearing glasses, and the police released the footage to ask for help naming them. Six months later, around 7 a.m. on May 2, 2026, two people broke into a food truck parked outside 599 Johnson Avenue and took a generator worth about $2,500 and a cooler of soda; about ten minutes later the same group took a second generator from another truck through an open window and drove off in a white pickup. Again, the public description came from surveillance images. Going back further, DNAinfo reported in 2016 that a man got into an office at 218 Randolph Street through an air vent and filled a cardboard box with computers, and the building's camera recorded him lowering himself in with his feet dangling.

Those three cases have something in common. In each, the camera was on the back side of the building or the side street, not the showy front, and in each the footage was what everyone went to first. In East Williamsburg the dangerous hours are when a warehouse is empty and the industrial blocks are quiet, and the entry points are rear doors, loading docks, vents and curbside vehicles. A system that is recording on the street side and dark on the back is worse than useless, because the owner believes the building is covered.

Power is the second pressure. Just before 1 p.m. on February 16, 2026, the FDNY answered a manhole fire at 101 Montrose Avenue, and by that evening 535 customers were without electricity. Con Edison said it was repairing damage done when snow and road salt seep into underground equipment, and the city opened a warming center at I.S. 49 on Graham Avenue. On August 7, 2025, a manhole fire just over the line in Bushwick, in front of 18 Irving Avenue at Jefferson Street, left about 240 customers dark at the peak; one resident told News 12 that the lights flickered, there was a loud pop, and everything went out. Camera systems rarely fail while the power is off. They fail as it returns: a recorder that was writing to its drive at the moment of the cut, a PoE switch port that took the surge, a power supply that came back with one channel dead.

The third pressure is change. East Williamsburg buildings change hands, change tenants and change use constantly. Former manufacturing lofts are being legalized as housing under the Loft Law; plans were filed in December 2024 to legalize 43 residential units in one East Williamsburg interim multiple dwelling, and 222 Varet Street is another loft building that has sought to legalize apartments. Industrial space is being carved into smaller units, such as the 10,000 square feet of affordable industrial space that Evergreen, NYCEDC and ANHD closed on at 500 Stagg Street. Each change brings contractors who cut cables, tenants who leave with the passwords, and landlords who inherit a recorder nobody can log into. Every one of those is a camera repair waiting to be discovered.

So a repair in East Williamsburg is not just a dead camera swapped for a live one. It means getting the system recording again on the sides of the building that are actually at risk, putting the recorder and switch on protected power, labeling the cables so the next contractor does not cut them, and making sure the person who owns the building, not a departed tenant, holds the login. Many of the loft and warehouse owners who call us for camera work also ask about the entrance; a building with a camera at the door usually pairs it with a working intercom or access control, and we can look at both on one trip.

Six kinds of buildings account for nearly every camera repair call we take from East Williamsburg. Each fails in its own way, and each decides where the recorder lives, how the cables travel, and who has to say yes before a technician touches anything.

Converted factory lofts

Brick, timber and concrete buildings from the manufacturing era, now studios, offices or legalized apartments. Long open floors, high ceilings, many tenants and many doors, often with cameras added piecemeal by different people.

Corrugated-steel warehouses

Single-story and two-story metal buildings on the industrial blocks, used for storage, food distribution, construction yards and fabrication. Heat, dust and metal walls shape every repair.

Brick workshops and small factories

Low-rise masonry buildings housing machine shops, woodworkers, breweries and makers, usually with a roll-up door, a small office and a recorder on a shelf near the machines.

Tenements and row houses

The older residential blocks near Graham Avenue, Bushwick Avenue and Grand Street, with a vestibule, a narrow stair and a recorder in the cellar or a hall closet.

Mixed-use avenue buildings

Shops on Grand Street, Graham Avenue and Metropolitan Avenue with apartments upstairs, where the store's cameras and the building's cameras may share a cellar, a power circuit or a cable path.

New glass apartment buildings

Recent construction on former empty lots, with network cameras, a managed internet system and a lobby full of glass, where the first failures are logins, handover gaps and glare.

Converted factory lofts: many tenants, one recorder, and decades of additions

The typical East Williamsburg loft building has been through several lives. It made something for decades, sat partly empty, filled with artists and small businesses, and in many cases is now being legalized as residential space under the Loft Law. Its camera system usually reflects that history. There may be an old analog system in the stairwells from the manufacturing days, a few network cameras a landlord added in the corridors, and tenants' own cameras inside their units or over their doors. The recorder is often in a ground-floor closet, the building's electrical room or the office of whichever tenant offered the space years ago.

On a loft repair, the first job is working out what belongs to whom. We trace each cable from the recorder to its camera, separate the building's cameras from tenants' private ones, and note which are connected to nothing at all. Long open floors mean long cable runs, and loft buildings often have network cameras at the far ends of runs that come close to the practical limit for an ordinary data cable, so an intermittent camera here is often a run, a connector or an extender rather than a dead camera. The heavy timber or concrete structure makes it hard to add new paths, so repairs usually reuse the existing cable routes, which is another reason to label them.

The other loft-specific issue is construction. A building going through legalization has crews in its walls and ceilings for months, installing fire-rated partitions, sprinklers, new electrical service and new stairs. Each crew can cut, unplug or bury a camera cable without realizing it. When a loft's cameras die one by one over a renovation season, we trace the runs, repair the damaged sections, and give the building's representative a labeled diagram to hand to the next contractor.

Corrugated-steel warehouses: heat, dust and walls that block signal

Metal warehouses on the industrial blocks are hard on electronics. In summer the inside of an uninsulated steel building can run far hotter than the street, and a recorder sitting on a shelf in a small office with no air movement wears out its drive and its power supply faster. In winter some of these buildings go unheated over weekends and holidays, which brings condensation. Dust from stock, cardboard, construction materials and trucks settles on everything, including fans and lenses. A warehouse repair often starts with moving the recorder to a cooler, cleaner spot and replacing a drive that has been cooked.

The steel itself matters too. Corrugated walls and roofs block Wi-Fi far more than brick does, so wireless cameras that worked in a showroom struggle inside a warehouse and fail outright between buildings on the same lot. Long wired runs across a yard to a gate or a second building pick up surges and take damage from trucks and forklifts. We repair the run, put the outdoor section in conduit or overhead where trucks cannot reach it, and add surge protection where a cable leaves the building.

Brick workshops and small factories: machines, sparks and one shelf for everything

The machine shops, metal fabricators, furniture makers and breweries in East Williamsburg's low-rise brick buildings usually have small systems: four to eight cameras, a recorder on a shelf in the office or above the workbench, and a modem and router on the same shelf. They fail in ways an apartment building's system does not. Welding and grinding throw sparks and metal dust that pit lenses and clog the recorder's fan. Compressors, saws and other large motors on the same circuit can cause momentary power dips that make cheap adapters drop their cameras. Steam, wood dust and flour-fine sawdust coat domes until the picture fogs.

Repairs here are about separation. We move the recorder away from the machines, put it and the switch on a battery backup that smooths out the dips when a motor starts, replace adapters with a central power supply, and choose housings and locations that keep lenses out of the spray. The cameras that matter most are almost always the ones on the roll-up door and the yard, and those get tested with the door closed and the outside lights off.

Tenements and row houses: the residential East Williamsburg

The older residential blocks near Graham Avenue, Bushwick Avenue and Grand Street hold small tenements and row houses with three to eight apartments, a street door, a vestibule and a narrow stair. Their camera systems are usually simple: a camera on the stoop or street door, one in the vestibule, one in the hallway, maybe one in the back yard, wired to a recorder in the cellar or a locked closet under the stairs. Many of these buildings are rent-regulated, and as the 2015 state housing ruling on a disconnected vestibule camera shows, a camera service tenants have long relied on can become one the landlord is expected to maintain.

The common tenement faults are the cellar recorder plugged into a power strip shared with a boiler or washing machine, a vestibule dome cracked or spray-painted, and hallway cameras that stopped when a ceiling was repaired after a leak. The neighborhood's broader record of door problems matters here too. HPD wrote 302 self-closing door violations in the East Williamsburg area in 2026 through November 11. A vestibule camera only helps if the door it watches closes and latches; when it does not, we say so in the written report.

Mixed-use avenue buildings: the shop's system and the building's system

On Grand Street, Graham Avenue and Metropolitan Avenue, the ground floor is a store, bar, restaurant or office and the floors above are apartments. Often there are two camera systems: the business's, covering its counter, stock and door, and the building's, covering the residential entrance and halls. Sometimes there is only one, installed by whichever party needed it first, and the other party relies on it. Repairs go wrong when nobody is sure whose system is whose.

We confirm ownership before touching anything, then keep the two systems separate if they are separate: different recorders, different power, different logins. If one recorder serves both, we can give the business and the building separate viewing accounts so each sees only its own cameras. A shared cellar or a shared power circuit is a common source of faults, especially when one party's work cuts power to the other's equipment.

New glass apartment buildings: newer cameras, newer problems

The newest East Williamsburg apartment buildings use network cameras connected to a managed internet system, often with a cloud viewing service set up by the developer's contractor. These systems rarely suffer the cable and connector failures of older buildings. Their first problems are administrative: the contractor who set it up has left, the cloud account is in the developer's name, firmware has not been updated, the recorder is set to keep only a few days, or some cameras were never added to the recorder at all. Glass lobbies bring a second issue: cameras pointed at windows that turn every visitor into a silhouette in the afternoon.

A repair in a new building often starts with documentation. We list what is installed, who holds which passwords, what each camera should be showing and how long each is keeping video, then fix what is wrong and hand the owner or managing agent a record that survives the next change of staff.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Repairing a Camera System in an East Williamsburg Loft or Warehouse

These notes describe how our technicians work through a typical East Williamsburg loft building or warehouse when several cameras are out and nobody knows why. They are the method, not a story about one building.

Copy first, test second. If anyone needs footage from a recent night, the technician exports it before restarting or unplugging anything, and writes down the recorder's clock compared with the real time.

Find every entrance. East Williamsburg buildings often run through the block or have doors on two or three streets. The technician walks every door, dock and gate and checks which ones have a camera and whether it actually sees someone using it.

Start at the power and the switch. In the office, closet or rack, the technician checks the outlet, the battery backup if there is one, the PoE switch and any central power supply, because an outage or a motor on the same circuit shows up there first.

Trace and label every run. Unlabeled cables are toned out and labeled at both ends as they are identified, so the recorder's channel names match the cameras on the walls and the next contractor can see what not to cut.

Check the far end in its real conditions. At each dark or weak camera, the technician tests power and signal at the camera, then looks for heat, dust, water, sparks or construction damage. Rear and yard cameras are checked with the gate down and the lights in their night state.

Price before parts, then leave a record. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. After the repair, every camera is checked by day and in low light, alerts for lost video and drive errors are switched on, and you get the logins and a list of what is connected where.

Fault by Fault

Outages, Construction, Heat, Logins and Blind Spots: Why East Williamsburg Camera Systems Fail

Five causes explain most of the camera failures we find in East Williamsburg lofts, warehouses, workshops and storefronts. Telling them apart early is what separates a $150 visit from a new system.

Outages: the damage happens when the power returns

When the electricity drops and comes back, three things can happen to a camera system. If the recorder was writing to its drive at the moment of the cut, the file system can be damaged, leaving it stuck at a setup screen or asking to format the disk. A surge on return can kill adapters, power supply channels or PoE switch ports, so some cameras come back and others never do. And settings held in memory, including the clock and network addresses, can reset. The February 2026 Montrose Avenue outage, which Con Edison tied to road salt in underground equipment, is exactly the kind of event that produces these calls. A battery backup on the recorder and switch, sized to ride through short cuts and shut down cleanly in long ones, prevents most of them.

Construction: the cable nobody saw

East Williamsburg has more construction inside existing buildings than almost anywhere in Brooklyn: lofts being legalized, warehouses being subdivided, new partitions, new sprinklers, new electrical service. Every crew works where camera cables run. When a camera dies the same week as a new ceiling, a new wall or a new internet line, the work almost always cut, unplugged or buried it. We trace the run, repair the damaged section, label it so the next crew can see it, and tell you who was working there so you can raise it with them.

Heat and dust: what a warehouse office does to a recorder

Recorders run around the clock and depend on a fan to stay cool. In a steel warehouse office in July, on a shelf above a workbench, or in a closed closet next to a boiler, the drive and the power supply run hot all day and wear out years early. Dust clogs the fan and makes it worse. The signs are a recorder that restarts on hot afternoons, a drive that starts reporting errors, and gaps in recording that line up with the hottest hours. The repair is a new surveillance-rated drive and, more importantly, a new home for the recorder where air can move.

Logins: when the person who set it up has moved on

A large share of East Williamsburg repair calls involve no broken hardware at all. The cameras work and the recorder records, but nobody can see anything, because the app account belongs to a former tenant, a former manager, a studio that moved to another neighborhood, or an installer who closed. In loft buildings with dozens of tenants, the person who once held the password may be long gone. The fix is to recover the recorder, set new passwords, register the account to the building's owner or manager, and write down who holds it.

Blind spots: the camera works, but not where it counts

Sometimes the system is fine and the coverage is not. The October 2025 Stagg Street burglary came through a rear door, the 2016 Randolph Street burglary came through an air vent, and the May 2026 food truck thefts happened at the curb. Plenty of East Williamsburg buildings have cameras aimed at the street-facing door and nothing useful at the back, the roof, the yard or the curb where vehicles park overnight. A repair visit is the right time to re-aim what you have, so the existing cameras cover the entry points that matter, before anyone pays for more equipment.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in East Williamsburg: Questions Answered

These are the situations East Williamsburg loft residents, building owners, warehouse managers, makers and shop owners describe when they call. Each answer is written for the buildings and blocks here.

Lofts and conversions

The camera screwed into a wooden beam in our loft slowly aims at the floor every summer and creeps back up every winter. Is the camera loose?

Usually the camera is fine and the wood is moving. Old heavy-timber beams swell and shrink with the seasons, and a small bracket held by short wood screws loosens a little each cycle until the weight of the camera and its cable tips it. We remount the camera with longer fasteners into sound wood, or on a plate or clamp that spreads the load, re-aim it, and check that the cable has some slack so it is not pulling the camera down. Where the beam is split or soft, we move the camera to the brick or a steel member nearby.

Contractors legalizing our building keep disconnecting the corridor cameras. How do we keep the system recording while the work goes on?

The best protection is a labeled system and a short briefing. We trace and label every camera cable at both ends, tag the runs where they pass through the areas being opened, and give the building's representative a one-page diagram to hand to each new crew. We also turn on alerts so the owner or manager hears within minutes when a camera goes dark, instead of finding out at the next incident. If a section of the building will be open for months, a temporary camera on battery backup can cover that stretch until the walls close, and we reconnect the permanent camera once the crew is done.

Our building's entrance camera used to show up as a channel on our apartment TVs, and since the cable boxes were replaced the channel is gone. Is the camera broken?

Often it is not. Older buildings sent the lobby or vestibule camera to tenants' TVs through the building's antenna or cable wiring, using a small device that turns the camera's picture into a TV channel. New cable boxes may not tune that channel, and the device or its cable may have been disconnected during the swap. In the 2015 case the state housing agency decided, its inspector found exactly that: the antenna cable disconnected from the security camera. We test the camera, the converter and the building wiring, and if the old method cannot work with the new boxes, we can show the owner other ways to give tenants a view of the entrance.

Warehouses, workshops and the industrial blocks

The building on Stagg Street was burglarized through its rear door. All of our cameras face the street. What should a repair visit change?

Start with coverage, not equipment. We walk the building with you and check every door, dock, vent, skylight and fence line against what each camera actually sees. Often one or two street cameras are covering the same sidewalk, and one of them can be moved inside to watch the rear door from where nobody can reach it, with another re-aimed down the side street or alley. The recorder should sit away from the rear entrance, so a burglar who comes in that way does not walk past it. We price any new camera separately, but in many buildings re-aiming and fixing what is there closes most of the gap.

Sparks from the welding bench keep pitting the lens of our shop camera. Is there a camera that survives that?

The answer is usually placement and a cover, not a tougher camera. Spatter travels in fairly predictable directions from a bench or a grinder, and a camera mounted higher, farther back, or to the side of that spray lasts far longer. A dome or bullet with a replaceable clear cover lets you swap a scratched cover for a small part cost instead of replacing the camera. We also check the recorder and switch, because metal dust that pits a lens also clogs fans, and keep them away from the work area.

Our office is inside a steel warehouse, and the recorder there shuts itself off on summer afternoons. Can it stay where it is?

It can stay if the space can be ventilated, but usually it should move. Recorders and their drives need moving air; a small office with no window unit inside a metal building can get hot enough to trip the recorder's protection or shorten the drive's life badly. We read the drive's health, replace it if it is failing, and either add ventilation or move the recorder to a cooler spot, such as a lower shelf, a different room, or a small ventilated cabinet, with the cables extended to reach it.

Curbside, venues and nightlife

We park our food truck overnight on Johnson Avenue. Can a camera on the building watch it well enough to help, or does the truck need its own?

A building camera is usually the better choice. It has steady power, records to a recorder inside, and does not drain the truck's battery. It has to be aimed at the spot where the truck parks, close enough to see faces and plates at night, with infrared or a camera with its own light that reaches the curb. We test the view at night with the truck in place. A camera on the truck itself can add an inside view, but it runs on the truck's power and needs its own connection, so it works as an addition, not a replacement. Alerts for motion near the curb during parked hours tell you while something is happening.

On show nights, the haze from our venue's fog machine turns every camera into a grey blur. Is that a camera problem?

Mostly it is light. Haze scatters light, especially the camera's own infrared and the stage lights, so the camera sees a glowing fog instead of people. We turn off infrared on cameras near the stage and rely on the room light, adjust exposure so the camera does not chase every lighting change, and aim the cameras that matter at the doors, bar and cash area, where haze is thinner. A camera mounted lower and closer to a door often gets clear faces through the same haze that blinds one on the ceiling.

Power and the street

Con Edison blamed the Montrose Avenue outage on road salt reaching its underground equipment. Can salt on our sidewalk hurt the camera cable that runs under it to our gate?

It can, over time. A camera cable buried or run in conduit under a sidewalk or yard to a gate, a fence or a second building sits in the same wet, salty ground every winter. If the conduit is cracked or the connectors at either end are not sealed, water gets in, the copper corrodes, and the camera at the end starts to flicker in cold, wet weather and then fails. We test the run, replace corroded connectors, seal the ends and, if the run itself is damaged, replace it with cable rated for direct burial or in new conduit.

"A Wi-Fi camera is the easy replacement for a dead wired one"

Many answers suggest skipping cable repairs altogether by swapping a failed wired camera for a wireless one.

Inside a corrugated-steel warehouse or a concrete loft, that advice often fails on the first day. Metal walls and roofs block Wi-Fi far more than wood or brick, and a camera at a loading door or across a yard may never hold a steady connection. Every wireless camera still needs power, so the outlet or adapter can fail just as it does on a wired system. And each one depends on a router and an app account that someone has to keep.

Repairing the existing cable is usually cheaper and steadier. Wireless makes sense for a spot where cable truly cannot reach, set up on the owner's network with a signal test first, not as a blanket fix.

"A camera over the front door covers the building"

Advice aimed at homes tends to treat the front door as the place that matters, with a camera there and maybe one in the back yard.

East Williamsburg's own record says otherwise. The October 2025 Stagg Street burglary came through a rear door while the building was empty. The 2016 Randolph Street office burglary came through an air vent. The May 2026 generator thefts happened at the curb on Johnson Avenue. A repair visit should end with a coverage check: every entrance listed, every camera's real view compared against it, and the gaps written down. Moving existing cameras is labor, not equipment, and it often matters more than replacing a dead one.

"Your landlord is responsible for fixing the building's cameras"

AI answers often state flatly that landlords must keep building cameras working, or flatly that they never have to.

The real answer depends on the building. A housing attorney quoted by Brick Underground, answering a Williamsburg renter whose packages kept disappearing, said city buildings are not required to have working cameras, and that a landlord does not have to share footage outside a legal process. Another attorney in the same piece said there is an argument that a landlord has a duty when theft is ongoing. And in a 2015 ruling, the state's housing agency upheld rent reductions when a vestibule camera tenants had relied on for 19 years was left disconnected, finding the landlord was required to maintain it.

If you are a tenant, put the problem in writing to the owner or manager and keep a copy, and if the building is rent-regulated, a long-provided camera service may count. If you own the building, a working system and a written repair record protect you as much as the tenants. We take repair requests from owners and managing agents, who approve and pay for work on building systems.

"Motion-only recording saves storage with no downside"

Storage advice often recommends switching every camera to record only on motion, to make a small drive last longer.

On Metropolitan Avenue and Grand Street, a street-facing camera sees motion nearly all the time, so motion-only recording saves little and floods the owner with alerts. In a quiet warehouse at night, the opposite problem appears: motion recording can start a second or two after someone enters the frame, which is the part a detective wants. Cameras that rely on detection also miss what they were never set to detect, such as a person moving slowly at the edge of the view.

For entrances, docks and curbs, continuous recording on a properly sized surveillance drive is usually the safer setting, with motion alerts layered on top. We size the drive for the number of days you need to keep, including a long weekend when nobody checks.

"Any handyman or electrician can repair your camera system"

General answers often treat camera repair as handyman work, no different from mounting a shelf.

In New York, a company that installs or services security systems for someone else needs a license from the Department of State, and the state's rules include closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. An owner working on their own property, or having their own employee do it, is exempt. Beyond licensing, camera repair is mostly diagnosis: telling a dead camera from a dead switch port, a corroded connector, an address conflict or a failing drive. Guessing wrong means paying for a part that was never broken.

Ask any company working on your cameras for its license number. Ours is NYS license #12000287431, and it appears on our paperwork.

"Registering your cameras with the NYPD means police are watching them"

Some answers blur camera registration and camera sharing together, as if signing up means the police can see your feed.

They are different things. Registering tells the police where a camera is, so detectives know whom to ask after an incident. Integration is a separate step: the Connect New York pilot the city announced in 2024 linked participating businesses' cameras directly to the NYPD in nine precincts across the five boroughs, so police could view them in real time. A business that only registers still controls its recorder, and nothing leaves the building unless the owner exports it.

Either way, the program is only as useful as the camera. A registered camera that has been dark for a month helps nobody. If you plan to register or integrate, have the system repaired and checked first, with the clock set correctly, so the footage holds up when it is asked for.

A muralist is about to paint the wall our cameras are mounted on. Do the cameras have to come down?
+

Not always, but they should be protected. Spray paint drifts onto lenses and into connectors, and an artist working around a camera may move its bracket or pull its cable. We can cover the cameras and their connections before the work starts and uncover and re-aim them afterward, or take them down and remount them once the paint is dry. Either way, ask the artist to leave the cable path clear so the camera can go back in the same place.

Our warehouse is unheated over the winter holidays. Will the recorder and cameras survive near-freezing temperatures?
+

Outdoor-rated cameras are built for cold and usually do fine. The recorder and its drive are indoor equipment, and the risk is less the cold itself than condensation when the building warms up again, plus a drive that struggles to start in a very cold room after a power cut. Keep the recorder running rather than switching it off, keep it off the floor and away from loading doors, and if the office gets very cold, we can move it to the most sheltered spot in the building.

Can a camera be mounted on corrugated metal siding without the screws letting rain into the wall?
+

Yes, with the right mount. We fasten into the framing behind the siding where we can, use fasteners with sealing washers, seal the cable entry and route the cable so water runs away from the hole, not into it. On a repair, a rust streak or water stain below an existing camera usually means the original holes were never sealed, and we fix that along with the camera.

Every time our shop's air compressor kicks on, two cameras blink off for a second. Is the compressor hurting them?
+

The compressor is causing it, but the cameras are rarely damaged. A large motor draws a surge of current as it starts, and if the cameras' adapters or power supply share that circuit, the voltage dips long enough for them to restart. We move the camera power to a different circuit or onto a battery backup that rides through the dip, and replace any adapter that has been weakened by the repeated restarts.

We keep tools in a shipping container on our lot. Can a camera inside the container record reliably?
+

Yes, if it is wired. A steel container blocks Wi-Fi almost completely, so a wireless camera inside it will rarely stay connected. A camera inside needs a cable run to the building, protected where it crosses the yard, and a housing that copes with condensation, which builds up quickly inside a closed steel box. A camera on the building aimed at the container door is often the simpler first step.

The camera cable in our loft runs right alongside the old steam riser. Does the heat matter?
+

It can. Ordinary camera cable is not made to sit against a hot pipe through every heating season, and over years the jacket can soften, crack or shrink, especially where it was tied directly to the pipe. If a camera on that run drops out mostly in winter, heat damage is a likely cause. We test the run, and if it is damaged we reroute the new cable a safe distance from the riser.

Can the cameras be powered from the building's emergency lighting circuit so they stay on in a blackout?
