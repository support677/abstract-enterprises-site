The Neighborhood

Why Wyckoff Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

Wyckoff Heights is not one of the names most Brooklynites could put on a map. Wikipedia describes it as an area that straddles the border between Bushwick in Brooklyn and the southwest corner of Ridgewood in Queens, urbanized from the late nineteenth century on land farmed by the Wyckoff family, and settled first by German immigrants, then Italian families, and more recently by Latino and Eastern European residents. Forgotten New York wondered in 2008 whether the hospital invented the name, and the sources do not agree on its edges; we say so below rather than pretend otherwise.

What every source agrees on is the building that gives the area its modern name. Wyckoff Heights Medical Center, at 374 Stockholm Street where it meets Wyckoff Avenue, is a 350-bed teaching and safety-net hospital with an emergency department open around the clock. It began as the German Hospital of Brooklyn, raised by a society of German immigrants, and took the Wyckoff Heights name after the First World War. Being close to it brings help no faster: a smoke detector does not ring there, and a fall on Himrod Street shows up on no screen on Stockholm Street. Help still has to be asked for, by the right signal.

The three local reports we built this page around show why the kind of signal matters as much as the alarm itself. The February 2026 break-ins on Myrtle Avenue near Stockholm Street happened through apartment front doors while the occupants were home, at a quarter past two in the morning, by men police said were armed. A loud siren at that moment can make things worse for the person inside; what that household needed was a way to ask for police without saying a word, and a door that announced itself the second it opened. The June 2024 robbery at Irving Avenue and Linden Street held an employee and a customer inside for more than two hours; a counter like that needs a silent hold-up signal that goes to police and nowhere else. And the October 2023 fire at 120 Menahan Street, blamed on a lithium-ion battery, filled the stairs with smoke before dawn; that signal has to wake everyone at once and reach the fire department even if nobody can call.

Add the building stock and the border, and you have the reason this page is organized the way it is. Wikipedia notes that the first houses here were attached wood-frame three- and six-family rows, followed by masonry once the fire codes changed, and in the 1940s by attached single-family houses with alleys and garages at the rear. Several households under one roof means several people who need different messages from one panel. The borough line runs down the middle of some of these streets, so the same panel’s address may be in Brooklyn while the house across the street is in Queens. So a Wyckoff Heights alarm is best planned signal by signal, on paper, before a sensor goes on a wall. Most owners who start with burglary end up adding smoke, heat and carbon monoxide reporting, and many pair the alarm with security cameras in Wyckoff Heights so a signal arrives with a picture.

Wyckoff Heights at a glance

  • Two boroughs, two ZIP codes: Wikipedia places the area largely in ZIP codes 11237 (Brooklyn) and 11385 (Queens). The Queens side changed to 11385 on January 13, 1980, after 93 percent of returned ballots supported the switch.
  • A post office with the neighborhood’s name: the Wyckoff Heights post office at 86 Wyckoff Avenue opened in 1951 to serve north Bushwick and the Brooklyn part of Wyckoff Heights.
  • The hospital: 374 Stockholm Street, 350 beds, emergency department, a New York State Primary Stroke Center and Level III Perinatal Center, per Wikipedia; the hospital also lists offices at 110 Wyckoff Avenue, 1411 and 1419 Myrtle Avenue and the Medical Arts Building at 1610 DeKalb Avenue.
  • Seventeen border streets: Forgotten New York counts Wyckoff Avenue among seventeen streets that border both Brooklyn and Queens, along with Flushing, Cypress, St. Nicholas, Gates and Irving Avenues and Menahan Street.
  • Police: the Brooklyn side is the NYPD’s 83rd Precinct, whose station house is at 480 Knickerbocker Avenue; the Queens side is the 104th.
  • Transit under the avenue: the L train runs beneath the whole length of Wyckoff Avenue, from Flushing Avenue to Cooper Avenue, and meets the elevated M at the Myrtle–Wyckoff Avenues station, rebuilt as a combined interchange in 2007.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Wyckoff Heights’s Architecture

Wikipedia’s short history of Wyckoff Heights describes three waves of building, and walking the streets adds two more: the factory blocks at the north end of Wyckoff Avenue and the shops and offices that line the avenues. Each changes where the devices can go and which signals matter most.

Attached wood-frame three- and six-family rows

The first houses, according to Wikipedia, were attached wood-frame three- and six-family row houses; one of the article’s photographs shows a row on St. Nicholas Avenue. For an alarm, two features dominate. First, the households: three or six front doors off one stair, a shared street door, and usually one cellar with the boiler and the meters. Second, the construction: wood frame, attached on both sides, with a shared roofline. Fire is the signal we take most seriously in this stock. Smoke detectors go at the top of the stair and in each household’s hall, a heat detector in the cellar near the boiler where dust and humidity would make a smoke detector unreliable, and the fire signal is set to sound in every household, not only in the one where it started. Burglary sensors follow each household’s own door and the windows that can be reached from the yard, a fire escape or a lower roof.

Masonry rows built to the newer fire codes

Around the turn of the twentieth century, Wikipedia says, builders moving toward the borough line and into Queens switched to masonry to meet new building and fire codes, and many of those buildings are now in the Cypress Avenue West Historic District on the Queens side. On the Brooklyn side, Forgotten New York photographed light-brown brick residences on Wyckoff Avenue between Willoughby Avenue and Suydam Street and suggested the bricks may come from the same Staten Island kilns that supplied Ridgewood. Brick changes the wiring, not the plan: these buildings usually get wireless sensors with a wired panel, tested for signal through the party walls.

Houses that turn their side to Cypress Avenue

Joseph Heathcott, who photographed the whole Brooklyn–Queens line for his BQ Borderlands project, asked his readers why so many houses along Cypress Avenue are turned ninety degrees away from the street. He called it one of the longest stretches he had seen anywhere in the city where a street is faced by the sides of houses rather than their fronts. For an alarm, the avenue side becomes a side wall of street-facing windows, often bedrooms, while the front door opens onto a side yard; we walk the outside before drawing the plan.

1940s attached houses with alleys and rear garages

The last large development, in the 1940s, built automobile-oriented attached single-family houses with alleys and garages at the rear; a 1945 New York Times headline describes builders buying the final eighteen acres of the Wyckoff holdings, which it calls a Queens farm, so most of this stock sits on the Queens side of the line. Where we find it, the rear is the real entrance: the garage door, the door from the garage into the house, and the alley gate. Those openings get their own sensors and are put on a separate notification so the family hears about the alley, while the monitoring center is asked to act only on doors into the house itself.

Factory blocks and lofts at the north end of Wyckoff Avenue

Forgotten New York found Wyckoff Avenue from Flushing Avenue to about Starr Street still largely industrial in 2008, with lofts filling up with artists and musicians moving east from Williamsburg, and a former candy factory converted to lofts at Willoughby Avenue. A 1974 Times real estate note records the former Schlitz brewery “in the Wyckoff Heights section of Brooklyn” being redeveloped. These buildings bring freight doors, long banks of old factory windows, roof hatches and shared corridors. A tenant’s plan covers the unit; the owner’s covers the roof hatch and the loading door. They are two plans, not one.

The Signal Plan

Five Messages From One Panel, and Why Each Gets a Different Answer

A modern alarm panel does not just “go off.” Each device is assigned to a zone, and each zone has a type that decides what the panel does when it trips. In practice there are five families of message, and most mistakes in old systems come from one set up as another. A hold-up button programmed as an ordinary burglary zone sounds the siren in the store. A smoke detector wired as a burglary zone stays silent when the system is disarmed, which is exactly when people are home. A medical button set up as a panic zone sends police instead of an ambulance.

So before we mount anything on a Wyckoff Heights wall, we write the plan out as a table, in plain words, and go through it with you. Each line answers four questions: what trips it, does a siren sound, who is told first, and who is told after that. The table below is the starting point we use; every household changes it.

Home When the Door Opens: What the February 2026 Myrtle Avenue Reports Change

News 12 Brooklyn reported on February 9, 2026, that just after 2:15 that Monday morning, three or four men walked through the front door of a 62-year-old man’s apartment in a building on Myrtle Avenue near Stockholm Street, showed guns and took property, then went into a second apartment in the same building, belonging to a 27-year-old man, again through the front door. The younger man was treated by EMS at the scene; a dog, bank cards and phones were taken. A thread on r/Bushwick, which we could read only as a search excerpt, later added that the dog had been left at Wyckoff Heights Medical Center and was reunited with its owner that evening.

Nothing in the reports says how the men got through those front doors, and we will not guess. What the case shows is the situation many alarm sales pitches skip: the people were home, it was the middle of the night, and the risk came through the door they use every day. For that household, three parts of the signal plan matter more than the siren.

  • The apartment door is an instant zone at night. In the setting most people call “stay” or “night,” the door that leads out of the apartment reports the moment it opens, with no entry countdown, and a chime or voice at the keypad in the bedroom announces it. The point is time: knowing the door opened before anyone reaches the bedroom.
  • A silent call for police, within reach. A key-ring or bedside button set as a silent hold-up, or a duress code at the keypad, lets someone ask for police without a siren that might escalate a confrontation. We set these silent by default in occupied homes and explain the trade-off: silent signals depend on monitoring, because without it nobody receives them.
  • The siren is a choice, not a reflex. For a break-in while the household is out, a siren helps; it tells the neighbors and shortens the visit. For a person inside with an intruder, the decision is the household’s, and the plan can sound the siren for an empty-apartment burglary and stay silent for a night-time panic button.

None of this replaces police advice: lock the door, do not open it to strangers, never confront an intruder.

Behind the Counter: Silent Signals for Money Transfer Stores, Bodegas and Pharmacies

ABC7 New York’s account of June 8, 2024 is short and specific. At about 7:45 on a Saturday evening, a man walked into a money transfer store at Irving Avenue and Linden Street, acted as if he were going to make a transaction, and then announced a robbery. Two employees and a customer were inside. One of the employees managed to get out and call 911. Arriving officers found the door barricaded with chairs, hostage negotiators talked with the man through a window for more than two hours, and he surrendered; the gun he had pointed at officers turned out to be fake.

An alarm system would not have prevented that robbery, and we would not claim it could. What a counter business on these avenues can have is a way to ask for police the moment a robbery is announced, without anyone running for the door. That is what a hold-up signal is for, and the way it is set up matters more than the brand:

  • Fixed buttons where staff actually stand. Under the counter at each window, at the safe, and in the back office, mounted so a knee or a hand reaches them without looking down. Each one is labeled on the plan by position, so the monitoring center can tell police which part of the store sent it.
  • A wireless button that travels. Robberies often come at opening or closing, when the gate is half up and one person is alone. A wireless button on a lanyard or key ring covers the walk from the door to the register.
  • A duress code. If someone is forced to disarm the system, a code one digit different from the normal one disarms it and quietly reports duress.
  • Silence, always. Hold-up zones are programmed with no siren, no keypad display and no chime. Nothing in the store changes when one is pressed.
  • No call back to the store. For a burglary alarm, the monitoring center usually calls the premises first. For a hold-up, it should not, because a ringing phone in a store being robbed helps nobody. We confirm that instruction on the account.

The store’s signals stay on the store’s plan, separate from the apartments above. Most owners pair this with cameras over the counter and the door, which our camera page for Wyckoff Heights covers, and some with a buzzer-controlled door, which is on our Wyckoff Heights door access page.

Fire in Attached Frame Rows: Smoke, Heat and the Battery in the Hallway

In attached wood-frame rows, fire is the signal that has to reach the most people the fastest. NY1 reported on October 17, 2023, that a fire caused by a lithium-ion battery broke out in a second-floor apartment at 120 Menahan Street in the Hope Gardens public housing complex in Bushwick shortly after 4:30 that morning. Five people were hurt, including two children. A resident described being told to go down the stairs and choking on the black smoke on the way. The FDNY said at the time that it had responded to 216 lithium-ion battery fires in the city that year, with 120 injuries and 14 deaths, and New York City’s Local Law 39, which had taken effect the month before, banned the sale or rental of e-bikes and e-scooters whose batteries were not certified by an accredited testing laboratory.

Hope Gardens is a seven-story building in Bushwick, not a frame row; we cite it as the closest documented case of batteries charging overnight indoors. A panel already being installed is the cheapest way to make a detector do more than beep in one room.

  • Smoke at the top of the stair and in each hall. In a three- or six-family row, smoke rises into the stair first. A detector at the top landing and one in each household’s hall, all on the panel, means smoke on the second floor sounds on the first and third as well.
  • Heat where smoke detectors lie. Cellars with boilers, kitchens and dusty storage rooms produce false smoke alarms; a fixed-temperature or rate-of-rise heat detector there still reports a real fire.
  • The charging spot gets its own detector. If a household charges a scooter or e-bike battery in the same place every night, a smoke detector above that spot is named for it in the app (“front hall charger”), so the first message says where to look.
  • Fire signals are 24-hour zones. They report whether the burglary side is armed or not, and they cannot be turned off from the app by mistake.
  • Every household hears it. On a shared panel, a fire signal sounds in every household’s area and goes to every household’s phone, even when burglary signals are kept private.

A monitored smoke detector is not a substitute for the smoke and carbon monoxide alarms New York City already requires in apartments. HPD’s rules put installing those on the owner and replacing their batteries on the tenant. We add monitored detectors alongside what the law requires, and we never remove a required alarm to replace it with one of ours.

Carbon Monoxide and the Boiler Everyone Shares

Most of the older multi-family houses here have one boiler in the cellar serving every floor. A cracked flue or a blocked chimney on that boiler is a risk to every household at once, and it tends to show up on the coldest nights, when windows are shut and everyone is asleep. HPD requires carbon monoxide detectors in apartment buildings, and in one- and two-family homes that are not owner-occupied where there are fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of the entrance to each sleeping room. Those alarms sound in the room. A CO detector on the alarm panel adds the part a standalone alarm cannot do: it tells someone outside the house.

  • Where they go: within fifteen feet of each bedroom door, as HPD’s rule sets, and one in the cellar at the distance from the boiler the instructions set, so that a leak is caught at its source as well as at the bedrooms.
  • What they send: a CO signal, separate from smoke, so the monitoring center and the fire department know it is a gas problem rather than a fire, and so you know not to go down to the cellar to look.
  • Who is told: every household the boiler serves, plus the owner, even an owner who lives elsewhere. A tenant on the third floor may not know who to call about the boiler; the owner does.
  • Life of the sensor: CO sensing elements wear out. The panel reports an end-of-life warning as a trouble signal to the owner, and we write the replacement year on the plan.

If you smell gas, leave and call 911 from outside.

The Hospital Is Close, but It Is Not on Your Call List

People who live within sight of Wyckoff Heights Medical Center sometimes assume that being near an emergency room makes a medical button pointless. It does not work that way. The hospital does not receive signals from home alarms, and nobody at the hospital knows your door has not opened since yesterday. An ambulance is sent through 911, and the 911 system decides which unit comes. Being close to the emergency department shortens the ride there; it does nothing to shorten the time before someone realizes help is needed.

That gap is what a medical signal closes. A wall button in the bathroom and the bedroom, and a wearable button for the person who lives alone or is alone during the day, can be set up as medical zones. With monitoring, the station’s procedure for a medical signal is to speak to the person through the panel’s speaker where the panel supports it, call back, and request an ambulance through 911 if there is no answer or the person asks for one, then work down a call list of relatives and neighbors. We write the list to include at least one person who lives within a few minutes and has a key, because an ambulance crew at a locked street door in a six-family is the delay families describe most.

An alarm panel is not a medical device and is not a medical alert service. It cannot detect a fall on its own, it does not monitor health, and a door sensor that has not opened is not proof of anything. For a serious condition, ask a doctor about a dedicated medical alert device; the panel adds a silent way to ask for help from the rooms where people fall.

Two Boroughs on One Street: What the Line Changes for an Alarm

The Brooklyn–Queens line runs straight through the neighborhood, and the sources describe it slightly differently. Forgotten New York says the county line arrives at Wyckoff Avenue from Gates Avenue near Myrtle Avenue and then runs down the middle of Wyckoff Avenue to George Street. A reader under Heathcott’s Urban Omnibus essay, writing in 2015, gave the 1925 route as Cypress Avenue to Menahan Street to St. Nicholas Avenue to Gates Avenue to Wyckoff Avenue and on to Eldert Street and Irving Avenue. Heathcott himself photographed a building at Irving Avenue and Eldert Street that the line slices through, marked by a thin grey line in the brick. Untapped Cities, cited on our Ridgewood page, describes the border along Cypress and Wyckoff. We follow the city’s own records for each address rather than any of these descriptions, and we say on this page that they do not match exactly.

For an alarm, the line changes three practical things, and our Ridgewood alarm installation page goes deeper into the Queens side.

  • The address on the monitoring account. A Brooklyn house here has a plain Brooklyn number and ZIP 11237; a house across the street in Queens may have a hyphenated Queens number and 11385. The account is set up with the borough, the ZIP and the nearest cross street written out, so nobody has to work it out at 3 a.m.
  • Which precinct and which firehouse. That is decided by the 911 system from the address, not by the monitoring center and not by us. Our part is making sure the address it receives is complete and correct.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Wyckoff Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions the local reports and the building stock raise. We could not open Google’s “People also ask” box, Answer the Public or Yelp from the environment this page was built in, so none of the questions below is copied from them; they are the ones the sources on this page lead to, grouped the way the signal plan is.

Who is home, and when

One of us works nights and sleeps from about nine in the morning until four. Can the panel switch itself into its at-home setting on a daytime schedule instead of at bedtime?

Yes. Most current panels let each area follow a schedule, and the schedule does not care whether “night” falls at 11 p.m. or 9 a.m. For a day sleeper we usually set the at-home setting to come on automatically at a fixed time on work days, with the doors to the apartment as instant zones and the motion detector in the hall left off, and to switch back to disarmed in the afternoon. The app sends a reminder if the schedule tries to arm while a door is open.

We live a block from Wyckoff Heights Medical Center. Does being that close to an emergency room change how a medical button on our alarm should be set up?

Not in the way people hope. The hospital receives no signals from home alarms, and an ambulance is sent through 911 no matter how close you live. What being close does change is the call list. Neighbors who live a few doors away and have a key matter more than relatives across the city, because the slowest part of a medical call in a walk-up is usually getting a crew through a locked street door and up the stairs. So a nearby key holder goes near the top of the list, and each button is labeled by room.

My father lives alone on the third floor of a walk-up with a locked street door. If he presses a medical button and cannot get downstairs, how does the ambulance crew get in?

This is the question to settle before the button goes on the wall, not after. There are three common answers, and many families use two of them. A neighbor in the building or on the block who holds keys and agrees to be called first. A key lock box at the street door, if the building owner allows one, with the code given to the monitoring center to pass on to responders. Or, where the street door already has an electric strike and an intercom, a way for a trusted person to buzz the crew in remotely; our Wyckoff Heights intercom page covers that side. Whatever you choose, it is written into the monitoring account’s notes, and the instructions say plainly which floor and which door.

Counters and cash

If an employee presses a hold-up button while a robber is still in the store, what does the monitoring operator actually know, and what do they pass on to police?

The operator sees the account, the address and cross street, the business name, the zone that sent the signal and its label, for example “hold-up, window 2,” and the time. They do not see or hear inside unless cameras or listen-in were agreed. What they pass to the 911 dispatcher is that a hold-up alarm has been received from that address and which button sent it. That is why labeling matters: “hold-up, back office” and “hold-up, front counter” tell responding officers different things. It is also why the account is set not to call the store back. If you want a manager’s phone called afterwards, that instruction goes on the account as well.

Our counter closes at nine and one person pulls the gate down alone. Where should the button be for those last ten minutes?

On that person, not on the counter. The last minutes of a business day are spent between the register, the back room, the light switches and the gate, and a fixed button under the counter is out of reach for most of them. A wireless hold-up button on a lanyard or key ring goes everywhere the closer goes, including outside to the gate. We pair it with a duress code at the keypad by the door, so that if the closer is made to disarm or re-open, the code does that and reports duress at the same time. The fixed buttons stay where they are for the hours when staff are behind the counter.

Shared houses, shared signals

Can the alarm send different signals to different people, for instance a break-in only to me, a fire to everyone in the building and a low battery only to the landlord?

Yes, and that is the heart of the signal plan. Each person in the app is given notification settings by area and by signal type. A tenant gets burglary signals for their own apartment and fire and CO for the whole building; the owner gets what was agreed. Trouble signals, such as a low battery or lost power, go to whoever is responsible for fixing them. The central station follows the same plan, and each household gets a printed copy.

Our three-family has one gas boiler in the cellar for all three floors. Whose phones should a carbon monoxide signal from the cellar go to?

Everyone’s, and the owner’s even if the owner lives elsewhere. A CO reading in the cellar means the boiler or its flue may be putting gas into the whole house, and every household needs to know at once so they can get out and not go downstairs to look. The owner needs to know because the owner arranges the boiler repair. With monitoring, a CO signal also goes to the central station, which calls the fire department. The plan should never route a CO signal to one person who might be asleep with their phone on silent.

Our attached frame house shares a roof space with the houses on both sides. Is a heat detector up there worth adding, and whose alarm should it report to?

Often it is. A fire that starts in or reaches a shared roof space can travel along the row before anyone below smells smoke, and a roof space is too dusty, hot and cold for most smoke detectors. A heat detector rated for that environment reports a real fire without the false alarms. It reports to your panel and your households, since it is on your property and your wiring. What you cannot do is make it report to your neighbors’ alarms. What you can do is agree with them, informally, to be on each other’s call lists for fire signals only, which some owners in attached rows do.

The street and the line

Our house is turned sideways on Cypress Avenue, so the bedroom windows face the avenue and the front door opens onto a side yard. Which openings should be on the alarm first?

The windows on the avenue side that someone can reach from the sidewalk, then the side-yard door, then anything at the back. In a house like this the usual instinct, front door first, puts the effort on a door that may face a neighbor’s wall and leaves the bedroom windows on a busy street unprotected. Avenue-side windows get contacts and glass-break, armed in the night setting. The side-yard gate, if there is one, gets a chime-only contact that tells the household and never calls anyone.

Our building shows two different house numbers by the door. Which one goes on the monitoring account?

The one the city uses for your building, which is the one on your property tax bill and in the city’s building records. Heathcott noted that some houses on the border display two addresses so the mail arrives, and Wikipedia records that residents of the Queens section were still lobbying for house numbers and street signs in 1901. A monitoring center will pass on whatever address it has, so we put the official address, the borough, the ZIP code and the nearest cross street on the account, add the second number in the notes as “also shown at the door,” and ask the center to read the address back to us during the first test.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Wyckoff Heights

We could not open Google’s AI Overview, its follow-up questions, Bing or DuckDuckGo from the environment this page was built in, so nothing below is quoted from them. The claims are the general advice that cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, kit makers and AI-written summaries tend to repeat about alarms. Each is set against what the Wyckoff Heights sources show.

“An alarm’s job is to make a loud noise and scare burglars away”

That is one of its jobs, for an empty home. The local reports on this page are mostly about moments when a loud noise is the wrong answer: armed men inside an occupied apartment at 2:15 a.m., a robber holding two people inside a store with the door blocked.

In those moments the useful signal is the silent one, sent to people who can act, while nothing changes in the room.

So a plan here starts by deciding which signals must stay silent, and only then decides where the sirens go and how loud they are.

“Arm it when you leave and you are protected”

Away arming protects an empty home. It does nothing for the household asleep upstairs, the person who sleeps through the day after a night shift, or the parent home alone in the afternoon who falls.

In the frame rows and walk-ups here, people are home a great deal of the time, and the risks the sources describe, a night-time entry, a fire before dawn, a gas boiler on a cold night, all happen with people inside.

A Wyckoff Heights system should be judged by what it does while you are home: which doors are instant at night, which smoke detectors wake every household, and which button calls for help.

“Smoke detectors are a separate thing; leave them out of the alarm”

The battery smoke alarms the law requires are separate, and they stay. But a standalone alarm only wakes the people who hear it.

In the October 2023 Menahan Street fire, a resident who lived several doors from the burning apartment told NY1 that police came banging on the door. A detector on a panel would have sounded in every household connected to it and reported out, even with nobody awake to call.

For an attached frame row, adding monitored smoke and heat detectors to a burglary system already being installed is one of the most useful things the money can buy.

“A panic button is a panic button”

On many systems the button on a fob or keypad is a loud panic: it sounds the siren. That is fine for scaring off someone at a window. It is wrong for a hold-up, where the siren can turn a robbery into something worse, and wrong for a fall, where the person needs an ambulance, not police.

Hold-up, duress, panic and medical are different zone types and lead to different responses. A button’s label in the app does not change what the panel sends; its programming does.

We set every button’s type on paper first, then program it, then test it with the monitoring center listening.

“Living next to a hospital or a precinct means help arrives fast anyway”

Help arrives fast once somebody asks for it. The hospital on Stockholm Street does not watch homes, and the 83rd Precinct on Knickerbocker Avenue does not watch closed stores.

The minutes that matter are the ones before anyone calls 911: a smoke-filled stair before dawn, a person on a bathroom floor, an employee who cannot reach a phone.

An alarm’s value here is measured in how quickly the right signal reaches the right person, not in how close the nearest emergency room is.

“Wireless sensors are unreliable in old buildings”

Wireless sensors have to be tested where they will live, and thick brick party walls do weaken signals. But wired systems in these houses mean opening plaster and running cable through occupied apartments, which most owners and tenants do not want.

The practical approach is a wired panel and siren where the wiring is easy, wireless sensors everywhere else, and a signal-strength reading at every device before it is fixed in place, with a repeater added where a reading is weak.

The panel also supervises each wireless sensor and reports one that stops checking in as a trouble signal to the owner, so a sensor that goes quiet does not stay quiet unnoticed.

“Budget a few hundred dollars and a monthly bill, and you’re covered”

A basic single-household system on our Brooklyn list does start at $600, and monitoring through our partners starts at $19.99 a month. That covers one household’s doors, a motion detector and a keypad.

If we decide against monitoring, which parts of the signal plan still work?
+

Burglary, fire and CO signals still sound the sirens and the detectors’ own sounders, and the app still sends each signal to the phones on the plan, so the people you chose are told. What stops working is everything silent. Hold-up, duress and medical signals would reach nobody, so we leave those devices out of an unmonitored system rather than install buttons that do nothing, and nobody calls 911 on your behalf for any signal. Monitoring is month to month, so it can be added later and the silent devices fitted then.

Can we keep the alarm company we already have and just add buttons or detectors from you?
+

Sometimes. New devices have to be programmed into the existing panel and added to the existing company’s monitoring account, and many panels are locked with an installer code that only the original company holds. If your company can give the panel access, we can add compatible detectors and buttons and leave the monitoring where it is. If not, the usual choice is a panel replacement on the existing wiring, $800 to $1,500 on our Brooklyn list, after which one company holds the panel, the plan and the account. Either way, we can test what you have and give you a written signal plan to take to your current company.

What exactly is in the written signal plan you hand over at the end, and how do we change it later?
+

One line per zone: its name in the app, the device, where it is, its signal type, whether a siren sounds, who is told first and who next. A second page lists each person’s notification settings and the special instructions on the monitoring account, such as do not call the store back after a hold-up, or the neighbor who holds keys. Codes are never written on it. Each household gets a copy on paper and by email. To change it, call or text us; many changes, such as call lists and notification settings, can be made without a visit, and we send the updated plan afterwards.

Our medical practice near the hospital already uses an after-hours answering service. Can that service receive our alarm signals instead of a central station?
+

Not for anything that needs police, fire or an ambulance. An answering service takes phone calls; it is not set up to receive signals from an alarm panel or to request a dispatch from them, so burglary, hold-up, fire and CO signals should go to a central station. The answering service, or the practice manager, can be on the call list the central station works through after it has acted, and opening and closing reports and trouble signals can go to the office by email or app.
