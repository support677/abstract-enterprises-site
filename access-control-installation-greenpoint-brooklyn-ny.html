The Neighborhood

Why Greenpoint Properties Need Access Control

Greenpoint is Brooklyn's northernmost neighborhood, wrapped by the East River on the west and Newtown Creek on the north and east, with ZIP code 11222 and the NYPD's 94th Precinct covering it. For a door installer the useful fact about Greenpoint is not its size but its mix. Within a fifteen-minute walk there are forty-story rental towers with parking garages, six-unit brick tenements with a single street door, flathouses with a shop below and apartments above, wood-frame houses split into two or three units, and former factories where seventy businesses share a few entrances. Each kind of building fails in its own way, and each needs a different answer.

The newest doors are on the waterfront. In 2005 the City Council rezoned 175 blocks of Greenpoint and neighboring Williamsburg, and Gothamist counted at least thirteen waterfront towers finished or nearly finished by 2023. The largest project, Greenpoint Landing, covers 22 acres and is planned for eleven towers and 5,500 apartments. CityRealty describes one of them, 16 Dupont Street, as a 40-story building of 380 rentals with 138 enclosed parking spaces and retail on the ground floor. Buildings of that size do not have one door. They have a lobby, a package room, bike storage, tenant storage cages, a garage ramp and the stairs and corridors that connect them, plus a retail tenant with its own staff entrance. The lobby is the door everyone watches. The others are where trouble tends to start.

Greenpointers reported one such case in September 2024. At about 1:32 in the morning someone came to 26 West Street, a luxury rental building, and, in the NYPD's words, "forcibly pulled the front door open," then left on foot with property; a resident told the site that items were taken from the building's storage unit. Nothing in that report suggests the door was unlocked. It was locked, and it was pulled open anyway. That detail matters more to us than any brand of reader, because a reader can only decide whether to release a lock. It cannot make a frame stiff, a strike strong or a door too heavy to bend. When we plan a Greenpoint lobby, the first test is a hard pull on the closed door, and the second is what the system does when the door opens without a credential.

The older doors fail differently. Brooklyn Post reported that two men walked into 182 Driggs Avenue "through the front door" at about four in the morning and took an $1,800 bicycle that was locked up in the lobby. In walk-ups and tenements the street door is usually closed by a tired closer onto a latch that barely catches, and anyone who reaches it a second after a resident, or presses every button until somebody buzzes, is inside. One Greenpoint resident put the delivery version plainly on Reddit: "Most drivers will ring multiple apartments at once to get into the building." HPD's records show how often the doors themselves are the problem. In ZIP code 11222, inspectors wrote between 222 and 284 violations a year from 2023 to 2026 for doors that were not self-closing, and 64 intercom violations since the start of 2023. A fob reader fitted to a door that does not shut on its own changes none of that.

Businesses here have been learning the same lesson for years. In 2017, Brooklyn Post reported that a burglar entered Baoburg on Manhattan Avenue "through an unlocked basement door" at 1:50 in the morning, then broke into a laundromat on Norman Avenue through a side window an hour later. In July 2020 Greenpointers reported a man smashing open Bagel Point's Norman Avenue entrance with a brick and an attempted break-in that destroyed the stained glass at The Greenpoint Palace on Nassau Avenue. In 2013, DNAinfo reported two burglars hitting a dozen North Brooklyn bars and shops in about six weeks, mostly through "street level entrances" late at night. A basement hatch, a staff door behind the kitchen and a cellar entrance on the side street are the doors a business owner checks least often at closing time, and they are the ones an access system can watch.

None of this means Greenpoint is getting more dangerous. The NYPD's CompStat report for the 94th Precinct for the week ending November 8, 2026 shows 87 burglaries so far this year against 127 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 31.5 percent, and burglary in the precinct is down 91.2 percent over 33 years. We cite those numbers so nobody reads this page as a scare story. The reasons Greenpoint buildings call us are more ordinary: residents lose keys and nobody knows how many copies exist, tenants move out and their fobs keep working, studios change hands and the landlord still has the old tenant's code, and back doors get propped for deliveries and stay open overnight.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Greenpoint's Architecture

Greenpoint was built in layers: shipyard and factory owners' houses in the nineteenth century, tenements and flathouses for the workers who followed, industrial complexes along the creek, and glass towers on the old industrial waterfront in the last fifteen years. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report for the Greenpoint Historic District describes "substantial rowhouses built for the owners and managers of nearby businesses and factories, more modest rowhouses and numerous flathouses (walk-up apartment houses) for the factory laborers." Each layer has its own door, and the door decides the hardware.

Brick tenements and walk-ups

Four- to six-story buildings on blocks such as Eckford, Leonard, Lorimer and Diamond Streets, usually one steel or wood street door into a narrow vestibule, a cellar door off the back yard or the sidewalk, and a roof bulkhead. One reader and strike at the street, a contact on the cellar door, and a controller in the cellar beside the old intercom equipment.

Flathouses with a store below

The long rows on Greenpoint Avenue and Manhattan Avenue that the Landmarks report calls "flathouses with commercial ground floors": a residents' door squeezed beside a shopfront, and a store that has its own front door, back door and often a cellar hatch. Two separate systems, or one system with walls between its groups.

Wood-frame houses of two to four units

Narrow frame houses, many now clad in siding, often with a side entrance up a short stoop and a separate door for the ground-floor unit. A small station with one button per household, a strike on the shared door, and power from a common circuit or the owner's meter.

Historic district rowhouses

Brick and brownstone houses on Milton, Noble, Kent and Oak Streets inside the Greenpoint Historic District, where Landmarks rules govern what goes on the front. One discreet device per door opening, fixed in plain masonry or on the fence, with the lock and cable kept out of sight.

Converted factories and loft buildings

Multi-story industrial buildings near Newtown Creek and along the old rail and dock streets, now divided among studios, workshops and small manufacturers. Street, freight and stair doors controlled by the landlord; each tenant's door on its own schedule and administrator account.

Waterfront towers

New rental and condo high-rises on West Street, Commercial Street, Dupont Street, Eagle Street and the Greenpoint Landing blocks, with aluminum-and-glass lobby entrances, garages, storage rooms and amenity floors. Networked readers on every door behind the lobby, elevator rules, and the tenant-data paperwork the city requires.

What Greenpoint's materials mean for the install

Tenement street doors are often heavy steel replacements set into old wood or pressed-steel frames that were never meant to take a lock with any force behind it. Before we cut for an electric strike we sound the frame: if it is hollow and soft at the strike height, the frame gets a steel reinforcement behind the strike, or the strike goes into a new frame section, because a strike is only as strong as the metal it is screwed into. On wood-frame houses the issue is the wall rather than the door. Under the siding there may be original clapboard, sheathing and an air gap, so a station fixed to the siding alone will loosen; we fix through to framing or a mounting block and seal every penetration against rain.

Inside the Greenpoint Historic District

The district was designated on September 14, 1982, and takes in property on Java, Franklin, Kent, Milton, Noble, Calyer, Leonard, Oak and Guernsey Streets. Work on the outside of a designated building needs the Commission's approval, and its permit guidebook sets the pattern for entry equipment: intercom panels "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," one "can also be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway," no more than "two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening," and the design, size and finish must be "neutral and discreet." For access control that last limit is the important one. A separate call box, camera and fob reader beside one door would be three devices where the guidebook contemplates one, so on district houses we specify a single combined station, sized to the plain brick of the opening, and keep the lock itself inside the door. The owner files with the Commission; we supply the drawings, product sheets and photographs, and the exterior work waits for the permit.

Pulled, Not Picked: Building a Greenpoint Door That Holds

The 26 West Street report is worth reading closely because of one verb. The person did not pick a lock, steal a fob or follow a resident in. They "forcibly pulled the front door open." Doors that open outward toward the street, which most lobby and vestibule doors do because exits must swing in the direction people leave, are pulled from outside, and the force goes straight into whatever holds the latch side of the door: a strike plate and a few screws, a magnetic lock and its armature plate, or the bolt of a panic device engaging a keeper in the frame. If any one of those is weak, the door gives way and the access system records nothing more interesting than a door that opened.

So we treat the door as the first piece of security equipment and the reader as the last. On a pull test we close the door, lock it, and pull at the handle and at the top and bottom corners on the latch side. A door that flexes at the top corner while the lock holds at the middle can be peeled open with a bar. A strike that moves in its frame will let the latch slip out. A magnetic lock that holds the door but whose plate is fixed with a single bolt through thin metal will tear the plate free. The fixes are mechanical and cheap compared with the electronics: through-bolted strikes with a reinforcing plate behind them, a latch guard on outward-swinging doors so a bar cannot reach the latch, a second lock point at the top of tall glass doors, and closers strong enough to bring the door fully home every time.

Then the electronics earn their place. A door position contact tells the controller whether the door is shut, independently of whether it is locked, which is how the system knows the difference between a resident walking in, a door propped for a delivery and a door that opened with no valid credential at all. The last of those is a forced-door event, and the controller can send it straight to a manager's phone, the front desk or a monitoring company, with the camera clip from the lobby attached if a camera is on the same system. That turns a theft discovered in the morning into an alert at 1:32 a.m.

Strikes, magnetic locks and panic hardware on Greenpoint doors

Each locking method has its place. Electric strikes suit the single swinging doors of walk-ups and frame houses: the latch of the existing lock stays in the door, and the strike in the frame releases it. They can be fail-secure, staying locked in a power cut while the inside handle still opens the door mechanically, which is what most residential street doors need. Magnetic locks hold a door with an electromagnet across the top of the frame. They are strong when properly mounted and simple to wire on glass and aluminum doors, but they are fail-safe by nature, releasing when power drops, and the NYC Building Code requires a release device on the door itself with a built-in switch that cuts the lock's power and frees the door immediately. Electrified panic hardware puts the lock inside the push bar of a lobby or storefront exit, which keeps the exit simple and is often the cleanest answer on tower doors.

Storage Cages, Bike Rooms and Parking Levels: The Doors Behind a Greenpoint Lobby

The waterfront towers were marketed on their amenities, and amenities come with doors. A building on the scale of 16 Dupont Street, with 380 apartments and 138 enclosed parking spaces, needs at least a garage entrance for cars and a pedestrian door from the garage into the building, and towers of that size typically add a bike room, storage, a package room and the corridors between them, before counting any gym or roof deck. Each of those rooms is used by a different subset of residents, and each holds things worth stealing. The lobby desk sees none of them directly.

We set those rooms up as separate groups on the building's system. The bike room opens for residents who have registered a bicycle; the storage room opens for residents who rent a cage; the package room opens for residents and the couriers or porters the building authorizes, on whatever schedule the manager sets. Removing a tenant who has moved out removes them from every room at once, without a lock change. The Brooklyn Post report about the $1,800 bicycle taken from the lobby of 182 Driggs Avenue is the reason we argue for getting bicycles out of lobbies and into a room with its own reader, and the 26 West Street report is the reason that room's door gets a contact and a forced-door alert, not just a reader.

Garages need two kinds of control. The overhead door or gate is usually opened by long-range tags on the windshield or a fob read from the driver's seat, and it should close behind each car so a person on foot cannot walk in under it. The pedestrian door from the garage into the building is the more important one: it bypasses the lobby entirely, so it should open from the garage side only for residents with parking rights, with an alert if it is held open, while still opening freely from inside as an exit. Where a garage has a separate retail or public parking operator, their staff get their own group and schedule, and their records stay apart from the residents'.

Seventy Tenants, a Few Front Doors: Access Control for Greenpoint's Loft Buildings

The best-known example of Greenpoint's working buildings sits at the end of Manhattan Avenue on Newtown Creek. Brownstoner's history of the Greenpoint Manufacturing and Design Center traces it to 1868, when the Chelsea Fiber Mills put up the first building of what became a rope works of eight buildings and 366,000 square feet. The city took the complex in a tax foreclosure in 1974, rented space month to month to artists and small manufacturers while the buildings deteriorated, and in the 1990s a nonprofit rebuilt them with a new roof, new infrastructure and spaces for individual tenants. Untapped Cities described the center as an umbrella for "the 70+ tenants and some 240 employees that occupy the spacious lofts," naming a jewelry designer, a metal spinner and a screen printer among them. We mention it to describe the kind of building, not as a customer of ours.

Smaller versions of the same building are scattered across north Greenpoint and the blocks toward the creek: two- to six-story brick or concrete factories now divided among woodworkers, fabricators, photographers, bakeries, rehearsal rooms and offices. Their door problems are the same at any size. Many tenants share a few street entrances, a freight elevator and a loading door. Tenants come and go every year or two. People work at all hours, a single studio may have a dozen part-time staff, and deliveries arrive by van at the loading door all day. A brass key cut for every tenant and every assistant is a key nobody can account for after the first year.

The answer is a single controller with partitions. The landlord administers the street doors, the freight door, the stairs and the corridors, and sets the hours the building is open. Each tenant company gets its own account, administers its own studio door and its own people, and can add an assistant for a month or remove a former employee in a minute without calling the landlord. A tenant's credential opens the street door during that tenant's hours and its own studio door, and nothing belonging to another tenant. When a lease ends, the landlord disables the company and every credential under it stops working at once.

Fobs, Faces and Phones: What New York Law Asks of a Greenpoint Door System

Most people asking for access control want one thing: a door that opens for the right people and nobody else. New York City law adds a second question, about the information the door collects along the way, and the answer depends on what kind of building the door belongs to. Greenpoint has every kind, sometimes inside a single address, so we settle this before choosing a reader.

Apartment buildings: the Tenant Data Privacy Law

For multiple dwellings, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, covers what HPD calls smart access systems: fobs, cards, phone apps, keypads tied to user records, and biometric readers. HPD's summary says owners must get tenants' express consent, keep a written data retention and privacy policy, collect only a short list of data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and never sell it. The system may not track tenants' location outside the building, may not be used to harass or evict, and an owner may not require smart access for a tenant to enter their own apartment. In practice, for a Greenpoint walk-up or tower, that means a written policy handed over with the system, logs that roll off on schedule, no face or fingerprint enrollment unless a tenant chooses it, and a working key for every apartment door.

The key question at the street door is often misunderstood. In 2019, amNewYork reported that the Department of Buildings classifies fobs, cards and metal keys all as keys, that it knew of no rule barring landlords from installing such systems, and that a traditional key method was still expected under city rules. The same article described tenants in a Hell's Kitchen loft building who sued after their landlord installed an app-based lock and stopped issuing metal lobby keys, and quoted the NYCLU's Lee Rowland: "It's fundamentally inappropriate for anyone to be told that a condition of living somewhere is giving up their data." Our practice follows from that history. Every residential street door we install keeps a mechanical key cylinder that the owner controls, and residents choose among fob, code or phone without any of them being the only way in.

Rent-stabilized buildings

Some of Greenpoint's older walk-ups include rent-stabilized apartments, and that changes the rollout. State housing regulators have treated a change from keys to fobs as a change in a required building service. In rulings summarized by Brick Underground in 2018, they held that fobs must be provided free to every lawful occupant, with extra fobs free and lost-fob replacement charges capped, and in 2020 the New York Attorney General settled with a security company whose rollout at a rent-stabilized Queens building had recorded tenants' driver's license numbers and capped fobs at two per unit. When we change over a stabilized building, we plan enough fobs for every occupant from the start and collect nothing beyond a name and apartment number.

Shops, bars and restaurants: the biometric signage rule

A different law applies to businesses. Since July 9, 2021, the NYC Administrative Code has required commercial establishments, which include retail stores, food and drink establishments and places of entertainment, that collect biometric identifier information from customers to post a clear notice near every customer entrance, and it bars any business from selling or profiting from biometric data, including employees' fingerprint or face data. Holland & Knight's summary of the law notes statutory damages of $500 to $5,000 per violation, with a 30-day window to fix a missing sign. A face-recognition reader on a Franklin Street bar's staff door used only by employees is a different case from a camera system that identifies customers at the front door, and we explain the difference before anything is ordered. For most Greenpoint businesses a card or code reader on the staff door does the job without collecting biometric data at all.

When the Power or the Internet Goes Out on the Waterfront

Every electronic door has to answer two questions in advance: what happens when its power fails, and what happens when its connection to the outside world fails. The Cooperator put the first one bluntly in its guide for co-op and condo boards: fobs stop working in a power outage, battery backup may not last through a long one, and during Superstorm Sandy buildings needed a person at the door. One of the people it interviewed, Halper, said: "Basically, you need the super to man the front door to keep it open and under surveillance." A building on the river or the creek should plan for a long outage on paper, not discover its plan during one.

For power, each door is chosen to be fail-secure or fail-safe on purpose. A residential street door with a fail-secure strike stays locked when power drops, and residents use their mechanical keys, while the inside handle still opens it so nobody is trapped. A lobby exit with a magnetic lock must release on power loss under the Building Code, so the building needs a second plan, such as a mechanical lock on the same door or staff at the desk, for an extended outage. Controllers and locks run on a battery-backed supply sized for the doors on it, and we tell the board or owner how many hours that battery should carry the doors, so nobody is surprised at hour five.

For the internet, the rule is simpler: a door should never need the cloud to open. The controller in the building holds the credential list and decides locally, so when an internet provider has an outage or a router is replaced, residents keep coming and going and the events are stored and uploaded later. What stops working is remote management, such as adding a new resident from an office in Manhattan, until the connection returns. Systems that send every decision to a remote server are cheaper to install and worse to live with, and we tell owners which kind they are buying.

After-hours doors that tell someone

The burglaries reported in Greenpoint over the years have a common pattern: they happen late at night or before dawn, when the business is closed, through a door the owner believed was locked. Every door has an armed period after closing, and a door that opens during that period, by any means, sends an alert with a camera clip to the owner and, if the business has central-station monitoring, to the monitoring company.

Rules that apply to Greenpoint business doors

  • Exits always open from inside. Under sections 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9 of the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked egress door released by a sensor needs a manual PUSH TO EXIT button 40 to 48 inches above the floor and within 5 feet of the door that unlocks it for at least 30 seconds, it must unlock when power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs release hardware on the door with a built-in switch that frees it immediately with one hand.
  • Listed equipment. The code calls for access control equipment on egress doors to be listed to UL 294; we install listed hardware and keep the listings in the job file for the landlord, insurer or inspector.
  • Apartments above. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors on newer class A buildings and an intercom with voice and remote release where there are eight or more apartments. A shop that shares the residents' door cannot put it on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Customer biometrics. A business that collects customers' biometric data must post a notice at every customer entrance and may never sell that data (NYC Administrative Code, effective July 9, 2021).
  • Landmarks. Storefronts and entrances inside the Greenpoint Historic District, including parts of Franklin Street and Greenpoint Avenue, need the Commission's approval for exterior equipment.
  • Electrical work. The city's electrical code treats signaling, communication, alarm and data wiring under 50 volts as low-voltage work outside its permit section. The 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician; AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Greenpoint: Questions Answered

Towers and newer buildings on the waterfront

Our front door was locked, yet someone yanked it open in the middle of the night and emptied part of the storage room. Which part of the door gave way, and what stops it happening again?

Usually the part that holds the latch side of the door: the strike and the frame around it, a magnetic lock's armature plate, or the keeper of a panic bar. When a locked door is pulled open without being broken, one of those has bent, torn loose or let the latch slip out, often because the top corner of a tall door flexes enough to clear the lock in the middle. We pull-test the door, look at where it moved, and fix that point first with through-bolted strikes and backing plates, a latch guard, a second lock point at the top of tall glass doors, or a stronger closer that seats the door every time.

Then the system is set to notice. A door position contact reports a door that opens with no valid credential as a forced-door event, and the controller sends it at once to the desk, the super's phone or a monitoring company, with the lobby camera's clip if one is connected. The storage room gets its own reader and contact, so even someone who gets through the front door meets a second locked door that reports itself.

In our tower, people on foot slip into the garage under the overhead door while it closes behind a car. What actually stops that?

Three things together. First, the overhead door's close timer and safety sensors are set so it begins closing as soon as the car has cleared, rather than standing open for a long countdown. Second, a camera covers the ramp, and the door's controller logs every cycle, so a door that opened once for a tag but saw two objects pass is a reviewable event. Third, and most important, the pedestrian door from the garage into the building is treated as a front door: it opens from the garage side only for residents with parking rights, it has a contact and a held-open alert, and it always opens freely from inside as an exit.

Walk-ups, tenements and flathouses

Couriers press every button on our walk-up's panel until somebody buzzes them in. Can a new panel stop one driver from ringing all six apartments at once?

Yes, and it is one of the most practical reasons to replace an old buzzer. A video panel calls one apartment at a time from a directory, so a driver has to pick a name, and residents see who is at the door before they release it. Most panels can also be set to ignore a second call for a short time after the first, which ends the habit of leaning on every button. One Greenpoint resident described the old pattern on Reddit: "Most drivers will ring multiple apartments at once to get into the building."

The other half is giving deliveries somewhere to go that is not the whole building. A package room or lockers in the vestibule, opened by a courier code that works only on that door and only during delivery hours, lets the driver finish the job without anyone upstairs being disturbed and without the stair hall being opened to them. Residents can still choose to let a driver in by video call; what changes is that nobody has to.

The landlord of our Greenpoint walk-up says the metal street-door keys will stop working once fobs go in. Can a landlord do that?

It is a risky plan, and we would not install it that way. In 2019 amNewYork reported that the Department of Buildings counts fobs, cards and metal keys all as keys, and that a traditional key method was still expected under city rules; the same article described tenants in a Hell's Kitchen building suing after their landlord replaced the lobby's metal keys with an app. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, passed after those disputes, also bars owners of multiple dwellings from requiring smart access for tenants to enter their own apartments. For rent-stabilized apartments, state regulators have treated a switch to fobs as a change in a required service that must be provided free to every lawful occupant.

Our standard for every residential street door is a mechanical key cylinder that stays in service alongside the fobs, controlled by the owner so copies can be limited. Tenants who prefer the fob use the fob. Tenants who want the key keep the key. If your landlord's plan goes further than that, the questions about your own lease and rent registration belong with a tenant lawyer or the state housing agency, not an installer.

The street door of our tenement opens outward and the latch is visible through the gap at the frame. Can a reader make that door any harder to slip open?

Not by itself, because the reader only decides when the latch should release; a latch that can be pushed back with a blade is still a latch that can be pushed back. What fixes it is mechanical: a latch guard, a steel plate fixed to the outside of the door that covers the gap so nothing can reach the latch, and an electric strike or lock with a deadlatch feature that stops the latch being pushed back once the door is shut. If the frame has spread over the years, it is shimmed or reinforced so the gap closes.

We run the bakery on the ground floor of the flathouse we own, under three apartments. If the shop door is unlocked all day, does the residents' door need its own system?

It needs its own control, whether that is a separate system or a separate group on one controller. The residents' entrance of a building with apartments has to stay self-closing and self-locking, while a shop's customer door is meant to be open during business hours. As long as the two doors share nothing but a wall, they are simply programmed differently: the shop door follows the shop's hours, the residents' door follows nobody's hours and opens only for residents' fobs, codes and video calls.

The cases that need care are the shared ones: a hallway that runs from the shop's back room to the residents' stairs, a cellar used by both, or a shop employee who also lives upstairs. Those doors get a reader on each side with different rules, so a customer who wanders into the back cannot reach the stairs, and the shop's records never include the residents' comings and goings. As the owner you see both, under separate logins.

Our house is inside the Greenpoint Historic District, and we want a fob reader and a camera at the front door as well as the bell. Can all three be one device?

They should be. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's permit guidebook says intercom panels go "within or adjacent to a door opening," that "a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening," and that installations should be "neutral and discreet." Three separate boxes beside one doorway runs against that pattern, while a single station holding the camera, the call button and the fob reader fits it. The guidebook also allows a panel "on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway," which helps when the door surround is ornamented.

We choose a station sized for the plain brick or stone of your opening, in a finish that does not stand out, and keep the lock inside the door. You or your architect file with the Commission; we provide drawings, product sheets and photographs, and the exterior work waits for approval. Inside the house, which the Landmarks rules do not reach in the same way, the controller and cable follow the most direct path.

Loft, studio and workshop buildings

When a tenant company moves out of our factory building, the landlord spends weeks chasing keys from its former staff. How does a credential system handle a tenant leaving mid-lease?

With one change in one place. Each tenant company is set up as its own group, with its own administrator who adds and removes its staff. When the company leaves, the landlord disables the group, and every fob, card, code and phone key belonging to it stops working at the street door, the freight door and the former studio at the same moment. There is nothing to collect and no lock to change, and the studio's reader is ready for the next tenant the same day.

The same structure helps while the company is still there. A tenant can give a freelancer access for two weeks, a delivery driver a code for Tuesdays, and an employee who leaves on bad terms nothing at all, without asking the landlord. The landlord keeps control of the building's hours and shared doors and can see door events when there is an incident, under rules written down when the system goes in.

Four workshops share the roll-up loading door in our building, and it keeps being left open over weekends. Can the door itself report that?

Yes. A heavy-duty contact on the roll-up door, mounted where a forklift or a hand truck will not knock it off, tells the controller whether the door is fully down. If it stays open past a set time, during hours when nobody should be loading, or at all over a weekend, the super, the landlord or the tenant who last used it gets a text. Where the door has a motor, the opener can be put on the access system so only tenants on the loading list can raise it, and each raise is logged against the person who did it.

Many loading doors are also exits or sit beside one, so we check what the building code requires of the personnel door next to the roll-up before changing anything. And because a roll-up door left half open is the most common case, the contact is set to report anything short of fully closed, not only a door standing wide.

Bars, restaurants and shops

Our kitchen staff prop the cellar door for the morning produce delivery and forget to close it. Can the door lock itself again and tell the owner if it opens after closing?

Yes, if the door can close by itself. A closer and a self-latching lock come first, with a hold-open that releases after a set time, so a propped door swings shut once the delivery is in. The electric strike or lock relocks automatically after each use. A contact on the door then reports two things to the owner's phone: a door held open longer than, say, ten minutes during the day, and any opening at all during the hours the restaurant is closed.

The 2017 burglary at a Manhattan Avenue restaurant that began at "an unlocked basement door" is the reason we push owners to schedule this door rather than trust the last person out. Each employee gets their own code, which ends when they leave, and the morning delivery can have a code that works only on that door during delivery hours. If the cellar is entered from a sidewalk hatch, the hatch gets its own contact, since an access system cannot lock a hatch held shut by a padlock underneath, but it can report when that hatch opens.

We'd like our bar's staff to clock in with a face reader at the back door on Franklin Street. Does the city's biometric law cover employees?

Partly. The notice requirement in the city's biometric identifier law, in effect since July 9, 2021, is aimed at customers: a commercial establishment that collects customers' biometric data must post a sign near every customer entrance. A reader used only by employees at a staff door does not trigger that sign. But the law's ban on selling, leasing, trading or otherwise profiting from biometric data applies to employees' data too, and employment, labor and state privacy rules may add their own requirements, so the decision to use face or fingerprint readers for staff belongs with your lawyer or accountant before it belongs with us.

Practically, most bars and restaurants get the same result with less risk from a card or code reader whose log doubles as a record of arrivals. If you do choose a face reader, we set it to store templates on the device rather than in a cloud account, keep the enrollment list to staff who agree, and make sure the door still opens with a card or a key for anyone who does not.

Field Notes: Count the Keys Before the Fobs

This describes how we start a Greenpoint walk-up job, not a particular customer. Before talking about readers we ask the owner a question most cannot answer: how many keys to the street door exist? The honest answer in an older building is usually "nobody knows," because keys have been copied for every tenant, super, contractor and relative for decades. FAQ

Greenpoint Access Control FAQ

Can a Greenpoint resident let a visitor in from their phone while they are at work?
+

Yes, with a video station that calls residents' phones. The visitor presses the apartment's button, the call rings the resident's phone wherever they are, and the resident sees the visitor and releases the street door from the call. The release is logged like any other entry, and residents who prefer can send a one-time code instead.

Can a shop employee's code be set to stop working on a chosen date?
+

Yes. Every credential can carry a start and end date, so a seasonal worker's code or a freelancer's card stops working on the last day without anyone remembering to delete it. Codes can also be limited to shift hours, so a morning baker's code does not open the back door at midnight.

Can forced-door alerts go to a central monitoring station as well as to our phones?
+

Yes. The controller can send forced and held-open door events to an alarm panel or directly to a central station, in addition to the owner's or super's phone. Which events go where is set per door, so a propped bike room texts the super while a forced cellar door after closing goes to the monitoring company.

What does our building's fire alarm contractor have to do when readers go in?
+

Usually provide and test a relay that tells the access power supplies to release the egress doors when the alarm or sprinklers activate. We wire our side to that relay, label which doors release, and test the release with the fire alarm contractor present before handover.

Is an electric strike or a magnetic lock better on a tenement street door?
+

On most tenement street doors, an electric strike. It keeps the existing latch, can stay locked in a power cut while the inside handle still opens the door, and does not need the extra release hardware a magnetic lock requires under the Building Code. Magnetic locks suit glass and aluminum doors where a strike cannot be fitted.

Can the super get a text when the cellar door has been left open?
+

Yes. A door contact on the cellar door tells the controller how long the door has been open, and after a set time, five or ten minutes for example, the super gets a text. The same alert can be limited to nights and weekends if the cellar is busy during the day.

Can the installation happen while tenants are living in the building and the shop stays open?
+

Yes. We work one door at a time, keep the existing lock and keys working until the new hardware is tested, and schedule noisy cutting around the shop's busiest hours. Residents are told in advance when the street door will be worked on, and the door is never left unable to lock at the end of a day.

Can a reader go on a glass storefront door without drilling the glass?
+

Yes. On a glass door the reader goes on the frame or the wall beside it, and the lock goes in the aluminum frame, the header or the door's push bar. Cable runs inside the frame. Nothing is fixed to the glass itself.

What happens to the old locks and holes when a new system replaces them?
+

Hardware that is no longer used comes off the door, and holes in the door are covered with plates or filled so the door stays solid. We do not patch or paint walls, so where an old box comes off a wall, a cover plate goes over the opening and any plastering or painting is left to the owner's painter.

Can one fob open both our apartment building and the studio we rent in a Greenpoint loft building?
+

Only if both systems read the same credential format and both administrators enroll it. In practice the two buildings usually have different systems and owners, so most people carry two credentials, or use phone keys for one and a fob for the other.

Does an access control system need a city inspection after it is installed?
+

Low-voltage access wiring falls outside the city's electrical permit section, so there is usually no separate electrical inspection for it. Locked egress doors must still meet the Building Code, landmarked exteriors need Landmarks approval first, and any fire alarm tie-in is tested with the fire alarm contractor, so we document each door's behavior in the job file.

How early should access control be planned for a new Greenpoint building before residents move in?
+

Before the walls close. Cable to every door, backing in the frames and space in the telecom room are cheapest while the building is still open, so the best time to plan is during construction drawings or the start of the fit-out. Readers and credentials can then be installed and enrolled shortly before the first residents arrive.
