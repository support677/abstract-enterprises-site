The Neighborhood

Why Financial District Properties Need Cat6 Repair: The Building Keeps Changing Around the Cable

Most horizontal Cat6 is designed for a fixed world: a closet on each floor, desks in rows, a ceiling that stays shut. The Financial District has not offered that kind of stability for a long time. Since the mid-1990s the neighbourhood has been turning offices into homes in waves. The first wave, under the city's 421-g tax incentive, converted nearly 13 million square feet — about 13 percent of the lower Manhattan office market — between 1995 and 2006, according to the Citizens Budget Commission. The second wave is under way now: Water Street, Broadway and Broad Street all have towers emptying and being rebuilt as apartments while neighbouring floors and neighbouring buildings stay at work.

Every one of those changes touches copper. A conversion two floors up sends dust, vibration and new trades through shared shafts. A landlord who splits a former trading floor into pre-built suites cuts some runs short and re-homes others. A firm that shrinks from three floors to one leaves cable behind in ceilings it no longer controls. And the cellars on the east side of the district, where many buildings kept their main telecom rooms, were reshaped after Hurricane Sandy filled them with brackish water in October 2012. None of this breaks a link in an obvious way. It shows up as a desk that drops at lunchtime, a panel port that passes one day and fails the next, a shop terminal that goes offline in cold weather.

The occupants have changed too. The Downtown Alliance counted more than 70,000 residents in Lower Manhattan for the first time in 2025, alongside 4.75 million square feet of office leasing — the busiest year since 2019 — and 90 new retail openings. That mix matters for a repair: the same tower can hold a law firm on one floor, rental apartments on the next and a restaurant at street level, each with its own cable, its own closet and its own idea of when a contractor may make noise. A repair that ignores the building's history tends to fix the symptom and leave the cause in place.

So our Financial District repairs start with a short conversation about the building rather than the network: what has been built, demolished, flooded, combined or handed back near your space in the last few years. Then we test. Most of the time the answer is a set of terminations to remake and a few cords to replace. Sometimes it is a run that a renovation crew crushed above a ceiling we cannot reach today. Either way you get a certified result for every link we touch and a plain statement of what is left. Firms that are also adding fibre between floors often pair this work with our Financial District fiber optic installation team, since both trades use the same ceilings and the same building approvals.

Financial District facts that shape a Cat6 repair

  • Two conversion waves. 421-g conversions turned 98 office buildings with 12.8 million square feet of commercial space into 12,865 apartments (Citizens Budget Commission). Current projects include 25 Water Street (about 1,300 apartments), 222 Broadway (798 rentals, completion expected May 2027) and 80 Broad Street (326 units filed in January 2026).
  • Older than the standard. Category 6 was added to the TIA-568 standard in June 2002. Many first-wave conversions were wired before it existed, so the cable behind a downtown apartment jack may be an earlier category.
  • The flood of 2012. Days after Sandy, The Real Deal found at least 17.3 million square feet of downtown office space shut with flooded basements in at least 15 east-side buildings, including 55 Water Street, 1 New York Plaza, 2 Broadway and 4 New York Plaza.
  • The carrier side went to glass. Verizon told local officials that 95 percent of the copper infrastructure feeding back to its Broad Street office had been destroyed, and chose to replace it with fibre (amNY, January 2013). The copper inside your suite was not part of that replacement.
  • Vacancy and re-letting. Overall Lower Manhattan vacancy was 22.2 percent at the end of 2025 (Downtown Alliance), down for eight straight quarters — which means many floors are being re-let, re-divided and re-cabled at once.
  • Protection still years away. The Financial District and Seaport coastal resilience plan released in 2021 is estimated at $5–7 billion and 15–20 years, and its funding had not been secured as of late 2025.

Building Specialists

Cat6 Repair for the Financial District's Architecture

Few neighbourhoods pack so many building eras into so few blocks. The same Cat6 fault — a split pair, a crushed jacket, a corroded contact — is reached and repaired differently in each.

Counting Houses and Dry-Goods Lofts

After the Great Fire of December 1835, more than 600 buildings went up in the First Ward in 1836, including the four-storey Greek Revival stores that still line Stone Street. Cable here runs in surface raceway, old chases and timber floors. Repairs follow existing paths; new openings are a last resort.

Bank and Exchange Buildings

Granite and marble halls such as 55 Wall Street, built as the Merchants' Exchange from 1836 and enlarged for National City Bank from 1907 to 1910, now hold event spaces and apartments. Cable reaches their great rooms through floors, plinths and service spaces, not through decorative walls.

Setback Towers of the 1920s and 1930s

Masonry-clad steel towers like 80 Broad Street (1935, 37 storeys) have small floor plates, deep cores and narrow ceiling voids crowded with pipe. Fault-finding relies on distance-to-fault readings because there is little room to open things up speculatively.

Post-War Plaza Towers

Big-floor towers along Water Street and around the plazas were built with ducts in the floor and arcades at the base. Their Cat6 often reaches desks from below, and their ground floors have been re-planned since the 2016 Water Street zoning change.

First-Wave Conversions

Apartment buildings created from offices between 1995 and 2006 under 421-g. Many were wired before Category 6 was published in 2002, and their in-unit cabling was often fitted around office-era shafts that were never meant to serve kitchens and bedrooms.

Conversions Under Way

25 Water Street, 222 Broadway and the filing for 80 Broad Street show the current wave. Until a building is fully residential, offices and construction share it, and tenants who stay have to live with the dust, the shutdowns and the new trades.

Mixed Towers With Shops at the Base

Restaurants on Stone Street and Pearl Street, banks on Broad Street and shops in former arcades all hang from towers whose closets were planned for offices. Their Cat6 crosses kitchens, vestibules and lobbies that were never in the original cable plan.

Commercial Cat6 Repair

Business Cat6 Repair in the Financial District

The district is small enough to walk end to end in twenty minutes, but its streets behave like separate markets. These are the corridors we work in most, and what tends to fail on each.

Wall Street and Broad Street

Exchange-era banks, law and advisory firms and the residential floors now stacked above them. Many sit in the Wall Street Historic District on the National Register, which 55 Wall Street joined as a contributing building in 2007. Cable faults here are mostly in older suites re-let several times, where each tenant added a layer and nobody removed the last one.

Water Street and Old Slip

Post-war towers with big floor plates, underfloor ducts and the plazas and arcades that the 2016 zoning change opened to retail. Several of these towers took water in their cellars in 2012. Typical calls: desk outlets in the floor, shops in enclosed arcades, links through rebuilt cellar rooms.

Maiden Lane, William Street and John Street

Mid-size pre-war and mid-century office buildings, many now split into small suites for smaller firms. Short leases mean frequent move-outs and pre-built suites, so we meet a lot of half-documented cable and worn jacks.

Pearl Street, Hanover Square and Stone Street

The oldest street pattern in the city, with restaurants filling the restored ground floors. Stone Street's eastern blocks go pedestrian in summer for outdoor dining. Point-of-sale and kitchen-screen links fail here from grease, heat and mop water as much as from age.

Broadway, Fulton Street and Trinity Place

Large office buildings on the west side of the district, some mid-conversion: 222 Broadway is being turned into 798 rentals with 40,000 square feet of commercial space. Tenants nearby ask about dust, shutdowns and links that cross into floors under construction.

Whitehall, South Ferry and the Battery

Towers at the southern tip such as 1 and 4 New York Plaza and 2 Broadway, all named in the post-Sandy flooding survey, plus ferry-side retail. Cellar and ground-floor links get the closest look for water history.

Compliance callouts for Cat6 repair downtown

Permits. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 says an electrical permit is not required for installing, altering, maintaining or repairing wiring that operates at under 50 volts for signalling, communication, alarm and data circuits, provided the work is done by a licensed master electrician, special electrician or qualified person as the electrical code defines them. Ordinary Cat6 repair falls in that category; work on life-safety systems does not.
Abandoned cable. The electrical code's communications article requires the accessible portions of abandoned cable to be removed, and allows cable to remain where it is tagged for future use. The current NYC Electrical Code, based on the 2020 NEC with city amendments, took full effect on December 21, 2025. We tag or remove; we do not simply cut and leave.
Designated interiors. The Landmarks Preservation Commission does not require a permit for ordinary repairs, but interior work needs one where it requires a Buildings permit, affects the exterior or takes place in a designated interior landmark — as the former banking hall at 55 Wall Street has been since 1999.
Firestopping and floor crossings. Where a replacement run passes between floors, through a rated wall or into a shaft, the penetration is sealed again with a listed system and photographed before it is covered. On two-floor tenancies we check that cable crossing the slab is rated for that use.
After the Water

Downtown Cellars Since 2012: What Sandy Left in the Copper

On the night of October 29, 2012 the storm surge came over the east side of the district and down into the cellars. At Verizon's 140 West Street building, reporters saw the surge had "breached the protective plugs" around incoming cables and flooded the basement cable vault, with three and a half floors of the five-level basement still under brackish water days later. Under Pearl Street, crews were pumping out two levels of tunnels where communications lines had flooded. The Real Deal's survey on November 1 found at least 15 east-side office buildings closed with water in their basements and sub-basements.

The carriers' response is well documented. Verizon reported that 95 percent of the copper cable feeding back to its Broad Street office — hubs, manholes, conduits and building connection points — had been destroyed, and decided to replace it with fibre. Its 104 Broad Street office came back in ten days. That rebuild stopped at the building's front door. The Cat6 inside office suites, shops and cellar rooms was the owners' and tenants' problem, and much of it was dried, tested for continuity and put back into service.

Continuity is a low bar. A pair can carry a link light and still have contacts that are slowly corroding where salt and silt dried inside the plug or the jack. We see the result years later as links that pass in the morning and fail in the humid afternoon, or that drop when someone touches the cord. When a downtown link with a cellar route keeps misbehaving, we open the terminations rather than trusting the test, look for the green and white residue that brackish water leaves on copper and on the plating, and cut back until the conductors are bright.

Many buildings also re-planned their cellars after the storm: equipment raised, rooms sealed, flood barriers stored on site. Each of those projects moved or re-routed some Cat6, and the records of what moved did not always reach the tenants. If your link passes through a cellar, tell us when the room was last rebuilt. We will test the run, check where it crosses any new seals, and leave those seals as tight as we found them. The city's long-term protection for the district — the 2021 Financial District and Seaport plan with buried walls and deployable gates — is still unfunded and years from completion, so the cellar remains part of the cable route to plan around.

Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in the Financial District: Questions Answered

These are the questions downtown tenants, owners and building staff bring us that are specific to how the district's buildings are made and how they are changing. General repair questions are answered on our Manhattan Cat6 repair page.

Floors that keep changing hands

Why do the Cat6 runs in our post-war tower's underfloor duct keep failing right at the floor outlets?

Because the outlet is where the cable is handled most and protected least. In a cellular underfloor system the cable travels along a steel cell cast into the floor, turns up through a fitting and ends in a box at carpet level. Manufacturers' descriptions show why it is crowded down there: a typical header duct has four compartments — one for power, two for voice, one for data — and the cells themselves carry power alongside communications. Over decades of furniture moves, fittings are opened, closed, abandoned and re-used. The Cat6 gets pinched under a lid, pulled tight around the sharp edge of an old insert, or re-terminated so many times that there is no slack left to make a clean jack.

Our repair starts above the floor. We test each failing link, read the distance to the fault, and if it sits within the first metre or two of the outlet — as it usually does — we open only that fitting. A damaged tail is cut back, the jack is remade with the pair twist held to the termination, the cable is dressed so the lid cannot bite it and the link is certified. When the damage is further along the cell, we check whether the cell is clear enough to pull a replacement through. We never share a power compartment, and if the only route is through one, we say so and offer a ceiling or surface alternative.

Our building is being converted to apartments — is any of the office Cat6 worth repairing for the new units?

Usually very little of the cable, and quite a lot of the pathway. Office Cat6 runs from desks to a closet on the same floor in a layout that matches open-plan rows, not bedrooms and kitchens. Once walls move, most of those runs end in the wrong place, at the wrong length and in a closet that will become part of somebody's apartment. Repairing them to certification standard and then abandoning them a few months later is money spent twice.

What is often worth keeping is the route: sleeves through the core, cable tray in corridors that will remain corridors, and conduit from the cellar to upper floors. We can survey an office floor before demolition, mark which Cat6 runs could plausibly serve a common area, an amenity room or a management office in the new layout, test only those, and recommend removing the rest so it does not end up as abandoned cable behind new drywall. For apartments themselves, the developer's own residential cabling design usually wins — each unit with its own panel — and we would rather tell you that than sell you a repair.

Demolition started on the floors above us and our patch panel now throws intermittent errors — can dust and vibration do that?

Yes, though usually by making an existing weakness visible rather than creating a new one. RJ45 contacts rely on a thin gold surface meeting gold. The connectivity standards call for at least 50 micro-inches of hard gold over a nickel barrier, and a jack built to them is rated for hundreds or thousands of matings. Fine gypsum and concrete dust settling into open ports and on plug contacts adds resistance at that interface, and vibration from jack-hammering and coring a few floors away can work a marginal plug back and forth. A port that was barely passing before the conversion started will be the first to complain.

The repair is mostly housekeeping done properly. We test the ports that log errors, replace contaminated cords rather than cleaning them, inspect jack contacts and remake any termination that has loosened, fit dust caps to unused ports and, where the closet door or ceiling has become a dust path, ask the general contractor to seal it. Then we re-certify the affected links so you have a before-and-after record. If construction damaged cable directly — a cut, a crushed bundle — that record is what the building and its contractor will want to see.

Our firm has two floors joined by an internal staircase — can the Cat6 between them go through the stair opening instead of the building riser?

Sometimes, and it is often how the original fit-out did it. Tenants who take two stacked floors downtown commonly cut a convenience stair between them, and their fit-out contractor may have run Cat6 through the same floor opening or a sleeve beside it rather than asking the landlord for riser space. That is fine as long as two things are true: the cable is listed for running between floors, and the penetration around it is properly firestopped. Office-grade cable lying loose along a stair stringer meets neither condition.

When those links fail, we test them first; often the fault is at a termination in the upper or lower closet, not in the crossing. If the run itself needs replacing, we put in riser-rated cable through a sleeve, seal the sleeve with a listed firestop system and photograph it. If the building engineer prefers the work to move into the riser, we can do that instead, with the building's approval. Either way the result is recorded so the next person does not have to guess which route your inter-floor links take.

Desks, shops and how they are used

Our hot desks are plugged and unplugged all day — can that wear out a Cat6 jack?

It can, and much faster when the jack was cheap. The international connector standard rates RJ45 parts for 750 mating cycles at one performance level and 2,500 at a higher one, with only a small rise in contact resistance allowed. On a shared desk where three or four people dock a laptop each day, a jack can see a thousand matings in a year or two. A trade association that tests connectors reports that poorly plated parts can lose their gold after as few as 10 to 15 insertions. Floors that were fitted out quickly with unbranded keystones are where we see this most.

The signs are links that work only when the cord is held at a certain angle, intermittent drops when someone bumps the desk, and ports that test fine with a new cord but fail with the one that lives there. We replace worn jacks with parts made to the plating standard, swap docking cords for factory-made ones of the right length so they are not strained, and add strain relief where furniture lets the cord hang. On hot-desk floors we also suggest a spare cord in each desk drawer: it costs little and keeps users from forcing a bent plug into a good jack.

Our shop opened in an enclosed former arcade along Water Street — where does its Cat6 run, and why does it give trouble?

The 2016 Water Street zoning change let owners of the street's privately owned public spaces fill in their arcades, mostly to bring in retail, with the larger arcades needing a special permit. A shop built into an arcade sits in space that was outdoors when the tower's closets and risers were designed. Its Cat6 therefore takes whatever route the fit-out contractor could find back to a closet: up into the soffit that used to be the arcade ceiling, across the tower's lobby or cellar, or through a new sleeve in the original exterior wall.

Those routes have predictable weak points. A soffit that was once outside may still be poorly insulated, so the cable sees larger temperature swings than it would indoors. The crossing from the shop into the tower often passes through a wall that was never meant to be penetrated, and its sleeve may not be sealed. And the run may be long enough to approach the length limit once it reaches a closet several floors up. We trace the route with a tone and the tester's length reading, repair the failure point, seal any open sleeve and certify the full link so you know how much margin it has.

Leases, records and older wiring

We are handing back two floors when our lease ends — does the Cat6 we leave behind have to come out?

Two documents decide it: your lease and the electrical code. Many commercial leases include a surrender or restoration clause; one published sample requires removal of "all electronic, fiber, phone and data cabling and related equipment" installed for the tenant, unless the landlord says in writing that it can stay. Others leave cabling in place for the next tenant. Separately, the code's communications article requires the accessible parts of abandoned cable to be removed unless the cable is tagged for future use. If the landlord wants it left, it needs to be identified as such; if not, it needs to come out.

We can help on either path. If the cable stays, we test it, label working links clearly, tag the runs for future use and give the landlord a test summary they can show the next tenant. If it goes, we remove what is accessible above ceilings and from closets, leave plates blanked, and give you photographs and a short written record for the surrender inspection. What we will not do is cut the cable at the panel and leave it in the ceiling, because that leaves the landlord with an abandoned-cable problem and can leave you with a dispute over the security deposit.

Wiring older than the Cat6 standard

Category 6 was ratified as an addition to the TIA-568 standard in June 2002. The first downtown conversion wave ran from 1995 to 2006, so a good share of the apartments it created were wired with whatever was current at the time. Plates in those buildings may say "data" and still be fed by an earlier category, and some were wired for telephone and television only. Before quoting, we read the printing on the cable jacket behind the jack. If it is not Cat6, we tell you what speed the link can realistically carry after repair, and whether a replacement run is the better use of your money.

Designated interiors on Wall Street

A handful of downtown rooms are protected inside as well as out. The former National City Bank banking hall at 55 Wall Street became a New York City interior landmark in 1999 and has operated as an event venue since 2006. Repairs to existing cable in spaces like that are ordinary maintenance, but anything that changes a protected surface — a new opening, a new surface raceway, a new device on a wall — may need Landmarks Preservation Commission approval. We check the designation before quoting and keep repairs to service spaces and existing paths wherever we can.

Local licensed repair versus the national alternatives

National marketplaces such as Angi and HomeAdvisor connect you with whoever is free, and cost guides such as Fixr publish national averages. That works for many household jobs. Downtown Cat6 repair is different in three ways that matter. The building wants a certificate of insurance and a named, licensed contractor before anyone touches its ceilings. The fault is often connected to something happening in the building — a conversion, a re-let, a flooded room — that a technician from outside the area has no reason to ask about. And the deliverable is not just a working desk but a certified link and a record the building, the landlord or your IT provider can rely on. What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in the Financial District

We could not view Google's AI Overview from our build environment, so we cannot quote it. The claims below are ones repeated widely in search summaries, forums and cost guides; each is corrected with what we know about downtown buildings and the sources listed in our research.

“Once flooded cable has dried out, it is fine”

Drying removes water; it does not remove what the water carried. The surge that filled downtown cellars in 2012 was brackish, and reporters described it as such in the Verizon cable vault on West Street. Salt and silt left inside a plug or a jack keep attracting moisture and corroding contacts long after the room looks normal again.

Continuity testers do not see this well. A pair with partly corroded contacts can still show as connected, and a link can still come up, while its resistance and return loss drift. The first sign is usually a link that fails only on humid days or only when the cord is moved.

For any link with a cellar route in a building that flooded, we open the terminations, inspect the copper and the plating, cut back to clean conductor and re-terminate with new parts, then certify. If the cable itself is discoloured inside the jacket, we recommend replacement rather than another termination.

“The carrier rewired downtown after Sandy, so the cabling is new”

Verizon did replace most of its damaged copper in Lower Manhattan with fibre, after reporting that 95 percent of the copper infrastructure feeding its Broad Street office had been destroyed. That work covered the carrier's network up to the building connection point.

Everything after that point — the building's own risers, the tenant's Cat6 from closet to desk, the cords and jacks — belonged to owners and tenants. Much of it was dried and reused rather than replaced, because it appeared to work.

So a new fibre service in the street tells you nothing about the copper in your suite. If your links pass through rooms that took water, treat them as cable at least fourteen years old with a water history, and test accordingly.

“Office cabling can simply be reused when a building becomes apartments”

This sounds efficient and rarely works. Office Cat6 was laid out for open-plan rows and a closet on each floor. Apartments need cable to rooms that did not exist, terminating in a panel inside each unit, at lengths that suit the new plan.

Reusing office runs usually means jacks in the wrong places, runs that are too long or too short, and closets that become private space. Some of the cable will also be older than the Category 6 standard if the floors were fitted out before 2002.

The reusable part is the pathway: sleeves, trays and conduit that keep their purpose in the new layout. We survey with that in mind and recommend removing the rest before the walls close, so it does not become abandoned cable.

“A keystone is a keystone; the cheap ones work the same”

They work the same on the first day. The difference is in plating and durability. The relevant standards call for at least 50 micro-inches of hard gold over a nickel barrier, and rate connectors for 750 or 2,500 mating cycles depending on performance level.

A connector trade association reports that defective plating can wear through after as few as 10 to 15 insertions. On a downtown hot-desk floor that is a matter of days.

The cost difference per jack is small compared with the cost of a technician returning to the same desk. We fit parts made to the standards and certify the link so you can see it passes with margin.

“Cable left by the last tenant is free spare capacity”

Sometimes it is. Often it is a mix of working runs, cut runs and runs that end somewhere nobody remembers. Downtown floors that have been re-let several times can carry two or three generations of cable above the same ceiling.

The electrical code treats untagged, abandoned cable as something to remove where it is accessible. Cable that is kept has to be identified as kept, which in practice means tested and labelled.

Before you plan on using it, have it tested. Runs that pass can be labelled and documented as spares; runs that fail or cannot be traced should come out, which also frees ceiling space for the cable you actually need.

“Any network repair in a New York building needs a DOB permit” — or “needs no qualifications at all”

Both are wrong. The city's Administrative Code exempts work on wiring under 50 volts for data and communication circuits from the electrical permit requirement, so a routine Cat6 repair does not need a permit filing.

The same section limits that exemption to work done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person as the electrical code defines them. It also excludes life-safety systems and connections that control light, heat or power.

Downtown managing agents add their own layer: insurance certificates, approved hours and sometimes approved-vendor lists. A licensed, insured contractor with a written scope gets through that process far faster than a handyman.

“A cost guide’s figure is what you should expect to pay below Fulton Street”

Cost guides publish national figures. Fixr, for example, listed Cat6 projects at $125–$250 per drop and labour at $45–$75 an hour in January 2025. Those are national averages for new installation, not repair, and not New York.

Our published rate for repair time is $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and replacement runs start at $175. Those figures cover a licensed, insured technician, a certification tester, matched parts and a written record.

Conversions Next Door

Staying Put While the Building Becomes Apartments

The current conversion wave rarely empties a whole building at once. Developers buy towers with tenants still in them, demolish vacant floors first and work around the leases that remain. For the firms that stay, the network has to survive months of construction in the same shafts and ceilings.

Three things put Cat6 at risk during that time. The first is dust: demolition spreads fine particles through shafts and stairwells, and they settle in closets and on unused ports. The second is shared pathways: tray and sleeves that carry your cable between floors may also carry the cable being stripped out above, and a crew removing abandoned cable can pull or cut a live run by mistake. The third is power and HVAC shutdowns, which can leave a closet hot, or restart switches in an order that hides a cable fault until Monday.

The best defence is a baseline. Before demolition reaches the floors around you, have the links that share paths with the work certified and labelled. Keep the report. If links fail later, a second test shows exactly what changed and where, and that comparison is what the developer's general contractor will ask for before accepting that their crew caused the damage.

During the work, cap unused ports, keep closet doors shut and sealed at the bottom, and ask the building to tell you before any shutdown that affects your floor. After it, re-test. Most of the time the result is a few contaminated cords and a loosened termination or two. Occasionally it is a run cut in a shared tray, and then the baseline report turns an argument into a work order.

A short checklist for tenants in a converting tower

  • Ask the managing agent for the demolition schedule by floor, and which shafts and trays the crews will use.
  • Have the links that share those paths certified before work reaches them, and keep the dated results.
  • Label your own cable where it passes through shared spaces, so a removal crew can tell it is live.
  • Cap unused ports and seal the closet door against dust.
  • Re-test after each phase that works near your paths, not only when something stops.
Leaving Cleanly

Moving Out of a Downtown Floor Without Leaving a Cable Problem Behind

Downtown re-letting has been busy: the Downtown Alliance recorded 4.75 million square feet of leasing in 2025, and every new lease is matched by a floor somebody else is leaving. Moving out is when cable most often turns into a dispute, because the departing tenant, the landlord and the next tenant all have different interests in what is above the ceiling.

Start with the lease. If it requires removal, find out exactly what — all cabling, cabling installed by the tenant, or only what the landlord does not want — and whether the landlord can elect to keep some of it. Ask in writing, early. A landlord re-letting the floor as a pre-built suite may prefer working Cat6 left in place, labelled and tested, rather than a bare ceiling.

Then find out what is actually there. Floors that have been re-let several times often hold cable from earlier tenants as well as yours. A test-and-tag survey separates working runs, failed runs and runs that cannot be traced, which tells everyone what can be offered to the next tenant and what has to come out under the code's abandoned-cable rule.

Finally, document the outcome. A short report with test results for anything left, photographs of ceilings and closets after removal, and a list of blanked plates is usually enough for a surrender inspection. It also protects the next tenant, who otherwise inherits a ceiling full of unknowns and a repair bill to find out what works.

Shared Desks

Hybrid Floors and the Jacks That Take the Wear

Some downtown floors now have fewer assigned desks and more shared ones. A shared desk changes the life of its cabling. Instead of one cord plugged in for years, the jack and the docking cord are handled by several people a day, each pulling a laptop free at a different angle. Cords get trapped in drawers and under monitor arms. Plugs are forced in upside down.

Connector standards anticipate some of this: jacks are rated for 750 or 2,500 matings depending on their performance level, provided the gold plating is what the standard calls for. Cheaply plated parts are another matter; a connector trade association reports that defective plating can wear off after as few as 10 to 15 insertions. On a busy hot-desk floor, both numbers can be reached quickly.

The repair is straightforward and inexpensive per desk: replace the worn jacks with parts made to the standard, use factory-made docking cords of a length that does not hang or stretch, fix strain relief where furniture leaves the cord loose, and certify each link. What makes it worth doing properly is the record. Once every shared desk has a known-good result, a future complaint can be checked against it in minutes rather than chased around the floor.

If you are re-planning a floor for hybrid working, tell us before the furniture arrives. Jacks can be positioned where docking cords will reach without strain, and spare ports labelled so that facilities staff can switch a desk to a good port without calling anyone.

Reading the Results

What a Downtown Certification Report Tells You

A certification tester does more than say pass or fail. For each link it records the length, the wire map, and a set of transmission measurements compared with the limits for the category: crosstalk between pairs at each end, insertion loss along the cable, return loss from reflections. It also records how far below or above each limit the link sits, which is called the margin.

Margin is the number to watch in a building that is changing. A link that passes with a comfortable margin can absorb some dust, some handling and some age. A link that passes by a whisker is the one that will fail when demolition starts upstairs, or on the first humid day after a cellar has been re-sealed. When we hand over a report, we point out the marginal links as well as the failures, so you can decide whether to deal with them now or watch them.

Distance-to-fault readings are the other valuable part. They tell us whether a problem sits at the jack, at the panel or somewhere along the cable, and how far along. In a post-war tower with underfloor ducts, that distance tells us which fitting to open. In an arcade shop, it tells us whether the trouble is in the soffit or back at the tower closet. In a converted building, it tells us whether a fault is inside your apartment or beyond its wall.

Keep the report and the native files together with your floor plan. If the building changes again — and downtown it will — the next test can be compared with this one, and the difference shows what changed.

Two Floors, One Firm

Links Between Stacked Floors Outside the Building Riser

Firms that lease two or more stacked floors downtown often connect them with a convenience stair and keep their network equipment on one of them. The cable between floors may then run through the stair opening, through a sleeve cut for the purpose, or through a small shaft the fit-out created. Those routes are convenient, but they are also where cable listings and firestopping are most often wrong.

Cable that passes between floors is expected to carry a listing for that use, and the opening around it has to be sealed with a listed firestop system. A fit-out that ran ordinary horizontal cable through an unsealed hole may have worked for years; it becomes a problem when a link fails and the repair has to be made properly, or when the landlord's engineer inspects the stair before a lease ends.

When we repair inter-floor links we test from closet to closet, identify the route and the cable type, and replace any run that is not suitable for the crossing. If the landlord prefers the links to move into the building riser, we can do that with the building's approval, and your inter-floor connection then sits with every other tenant's in the managed space.
