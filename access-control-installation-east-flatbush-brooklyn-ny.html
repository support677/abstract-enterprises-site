The Neighborhood

Why East Flatbush Properties Need Access Control

East Flatbush is a big place to talk about doors. Wikipedia puts its edges at Empire Boulevard and East New York Avenue to the north, East 98th Street and Brownsville to the east, the Bay Ridge Branch tracks and Flatlands to the south, and New York and Brooklyn Avenues to the west, and it counts 135,619 residents at the 2010 census. The neighborhood is all of Brooklyn Community District 17, its main ZIP code is 11203, and the 67th Precinct covers it from 2820 Snyder Avenue. Locals divide it into Erasmus in the west, Farragut in the middle and Remsen Village and Rugby in the east. We mention those names because callers use them, and because each part has a different mix of front doors.

The doors that worry people most here are not on houses. They are on large rental buildings, where one broken latch exposes dozens or hundreds of apartments. Flatbush Gardens, the complex that was built as Vanderveer Estates, is the clearest example. REBusiness Online described it in 2009 as 59 six-story brick buildings with 2,469 rent-stabilized apartments on 30 acres bounded by Newkirk, Nostrand, Farragut and Brooklyn Avenues. That year's renovation, by the same report, included new cameras, repairs to the front-door intercoms and round-the-clock security staff. Yet in June 2022 a Council Member stood with the tenants association outside the management office at 3301 Foster Avenue and listed "broken doors" among the conditions, and in September 2024 News 12 reported that a man got into one of the buildings and kicked in a 58-year-old woman's apartment door before taking her phone. A tenant named Dannie told the station: "if the basic front door isn't working, yeah, I feel unsafe."

Rutland Plaza, near East 93rd Street, tells a similar story from the other side of the neighborhood. In February 2025 a resident there listed "the unlocked doors" among the building's problems, and News 12 said its crew entered several buildings in the complex without a key. In July 2025 the station reported that the front doors of the 80 East 93rd Street building were "routinely left unlocked" and that a single security guard had been covering five buildings and 428 units before leaving the post. By February 2026, tenants were describing elevators out for weeks; one sixth-floor resident asked how emergency services would even reach the upper floors. We are not involved with either complex and we do not know what hardware is on their doors today. We cite them because they show, in tenants' own words, what fails first in a big East Flatbush building: the door itself, the latch, and the system that is supposed to tell someone when either one stops working.

That is why we treat access control here as a door job before it is an electronics job. HPD's own rule is blunt: apartment doors and hallway doors must swing closed and latch by themselves, inspectors check the building entrance as part of the public areas, and any defect that stops a door from closing or latching is a class C, immediately hazardous violation that has to be corrected within 14 days. A fob reader cannot fix a closer that has lost its tension or a strike plate that no longer meets the latch. What it can do, once the door works, is replace keys that have been copied for decades with credentials that can be cancelled one at a time, keep a record of which door failed to close, and let a manager see a door stuck open from a phone instead of from a tenant's complaint.

The smaller buildings matter as much. The calls we get from the side streets between Church Avenue, Clarendon Road, Avenue D and Foster Avenue are mostly about attached brick row houses, two- and three-family houses, and walk-ups of four to six stories, and a good number of those buildings have recently changed owners. A News 12 report from April 2026 followed tenants of a four-story Linden Boulevard building who could not even reach their new landlord: "We tried calling them, and nobody answered," one of them said. When ownership turns over, the old keys, the old intercom login and the old list of who holds a fob rarely come with the deed. Moving the building to a system the new owner controls, with a written roster of who has access, is one of the most practical first steps a buyer can take.

The newest buildings bring a different job. On the former Kingsboro Psychiatric Center campus off Clarkson Avenue, 6sqft reports, Sparrow Square's first phase is two ten-story buildings with 261 apartments, 117 of them supportive homes for formerly homeless New Yorkers, plus on-site services, bike storage, a fitness room and a roughly 10,000-square-foot space for Brooklyn Ballet, with completion slated for early 2029. The neighboring Utica Crescent project adds a health care center, retail, a grocery and a community facility. Buildings like these have residents, case managers, program staff, retail tenants and visitors all passing the same front door. Their access control is about separating those groups cleanly, and about the tenant privacy rules that apply to every fob and phone credential in a New York City rental.

None of this is a reason for fear. The 67th Precinct's CompStat report for the week ending November 8, 2026, shows 114 burglaries so far this year against 158 at the same point in 2025, a drop of 27.8 percent, with robbery down 15.8 percent and grand larceny down 13.5 percent. The same report shows 14 burglaries in the last 28 days against 10 a year earlier, a reminder that a good year can still include a bad month. We do not sell door hardware on crime numbers. We sell it on the simple fact that a door which locks every time, and a list of keys that is actually true, protect a building on its best weeks and its worst ones.

What brick, limestone, old wood and aluminum mean on install day

Brick row houses and walk-ups are the forgiving case. Readers and stations are fixed into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so if a device is ever moved the repair is a little repointing, not a broken brick. Cable comes up from the cellar through the floor of the vestibule or along the inside of the door frame, which is far neater than a run stapled across the facade. Where a stoop has a cellar door under it, that door is often the easiest way into the building for a burglar and the hardest for a tenant to see, so it gets at least a door contact even when the budget covers only the front door.

The limestone and brownstone fronts on East 25th Street need more care, because the street is a designated historic district and work visible from the street can require a Landmarks Preservation Commission permit. We keep devices small, set them on the side of the opening rather than on the carved surround, and run cable from inside. Old wood frames, landmarked or not, are the other common problem: after a century of mortise locks, deadbolts and rim locks, a jamb can be so bored out that a strike has nothing solid to bite. We fill or replace that section of jamb before the strike goes in. On new buildings the entrance is usually an aluminum storefront door with narrow stiles, so the electric lock goes into the panic bar or the frame header instead of the edge of the door.

The Door Comes First: Why Every East Flatbush Job Starts With the Latch

The single most useful thing we do on an East Flatbush walk-up has nothing to do with fobs. Before we quote any electronics, the technician opens the street door, lets go of it and watches. Does it swing all the way shut on its own? Does the latch drop into the strike, or does the door stop a quarter of an inch short and drift open in the draft from the stairwell? Then we do the same with the inner vestibule door, the cellar door, the roof door and the door to the yard. On a typical older building at least one of those doors fails the test, and that door is where the building is actually open.

The reasons are almost always mechanical and almost always cheap compared with what they cost a building in break-ins and violations. Closers lose their adjustment with every season of hot and cold. Hinges wear, so the door sags and the latch rides below the strike opening. Somebody fitted a bigger lock years ago and the strike plate was never moved to match. Tenants and delivery workers kick the bottom of the door, bend the latch and wedge it with folded cardboard. HPD treats all of these the same way: a door that will not close and latch by itself is a class C violation, and the owner has 14 days to fix it after the notice. HPD's page also notes that under Local Law 71 of 2022 it proactively inspects self-closing doors in 300 class A buildings every year.

So our written quote has two parts. The first lists the door work: a new or readjusted closer, hinge repair, a strike plate moved or replaced, a jamb repaired, a latch guard where a door has been pried. The second lists the access control. Owners sometimes ask us to skip the first part to save money. We would rather install nothing than install a reader on a door that does not latch, because the building would then have paid for electronics, still have an open door, and have a log full of entries that prove nothing.

Once the door does latch, access control adds the part that mechanical hardware cannot: memory. A door position contact tells the controller whether the door is actually shut. If it has been held open for longer than you choose, the super or the managing agent gets an alert. If it was forced, the alert is different. Over a month, the log shows which door causes trouble and at what times, which is far better evidence for a repair than a tenant's complaint that "the door never locks." That record also helps when HPD comes back to reinspect, because it shows when the door was fixed and that it has stayed fixed.

Keys to Fobs in a Rent-Stabilized Building: The Rules That Shape the Rollout

Many East Flatbush apartment buildings are rent-stabilized, and that changes how a move from metal keys to fobs has to be run. The rules come from three places, and an owner who knows them before the first reader goes up avoids most of the disputes we see.

The first is the state's rent regulator. In a 2016 administrative review, docket DR430034RT, summarized by the publisher LandlordvTenant, DHCR allowed an owner to replace street door keys with fobs on conditions: occupants, including children, got their fobs free; tenants could get up to four more free fobs for guests or employees; a replacement could cost no more than $25; people had to show identification to receive a fob, but the owner could not record it; and the building had to remain accessible with metal keys in an emergency. Not every case will be decided the same way, and we are installers, not lawyers, but those conditions are a sensible baseline for any rollout in a regulated building, and we plan the hardware so the owner can meet them.

The second is the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, which HPD enforces. Owners of multiple dwellings who use smart access, which includes key fobs, cards, phone apps and biometrics, have to give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy. They may collect only the minimum data needed, may use it only to grant access and monitor entrances, may not sell it, may not use it to harass or evict, and may not use the system to limit when a tenant or a tenant's guest can come in unless a tenant asks for that. The law also says a smart system cannot be required for a tenant to enter his or her own apartment. In practice, that means we set up the software so the owner sees what it needs to run the doors and nothing more, and so records age out on a schedule the policy states.

The third is the Multiple Dwelling Law. Section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors and, for buildings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after 1968, an intercom from the entrance to each apartment that can release the door. Older buildings must install them when tenants in a majority of apartments ask in writing. A fob system does not replace that intercom; a video entry panel that calls each apartment and releases the door can do both jobs at once. In a rent-regulated building the same section says the rent agency sets how the cost of a required installation may be recovered from tenants, which is a question for the owner's lawyer and DHCR, not for us.

Put together, a good rollout in a rent-stabilized East Flatbush building looks like this. The privacy policy is written and handed out before any fob. Fobs are issued at a table in the lobby over two or three evenings and a Saturday morning, so tenants who work days can come. Metal keys keep working through the changeover, and an emergency key path stays afterward. Each apartment's fobs are listed against the apartment, not against a scanned driver's license. And the old buzzer keeps releasing the door until the last household has its fobs.

Fifty-Nine Buildings, One Office: Access Control for Courtyard Complexes

Courtyard complexes are where East Flatbush access control gets big. A property like Flatbush Gardens, which REBusiness Online counted at 59 buildings and 2,469 apartments, has hundreds of doors: a street entrance and a courtyard entrance on many buildings, cellar and roof doors on every one, laundries, garages, a management office and a mail center. The Brooklyn Eagle's 2023 history of the complex notes that it is about 2,500 largely rent-stabilized units, that it had more than 4,000 open violations as of October 2022, and that the owner and HPD signed a 40-year regulatory agreement in July 2023 covering tax relief, rents and capital improvements over three years. A capital program like that is exactly when door hardware should be planned, because the cable and the controllers go in far more cheaply while walls, ceilings and entrances are already being opened.

The engineering on a complex is not harder than on a single building. The management is. A single walk-up with forty apartments might have a hundred fobs in circulation. A complex has thousands, and every week some households move in, some move out, some add a relative, and some lose a fob on the bus. If those changes are made on paper in the office and typed into the system later, the roster drifts away from reality within months. The fob of a tenant who left two years ago still opens the door. The roof is reachable from a fob that was issued to a contractor for one job. And nobody can say with confidence who can get into building 14 at three in the morning.

So we build complexes around three rules. First, one roster for the whole property, kept in the access software itself and tied to the lease, so that when a lease ends, the credentials end the same day without anyone having to remember. Second, building-limited credentials: a resident's fob opens the street and courtyard doors of his or her own building, the laundry and the garage if they rent a space, and nothing else, which stops a lost fob from opening the whole property and stops people cutting through buildings that are not theirs. Third, monitored doors: every street, courtyard, cellar and roof door gets a door position contact, so the office sees which doors are standing open or failing to latch on a single screen instead of waiting for tenants to report them.

Then there is the question of who watches. REBusiness Online's 2009 report says the renovation then included round-the-clock on-site security staff; News 12's 2025 reporting from Rutland Plaza describes one guard covering five buildings before leaving the post altogether. Guards come and go with budgets. A system that logs door status, sends alerts to whoever is on duty and records which credential opened a door that later had a problem keeps working on the nights the guard post is empty. It does not replace a person; it makes sure the person on duty knows where to look.

Complexes also have a privacy duty that scales with them. The Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to every one of those thousands of credentials. Owners need the written policy, a defined retention period, and a short list of staff who can see entry records at all. We set up separate administrator roles for the office, the super and any security contractor, so that the person who issues fobs is not automatically the person who can browse a tenant's comings and goings.

Residents, Staff and Services at One Front Door: New Buildings on Clarkson Avenue and Beyond

The buildings rising on the former Kingsboro Psychiatric Center campus will be some of the largest new front doors in East Flatbush. 6sqft reports that Sparrow Square's first phase, at 681 Clarkson Avenue, is two ten-story buildings with 261 apartments, 117 of them supportive homes for formerly homeless New Yorkers, with on-site supportive services, bike storage, a fitness center and a roughly 10,000-square-foot facility for Brooklyn Ballet, reached by a new private drive called Sparrow Way that runs parallel to East 43rd Street. The same report says the nearby Utica Crescent project has 322 units, including 89 with on-site supportive services for older residents, along with a health care center, retail, a grocery store and a community facility. Further south, Bethany Senior Terraces at 610 East 40th Street was completed in May 2026; the state housing lottery listing named an attended lobby and an elevator among its amenities.

We have no role in any of those projects and do not know what entry hardware their designers chose. What we do know is the pattern a building like that sets for access control. There are residents, who should open their own building, their own floor's amenity rooms and nothing else. There are staff, social workers and case managers, whose credentials follow their working hours and end the day their job ends. There are outside groups using a community or arts space, whose visitors should reach that space without passing into the residential lobby. There are retail tenants and a clinic with their own customers and their own hours. And there are deliveries, ambulances and visitors who need to reach the right person quickly. A good design gives each group its own door, or at least its own schedule on a shared door, and keeps their records separate.

The privacy law matters even more in supportive housing. Local Law 63 bars an owner from using a smart access system to limit the times a tenant or a guest may enter, unless the tenant asks for it. A supportive building may have curfew-style rules in a program agreement, but the access system itself should not be the tool that enforces them on tenants who have leases. We set up such buildings so that staff doors, office doors and program rooms can follow schedules, while each resident's credential for the street door and his or her own floor works at any hour. Where a resident does request a limit, for example on a guest, it is recorded as the resident's request.

Senior buildings add one more concern, the physical door. YIMBY readers asked of the new senior building on East 40th Street where the ramp was for people with walkers and wheelchairs. Whatever the answer at that building, the point is a fair one for every entrance we touch: a reader that is too high, a door that is too heavy, or a button placed where a wheelchair user cannot reach it before the door swings makes a secure door into a barrier. We mount readers and exit buttons at accessible heights, pair heavy doors with power operators that open on a valid credential, and set the release time long enough for someone moving slowly.

Rules that apply to East Flatbush business doors

  • Exits always open. The NYC Building Code requires electrically locked egress doors to release from the inside, to unlock on loss of power and on fire alarm activation where the code calls for it, and requires listed hardware; magnetic locks need a release built into the exit hardware.   • Who may do the wiring.   • Apartments above. If a storefront building has residential tenants, the Tenant Data Privacy Law governs any fob or phone system on the residential doors, whoever owns the shop.
  • Landmarks. Storefronts and row houses inside the East 25th Street Historic District need Landmarks Preservation Commission review for work visible from the street.
  • Accessibility. Readers, call buttons and exit buttons belong at heights a wheelchair user can reach, and heavy doors need a power operator if they are to be usable by everyone.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in East Flatbush: Questions Answered

Courtyard complexes and large rental buildings

Our complex has handed out fobs for years, and nobody knows how many are still in the pockets of people who moved away. How do we find them and switch them off?

Start with an export of every active credential from the existing system, then compare it, apartment by apartment, with the current rent roll. Any fob tied to an apartment that has turned over since it was issued, any fob with no apartment at all, and any fob that has not been used in many months goes on a review list. On most older systems the review list is long. Fobs on that list are suspended rather than deleted, so a resident who turns up at the office with a dead fob can be reactivated in a minute while the rest stay off.

If the existing system cannot export its users, or it uses the old unencrypted 125 kHz fobs that can be copied with a cheap device, the cleaner route is a re-issue: new encrypted fobs handed out against the rent roll over a few evenings, and the old fob format switched off at the readers on a date announced in advance. Either way, the fix that stops the problem coming back is linking credentials to the lease in the access software, so the move-out date ends the access without anyone having to remember.

A man got into one of our buildings and kicked in a tenant's apartment door. Would a reader on the street door have stopped him, and what else should change?

A reader on a street door that latches every time makes that kind of entry much harder, because a stranger can no longer walk in through a door that has been left on the latch, propped, or opened with a copied key. It does not stop someone who follows a resident inside, or who is buzzed in by a tenant who did not check. That is why we pair the street door reader with a video entry panel, so tenants see who is asking to come in, and with a door contact that alerts the office when the door is held open.

Inside, the apartment doors matter too. City rules require a heavy-duty latch, a deadbolt and a chain guard on each apartment door in a class A building, and a door that can be kicked in usually has a weak frame or a strike held by short screws. Replacing those strike plates with reinforced ones, fitted with long screws into the wall framing, is ordinary locksmith work and worth doing on any floor where a door has been forced. After an incident like this, we also pull the entry log so management can see which door the intruder used.

When our elevators are out for weeks, tenants on the top floors cannot come down to let a visitor or a home aide in. What lets them open the street door from upstairs?

The answer is a door station that calls a phone. Older intercoms ring a handset on the apartment wall, and a handset that no longer works means the tenant has to walk down six flights. A video entry panel can call a resident's mobile phone or landline, show the visitor on the screen if it is a smartphone, and release the street door from the call. The tenant never leaves the apartment. For a home aide who comes every day, the tenant can also ask the office for the aide to have a personal code or fob that works only on the days and hours of the visits.

There is an emergency side to this as well, and one Rutland Plaza tenant asked about it directly: how do ambulance crews reach the upper floors? A controlled street door must never stand between a crew and a patient. A way in for the fire department and EMS, the intercom release from each apartment, and someone who can open the entrance remotely all have to be in place, and the system must release the doors that the code requires on a fire alarm. We test every one of those paths on installation day.

Our super looks after three buildings on different blocks. Can he run all three from one phone while each building's residents stay separate?

Yes. A cloud-managed system lets one administrator account see several sites, each with its own doors, its own residents and its own logs. The super opens the app, picks the building and sees which doors are open, which reader is offline and who needs a new fob. Residents of one building have no credential at the others, and the managing agent can give the super permission to issue and suspend fobs without giving him the right to browse entry history, which the privacy law expects to stay on a short list of people.

The one thing to plan for is the internet at each building. If a building's connection drops, its controller keeps opening the doors for the fobs it already knows; it simply cannot report to the phone until the line comes back. For a building with a weak connection we add a small cellular link for the controller, so the super's view of all three buildings stays live.

Reporters walked into some of our buildings without a key. Once the doors are fixed, how do we show tenants that every entrance now locks?

Tenants believe what they can check. After the door work and the readers are finished, the door position contacts give a daily record of every monitored door: when it opened, whether it closed and latched, and how long it was held. A simple monthly summary from that record, listing each entrance and the number of times it was left open or failed to latch, can be shared with the tenants association without naming any tenant. When a door shows up on that list more than once, it goes back on the repair schedule before a tenant has to complain.

We also recommend a walk-through with the association's representatives once the work is done: every street, courtyard, cellar and roof door opened, released and watched as it closes. It takes an hour on a large building and does more for trust than any letter from the office.

Supportive, senior and new buildings

Should a supportive or senior building ever limit the hours a resident's fob works?

For a tenant with a lease in a New York City multiple dwelling, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law says an owner may not use a smart access system to limit when a tenant, or a tenant's guest, can enter the building unless a tenant requests it. So a resident's fob for the street door and his or her own floor should work at any hour. Program rules about visitors or quiet hours, where they exist, belong in the program agreement and the staff's work, not in the door schedule.

Schedules still have a place in these buildings, for everyone who is not a resident. Case managers, program staff, contractors and volunteers can have credentials that follow their working hours, community rooms can open only for booked programs, and offices can lock at the end of the day. If a resident asks for a limit, such as cancelling a former guest's access, that request is recorded as the resident's.

Row houses and two-family homes

Our mother can no longer manage the stoop easily, and fumbling for keys at the top of the steps is the hardest part. Where should the keypad go on our row house?

Put the lock where the effort is least. On an attached row house with a stoop, the keypad or reader goes at hand height beside the front door, close enough to the handrail that she can hold on with one hand and enter a code or tap a fob with the other, and the strike releases the door so it opens with a push rather than a turn of the key. A backlit keypad helps on a dim street. If the house has a door under the stoop at sidewalk level and it leads safely into the house, that door can become her everyday entrance instead.

For a family member who comes to help, a separate code means nobody has to hide a key under a planter. Each code can be named, scheduled and cancelled on its own. And if your mother would rather not use a code at all, a fob on a lanyard or a small station that you can release from your phone when she rings are both easy to add.

Shops, clinics and offices

Our practice near Kings County Hospital has staff whose shifts start in the evening and end the next morning. Can a schedule cross midnight without leaving a gap?

Yes, as long as the schedule is built as a single shift rather than two separate days. Good access software lets you define a time window such as 7 p.m. to 7:30 a.m. that runs across the change of date, and assign it to the night staff. A schedule set up the lazy way, as "Monday 7 p.m. to midnight" plus "Tuesday midnight to 7:30 a.m.," tends to break the first time someone edits one half and forgets the other, and a nurse is locked out at four in the morning.

We also set a short grace period at each end of a shift, so staff who arrive early or leave a little late are not stuck at the door, and we keep a supervisor credential that works at any hour for emergencies. Holidays and daylight saving changes are checked in the software before handover, because those are the nights an overnight schedule is most likely to misbehave.

Our grocery on Church Avenue stays open past ten, but we depend on walk-in trade. Is a buzz-in front door realistic for a store like ours?

It can be, if it is used only for the hours that need it. A buzz-in door locks the outside handle after a set time while the inside always opens freely, and the clerk releases the lock with a button behind the counter after seeing the customer through the glass or on a camera station. Most customers adjust within a few nights. The cost of a slower door is a few lost sales on a quiet night; the gain is that nobody walks in unseen when the store is busy with one clerk and the street is empty.

What a buzz-in door cannot do is make a robbery impossible, because a person can still be let in. It works best as one part of a plan: the cash register and safe out of reach from the door, a locked stockroom, a staff-only back door, and a camera covering the entrance. The lock must always release from inside with one motion and must release in a power failure.

We sublease the back half of our Church Avenue storefront to another business. Can we share the street door and still keep separate codes and separate records?

Yes. One controller can run the shared street door and the door between the two halves, with each business's staff in its own group. Your people open the street door and your half; theirs open the street door and their half. Each business can be given its own administrator login that sees only its own staff and its own door events. If the sublease ends, that business's group is removed in one step and your codes are untouched.

Settle in writing who pays for the system, who owns it when the sublease ends, and who is responsible for the shared door's hardware. Those questions are much easier to answer before installation than after a dispute.

New owners and the rules on who does the work

We bought our building recently, and the old management company will not hand over the login for the door system. What are our options?

First, ask in writing, through your closing attorney if needed, for the administrator credentials, the controller's location and the account with the manufacturer or cloud provider. Some systems can be transferred to a new owner by the manufacturer once ownership is proved. If that fails, a technician can usually reset the controller to factory settings on site and set it up again under your account, but every fob and code is then lost and has to be re-issued.

If the hardware is old or uses copyable fobs, a reset is often the moment to replace the controller and credentials rather than rebuild the old system. Either way, keep the new administrator account in the owner's name, not the managing agent's, so the next change of management does not repeat the problem.

Since the city's new electrical code took effect in December 2025, who is allowed to run the wiring for a reader in our building?

The NYC Administrative Code, as amended with the 2025 Electrical Code effective December 21, 2025, does not require an electrical permit for wiring that runs below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits, but only when the work is done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician, or a qualified person as the Electrical Code defines that term. Some listed exceptions must be done by a master or special electrician. In plain terms, a handyman with a drill is no longer a safe choice for an access control job in a New York City building.

The 120-volt power that feeds a lock power supply or a controller is electrical work and is handled separately. We work under New York State license #12000287431, tell you on the quote which parts of the job are low-voltage and which need an electrician, and coordinate the two so they are done on the same visit where possible.

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in East Flatbush

These are the generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each one is set against what an East Flatbush door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources.

"Installing a fob system secures the building."

This is the most common claim, and in East Flatbush it is the most dangerous one. A fob system secures a door only if the door closes and latches by itself. The reporting from two of the neighborhood's biggest complexes is about doors that were broken or unlocked, not about doors that lacked fobs.

HPD writes a class C violation for a door that will not close or latch, and gives the owner 14 days to fix it. That is the standard a door has to meet before any electronics are worth buying. A reader on a door that drifts open adds cost and a log, and nothing else.

The honest sequence is door first, then lock, then credentials. That is why our quotes list closer, hinge, strike and frame work as their own lines, before the reader and the fobs.

"Access control costs a few hundred dollars per door."

Online answers often quote the price of a reader or a smart lock and call it the price of access control. A working controlled door also needs an electric strike or lock, cable from a controller, a power supply with a battery, a door contact, an exit device where the code requires one, and labor to fit them to an old frame.

One installer's cost guide, Swiftlane, updated in September 2026, puts an installed key fob door at $1,000 to $3,000 and a video intercom entry point at $2,000 to $8,000. Our Brooklyn list puts a single controlled door at $1,500 to $2,500 and a video panel with fob access replacing a buzzer at $1,500 to $3,000.

The part online answers leave out is the door repair. On an East Flatbush walk-up that is often a new closer or a rebuilt jamb, and it is priced as its own line so you can see it.

"A landlord can simply swap the keys for fobs."

In a rent-stabilized building, which many East Flatbush apartment houses are, the switch comes with conditions. The 2016 DHCR ruling summarized by LandlordvTenant allowed fobs only with free fobs for occupants, up to four free extras, a $25 cap on replacements, no recording of tenants' ID, and metal-key access in an emergency.

On top of that, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law requires a written privacy and retention policy, minimum data, no sale of data and no use of the system to limit when tenants come and go.

An owner who ignores those rules can end up with a building full of disputes and a system that has to be reconfigured. We plan the rollout with the rules in front of us, and we recommend the owner's lawyer reviews the policy before the first fob is handed out.

"Phone apps are replacing fobs, so buildings should go phone-only."

Phone credentials are convenient and we install them every week. But a phone-only building assumes every resident has a smartphone with a charged battery and a working data plan at the door, and that every aide, relative and contractor does too.

In an older East Flatbush building with long-time tenants, some residents use a basic phone or a landline. In a supportive building, some residents may change phones often. A door that opens only for an app will lock some of those people out.

We set up phones as one option, never the only one. Every resident gets a fob or code as well, and the video panel can still call a landline. The privacy law also says the smart system cannot be the only way into a tenant's own apartment.

"Face recognition is the most secure way to run a lobby."

Face recognition panels exist, and the cost guide above puts them at $2,500 to $10,000 a door. In a New York City rental building, biometric data is on the Tenant Data Privacy Law's list of protected information, with the same limits on collection, use and retention as every other entry record and far more at stake if it leaks, because a face cannot be reissued like a fob.

The security gain over an encrypted fob is small for a residential lobby, while the privacy duty and the tenant objection are large. Some panels that offer face recognition can run with that feature switched off.

We install those panels for their cameras and their directories and leave biometrics off in rental buildings unless the owner has a clear reason, legal advice and the tenants' consent.

"Any handyman or locksmith can wire an access control door."

A locksmith is the right person for a mechanical lock, a strike plate or a door closer, and we often work alongside one. The wiring is different. Under the NYC Administrative Code as amended with the 2025 Electrical Code, low-voltage signaling and data work is exempt from an electrical permit only when a master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person performs it.

The egress rules in the Building Code also apply: an electrically locked door has to release from inside, on power loss and on fire alarm where required, with listed hardware.

A badly wired maglock that does not release on a fire alarm is a life-safety problem in a six-story building, not a cosmetic one. Ask any installer for a license number before work starts. Ours is NYS #12000287431.

"Crime is down, so building doors can wait."

It is true that 67th Precinct burglaries are down 27.8 percent this year, according to the CompStat report for the week ending November 8, 2026. We are glad to repeat that. The same report shows the last 28 days running at 14 burglaries against 10 a year earlier.

Year-to-date averages say nothing about a particular building's front door. A door that has not latched for months is open whether the precinct had a good year or a bad one.

We do not use statistics to sell door hardware, in either direction. We look at the door, the keys in circulation and the people who need to get in, and we leave the forecasting to the precinct.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover one row house door and a complex of many buildings. What sets East Flatbush jobs apart is step two and step four: we test every door's latch before we price anything, and we fix the door before we fit the electronics.

1. Door photos and a call

You send a photo of each door from outside and inside, a short clip of it closing, and tell us who uses it and whether the building is a house, a walk-up, part of a complex, a new building or a shop.

2. Latch test and survey

A technician opens and releases every door to see whether it closes and latches on its own, then checks the frame, the cellar or closet route for cable, the power source and the phone and internet signal at each door.

3. Written door schedule and Brooklyn price

Each door gets a line: the repair it needs, the hardware, who holds which credential, and what it does in a power cut and on a fire alarm, priced from the published Brooklyn list.

4. Door repair before electronics

Closers, hinges, strike plates and damaged jambs are put right first, so the door latches by itself every time before a reader ever goes on the wall.

5. Cable, controller and power

The controller and its battery go in a locked enclosure in the cellar or a closet, cable runs inside the building to each door, and power comes from the owner's or common circuit.

6. Credentials and the roster

Fobs, codes and phone keys are issued against a written roster tied to each apartment or employee, with schedules and end dates for aides, staff, vendors and contractors.

7. Power-off and alarm test

Every door is tested on battery, with all power removed and with the fire alarm signal, to prove the inside always opens it and each lock behaves as the schedule says.

8. Handover and first audit

The owner or manager receives administrator accounts, the roster and a one-page guide, and a follow-up after the first weeks checks the closers and the user list.

Every season: a ten-minute door walk for supers and owners

At the start of each season, walk every controlled door with the app open. Open each door, let go and watch it latch. Check that the app shows the door closed afterward. Look for a dangling cable or a loose reader cover. Read the roster and suspend anyone who has moved, finished a job or left your staff. Check the battery status of every keypad and controller before the first cold week of winter and the first heat wave of summer, when closers change and batteries work hardest. Anything that drags, sticks or bounces is cheaper to fix now than after a violation.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ten Seconds at the Street Door

This describes how we start on an older walk-up, not a particular customer. The first thing the technician does is not take out a meter or open the buzzer panel. He stands in the vestibule, opens the street door to its full swing, lets go and counts. A good closer brings the door home in a few seconds and the latch clicks. On many buildings the count ends with no click at all: the door stops just short, rests against the strike, and a light push from outside opens it. That door has probably been that way for months, and every tenant who lives there knows it.

Then we go to the cellar, because the cellar decides the price. We look for the meters, a dry wall away from the boiler for the controller, and a clear run along the joists to the spot under the vestibule. On the way back up we try the cellar door to the yard and the roof door at the top of the stairs, and ask the super how many keys there are to each. Nobody ever knows exactly. Only when the doors latch, the cable route is clear and the key count is honest do we talk about readers. A building with one good lock on a door that closes is safer than a building with a reader on a door that does not.

FAQ

East Flatbush Access Control FAQ

Does a complex with dozens of buildings in East Flatbush pay less per door than a single walk-up?
+

Usually yes, though not because of a discount on the list. On a complex, the survey, the design, the software setup and the rollout plan are done once for many buildings, and doors are installed building after building by the same crew, so the cost per door falls toward the lower end of each Brooklyn price range. A single walk-up carries all of those fixed steps on a handful of doors. The written quote shows each building as its own section so the owner can phase the work.

Can access control go into an East Flatbush building that HPD has placed in its Alternative Enforcement Program?
+

We know of no rule that stops an owner from adding access control to a building in that program, but it does not replace the repairs HPD has ordered, and door violations still have to be corrected on their own terms. In practice we fix the doors that HPD cited so they close and latch, then add the readers. The owner should tell HPD's contact what work is planned so inspections and contractors do not collide.

Do tenants have to be home while access control goes into their walk-up?
+

For the street door, cellar, roof and hallways, no; that work happens in common areas. Tenants only need to be home if their apartment gets an indoor screen or a wired station, or if cable has to pass through the apartment. For a phone-based video panel nothing goes inside the apartments at all. Each tenant does need to collect fobs, which is why we hold fob handouts on evenings and a weekend morning.

Our steel street door has been welded and patched after several break-ins. Can it still take an electric strike?
+

Sometimes. The question is the frame, not the door. If the frame around the latch is straight and solid, a heavy-duty strike can be cut in and a latch guard added. If it has been bent, filled or welded so the latch no longer lines up, replacing the frame section, or the whole door and frame, is usually cheaper over a few years than fighting it. We show you both prices on the quote.

Can a reader work on the narrow aluminum and glass shop doors along Church Avenue?
+

Yes. Aluminum storefront doors have stiles too narrow for most strikes, so the electric lock goes into the frame header, into a panic bar, or into a special narrow-stile strike made for that kind of door. Cable runs inside the frame from the ceiling. Where there is a roll-down gate in front, the reader goes inside the gate line or on the side wall so the gate does not crush it.

How long after the site visit does the written price for an East Flatbush job arrive?
+

Small jobs, such as a row house door or one street door, are often priced on the visit itself. Larger buildings and complexes take longer because each door gets its own line for repairs, hardware and credentials. Either way the price is written, taken from the Brooklyn list, and fixed before any deposit is asked for.

What should we have ready before the technician's first visit to our building?
+

Access to every door you want controlled, including the cellar and the roof, and to the cellar itself so the cable route can be checked. A list of apartments and how many people live in each. The brand of any existing intercom or fob system and, if you have it, its administrator login. And, for rent-stabilized buildings, any letters tenants have sent about the doors, so the plan answers them.

Will the system tell us if someone pries a reader off the wall or cuts its cable?
+

Yes, on most systems we install. Readers and controller enclosures have tamper switches, and the controller reports a reader that stops communicating. Those alerts go to the super or the managing agent by app notification. Because the controller and the wiring that actually releases the door are inside the building, pulling a reader off the outside wall does not unlock the door.

What happens if a street door reader in East Flatbush is sprayed with paint or jammed with glue?
+

Tenants can still get in: every building we set up keeps a second way through the street door, usually a key path for emergencies and a phone release from the video panel. The reader itself is a replaceable part. On doors that have been vandalized before, we use vandal-resistant readers with metal housings, mount them flush, and place them where the camera covering the entrance can see anyone who touches them.

Can the gates around a courtyard complex go on the same system as the building doors?
+

Yes. Walk gates into the courtyards and vehicle gates into garages can take electric locks or operators with readers, on the same roster as the building doors. A resident's fob then opens the courtyard gate and his or her own building, and nothing more. Gates need their own power and a weatherproof reader on a post, and each one must still open freely from inside where it is a way out.

Our super carries a ring of keys for boiler rooms, meter rooms and the roof. Can those service doors go on the system too?
+

Yes, and on older buildings it is often the most useful part of the job. Each service door gets a keypad or reader, and only the super, the porter and named contractors hold credentials for it, so the building knows who opened the boiler room and when. A roof door is treated differently: it has to stay openable from the stair side without a key, so it gets a door contact and an alarm rather than a lock that could trap anyone.

Are readers on a row house front door affected by snow, rain and summer heat?
+

Outdoor readers and keypads made for street doors are sealed against rain and rated for cold and heat, and we mount them under the door head or a canopy where one exists. What changes with the seasons is usually the door, not the reader: a wood door swells in summer and shrinks in winter, so the latch and strike can drift out of line. That is why the seasonal door walk includes checking that the door still latches on its own.
