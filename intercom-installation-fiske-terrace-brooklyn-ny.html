The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Intercom Installation

Fiske Terrace is a small planned enclave on the southern edge of Flatbush, laid out by the T.B. Ackerson Company from 1905 between Foster Avenue, Ocean Avenue, the Brighton line cut and the freight tracks of the Bay Ridge Branch. Together with Midwood Park to the north it forms the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in 2008. That history is brief context here. What matters for an intercom is the kind of house it left behind: big, detached, wood-framed and spread over three or four levels.

Take the listings. 74 Wellington Court, built in 1905 as one of the development's model houses, has six bedrooms across the second and third floors, a finished basement, a motorized gate and a driveway that leads to a two-car garage with a studio apartment above it, according to 6sqft. 771 East 18th Street has seven bedrooms spread over two upper floors, Brownstoner reported. 807 East 19th Street is a three-story center-hall house with a two-car garage and room for four more cars in its private driveway. In houses like these the problem is not that the doorbell is broken. It is that the person who needs to hear it is two floors up, in the yard, or in the garage at the back of the lot.

The second reason is who lives in the house. Many of these houses hold more than one household: grown children on the top floor, a parent in a cellar suite, a tenant in a rented floor or in a studio over the garage. PLUTO counts 37 two-family lots on the blocks around the district, and houses that are legally one-family still often have someone living in a separate part of the building. Each of those people wants to know when the door is for them, and wants to let their own visitor in without walking through the rest of the house.

The third reason sits at the edges. Most of the homes on the surrounding blocks are not in houses at all. They are in about three dozen apartment buildings along Ocean Avenue and on East 16th and 17th Streets north of Foster Avenue, and those buildings carry every intercom violation HPD has written on these blocks. A lobby intercom in a 1920s or 1930s co-op is a different job from a house system, with a riser, a board, a law that requires it and a phone line that may not be there much longer. We do both kinds of work, and this page covers both.

Six Fiske Terrace facts that change how an intercom is planned

  • Houses are freestanding and deep. The designation report describes about 250 detached houses on large lots, most built and occupied by 1914, many with a porch at the front and a freestanding garage at the end of a concrete driveway. A visitor can arrive at three different doors, and the house may need to hear all of them.
  • Some houses were built in series. The report quotes a period account of Ackerson building "in series from ten to 20" houses, and the Corbin company's Midwood Park houses were precut in a factory. Neighbors on the same block often share a floor plan, which helps with cable routes but never replaces a walk-through.
  • The district covers yards as well as facades. The Commission's Permit Guidebook says permits are needed for work "even on parts of the building that are not visible from the street, as well as work in front, side, and rear yards." A trench to the garage or a station on a gate post is part of the landmark question.
  • Every intercom violation is at an apartment building. NYC Open Data shows 31 intercom and buzzer violations on these blocks since 1994, all at apartment buildings and none at a district house. Nine were written in 2026, and all nine were still open on November 11, 2026.
  • One apartment house sits inside the district. 800 East 17th Street, a six-story co-op from 1936 on Wellington Court, lists 27 units and a voice intercom on Compass. It is the one building inside the district where a lobby intercom is a legal requirement.
  • The phone lines run through the back yards. A former Fiske Terrace Association president told the Brooklyn Paper, "We have phone lines in the backyards of our homes," and Verizon asked federal regulators in September 2026 to retire copper landline service in New York. Any entry panel that still dials out over copper has a short future.

Where this page applies. It covers the houses between Foster Avenue and Avenue H, from the Brighton line cut east to Ocean Avenue, including the courts (Wellington, Waldorf, Marlborough and DeKoven), the Glenwood Road and East 17th Street malls, and the Midwood Park blocks north of Glenwood Road, plus the apartment buildings on the Ocean Avenue and Foster Avenue edges. Repair Calls in Fiske Terrace

Intercom Trouble in Fiske Terrace? A Kitchen Master Station Gone Quiet, a Garage Nobody Can Reach, or a Co-op Panel With Open HPD Orders

Not every call that starts as an installation is one. In the houses, the common first call is about an old room-to-room intercom: the master station in the kitchen still lights up, but two of the bedroom stations hiss, the porch speaker crackles and nobody remembers how the radio function worked. In the apartment buildings, it is a lobby panel that rings some apartments and not others, or a door that buzzes and stays locked. Either can turn out to be a repair, a partial replacement or a new system, and the first visit is about telling which.

The test is the same in both. We find where the wires end, usually in the cellar near the electrical panel in a house and at a control box in the cellar or lobby ceiling in an apartment building, and test each run. If most of the wire is sound, a new system can often reuse it and the job gets smaller. If the wire was cut during a kitchen or bathroom renovation, or was never more than a pair of thin bell wires, the plan changes. You hear the result before any price is set, and if a straight repair is the right answer we say so and book it at the Brooklyn service rate on our Brooklyn intercom page.

What We Install

Intercom Installation Services in Fiske Terrace

Most Fiske Terrace intercom jobs come down to three choices: where the visitor stands when they ring, where the people inside need to answer, and which doors or gates the system should be able to open. The services below are the pieces we combine to answer those three questions for a house, a house with a tenant, or an apartment building.

House-wide answering

Indoor stations on every floor that answer the porch door, from the kitchen to the top-floor bedrooms and the cellar suite, so nobody has to run down two flights to see who is there.

Room-to-room calling

Stations that call each other as well as the door, for the parent on the third floor who wants to reach the kitchen, or the office in the attic that wants to be left alone until lunch.

Porch door video stations

A slim camera station beside the front door under the porch roof, placed so the face of the person ringing is lit and in frame, not the top of their hat.

Drive, gate and garage stations

Stations at a driveway gate, at the side door off the drive, and in or on a detached garage, all on the same system as the house so a visitor at any of them reaches the right person.

Second households

A separate button, separate indoor stations and a separate release for a rented floor, a cellar suite or an apartment over the garage, so each household answers only its own visitors.

Old intercom replacement

Testing and replacing room-to-room intercoms from the 1960s through the 1990s, reusing their wires where the count and condition allow and running new cable where they do not.

Phone and app answering

Calls from the porch or gate to household phones, for the yard, the garage workshop or when nobody is home, with indoor stations kept for the times the phone is in another room.

Lobby intercoms for co-ops and rentals

Street panels, apartment stations, risers and releases for the apartment buildings on Ocean Avenue, Foster Avenue, Glenwood Road and Avenue H, including the one co-op inside the district.

Shop and practice doors

Stations at shop doors on Avenue H and Foster Avenue and at side entrances of houses used as offices or practices, so clients ring the business and not the family.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Fiske Terrace's Architecture

The designation report describes the district's houses by form rather than by style: foursquares, temple-front houses and bungalows, finished in Arts and Crafts, Colonial Revival and Dutch Colonial Revival dress, with a few Tudor Revival and stucco houses among them. Around the district stand prewar elevator co-ops, walk-ups over Foster Avenue and a newer doorman rental on Ocean Avenue. Each type gives an intercom a different set of walls, distances and decision-makers.

Three-floor foursquares and center-hall houses

Square houses with a full attic floor, often six or seven bedrooms, where the issue is reaching the top floor and the cellar from one door.

Bungalows and low porch houses

One and a half stories under a wide roof, where the stations are few but the porch roof and the deep eaves decide where the door station can sit.

Houses with a garage apartment

Lots where the old garage has a studio or apartment above it, which turns the intercom into a two-building system across the back yard.

Houses with a rented floor

Two-family houses and houses with a separate suite, where two households share a front door or use separate porch and side doors.

Prewar elevator co-ops

Six-story buildings from the 1920s and 1930s on Ocean Avenue, Avenue H and Wellington Court, with an original riser, a board and a lobby that matters to shareholders.

The newer doorman rental

An eight-story building from 2014 on Ocean Avenue with a full-time doorman and a voice intercom, where the desk is part of the design.

Three-floor foursquares and center-hall houses: the top floor is the hard part

In a big Fiske Terrace house, the porch door is on the first floor and the people who most need to hear it are often on the third: teenagers, a home office, a grandparent's suite. An old doorbell chime in the front hall does not carry that far, and a wireless chime plugged in upstairs tells you someone is there but not who, and gives you no way to talk or let them in. The answer is an indoor station on each floor where someone spends time, wired back to a controller in the cellar.

Getting cable to the third floor is the work. The usual paths are a closet stacked over a closet, a chase beside the chimney, the space behind a back stair, or the attic eaves on the way down an outside wall. Houses built in the same series often hide the same chase in the same place, and once we have found it in one house on a block, the next house on that block usually goes faster. We still open every route from both ends before quoting, because a hundred years of renovations rarely leave two houses the same inside.

The station itself should go where the household actually stands: at the top of the stairs rather than inside a bedroom, by the desk in an attic office, beside the kitchen door rather than in the formal front hall. A station mounted where nobody walks gets ignored, and then the family goes back to shouting down the stairs.

Bungalows and low porch houses: deep roofs and close doors

The district's bungalows and temple-front houses sit lower, often with one and a half stories under a broad roof and a porch tucked beneath it. The distances are short and two or three indoor stations usually cover the house. The design question moves outside: under a deep porch roof the light at the door can be poor even at noon, so a video station needs a camera that handles shade and a porch light that does not shine straight into its lens at night.

Because the porch roof often comes down close to the door frame, there may be little flat trim beside the door for a station. We measure the plain board beside the frame before choosing a model; a narrow single-button station fits where a wide panel will not, and choosing it before the visit saves a second trip.

Houses with a garage apartment: two buildings, one system

Some Fiske Terrace lots now have a studio or apartment above the old garage, like the carriage-house loft described in the 74 Wellington Court listing. Whoever lives there needs to hear their own visitors, and the main house may want to call out to the garage without walking across the yard. That makes the intercom a system for two buildings joined by a back yard, which is the part of the lot where the Landmarks Commission's yard rules apply.

The connection between the two buildings is the main decision. Where a conduit or cable already crosses the yard, for power to the garage or for an older phone line, we test whether a new intercom cable can share it. Where nothing crosses, the options are a new run along a fence or the garage's own power path, or a wireless link between the buildings, and each has trade-offs we explain on the visit. The garage apartment then gets its own button at the door its visitors use, and its own station and release.

Houses with a rented floor: two households, one porch

When a tenant lives upstairs and shares the porch door, the station needs two clearly labeled buttons, and each household needs its own indoor stations and its own control of the release. If the tenant uses the side door off the drive instead, that door gets its own station. Either way, the owner's household should not hear the tenant's visitors and the tenant should not be able to open the owner's part of the house.

Tenants change. When one moves out, the next one should start with a clean station and new phone access, not the old tenant's name on the button and the old tenant's phone still able to open the door. We set the system up so the owner can make that change without a service call, and leave written steps in the cellar beside the controller.

Prewar elevator co-ops: the riser decides the budget

The co-ops around the district are substantial buildings: 1212 Ocean Avenue has 48 apartments over six floors and dates from 1925, 1280 Ocean Avenue has 66 apartments from 1935, and 1710 Avenue H, across the street from the district, has 37 apartments behind an arched entrance with a crenellated top, Brownstoner reported. In buildings of this age, the wire inside the walls from the lobby to each apartment, the riser, decides most of what a new intercom costs.

If most pairs in the riser test good, a new lobby panel and new apartment stations can run on the old wire and the job stays mostly at the lobby and inside each apartment. If the riser is broken in many places, the board weighs new cable against a system that uses the building network or residents' phones. We test before anyone prices the panel, so the board compares real numbers rather than guesses.

The newer doorman rental: the desk is part of the system

1256 Ocean Avenue, an eight-story rental from 2014, lists a full-time doorman and a voice intercom on Compass. In a building like that, the street door rarely rings an apartment directly; visitors go to the desk and the doorman calls up. Any new intercom has to keep that routing, and also work when the desk is empty for a break or overnight, so calls fall through to apartments or phones on a schedule.

Newer buildings also tend to have a network room and empty conduit, which makes networked stations practical. The design question becomes how many residents want phone answering, how the desk station handles a queue of deliveries, and who in management holds the administrator login.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Stand on the Sidewalk, Then Climb to the Attic

On a house survey in a neighborhood of detached houses like this one, the first thing we do is the least technical. One person rings from the sidewalk, from the porch, from the side door off the drive and from the garage, and another listens from every floor, ending in the attic. The rooms where the old chime cannot be heard become the list of stations. The doors visitors actually use, which often turn out to be the side door and not the front, become the list of door stations.

Then we go to the cellar. Old bell and intercom wires usually gather near the electrical panel, and counting and testing them tells us how much of the house is already wired and how much needs new cable. A household that walked the house with us knows exactly why each station is on the quote, and that is the point: a house intercom is only worth installing if it puts an answer where people are, and the walk is how we find out where that is.

The Old System in the Wall

The Master Station in the Kitchen Wall: Replacing a Fiske Terrace House's Old Room-to-Room Intercom

Plenty of houses in the district had a whole-house intercom installed decades ago, usually a master station in the kitchen with a radio built in, smaller room stations in the bedrooms and a speaker at the front door. Many of those systems have been quiet for years. The good news for a new installation is that they leave wires behind, already run from the kitchen to the bedrooms and out to the porch, through walls that are hard to open in a house of this age.

Whether those wires can be reused depends on how many there are and what state they are in. Home Controls, which sells replacements for these systems, explains that "there are four distinct wiring configurations" for old NuTone and M&S intercoms, three-wire, four-wire, six-wire and a rare eight-wire, and that any retrofit unit has to match the configuration. Counting is done at a room station rather than the master, by taking it off its wall plate and counting the strands on its terminals. If you can do that safely, a photo of the back of one room station tells us a great deal before we visit.

There are three paths from there. The first is a like-for-like retrofit that reuses every wire and keeps the room-to-room calling the family is used to. The second keeps the old wires as pull strings: we tie new cable to them and pull it through the same holes, which gives a modern system without opening walls, provided the old wire is not stapled tight along the way. The third is a new system on new routes, chosen when the old wire was cut during renovations or never reached the rooms the family now uses. We test before choosing, and the quote says which path it is.

The old station boxes matter too. When a room station comes off the wall, it leaves a cutout in the plaster. A new station can often be chosen to cover that opening, or the opening can take a blank cover plate; either is better than patching plaster in a hundred-year-old house only to cut it open again. We measure the old cutouts on the survey so the new stations are picked to fit them.

Drive, Gate and Garage

The Gate, the Side Door and the Garage: Taking a Fiske Terrace Intercom Past the Porch

The designation report describes concrete driveways with grass center strips leading to freestanding garages, many of them catalog designs from about 1910 onward. On those lots, the people who visit most often do not use the front door at all. Family comes in from the car through the side door off the drive, contractors go straight to the garage, and deliveries land wherever the driver decides. A house intercom that only covers the porch misses most of the traffic.

Some houses have added gates across the drive. The 74 Wellington Court listing mentions a motorized gate in front of its heated driveway. A gate station lets a visitor ring from the car, and a properly built intercom can open the gate as well as the door: the 2N guide to house intercoms notes that "a proper intercom includes a relay output wired to an electric door strike, magnetic lock, or gate motor," and many stations have two or more of those outputs. We connect the intercom to the gate operator's own input for an opening signal; the gate motor, its power and its safety sensors stay with whoever installed and services the gate.

The garage is the far end of the system and the hardest place to reach. Detached garages sit at the back of the lot, the cable has to cross the yard, and the Commission treats work in the yard as part of a landmark property. Before trenching anything, we look for a path that already exists: the garage's power feed, an old phone or alarm line, or a route along a fence or the side of the drive. If nothing works, a wireless link between the house and the garage can carry the signal, and we test the signal through the actual walls before relying on it.

Each extra door is a separate choice. Some families want the garage to ring the kitchen only; some want the gate to ring every phone in the house; some want the side door to unlock with a code for the children and ring for everyone else. We write down who should hear each door before choosing equipment, because the answer decides how many buttons, stations and releases the system needs.

The Law at the Lobby Door

What New York Requires of the Apartment Buildings Around Fiske Terrace, and What a New Lobby Intercom Has to Fix

A house in the district has no legal duty to have an intercom at all. The apartment buildings around it do. Habitat summarized the rule in February 2024: buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units must have a two-way voice intercom linking the front door to each apartment, the city's housing code requires a functioning intercom, and courts have treated intercoms as an essential service. Brick Underground adds that buildings from before 1968 with eight or more apartments must provide one if a majority of tenants ask for it. As one lawyer told Habitat about a co-op client, "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom."

The record around Fiske Terrace shows how often that obligation goes unmet. All 31 intercom and buzzer violations HPD has written on the surrounding blocks since 1994 are at apartment buildings, and the nine written between June and October 2026 were all still open in November. The wording on the orders describes the path of the failure, such as an intercom system "at building entrance door at public hall, 1st story," and 311 records show several 2026 inspections closed because the inspector could not get access. A lobby intercom that keeps generating orders is usually a sign that the problem is in the riser or the power supply, not the panel the residents see.

That changes how a replacement should be scoped. A new panel bolted onto a failing riser will collect the same orders within a year. For an Ocean Avenue co-op with open orders, the order of work is: test the riser and the release, fix or replace what fails, then choose the panel. The board gets a written test result for each apartment line, which is also what its managing agent needs to answer HPD.

800 East 17th Street is the special case: the one apartment house inside the historic district, a 27-unit co-op with a voice intercom. It carries the same legal duties as the Ocean Avenue buildings and the same landmark rules as the houses around it, so its street panel is planned with both in mind. Next door to the district, the Ocean Avenue co-ops are outside the boundary, which the designation report draws to exclude the Ocean Avenue frontages, and their panels need no Landmarks review.

Phone Lines

Copper Is Leaving: Entry Panels That Still Dial Out Over a Landline

Some lobby panels in older Brooklyn co-ops do not run wires to the apartments at all. They dial residents' phones over a landline the building pays for every month, and the resident presses a number on the phone to open the door. Those systems depend on copper phone service, and copper is going away. Alarm Grid reported that Verizon asked the FCC in September 2026 to retire copper landline service, with a second filing that included New York, and that the FCC paused automatic approval on September 28 after public comments. In its words, the plan was "paused, not canceled."

The pause does not mean the copper is healthy. WAMC reported in November 2026 that New York has the highest landline use of any state, and quoted a Verizon spokesperson saying, "Repairs are taking longer, and that puts our customers at significant risk if they can't communicate with folks." For a building, a dial-out panel that goes silent because the line is down for a week is the same as a broken intercom, with the same HPD consequences.

In Fiske Terrace itself, the copper is literal: residents have told the Brooklyn Paper that phone lines run through their back yards. When we find a phone-line panel, we tell the board or owner what that line costs each month, whether the panel can move to a cellular connection or the internet, and when replacing the panel makes more sense than converting it. Houses with an old intercom tied into the phone system get the same advice.

Apps and Tenant Privacy

Phone Apps, Codes and the City's Tenant Data Privacy Law for Ocean Avenue Rentals

Most new intercoms can send calls to residents' phones through an app, and many can open the door with a code, a fob or the phone itself. For a family house, that is simply a convenience. For an apartment building with tenants, it brings legal duties. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law page says owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems may not "require use of a smart access system to gain entry to the tenant's dwelling unit," must get express consent before collecting data, and must destroy authentication data "no later than 90 days" after it is collected.

In practice, that means a rental building on Ocean Avenue that adds app entry keeps a physical key working for every tenant, collects only what the system needs to run, and has a written policy on how long access logs are kept. Before installing an app-based system, we go through its settings with the landlord or managing agent so the defaults match those duties, and we leave the settings recorded with the building's paperwork.

Co-ops face a version of the same question. Shareholders may want app answering while some older residents want nothing to do with it. A good system lets each apartment choose: an indoor station, a phone, or both. We set it up so a resident who declines the app is not worse off at the door than a resident who uses it.

Historic District

Fitting a New Intercom to a House in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District

Almost every house on this page sits inside a designated historic district, so anything mounted on the outside goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. That sounds heavier than it usually is. Chapter 11 of the Commission's Permit Guidebook says intercom panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," may also be mounted "on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway," should attach at plain wood, masonry or metalwork rather than "on any decorative surfaces," and may show at most "approximately ten inches of visible conduit." In-kind replacement of an existing intercom is exempt from elevation drawings, and the Guidebook asks for the maker's cut sheet.

For a porch house, those rules point to the same place a good installer would choose anyway: a slim station on the plain trim beside the door, with its cable coming through the wall behind it, in a finish that matches the trim. A gate station on a fence post in front of the drive fits the "fence or wall in front of the entranceway" wording. Indoor stations, controllers and cable inside the house are interior work and do not touch the facade.

The yard is the part owners overlook. The Guidebook's general rule covers work "in front, side, and rear yards," which takes in a trench to a garage or a new post for a gate station. We plan those pieces so the owner knows before ordering whether the work needs a staff-level permit, and we supply photos, a sketch and the cut sheet for the application when it does.

Compliance points for Fiske Terrace business intercoms

  • Free exit. Any door the intercom unlocks must still open from inside with one motion, without a key or special knowledge, whether the power is on or off.
  • Accessible entry. A station used by the public should have buttons within reach and give both a sound and a visible sign that the call went through, so a visitor who cannot hear well still knows someone answered.
  • Landmarks. A business inside the district follows the Commission's door-opening rules for its station and any signage; a business on the Ocean Avenue edge, outside the boundary, does not.
  • Fire alarm ties. Where a door's lock is connected to a building fire alarm, as it can be in larger Ocean Avenue buildings, the intercom release is wired so the fire alarm still controls the lock.
  • Video and privacy. A video station records whoever stands in front of it. A practice should treat its door station like a camera and say so in its notice to clients if it keeps recordings.
  • Landlord approval. A shop or office that rents its space needs the building owner's written OK before anything is mounted on the facade or wired into the building's intercom.
Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Fiske Terrace: Questions Answered

These are the questions Fiske Terrace homeowners, tenants, boards and managing agents bring to us, grouped by the kind of property they come from. The shorter questions at the end of the page are in the FAQ.

Big houses and the systems they already have

The old master station in our kitchen has an AM/FM radio built in, and the family still turns it on every morning. Does a new intercom mean losing the music?

Not necessarily. Retrofit master stations built to run on the old wires are still sold, Home Controls names NuTone, M&S and Intrasonic among the makers, and some models include music features, so a like-for-like swap may keep the kitchen music and the room speakers. We check which models fit your wire count before promising it. If the family would rather move to a modern video system, the radio can be replaced by a small speaker or the smart speaker you already own, and the old room stations come off the walls. We show you both before you choose.

We took one bedroom station off the wall and counted six wires on its terminals. Is that better or worse than four?

It is neither better nor worse, just a different configuration, and it tells the retrofit maker which units fit. Home Controls lists three-, four-, six- and eight-wire systems, with three and four the most common. A six-wire count usually means the old system carried extra functions such as music or a door release, and a matching retrofit can use all six. The count only helps if the wires are intact, so we still test each run from the cellar before ordering.

Our house has seven bedrooms. Do we need a station in every bedroom, or will a station on each landing do?

A station on each landing is often enough, as long as it is loud enough to be heard with bedroom doors closed. We test that on the survey by ringing from the porch with the doors shut. Bedrooms that belong to someone who works from home, sleeps late or has trouble with stairs are the ones that usually earn their own station; the rest of the house hears the landing.

Porches, gates and the garage

Our porch faces north and sits under a deep roof. Will a video station show faces, or only a dark shape against the bright street?

That is the most common video problem on porch houses, and it is solvable. A station with a wide dynamic range camera handles a shaded porch with a bright street behind the visitor, and a porch light aimed down at the visitor rather than at the lens helps at night. We check the light at the door on the survey and choose the station for it, rather than the one that looks best in a catalog.

Our motorized driveway gate came with its own keypad on a post by the curb. Can the intercom station take the keypad's place on that post?

Usually, yes. Many video stations include a keypad, so one device on the post can take codes and ring the house, and its relay can send the same open signal the old keypad did. On a landmark lot, keeping one device on the existing post is also the simplest approach under the Commission's rules. We check the gate operator's inputs and the cable already running to the post before quoting.

Our detached garage has no electricity at all. Can it still have a station?

It can, if a cable can reach it. A networked station takes its power over the same cable that carries its signal, so a garage with no outlet can still have a working station as long as the cable from the house reaches it. The question is the route across the yard, which is landmark territory, and we look for an existing path before suggesting anything new.

We share a driveway gate with the house next door. Can one gate station let visitors choose which house they are calling?

Yes. A two-button station, or a station with a short directory, can ring either house, and each house controls the gate for its own visitors. The two households need to agree who owns the station, who pays for service and what happens if one house sells, and we put that arrangement in writing on the quote so neither side is surprised later.

Two households under one roof

Our grown son lives in the third-floor suite and wants his guests to ring only him. Can he have his own button without a second panel on the porch?

Yes, and on a landmark house that is the better way to do it. The Commission's staff-level limit is one intercom panel per door opening, so the porch keeps one station with two labeled buttons, or a small directory. His button rings only his suite and his phone, and his release can be set so it opens the front door but not the inner doors of the main house.

There is an old alarm keypad by our side door and a dead intercom by the front. Can one new system replace both?

The intercom and the alarm stay two systems, but they can share a plan. A new door station can go by the side door where the family actually enters, and the alarm keypad can be replaced or moved at the same visit, with both cables run together where that makes sense. Removing the dead front-door intercom also means patching or covering its opening, which we include on the quote.

Apartment buildings on the edges

Our Ocean Avenue co-op's lobby panel dials residents over a Verizon line, and we read that the FCC paused the copper shutdown. Should the board wait?

The pause gives the board time to plan, not a reason to do nothing. Alarm Grid's summary of the filings calls the plan "paused, not canceled," and repairs to aging copper are already slow, according to Verizon itself. A sensible board gets a written option now, either a cellular or internet connection for the existing panel or a new panel, and decides on its own schedule rather than after the line goes dead.

We have two open HPD orders on our lobby intercom and a board vote in two months. Can the orders be cleared before the full replacement?

Often they can. The orders are usually written against a specific fault, such as a dead station or a release that does not work, and a repair to that fault can clear them while the board decides on a full system. We fix what the order names, give the managing agent a written test result for every apartment line, and price the replacement separately so the vote is about the new system, not about the violations.

Our rental building is adding app entry. What do tenants who refuse the app get at the door?

They keep everything they had. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law page says an owner may not require a smart access system for entry to a tenant's own apartment, and a well-planned system still lets visitors call a tenant's indoor station or phone number without the app. We set the system so a tenant who declines the app answers and opens the door exactly as before.

The lobby of our 1925 co-op has marble walls. Where can a larger video panel go without drilling the stone?

The best spot is usually where the old panel was, reusing its holes and its cable. If the new panel is larger, an adapter plate can cover the old cutout and the extra size, fixed through the old holes. Where there is no old location to reuse, mounting into the mortar joints or onto the door frame avoids the stone face, and anything that has to go through marble is drilled once, slowly, with the board's approval of the exact spot.

What AI Gets Wrong About Intercom Installation in Fiske Terrace

Search summaries and AI answers about intercoms are written for an average house or an average apartment building, usually somewhere else. Fiske Terrace has neither. Below are seven common claims, what they leave out, and how the jobs actually go here.

"A home intercom costs about $2,500"

Cost guides such as HomeAdvisor's put the national average for an intercom at about $2,500, with a normal range of $1,200 to $8,500, and hard-wired systems anywhere from $1,000 to $15,000. Those numbers get repeated as if they were a price for any house.

They blend ranch houses with one floor and two doors together with mansions and gated estates. A Fiske Terrace house often has three floors, a cellar, a porch door, a side door, a garage and sometimes a gate, and the count of stations and doors moves the price more than anything else. So does the wire: an old intercom that tests good can cut the labor sharply.

The only number worth planning around is the one for your house. We price after the survey, list each station and door on one quote, and show you which items you could drop or defer.

"Wireless intercoms are the easy answer for old houses"

AI answers often recommend Wi-Fi intercoms for older homes so nothing has to be wired. For a small house with good Wi-Fi on every floor, that can work.

The houses here are deep and tall, and many have plaster on wood or metal lath, which weakens Wi-Fi from floor to floor. The garage sits across a yard. A wireless door station still needs steady power at the porch, and a phone-only system goes quiet whenever the internet does. The 2N guide to house intercoms puts it plainly: "a wired IP intercom will consistently outperform a wireless alternative over the long term."

We use wireless where it earns its place, often for the link to the garage after a signal test, and wire the rest, reusing old intercom wire where it tests good.

"Old intercom wiring is useless and has to be torn out"

Many summaries say a decades-old intercom must be fully rewired before anything new can go in.

Home Controls, which sells retrofit stations, explains that old NuTone and M&S systems use three-, four-, six- or eight-wire runs and that matching retrofit units are made to reuse them. Even where a new system needs different cable, the old runs can serve as pull strings through walls that are hard to open. In a Brooklyn co-op example reported by Security Sales, an installer replaced a 148-unit building's audio intercom with video, reusing the old cable for all but two apartments.

So the first step is a test, not a demolition. We count and test every old run before we design anything.

"In a historic district, an intercom needs a public hearing"

Generic advice warns that landmark properties face long reviews for any exterior change.

The Landmarks Commission's Permit Guidebook sets out staff-level rules for intercom panels: within or beside the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, one panel per door opening, attached to plain material, with no more than about ten inches of visible conduit. In-kind replacement of an existing intercom is exempt from elevation drawings. Work that meets those rules goes through staff, not a hearing.

What people miss is the yard. The Guidebook covers work in front, side and rear yards, so a trench to a garage or a new gate post is part of the conversation. We flag those pieces at the survey.

"Houses don't need an intercom; only apartment buildings do"

It is true that the law requiring intercoms applies to apartment buildings, and some answers stop there.

Legal requirement is not the same as need. A seven-bedroom house with a rented suite and a garage apartment has three households' worth of visitors and no way, without an intercom, for the right person to hear and answer. The PLUTO count of 37 two-family lots around the district, plus suites and garage studios in one-family houses, means many Fiske Terrace houses work more like small buildings than single homes.

We plan a house intercom the way a household uses its doors, not the way the law classifies the building.

"A phone-line entry panel will keep working"

Older telephone-entry panels that dial residents over a landline are often described as simple and dependable.

They depend on copper phone service, and Verizon asked the FCC in September 2026 to retire copper landline service in New York and other states. The FCC paused automatic approval, but Alarm Grid described the plan as "paused, not canceled," and Verizon's own spokesperson told WAMC that repairs are taking longer.

When we find a dial-out panel in an Ocean Avenue co-op, we price both a conversion to cellular or internet service and a replacement, so the board chooses before the line fails.

"App-only virtual intercoms replace every station"

App-based systems are often presented as the modern answer for every building: no indoor stations, just phones. Swiftlane's cost guide, for example, lists software fees of $5 to $15 per unit per month.

For an apartment building, that model has limits. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law bars owners from requiring smart access for entry to a tenant's apartment, and some residents will not use an app. For a house, a phone left in another room means a missed visitor. One New York brownstone owner on BiggerPockets wrote that a well-known app system at "$3,000 is way out of my budget," and another reply warned that "most brands will charge a recurring fee."

Most systems we plan here mix wall stations with optional phone answering, and the quote states any monthly fee before anything is ordered.

Fiske Terrace Intercom Installation FAQ

Is a station on every floor of a big house priced per station, or as one job?
+

As one job, with each station listed. The quote shows the base system for the porch and the main floor, then each added station, door and release as its own line, so you can see what the third floor or the garage adds and decide whether to include it now or later.

Will we be without a working doorbell while the new system goes in?
+

Usually only for a few hours. We keep the old bell working while cable is pulled and stations are mounted, and switch over at the end of the day once the new door station is tested. If the job runs over more than one day, the porch is never left without a way to ring the house overnight.

Is video at the porch worth it when our house sits back from the sidewalk behind a lawn and a porch?
+

For most houses here, yes. The farther the porch is from the rooms where people sit, the more useful it is to see who is there before walking down two flights or opening the door. Video also tells a household which visitor is for which person, which matters in a house with a tenant or a suite.

Do you install the driveway gate motor too, or only the intercom side?
+

Only the intercom side. We connect the station to the gate operator's input so the intercom can send the open signal, and we test it with the gate company's settings. The motor, its power, its safety sensors and its service stay with the gate installer, and any new outlet for it is an electrician's job.

Will a wireless link reach a garage at the back of a deep lot?
+

Often it will, but we test before relying on it. The signal has to pass from the cellar or a back window through the yard and into the garage walls, and trees, siding and the distance all matter. If the test is weak, we look for a cable route instead, using any path that already crosses the yard.

Can our managing agent get the riser test results in writing to answer HPD?
+

Yes. After the test we send a list of every apartment line with its result, what was repaired and what still needs work. Agents use it to show HPD what was done about an order and to plan the rest of the work with the board.

Our co-op pays every month for a phone line used only by the lobby panel. What happens to that bill after a conversion?
+

Once the panel runs on a cellular or internet connection, or is replaced by a system that does not dial out, the building can cancel that line. A cellular connection may carry its own smaller monthly fee, and the quote states it so the board can compare the two costs before deciding.

Do indoor stations come in finishes that suit period woodwork and plaster?
+

There is a fair range. Most makers offer white, black and silver, and some offer cover plates in other colors or flush mounting that sits closer to the wall. In rooms with original woodwork we place stations on plain plaster beside the trim rather than on the trim itself, and we bring samples when the finish matters to you.

Can the door station at our Avenue H shop ring my phone after we close?
+

Yes. The station can ring the counter during business hours and switch to your phone after closing, so a late delivery can still reach someone. If the apartments upstairs share the building, their intercom stays separate and their visitors never ring the shop.

Can the indoor stations be used to listen in on a sleeping child's room?
+

Some systems have a listen or monitor function between stations, and some do not. If that matters to your family, tell us before the quote, and we choose a system that has it and show you how to switch it off so nobody listens in by accident.

We are buying a house in the district this winter. What should we check about its intercom before closing?

Ask the seller which stations work, where the controller and power supply are, and whether any app or phone accounts are tied to their name.
