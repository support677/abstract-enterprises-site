Date the Building First · Then Draw the Plan · Brooklyn Base Pricing · NYS Licensed

On a single Ocean Hill block you can find a row house finished before 1900, a two-family put up during the rebuilding that began in 1993, a renovated public-housing building and a six-story affordable building that opened in the last ten years. They look like neighbours. To an alarm installer they are four different jobs, because the walls, the doors, the cellar, the wiring paths and the person who is allowed to say yes to a drill hole all change with the year the building went up. So every Ocean Hill alarm we plan starts with one question: when was this place built, and what has been done to it since? The answer decides where the cable goes, which openings matter, and whether the system should be wired, wireless or a mix.

The Neighborhood

Why Ocean Hill Properties Need Alarm Installation

Start with the name, because even that is argued over. Wikipedia describes Ocean Hill as a subsection of Bedford-Stuyvesant running from Broadway and the Bushwick line in the north to East New York Avenue and Brownsville in the south, and from Ralph Avenue in the west to Van Sinderen Avenue and East New York in the east, with ZIP code 11233. CityNeighborhoods.NYC draws it smaller, with Fulton Street on top, Ralph Avenue on the west and Eastern Parkway wrapping the south and east. Brooklyn Community Board 16, which covers the area together with Brownsville, uses Broadway, Van Sinderen Avenue and a western line made of East 98th Street, East New York Avenue, Atlantic Avenue and Saratoga Avenue, which puts the blocks between Saratoga and Ralph north of Atlantic in Bedford-Stuyvesant's board instead. Many people who live there simply say Bed-Stuy, and Wikipedia notes that developers use "Bedford-Stuyvesant/Ocean Hill" so buyers do not confuse it with Brownsville. We are not going to settle that argument. We serve every one of those versions at the same Brooklyn rate, and the boundary only matters to an alarm in one practical way, which we come back to in the questions below: the monitoring station must know exactly which cross streets and which police precinct belong to your door.

What is not in dispute is how the place was built, knocked down and built again. The Brownstone Detectives local-history site quotes an 1848 Brooklyn Daily Eagle writer calling Ocean Hill "the highest point on the avenue", meaning Fulton Street, and Wikipedia says the neighbourhood was founded in 1890 and named for its slightly hilly ground. Brownstoner's Suzanne Spellen describes Ocean Hill blocks of wood frame, limestone and brick row houses from the late nineteenth century, two-family row houses from the 1920s and prewar apartment buildings, and she counts fourteen one-block "Place" streets between Atlantic Avenue and Herkimer Street, between Rochester and Van Sinderen Avenues. Her example, the ten two-family houses at 1–19 Jardine Place, were built with a rental unit on the top floor for middle-class buyers, after sewers for those streets were approved in 1896.

Then came the long gap. Wikipedia records looting and arson in the July 1977 blackout, including a fire from a looted warehouse that badly damaged several tenements, and years of abandoned buildings and empty lots. Community Board 16's statement of district needs describes a blighted 28-block area north of East New York Avenue, beside small, well-kept, owner-occupied limestone and brownstone houses, and says that in 1993 construction and rehabilitation of over 1,500 housing units, plus commercial and open-space work, began in the part of Ocean Hill generally bounded by Broadway, Saratoga Avenue, Atlantic Avenue and Van Sinderen Avenue. That one sentence explains a great deal of what we find inside Ocean Hill houses today: original shells with 1990s interiors, and whole rows of houses that are thirty years old rather than a hundred and thirty.

The rebuilding has not stopped. NYCHA demolished the Prospect Plaza towers between St. Marks Avenue and Sterling Place, Howard and Saratoga Avenues, and replaced them with low-rise affordable buildings, many of them four-story walk-ups designed to look like townhouses. Brownstoner reported in 2014 on two six-story buildings with 134 supportive and low-income apartments planned for Broadway at Decatur Street, on a city-owned lot that had sat empty for decades, right against what the writer called "the very loud elevated train tracks". The 2016 East New York rezoning, which City Limits reports took in a 15-block area of Ocean Hill, raised what can be built along the main streets, and in 2023, according to the city's Small Business Services assessment, the city committed $95 million to pedestrian safety and transit connections at Broadway Junction. And NYCHA's September 2025 community plan for its Ocean Hill + Stuyvesant Gardens PACT project describes a three-year renovation of five developments, including Ocean Hill Apartments and Saratoga Village, with new windows, fob access control at front and back doors, and over 1,000 new cameras in common areas.

Put those together and you get a neighbourhood where the right alarm for one house is the wrong alarm for the house next door. A rule of thumb that works on Fulton Street's century-old brick fails on a 1990s frame house two blocks away, and neither applies inside a public-housing apartment, where the tenant controls far less of the building. That is the thesis of this page: in Ocean Hill, the date on the building is the best predictor of the alarm it needs. Cross-sell note, honestly made: most Ocean Hill house owners who call us about an alarm also ask about a camera on the stoop or the yard; where that makes sense we quote it on the same app, and where it does not we say so.

Ocean Hill facts that change an alarm plan

  • Population: 37,952 in the 2020 census, up 18.8% from 31,935 in 2010, according to Wikipedia; the 2010 figure works out to 69.3 people per acre over 460.85 acres.
  • Police: the NYPD's 73rd Precinct, at 1470 East New York Avenue, "serves a northeastern area of Brooklyn containing Brownsville and Ocean Hill"; the 81st Precinct at 30 Ralph Avenue serves Bedford-Stuyvesant and Stuyvesant Heights, so blocks on the western edge can fall to either depending on the line.
  • Transit: the A, C, J, Z and L trains meet at Broadway Junction; the J and Z run on the elevated structure over Broadway; buses listed for the area include the B7, B12, B15, B20, B25, B47, B60 and Q24.
  • Shops: the city's 2023–2024 assessment counted 560 storefronts on Broadway, Ralph Avenue, Rockaway Avenue and Fulton Street, with 11.3% vacant, and found houses of worship to be the single largest category at 79, ahead of 65 beauty salons and barbershops and 52 delis and bodegas.
  • Night-time: the same assessment lists insufficient street lighting, "particularly under the elevated train on Broadway", as contributing to poor perceptions of safety at night; safety was the change most wanted by both merchants (42%) and shoppers (48%).
  • Cellars: Brownstone Detectives recounts the storm of 9 October 1903, when Macon Street cellars were flooded by 7 a.m., paths in Saratoga Park "had become running streams", and the Halsey and Putnam streetcars stopped.
Date the Building

Five Building Ages, Five Different Alarm Jobs

The ages below are not official categories. They are the way our technicians sort Ocean Hill properties at the first visit, because each age comes with its own walls, its own weak openings and its own rules about who can approve the work. Most buildings fit one of them; a few, like a 1900 shell rebuilt inside in 1995, fit two at once, and we plan for both.

Age one: houses finished between the 1870s and the 1920s

These are the brownstone, limestone, brick and wood-frame row houses that CityNeighborhoods.NYC describes as Italianate and Neo-Grec, with carved lintels and stoops, plus the later two-family rows like Jardine Place. The walls between houses are solid masonry, so there is no cavity to fish a cable through sideways; the useful path is vertical, from the cellar ceiling up through floor openings beside radiator pipes and inside closets. Front doors often come as an outer pair with an inner vestibule door, windows are double-hung with weights in the jambs, and the cellar may have its own door under the stoop or from the yard. The parlor floor sits a full storey above the sidewalk, which helps at the front and does nothing at the back, where the garden-level windows and the kitchen door face a yard surrounded by other yards. On these houses we wire what the cellar reaches and go wireless above the second floor if the plaster is intact.

Age two: old shells rebuilt inside, mostly in the 1990s

Wikipedia and Community Board 16 both describe the abandoned buildings and brownstones that were rehabilitated from the 1990s on. Many of these houses kept the front wall and the party walls and lost almost everything else: new joists, drywall on wood or steel studs, dropped ceilings, replacement vinyl windows and a new staircase. That is good news for an alarm, because a rebuilt interior usually has hollow walls and an accessible ceiling space, and bad news only if the rebuild was done cheaply and the walls hide surprises. We open one cover plate, look, and decide; the answer changes the price by more than any other single factor.

Age three: houses built new in the 1993 rebuilding and after

North of Atlantic Avenue in particular, whole rows are about thirty years old. They are typically two- or three-family houses with framed walls, insulation, vinyl or aluminium windows, front yards or parking pads, and a basement that may have been finished as living space. The openings are predictable and the walls are friendly to cable. What catches people out is the insulation, the age of whatever alarm the builder supplied, and the fact that a house built as a two-family is usually lived in as one, with separate doors that each need a plan.

Age four: public housing and its renovations

NYCHA's PACT plan describes Ocean Hill Apartments as four buildings with 236 apartments and Saratoga Village as one building with 125, managed from 24 Mother Gaston Boulevard, in a project of fourteen buildings and 927 apartments constructed between 1914 and 1986. In these apartments the tenant does not own the walls, the door or the windows, and any alarm work needs the property manager's permission first. The renovation now under way adds fob access, cameras and new windows, which changes both what an apartment alarm should do and when it should go in.

Age five: buildings opened since about 2010

The Prospect Plaza walk-ups and elevator buildings, the Broadway buildings beside the el and the newer infill along the main streets are concrete, block and steel with modern windows and doors. Apartments in them are rented, often through housing lotteries, and the building's own access control, cameras and intercom are already in place. Here an alarm is a tenant's system, mounted without damage, reporting by cellular, and planned around the building's systems rather than in competition with them.

One consequence of all this surprises people: the newest building on the block is often the cheapest to alarm per room, and the oldest is not always the most expensive. A 1910 row house with an open cellar ceiling can be wired quickly from below, while a 1990s house with a finished basement and closed ceilings may need wireless devices throughout. We price the building in front of us, not its reputation.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Ocean Hill's Architecture

Brownstone and limestone rows

Three-story houses with stoops and carved lintels. Solid party walls, an open cellar ceiling in many, vestibule doors at the front and the real exposure at the back.

Wood-frame and brick rows

Lighter houses from the same decades. Easier to fish cable through, more windows that open, and back extensions added over the years.

1920s two-family rows

Houses like the Jardine Place row, built with a rental unit on top. Two front doors or one shared hall, and a cellar both households pass through.

1990s new-build houses

Framed two- and three-family houses from the rebuilding. Predictable openings, insulated walls and basements that are often finished.

Public-housing apartments

Ocean Hill Apartments, Saratoga Village and similar buildings. Tenant-installed wireless only, with permission, and timed around the renovation.

New affordable buildings

Prospect Plaza and the Broadway buildings beside the el. Concrete and block, building-wide access control, and apartments rented through lotteries.

Row houses: wire from below, keep the plaster whole

In an untouched old row house the cellar is the installer's friend. With the ceiling open we can drill up into the hollow under each first-floor door and window, drop a contact cable into the frame and finish with a recessed contact that shows nothing when the door is shut. The parlor floor is reached the same way through the floor above the cellar, using the spaces beside pipes and inside closets. Above that we stop drilling: two floors of original plaster and moulding are not worth risking for cable, so the upper windows and the stair landing get wireless contacts and a motion detector, and the panel sits in the cellar or a back closet with a keypad at the door the family actually uses. If the cellar ceiling has been closed with drywall, we look for a utility chase or an old dumbwaiter shaft before deciding to go wireless on the lower floors too.

Rebuilt houses: read the rebuild before you trust it

A house rebuilt behind its original front wall usually has a hollow interior, but the quality of the rebuild varies a great deal. We check three things: whether the walls are drywall on studs or drywall glued straight onto brick, whether the dropped ceilings are accessible, and whether the replacement windows sit in wood bucks or are foamed straight into the masonry opening. Drywall on studs and accessible ceilings mean a mostly wired system at a lower price. Glued-on drywall and foamed windows mean surface or wireless contacts, because there is nothing behind the finish to run cable through.

1990s houses: the walls are easy, the details are not

Framed walls take cable well, and attic and basement access is usually good. The details to check are foil-faced insulation or foil wrap, which can weaken wireless signals; the builder's own alarm cable, which may be sitting unused behind cover plates; the basement, which may now be a bedroom or a rental space with its own door; and the doors themselves, often steel or fibreglass with glass panels beside them. We plan contacts for every exterior door, glass-break detection for the glass beside them, and a motion detector covering the stairs between floors.

Apartments: everything removable, nothing without permission

The NYCHA resident handbook, as recorded in our silo research, says residents may not add fixtures or fittings without prior written consent from the property management office, and allows a wireless video doorbell attached with adhesive only. For a public-housing or PACT apartment we therefore plan an alarm that is entirely removable: an adhesive contact on the entry door, contacts on reachable windows, one motion detector, a small panel and siren that sit on a shelf, and a cellular communicator. The written permission comes first; we will help word the request but we will not install without it. In new lottery buildings, the lease is the rulebook, and most leases say the same thing in different words.

Rows, Places and Yards

Ocean Hill Row Houses From the Back Yard Forward

The fronts of Ocean Hill's old rows are watched. They face the street, the stoop puts the main door a storey up, and neighbours notice who climbs the steps. The backs are another matter. A row of twenty houses encloses a block interior of back yards divided by fences and walls, and anyone who gets into one yard can often pass along several. The garden-level door and windows, the kitchen extension and, in some houses, the cellar door to the yard are the openings that nobody on the street can see. That is where we count first on every row house, and it is where most of the contacts go.

The one-block Place streets between Atlantic Avenue and Herkimer Street add a twist. Brownstoner counts fourteen of them, short streets of two-family houses like the Jardine Place row. Their back yards sit close to Atlantic Avenue, which the city's East New York neighbourhood profile describes as more auto-oriented, with gas stations, car washes, repair shops and storage. That mix of busy-by-day and empty-by-night business frontage behind residential yards is exactly where we recommend motion detection inside the back rooms, rather than outdoor sensors that would trigger on every late delivery.

The cellar deserves its own word. Old row-house cellars in Ocean Hill hold the boiler, the meters and often a workshop or storage; they are entered from the yard, from under the stoop or by a hatch, and they connect straight to the house by an interior stair. The October 1903 storm that flooded Macon Street cellars by seven in the morning is a reminder that these spaces also take water. We put a contact on every cellar door that opens to the outside, a motion detector at the foot of the interior stair if the cellar is not used at night, and a water sensor on the floor beside the boiler and the lowest drain. The panel and its backup battery go above the cellar floor, never on it.

Two-family houses complicate the count only slightly. A house built like the Jardine Place row, with a rental unit on top, usually has one street door and a shared hall, or two doors side by side on the stoop. The plan follows the people who live there: if one family uses the whole house now, it is one system with every door on it; if a tenant lives upstairs, the owner's floors are an area of their own and the tenant's door is either on its own area or left off, as the tenant prefers. Either way, the shared cellar stair belongs to whoever has the boiler and the meters, which is nearly always the owner.

What we fit on a typical Ocean Hill row house

  • Garden level: recessed contacts on the back door and any front door under the stoop; contacts on every window; a glass-break detector where a large pane faces the yard.
  • Parlor floor: contacts on the main door (or the inner vestibule door, see the questions below), contacts on rear windows, and a motion detector covering the stair hall.
  • Cellar: contacts on each outside door, motion at the stair foot, a water sensor by the boiler.
  • Upper floors: wireless contacts on rear windows and any window reachable from an extension roof; nothing on front windows high above the sidewalk unless a fire escape or porch roof reaches them.
  • Roof: a contact on the roof hatch or bulkhead door, because a continuous row of roofs is a walkway.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Ocean Hill

The Small Business Services Neighborhood 360° assessment, carried out with Bridge Street Development Corporation between September 2023 and June 2024, is the best recent picture of Ocean Hill's shops. It surveyed 149 merchants and 316 shoppers and inventoried 560 storefronts on four corridors. Its findings shape how we plan a commercial alarm here: 83% of merchants rent their space, so the landlord's permission matters for anything drilled; storefront condition was rated poor for 19%, average for 56% and good for 25%; and Fulton Street between Howard and Rockaway Avenues was noted for the poorest storefront conditions. The report also counted one bank across the whole district, which means more cash stays on premises overnight than in a neighbourhood with a branch on every corner.

Broadway

Under the J and Z el from Lexington Avenue to Conway Street in the study area, with the highest concentration and variety of storefronts. Dark at night under the structure, which affects how an alarm is verified.

Fulton Street

Two- to four-story buildings with small stores on the ground floor and wide sidewalks, assessed from Utica Avenue to Mother Gaston Boulevard. Many storefront churches, salons and food shops.

Rockaway Avenue

Assessed from Broadway down to East New York Avenue, a mix of delis, salons and service businesses close to Broadway Junction.

Ralph Avenue

The western corridor, also from Broadway to East New York Avenue, shared in feel with Bedford-Stuyvesant and Crown Heights.

Atlantic Avenue

Auto-oriented frontage with gas stations, car washes, repair shops and storage, according to the city's East New York neighbourhood profile. Large roll-up doors and yards.

Broadway Junction

Street vendors and transit crowds by day; the area between Broadway, Atlantic, Rockaway and Van Sinderen is mostly zoned for light commercial and industrial use, per Wikipedia.

Storefront churches: the largest category nobody plans for

Seventy-nine of the 560 storefronts were houses of worship, more than any other kind of business in the count. The same report notes that many of the neighbourhood's historic synagogue buildings have become churches since Ocean Hill's population changed in the 1960s. A house of worship is an unusual alarm customer: it is empty most of the week, used intensely for a few hours, holds sound equipment, instruments and sometimes the collection, and is run by volunteers rather than staff. Doors in older sanctuaries are often held by drop bars or panic hardware rather than keyed locks, halls in the basement are lent to other groups, and the people with keys change every few years. We plan these buildings around three questions: who unlocks, who locks up, and who gets the call at three in the morning.

Salons, barbershops and delis

Sixty-five salons and barbershops and fifty-two delis and bodegas make up the next two categories. Salons and barbershops keep tools and stock at stations close to the front glass and often have chair renters who come and go on their own schedules, so we give each person their own code and keep the stock room as its own zone. Delis close late and open early, carry cigarettes, lottery and cash, and usually sit behind a roll-down gate. For both, the alarm watches the front glass behind the gate, the back door and the basement or stock room, and reports to the owner's phone when the last person arms it.

Compliance notes for Ocean Hill businesses

  • Siren time limit: NYC Administrative Code § 24-238 requires a building's audible burglar alarm to be capable of terminating automatically within fifteen minutes of activation. Every siren we program stops in that time.
  • Installer licensing: New York State's Department of State licenses anyone who installs, services or maintains security or fire alarm systems under General Business Law Article 6-D. Ask for the license number; ours is #12000287431.
  • Landlord consent: with 83% of Ocean Hill merchants renting, check your lease before anything is drilled into the facade, the gate box or a shared wall. We can work entirely inside your unit if needed.
  • Fire alarm is separate: a burglar alarm with smoke detectors on it is not a building fire alarm system; where a building needs a code-required fire alarm, see our fire alarm page and plan it on its own.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Ocean Hill: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come up because of how Ocean Hill was built and rebuilt. Each is answered once on this page; the shorter general questions are in the FAQ further down.

Old houses and rebuilt shells

The inside of our house was rebuilt in the 1990s behind the original front wall. Does that make running alarm wire easier than in a house nobody has gutted?

Usually yes, but only after we have confirmed what the rebuild left behind. If the new walls are drywall on studs and the ceilings are dropped or open from a floor above, cable runs almost anywhere and nearly every device can be wired, which lowers the price and removes most batteries from the system. If the drywall was glued directly to the brick, or the windows were foamed straight into the masonry, there is no hollow to use and those openings get surface or wireless contacts. We take off one switch plate on an outside wall and one on an interior wall at the first visit; those two looks decide most of the plan.

The one thing a rebuild rarely changes is the party wall. It is still solid masonry shared with the next house, so we never plan runs sideways through it. Vertical paths, from the cellar up or from the attic down, do the work.

Our row house has an outer pair of street doors and an inner vestibule door behind them. Which one gets the contact?

Both, as a rule, but they do different jobs. The outer doors are the real perimeter: a contact there reports the moment someone opens them, while they are still in view of the street. The inner door is where the entry delay starts and where the keypad belongs, just inside, so you can disarm without standing in the vestibule with the outer doors open. If the outer doors are a heavy pair held by a bolt into the floor, we put the contact on the leaf that moves first and check that the fixed leaf cannot be pushed in on its own.

Some households leave the outer doors open all day in summer. In that case the outer contact goes on its own zone, which is bypassed in the daytime setting and armed at night, while the inner door stays on the entry delay at all times.

Our house is on one of the one-block Place streets between Atlantic Avenue and Herkimer Street that many drivers have never heard of. How do we make sure responders find it?

Give the monitoring station more than the street address. When we set up monitoring we record the house number, the Place name, the two avenues at each end and the nearest cross street a driver would know, and we add a short note such as "one-block street, enter from Atlantic Avenue side". If the house has a side or rear entrance that responders should use, we note that too. The station reads those notes to the dispatcher with the alarm.

It also helps to make the house number visible from the street at night, since many fronts on the short streets are similar. That is not an alarm device, but it is the cheapest improvement to response time you can make.

Houses from the 1993 rebuilding

The builder ran alarm cable through our 1990s house that was never connected to anything. Can it still be used?

Often it can, and it can save a good part of the labour. Builders of that period commonly left thin multi-conductor cable at each door, at some windows and at a keypad position, ending in a closet or the basement. We trace each run with a tone generator, test it for breaks and shorts with a meter, and label what we find. Runs that test clean become wired zones; runs that are cut, chewed or lost behind later renovations are abandoned and the opening goes wireless.

The cable itself does not wear out sitting in a wall for thirty years, but it can be damaged by later work: a new kitchen, a basement finished after the house was sold, a door replaced and the wire cut flush. The test tells us which, and you pay only for what we actually connect.

Our newer house has foil-faced insulation in the walls. Will that block wireless sensors from reaching the panel?

It can weaken them, especially through several walls or between floors. Foil facing and foil house wrap act as a partial shield, so a sensor that would reach easily in an old plaster house may report weakly in a 1990s framed one. We check signal strength from every wireless device to the panel during installation, not after, and where a sensor reports weakly we either move the panel to a more central spot, add a wireless repeater, or run a cable to that opening instead.

This is one reason we do not quote a fully wireless system for a newer house without seeing it. The device list can look identical on paper and still need an extra receiver once the walls are tested.

If we sell the house, does the alarm go with it, and can the buyer take over the monitoring?

The equipment you paid for is yours, and it normally stays with the house as a fixture unless your contract of sale says otherwise. Because we do not tie monitoring to a long-term contract, nothing needs to be cancelled with a penalty. Before closing you delete your own user codes and your app account, and give the buyer the installer's details. The buyer can then book a visit to set a new master code, create their own users and start monitoring in their name.

If you would rather take some devices with you, say a camera or a keypad you added later, list them in the contract of sale so there is no argument at the walkthrough.

Public-housing and affordable apartments

PACT crews will be in our apartment for about three weeks putting in a new kitchen and new windows. Should the alarm wait until they finish?

For the windows, yes. NYCHA's community plan for the Ocean Hill + Stuyvesant Gardens project says new windows will be installed and that the work inside each apartment is expected to take about three weeks, within a construction period of roughly three years across the developments. Contacts stuck to old window frames would be thrown away with them. The sensible order is to get written permission from the property manager now, install the entry-door contact, motion detector and panel if you want protection during the work, and add the window contacts once the new windows are in.

During the renovation weeks, give the crew access the way management arranges it and keep the alarm disarmed or in a setting that covers only rooms they are not working in. A wireless system can be moved off a wall in minutes if the crew needs the space.

Our building is getting fob readers on its front and back doors. Can our apartment alarm use the same fob?

Generally not. The PACT plan describes fob access control at building front and back doors as part of the building's own system, run by the property manager, and an apartment alarm is a separate system that you own. The two use different technology and different databases, and the manager will not let a tenant's device read building credentials. What you can do is carry a small alarm keyfob on the same ring, so arming and disarming feels like one step.

The building's fobs and cameras do change your plan in one useful way: with the street doors controlled, your apartment alarm can concentrate on your own entry door and your own windows rather than trying to watch the hallway.

Storefronts and houses of worship

Our congregation has no owner, just a pastor and trustees. Who should sign the monitoring agreement for a storefront church?

Whoever can legally commit the congregation, which is usually an officer named in its incorporation papers or a trustee the board authorises in writing. The signer becomes the account holder: the person who can add or remove users, change the call list and cancel the service. We suggest the board picks two people for that role so the account does not stall when one moves away, and records the decision in its minutes.

If the congregation rents the storefront, the landlord's consent is needed for anything drilled into the building, and the lease may already say who is responsible for alarms. Read it before signing anything with us.

Choir, a food pantry and a Tuesday meeting all use our church hall. How many separate codes can one panel hold?

More than most congregations need. Current panels hold dozens of user codes and many hold far more; the exact number depends on the model, and we tell you the figure for the panel we quote. Each group leader gets their own code, so the event log shows which group armed and disarmed, and a code can be deleted the day a leader steps down without changing anyone else's.

Keep the master code with the account holders only, and resist the habit of one shared code for everyone. A shared code turns every change of volunteer into a building-wide reset.

Our postwar storefront has one huge display pane across the front. Can one glass-break detector cover glass that size?

Sometimes, but a very wide pane is usually safer with two. A glass-break detector has a rated range and needs a clear line to the glass; a long storefront pane can put its far edge outside that range or behind a display rack. We test with a glass-break simulator held at the far corners. If the detector does not respond at both ends, we add a second one.

If a roll-down gate covers the pane at night, the glass behind it still deserves detection, because gates can be cut or lifted and the detector reports the moment the glass goes.

A sidewalk shed has stood in front of our shop for months. Does it change what the alarm needs?

Yes, in two ways. A shed hides the storefront from people across the street, so a break-in at the front is less likely to be seen; and its roof can put second-floor windows within reach of someone who climbs it. For the months the shed stands, we suggest adding contacts to any upper windows the shed roof reaches and a motion detector just inside the front, and asking the monitoring station to treat front alarms as unverified until a camera or a keyholder confirms them.

The extra devices can stay when the shed comes down; they are cheap insurance for the next time scaffolding goes up, which on older buildings is often only a few years away.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Ocean Hill

We could not open Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask boxes or related searches from our research environment, so nothing below is a quotation of them. These are general claims that turn up again and again in automated answers and generic advice about alarms, checked against what we know about Ocean Hill's buildings.

"Ocean Hill is just part of Brownsville."

The two have been paired since the 1960s, when the Ocean Hill-Brownsville school district became the centre of the 1968 teachers' strike, and they share Community Board 16 and the 73rd Precinct. But Brownstoner's architectural writer describes Ocean Hill as more aligned with its neighbours to the west than with Brownsville, and Wikipedia treats it as a subsection of Bedford-Stuyvesant.

For an alarm the difference is real. In Brownsville, our silo research found NYCHA owning about a third of the housing stock in eighteen developments; Ocean Hill's blocks carry many more privately owned row houses and 1990s two-families. An answer that assumes every Ocean Hill household is a tenant will recommend the wrong system to a homeowner with an open cellar ceiling.

So when someone tells you what "people in Brownsville" need, check whether they are describing your building. The year it went up tells you more than the name on the map.

"Old houses can't take a wired alarm."

This is backwards for many Ocean Hill row houses. An unfinished cellar ceiling under the first floor is the easiest wiring path there is, and the hollow frames of old doors and windows take recessed contacts that disappear when the door shuts. What old houses cannot take easily is cable through finished plaster on the upper floors, which is why we mix wired and wireless.

The houses that are genuinely hard to wire are often the rebuilt ones with drywall glued to brick, and the newest concrete buildings, where every wall is solid. Age alone does not decide it; the construction does.

Ask your installer how they plan to reach each opening. If the answer is "wireless everywhere" for a house with an open cellar, you may be paying for batteries you do not need.

"Wireless sensors work the same in every house."

Wireless range on a box is measured in open air. Inside a house it depends on what lies between the sensor and the panel: plaster on wood lath, masonry party walls, steel doors, and in newer houses foil-faced insulation and foil wrap. A sensor that reports perfectly in one Ocean Hill house can report weakly in the 1990s house across the street.

That is not a reason to avoid wireless. It is a reason to test every device's signal during installation and to fix weak links with panel placement, a repeater or a short cable run before the technician leaves.

We write the signal reading for each wireless device on the handover sheet, so a later fault can be compared with the starting point.

"Tenants in public housing can't have an alarm."

The NYCHA resident handbook does not ban alarms. It says residents may not alter their apartments or add fixtures and fittings without prior written consent from the property management office, and it allows a wireless video doorbell attached with adhesive. That leaves room for a removable, adhesive-mounted alarm with the manager's written permission.

The PACT conversion now under way at Ocean Hill Apartments and Saratoga Village moves day-to-day management to a private manager, which can mean a new person to ask. Ask in writing, keep the answer, and do not let anyone install before you have it.

If permission is refused, a portable panel with a door contact held by tape that leaves no mark is sometimes acceptable when a drilled system is not. Ask that question specifically.

"Cameras have replaced alarms."

The PACT plan for Ocean Hill's NYCHA buildings includes over 1,000 new cameras in stairwells, elevators, hallways and lobbies, and many shops on Broadway and Fulton Street have cameras. Cameras record; an alarm detects and reports in the moment, and a monitoring station acts on it.

A camera in a hallway does not see the window at the back of your apartment, and a camera over a store counter does not call anyone when the back door is forced at four in the morning unless it is tied to something that does. The two work best together, with the alarm deciding when something is wrong and the camera showing what.

If you already have cameras, we can link them to the alarm on one app so an alarm event opens the right camera view.

"Monitoring means a three-year contract."

Plenty of companies tie monitoring to multi-year agreements, often to spread the cost of equipment. It is not a technical requirement. Our monitoring starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract, and the equipment is yours.

For Ocean Hill households who move, sell or change who lives upstairs every few years, a month-to-month arrangement avoids cancellation fees and arguments when circumstances change.

Whoever you choose, read the cancellation clause before you sign. It tells you more about the company than the brochure does.

"A home alarm costs about the national average."

National averages mix rural houses, suburban houses and city apartments, and they hide the two things that move an Ocean Hill price most: whether the walls are hollow and whether there is cable already in them. A wired row house with an open cellar and a 1990s house with a usable pre-wire can both come in well under a fully wireless plan for the same number of openings.

Everyone gets a personal code, the call list and address notes go to the monitoring station, a test signal is confirmed, and the owner gets the device list and app login.

After handover: the yearly building check

Once a year, put the system in test mode and open every outside door, including the cellar door and the roof hatch, and confirm the keypad sees each one. Walk past each motion detector. Look at the battery status of wireless devices, especially any on the far side of foil-faced walls, which can drop out first. If anything in the building changed during the year — new windows, a new kitchen, a finished basement, a sidewalk shed — call us to check the devices it touched. Delete codes for anyone who no longer needs one, and ask the monitoring station to confirm it received your test signal.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Two Cover Plates and a Tone Generator

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular customer. The first quarter of an hour in any Ocean Hill house decides most of the job, and it always goes the same way. We unscrew one switch or outlet plate on an outside wall and one on an interior wall, shine a light in, and look. Plaster on lath, drywall on studs, drywall glued to brick and bare block each give a different answer about where cable can go.

Then the cellar: is the ceiling open, and do pipes or old cable runs show a path up to the first floor? Then a tone generator on any unexplained thin cable coming out of a closet or a basement wall, because in 1990s houses that cable is often a builder's alarm pre-wire nobody connected. A clean test on that cable can take hundreds of dollars off the quote.

Only after those three checks do we count openings. Doing it in this order stops us quoting wireless devices for a house that could be wired cheaply, or promising wired contacts in a house whose walls will not take them. Every finding goes on the plan, next to the opening it affects, so you can see why the price is what it is.

What would a beauty salon or barbershop pay for an alarm on your Brooklyn list?
+

The number goes up with a second pane needing its own glass-break detector or a basement stock room, and down if the shop already has a sound wired system we can take over. Monitoring starts at $19.99 a month with no long-term contract.

Do door and window sensors fit the vinyl replacement windows that many rehabbed houses have?
+

Yes. Vinyl frames are hollow in places and solid in others, so we either recess a small contact into a solid part of the frame or fit a slim surface contact with its magnet on the sash. We avoid drilling into the chambers that drain water from the frame, and we check that the window still locks fully with the contact in place.

How do you run alarm cable along a solid brick party wall that has no hollow space behind it?
+

We avoid running along it. Cable goes vertically through floors from the cellar or down from the attic, then along floor edges or inside closets to the opening. Where a short horizontal run on brick cannot be avoided, it goes in a slim painted raceway at the baseboard or ceiling line, or the device goes wireless instead.

Will stick-on sensors hold on painted-over brick?
+

On smooth, sound paint, usually yes after the surface is cleaned. On rough or flaking paint, adhesive alone is not reliable, so in a house we use a small screw into a mortar joint, and in an apartment where nothing may be drilled we mount the sensor on a frame or trim instead of the brick.

Can a congregation or block association get an itemized quote to attach to a grant application?
+

Yes. We give a written, itemized quote listing each device, the labour, monitoring and tax separately, on our letterhead with our New York State license number. If the funder needs a particular format or a validity period, tell us and we will match it.

Can motion detectors be mounted on a pressed-tin ceiling?
+

We prefer not to drill a decorative tin ceiling. Motion detectors work best mounted on a wall corner at about seven feet anyway, so in an old storefront or parlor we mount them on the wall and run the cable down a corner or along the cornice line rather than through the tin.

Is it worth putting sensors in a cellar that only holds the boiler and the meters?
+

Usually yes, if the cellar has its own door to the yard, the sidewalk or under the stoop. That door gets a contact, and a water sensor beside the boiler costs little and warns of a flooded floor or a leaking tank. If the cellar has no outside door and is reached only from inside the house, a motion detector at the top of its stair can be enough.

Do you cover the Broadway Junction blocks east of Rockaway Avenue?
+

Yes. We cover all of Ocean Hill including the blocks around Broadway Junction between Broadway, Atlantic Avenue, Rockaway Avenue and Van Sinderen Avenue, at the same Brooklyn base rate as the rest of the neighbourhood.

Our church's double front doors are held shut by a drop bar on the inside instead of a lock. Where does the contact go?
+

On the leaf that opens, positioned so it reports the door moving whether or not the bar is set. If the bar is lifted from inside by the last person out, we also check that the second leaf cannot be pushed open on its own; if it can, it gets its own contact.

We read each of the following in October 2026 while writing this page. Prices are not from outside sources; they are our own published Brooklyn alarm figures, which Ocean Hill pays at the base rate. Building-age categories, survey order and device choices are AESS working practice.

  • Wikipedia, "Ocean Hill, Brooklyn" (boundaries, ZIP 11233, Community Board 16, 73rd Precinct, 2000/2010/2020 populations, 1890 founding and name, 1968 strike, 1977 blackout, 1990s rehabilitation, Broadway Junction and Jamaica Pass, transit and buses)
  • CityNeighborhoods.NYC, "Ocean Hill" (alternative boundaries, Italianate and Neo-Grec row houses with carved lintels and stoops, interior streets, Fulton Street commerce)
  • Brooklyn Community Board 16, Statement of Community District Needs for FY 2016 (district boundaries; the 28-block area north of East New York Avenue; over 1,500 housing units begun in 1993 between Broadway, Saratoga, Atlantic and Van Sinderen Avenues)
  • Brownstoner, "Building of the Day: 1-19 Jardine Place", January 23, 2015 (two-family houses with top-floor rental, 1887 street renaming, 1896 sewers, fourteen Place streets, Ocean Hill housing types)
  • Brownstoner, "Affordable Housing Proposed for Long-Empty Lot at Decatur and Broadway in Ocean Hill", June 19, 2014 (two six-story buildings, 134 units, beside the el)
  • Brownstone Detectives, Ocean Hill posts (1848 "highest point on the avenue", Macon Street in 1908, the flood of 9 October 1903)
  • NYC Department of Small Business Services with Bridge Street Development Corporation, Neighborhood 360° Commercial District Needs Assessment: Ocean Hill-Weeksville (2023–2024 surveys and storefront inventory)
  • NYCHA, Ocean Hill + Stuyvesant Gardens PACT Community Plan, September 2025 (developments, buildings and apartments, renovation scope, fob access, cameras, timeline)
  • Wikipedia and NYCHA/YIMBY reporting on Prospect Plaza (site boundaries, demolition, low-rise replacement)
  • City Limits and the Mayor's Office on the April 2016 East New York rezoning (190 blocks plus a 15-block area of Ocean Hill)
  • Wikipedia, Brooklyn Public Library blog and Brownstoner on Saratoga Park and the Saratoga Library; Patch on the library's 2023 reopening
  • NYPD, 73rd and 81st Precinct pages; NYC Administrative Code § 24-238; New York State Department of State, security and fire alarm installer licensing; NYCHA resident handbook (via our alarm silo research)

Some sources we normally check were out of reach: Google's People Also Ask boxes, related searches and AI Overview, Answer the Public, Bing and DuckDuckGo suggestions, and Yelp reviews. Our Reddit searches returned no threads about alarms or burglary in Ocean Hill, and our news searches found no recent burglary report placed specifically in Ocean Hill. Nothing on this page relies on those sources, and we have not quoted any crime statistic for the neighbourhood.
