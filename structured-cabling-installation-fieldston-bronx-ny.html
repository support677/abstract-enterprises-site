The Neighborhood

Why Fieldston Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Fieldston's houses were built to last a century, and they have; the trouble is that almost none of them were built with a single cable for data in mind. The land history explains why. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report records that Joseph Delafield bought the property in 1829 and that the family built a larger house in 1869 and named it Fieldston. Development of the eastern part of the estate as a residential community began in 1909, and the first house, according to neighborhood histories, was started in 1910 and finished in 1911. Over the next decades the architects the Delafields and the property owners favored, with Dwight James Baum credited with 62 houses and Julius Gregory with forty-two, filled the hillside with picturesque revival houses in Medieval, English Tudor, Mediterranean, Dutch and Georgian Colonial styles. Those houses were wired, when they were wired at all, for lights, bells and later a telephone. The network that a family, a home office or a house full of cameras depends on today has had to find its way in afterward, and in most houses it has done so badly.

What makes that hard is the way the houses were built. The BHS architectural tour of Fieldston describes 355 West 246th Street with fieldstone and stucco-coated facades, false half-timbering and slate-shingle gable roofs, and 4620 Delafield Avenue with a solid fieldstone base, a half-timbered main facade, tapestry brick, steel casements and a fieldstone chimney. Urban Archive notes that Baum's designs, like many others in Fieldston, take advantage of the rocky terrain. In practice that means a cable leaving the cellar has to pass through or around stone that does not drill like a modern block wall, rise inside walls finished in plaster or stucco rather than drywall, and reach rooms set into a hillside at different levels. A Wi-Fi signal has the same problem in reverse: it loses strength every time it passes a masonry wall, and many of these houses have several between the room where the provider's line arrives and the rooms where people actually work and watch.

Then there is distance. Brownstoner counts about 257 houses and related buildings across roughly 140 acres in the historic district, and other sources give slightly different counts, including 252 from Wikipedia. Whichever figure you use, that is far more land per house than almost anywhere else in the five boroughs, and the houses on it are large, often with wings, service stairs, finished attics, garages and studios. Copper Ethernet has a working limit on how long a single run from the switch to a device can be, and in a long house with a cellar entry at one end and a studio over the garage at the other, a single equipment closet can leave the far rooms out of reach. Planning where the equipment lives, and whether the far end needs its own small closet joined by fiber, is the first real design decision in a Fieldston cabling job, and it is one that a provider's installer is not there to make.

The street is the next difference. Fieldston is one of very few neighborhoods in New York City that is privately owned. The Fieldston Property Owners Association, formed in August 1923, took formal title to the street deeds in 1948, and it collects dues to maintain the private streets, trees and sewers and to provide security patrols. The Riverdale Press has quoted the Association that "the streets are owned by the FPOA and are recognized to be private, as is the sewer system that lies beneath," and reported that buses and commercial vehicles are prohibited in line with long practice, while noting the Association's plan to grant access to commercial vehicles traveling to Fieldston homes. For a cabling job that matters in two ways. The work van needs to be expected and allowed in. And any new line from the street to the house, whether a provider's or a contractor's, crosses ground that belongs to the Association, not to the city or to the homeowner.

Third, the house itself answers to the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The LPC voted unanimously on January 10, 2006 to designate the Fieldston Historic District, and the New York Preservation Archive Project explains that the Commission later gave owners a district master plan written for separate single-family houses rather than rowhouses and brownstones. Under the rule that implements it, LPC staff can issue a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit for Minor Work for work that complies with the master plan. Inside walls are not the Commission's concern on these houses, but the places where a cable leaves the house are: a new entry through a fieldstone wall, a box or conduit on a visible facade, a dish or antenna on a roof. We plan those exterior points first, keep them on secondary elevations and out of sight wherever the house allows, and tell you before we start whether a filing is likely.

Fourth, the ground. Fieldston lies within the Special Natural Area District, and the zoning rules for that district protect an "area of no disturbance" against excavation, regrading, tunneling for utilities and removal of trees. The Riverdale Nature Preservancy notes that the district protects trees of 6-inch caliper or greater, rock outcrops and erratic boulders, and the LPC master plan itself notes that Fieldston is subject to the Natural Area District. The Association cares for about 1,000 trees on its own land. A cable from the house to a garage, a pool house or a gate camera cannot simply be trenched across the lawn the way it might be in a suburb. Many outdoor runs here are better made through existing structures, along existing paths or in the trench of an earlier utility, and some are better not made at all.

The neighborhood's security news is the last reason owners call us. NY1 reported that in November 2019 burglars broke a balcony window at a home on Grosvenor and took jewelry and luggage worth more than $140,000. The Riverdale Press has reported car vandalism along Fieldston Road north of the district, where a resident's cameras caught only the driveway instead of the street, and CBS News covered car break-ins along Fieldston Road and Liebig and Tyndall Avenues. Cameras, gate stations and alarm communicators all work better on cable than on a stretched home Wi-Fi network, and they are far easier to place where they will actually see something when the cable is planned with them in mind. Where this page describes what is behind a wall, it draws on the documented materials of these houses and on what we find in houses of that kind generally.

Fieldston facts that shape a cabling job

  • Old houses, documented materials: fieldstone bases, stucco, false half-timbering, tapestry brick, slate gable roofs and steel casements appear in descriptions of district houses, so most routes are planned around masonry and plaster rather than through hollow drywall.
  • Private streets: the Association owns the streets and the sewers beneath them, and has restricted commercial vehicles; a cabling visit and any new service line need to fit its rules.
  • A landmark district with a master plan: designated January 10, 2006, with LPC staff able to approve conforming work through a Certificate of No Effect or Permit for Minor Work.
  • Protected ground: the Special Natural Area District restricts excavation and tunneling for utilities in areas of no disturbance and protects trees of 6-inch caliper or more and rock outcrops.
  • Room to spread out: about 257 houses and related buildings on roughly 140 acres (sources vary), so distance from the service entry to the farthest room is a real design limit.
  • A different edge: inside Wikipedia's boundary lines, the only apartment buildings are a short run on Broadway, Cayuga Avenue and Post Road, including the 1961 co-op at 6035 Broadway known as The Fieldston and the 1930–31 co-op at 4410 and 4414 Cayuga Avenue.

What owners add once the cable is in. Once a Fieldston house has a proper closet and tested drops, the next requests are usually cameras placed where they see the approach rather than just the driveway, a video station at the gate pier, and access points in every wing. All of those ride on the same pathways. Tell us at the walk-through what the house may add in the next few years and we will pull for it now, so nobody has to open the same walls twice.

The Path a Cable Travels to a Fieldston House, and Who Has a Say at Each Step

Every wired connection makes the same trip: from the provider's line in the street, across the ground to the house, through the outside wall, to wherever the equipment lives, and out to each room. In most of the city the only people who care about that trip are the owner and the provider. In Fieldston, the street belongs to the Association, the exterior answers to the Landmarks master plan, the ground answers to the Natural Area rules, and the inside belongs to you, or to your board if you live in one of the co-ops on the Broadway edge. The Association's streets: plan the visit and any new line

Because the streets are private, a cabling visit is not quite like one elsewhere in the Bronx. The Riverdale Press has reported that buses and commercial vehicles are prohibited on Fieldston's streets under long practice, with the Association stating it intends to grant access to commercial vehicles going to Fieldston homes. On the day each year when the streets close to outside traffic, only authorized vehicles and visitors with a resident sticker may use the roads, and delivery trucks need proof that they are delivering to a Fieldston residence. We ask for the address and the visit date early, so that the household can let the Association know a contractor is expected, and we avoid closure days unless you have arranged access. For a new provider line, the question of who owns the strip between the curb and your lot matters too; the master plan notes that the Association owns the streets and, in many cases, areas of yards abutting them, including sidewalks.

The master plan: what the outside of the house shows

Cabling is mostly invisible work, and in a Fieldston house it should stay that way. The exterior points are where the master plan comes in: where a new line enters the house, any junction box or conduit on a wall, a camera bracket, a gate station, a roof antenna. LPC's general rule for health, safety and utility equipment asks that such equipment be finished to match the building or otherwise not call undue attention to itself, and its permit guidebook says that conduit for an installation should be concealed. We use existing openings where they exist, bring cable in at rear or side elevations and below grade where the house allows, paint anything that must show to match, and keep conduit inside. Where a visible change is unavoidable, we tell you before the work and help you describe it for a staff-level filing. The Riverdale Press has published a letter describing violations at a local institution's properties that included security cameras installed without a permit; that is exactly the kind of after-the-fact trouble a little planning avoids.

The Natural Area: what the ground allows

The Special Natural Area District rules exist to protect the ridge's steep slopes, rock outcrops and mature trees, and they reach directly into a cabling job the moment a cable has to go underground. An area of no disturbance is protected against excavation, regrading, tunneling for utilities and removal of trees. We do not plan trenches across protected ground. When a garage, studio or gate needs a connection, we look first at what is already there: an existing conduit, a buried line for lighting or power that can be followed, a covered walkway, or a structure that connects the two buildings. Where none exists, we tell you plainly which options would need City Planning involvement and which do not, and sometimes the honest answer is a well-placed outdoor wireless bridge rather than a cable.

Measure the house before you count the jacks

In a smaller house, the drop count drives the job. In a large Fieldston house, distance does. Before you worry about how many jacks each room needs, think about where the farthest wired device will be. If the internet arrives at the cellar under the kitchen and the studio is over the garage at the opposite end, that is the run that decides the design. Pace it out roughly if you can, or send a floor plan, and tell us about any floor changes along the way; houses set into the rocky terrain often step up or down between wings, and each step is a place cable has to cross.

Then count what plugs in, and add a margin

Walk each room and list what connects now: computers, printers, televisions, consoles, cameras, access points, a gate station, an alarm communicator. Then think five years ahead. A guest room becomes an office; a parent moves into the ground-floor suite; a teenager's room needs a wired desk. Pulling two cables to a location costs much less than coming back for the second, because most of the cost of a drop in a stone-and-plaster house is the labor of making the path. Where a wall is hard to reach, we often recommend leaving a spare cable or a pull string coiled at the far end for the future.

Stone below: bringing cable in and keeping water out

In many Fieldston houses the cellar is the honest place for the equipment and the starting point for every route, because that is where earlier utilities came in. The walls there are often stone, and stone is where planning pays. We look for the opening the telephone or cable company used before, or a sleeve left by a plumber or electrician, and reuse it where it is sound. Where a new entry is needed, it goes through a mortar joint on a side or rear wall, sized to the cable with room for one more, and is sealed inside and out so that water from the hillside does not follow the cable in. Inside, cable runs along the cellar ceiling, supported rather than stapled to old joists, to a point beneath each vertical route. We also look hard at the cellar itself: a corner that stays damp after rain is not a place for a rack, and a boiler room that runs hot all winter is not either.

Plaster in between: using the house's own routes

Upstairs, the walls are plaster, often on masonry, and they carry woodwork, paneling and moldings that nobody wants cut. The cleanest routes in houses of this age are vertical stacks the house already provides: closets that sit one above another, a laundry or linen chute, the space beside a plumbing stack, a disused flue that has been properly closed off, the void behind built-in cabinetry. From those spines, cable branches out through floor framing to each room and comes up behind baseboard or inside a cabinet. Where a wall has to be opened, we make the smallest opening that will do, in a spot your plasterer can patch invisibly, and we tell you in the written plan exactly where those openings will be. Steel casement windows and stone window surrounds are left alone; we do not route cable through them.

Roofs above: attic work without touching the slate

Many district houses carry steep slate or tile roofs over attics, some unfinished and reachable by a hatch, some finished as bedrooms or studios. An unfinished attic is often the best way to reach the top floor: cable crosses the attic framing and drops down interior partitions into each room below. We work from inside, step only on framing, avoid disturbing insulation more than necessary and never fasten anything to the roof deck in a way that could reach the slate. In a finished attic, the knee walls usually hide a space we can use. If the top floor is far enough from the cellar closet that copper would be too long, a small switch in an attic closet, joined to the cellar by fiber, solves it neatly.

Mid-century houses: few hiding places, so plan with the millwork

The modern houses that the Association's relaxed guidelines allowed in the 1950s are the opposite problem. Flat roofs leave little or no attic, glass walls leave nothing to fish through, and open plans mean few interior partitions. Here we work from below wherever there is a cellar or crawl space, bring cable up inside built-in cabinets, bookshelves and kitchen millwork, and use floor boxes where the floor construction allows. Access points often go on the underside of a soffit or in a cabinet top rather than in the middle of an exposed ceiling. These houses are often prized for their original details, so we discuss each location with you before committing.

The schools and the university at the edges of Fieldston run campuses with their own facilities departments, technology staff and specifications. Cabling work for an institution follows its standards for cable, labeling and testing, its approvals, and its calendar, which usually means school breaks and evenings. The neighborhood context still applies: the Riverdale Press letter about one school's properties listed camera installations among Landmarks violations, so any exterior change on a campus building inside the district deserves the same care as on a house. If you manage a building on or near the district and want a second opinion on a scope, we are glad to review it.

Home offices and practices: business-grade inside a house

Many Fieldston houses hold a working office, a consulting room or a studio. Those spaces need what an office needs: wired connections at each desk, a dependable access point, a printer that does not wander off the network, and a battery backup so a brief power dip does not end a call. They also need to stay separate from the family's devices and guests, which we handle with separate network segments on the switch and clearly labeled ports. The cable itself goes in the same way as anywhere else in the house; the difference is in how the closet is laid out and documented.

Rules and practical limits for Fieldston cabling

  • Data wiring and the electrical permit. NYC Local Law 128 of 2024 added Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, effective December 21, 2025 with the 2025 Electrical Code: no electrical permit is required to install, alter, maintain or repair wiring designed to operate below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, when a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person does the work.
  • Security systems need a state license. A New York licensing roadmap notes that burglar alarm, fire alarm, CCTV and access control work requires an Article 6-D license. Cameras and gate stations on the same cable are a licensed security job, not just cabling.
  • Home improvement rules. NYC DOB guidance notes that a city-licensed Home Improvement Contractor is required to alter work in one- to four-family homes; most Fieldston houses fall in that range, so ask any contractor which licenses apply to your scope.
  • Fire alarm wiring is separate. Fire alarm circuits have their own licensing and filings and are never mixed into a data job.
  • New receptacles are electrical work. Our work is low-voltage. Where a closet, recorder or access point needs a new outlet, that circuit is for a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • Exterior changes and the master plan. Visible equipment, conduit, entries and antennas are checked against the Fieldston master plan, and where a staff-level filing is needed we say so before work begins.
  • The ground and the trees. Excavation and tunneling for utilities in areas of no disturbance are restricted by the Special Natural Area District; we do not trench protected ground or fasten cable to protected trees.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Fieldston House Before Choosing Where the Rack Goes

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine our technicians follow in a large, older house before anyone talks about cable.

We start outside, at the point where the provider's line reaches the house, and note whether it comes overhead or from the ground and which elevation it enters on. We look at the trees and rock along that path, because they decide what can change. Inside, we find where the line comes through the wall and follow every cable from there with a light, photographing splitters, old telephone blocks and any equipment shelf as we find them. Then we walk to the farthest room anyone wants wired, counting doors, stairs and level changes, and estimate the real cable length along the route a cable would take, not the straight line. That number decides whether one closet is enough. We open the attic hatch and the closet ceilings, check the cellar after asking whether it ever takes water, and feel the boiler room for heat. Only then do we propose where the equipment should live, and only after you agree to the routes does anyone drill.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Fieldston: Questions Answered

Getting the service from the street into the house

Our internet arrives on an overhead line from a pole on our private street. Should the new service come into the house underground instead?

It depends on what the ground allows, and in Fieldston the ground is protected. An underground entry is tidier and is not exposed to falling limbs; the Riverdale Press reported a tree falling on overhead wires in front of 5435 Fieldston Road, north of the district, that damaged three poles and left 142 ConEd customers without service. But moving a drop underground means excavation, and the Special Natural Area District restricts excavation and tunneling for utilities in protected areas, while the strip by the street may belong to the Association. Where an existing buried route, conduit or trench can be reused, underground can make sense. Where it cannot, we keep the overhead drop, give it a sound entry point on a secondary elevation and make sure the inside of the house is built to recover quickly if the line goes down.

Where can a new cable enter a house whose lower walls are solid fieldstone and whose upper walls are stucco?

Usually low and to the side or rear. The best entry is one that already exists: the hole an earlier telephone or cable line used, or a sleeve left by a plumber. If a new one is needed, we core through a mortar joint in the stone rather than through a stone face, on an elevation the street does not see, and size it for the cable plus one spare. Stucco above is harder to patch invisibly, so we avoid it for entries. Inside and out, the opening is sealed against water, which matters on a hillside lot where groundwater moves along foundations. Anything that has to show outside is kept small and finished to match, in line with LPC's general guidance that utility equipment should not call undue attention to itself.

Verizon says Fios is in Riverdale, but our house has only ever had Optimum coax. What has to happen for fiber to reach the house itself?

The Riverdale Press reported that Verizon installed 13 miles of underground and aerial fiber in Riverdale and that, with those feeder cables in place, almost every building needed only a street-to-building lateral to get Fios. A lateral is exactly the piece that passes the street and your frontage, which in Fieldston are owned by the Association, so the provider's own process for private streets applies. Start by checking your address with the provider. If service is offered, the inside of the house is where we help: a clean entry point, a path from there to a sensible equipment location, and a closet ready for the provider's box so the technician is not forced to drop it wherever the line happens to come in.

Landmarks, the ground and the rules

Under the Fieldston master plan, can a small exterior change like a cable entry be approved by LPC staff instead of going to a public hearing?

Often, yes. The rule that implements the district's master plan lets LPC staff issue a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit for Minor Work for work that complies with the plan, which is the point of having a plan written for Fieldston's detached houses rather than rowhouses. Whether a particular change qualifies is LPC's decision, not ours, and the master plan's telecommunications provisions were not something our research could read directly. What we can do is design the exterior points to be as modest as possible, document them clearly with photos and dimensions, and give you or your architect what is needed for a staff-level application. Work that is entirely inside the house is generally not the Commission's concern on these properties.

We want a connection out to the pool house at the back of the lot. Do the Natural Area rules limit how the cable gets there?

They can. The zoning rules for the Special Natural Area District protect an area of no disturbance against excavation, regrading, tunneling for utilities and tree removal, and they protect trees of 6-inch caliper or more and rock outcrops. A new trench across a lawn with mature trees or exposed rock may need City Planning involvement or may not be possible at all. We look first for a route that already exists: power or lighting conduit to the pool house that has room, a buried line we can follow, a fence line or walkway structure. If none exists, a point-to-point wireless bridge between the two buildings, mounted discreetly, is often the practical answer, and fiber is the right choice where a cable can be run, because it does not carry electrical surges between buildings.

A neighbor says the federal satellite dish rule overrides historic district limits. Does it?

Not entirely. The FCC's rule on reception antennas and dishes limits restrictions that impair installation, but its own text says it does not prohibit safety restrictions or restrictions designed to preserve historic districts. We could not find the Fieldston master plan's own section on dishes and antennas, so we will not tell you what it says. For comparison only, LPC's master plan for the Douglaston Historic District, which Fieldston's followed, allows dishes and antennas under 24 inches on a roof or secondary facade if they are not visible, or only minimally visible, from a public thoroughfare. Assume Fieldston expects similar discretion, and ask LPC before mounting anything visible.

Inside large and old houses

The house is long enough that the far wing is a long walk from where the internet comes in. Does one equipment closet work, or do we need two?

It comes down to the real cable length along the route, not the straight-line distance. Copper Ethernet has a working limit for a run from the switch to the device, and a Fieldston house with a cellar entry at one end, a level change, a stair and a studio at the other can approach it once the cable follows walls and floors. When the far rooms are within reach, one well-placed closet is simpler. When they are not, or nearly not, we put a small switch in a closet at the far end and join the two closets with fiber, which has no practical distance concern inside a house. We measure the route on the walk-through and put the answer, with the reasoning, in the written plan.

Can cable reach the top-floor rooms through an attic under a slate roof without disturbing the slates?

Yes, if the attic is reachable. Work stays entirely inside: we cross the attic on the framing, drop cable down interior partition walls into the rooms below, and never fasten anything that could reach through the roof deck. The real constraints are access, insulation and whether the attic is finished. A hatch-access attic is usually the easiest route in the house. A finished attic room usually has knee walls with space behind them. Where insulation has been blown in deeply, we move it aside only where needed and put it back. Steep slate gables like those described on district houses often leave generous attic space at the center.

Our 1960s house has glass walls, a flat roof and almost no attic. Where does the cable go?

Down, and into the furniture of the house. With no attic and few partitions, we work from a cellar or crawl space where there is one, bringing cable up into built-in cabinets, bookcases, kitchen millwork and the backs of closets. Floor boxes are possible where the floor construction allows a clean core. Access points go on soffits, the tops of cabinets or the one solid interior wall rather than the middle of an exposed ceiling. The mid-century houses the Association allowed from the 1950s are often valued for their original detail, so we review each location with you before any opening and keep a record of what was changed.

Our ceilings have original plaster moldings. Can access points go up without cutting into the decoration?

Almost always. An access point does not have to sit in the dead center of a room; it needs a clear line to the spaces it serves. We look for a plain field of ceiling between moldings, a closet ceiling just off a hall, the top of a wall in a corridor, or the underside of a stair, and we feed the cable from the floor above or a closet so the only opening is the small one behind the access point itself. Where a molded ceiling leaves no good option, a high-wall mount or a placement in the adjoining room often covers the same space. The plan shows each location before we drill.

Security systems on the same cable

Burglars in a nearby Riverdale burglary took the camera recorder with them. Where should the recorder and rack sit in a Fieldston house?

Somewhere a stranger would not find it in a hurry. The Riverdale Press reported a Riverdale Avenue restaurant owner saying burglars even took the DVR box for the security cameras, and the recordings went with it. In a house, the recorder should live in the equipment closet, not on a shelf by the front door or under the television, ideally in a locked cabinet in a cellar room or closet that is not the obvious first stop. Cable is what makes that possible, because the recorder can sit far from the cameras. We also recommend sending recordings or alerts off-site so that losing the recorder does not mean losing the footage, and we put the recorder on the battery backup.

Our outdoor cameras only see the driveway, not the approach to the house. Can new cable put them where they will actually help?

Usually, and cable is often the reason the cameras ended up in the wrong place. The Riverdale Press described a resident on Fieldston Road, north of the district, whose outdoor cameras only caught the driveway rather than the street. Wireless cameras tend to go wherever the Wi-Fi reaches and a socket is handy; wired cameras can go where they see something. We map the approach to the house, the doors and the balcony and ground-floor windows, choose positions that are discreet on a landmarked exterior, often under eaves or on secondary elevations, and run PoE cable to each from inside. The camera work itself is licensed security work, done under the appropriate license and planned with the master plan in mind.

Our alarm is monitored by the company the Association uses for its patrols. Will a new network disturb that system?

It should not, as long as we know it is there. The Riverdale Press quoted the Association's president that security alarm installation and monitoring is available from Excel Security, the company it uses for tasks including street closures. Whoever monitors your alarm, the alarm panel and its communicator have to keep working while we rearrange the network. We identify the panel and its connection on the walk-through, leave its cable untouched unless you and the alarm company want it moved, and if the communicator uses the internet, give it a dedicated, labeled port on the battery backup. If it needs reprogramming because the router changes, we arrange the timing with the alarm company before the switchover.

New houses and co-op apartments

We are building a new house in the district under an LPC approval. Should the low-voltage plan be part of the drawings?

The exterior parts of it should. LPC reviews what shows: service entries, meters, equipment on walls and roofs, gate posts, lighting. If the cable entry, any exterior junction box, camera positions and gate equipment are drawn into the set before approval, there is no awkward second filing later. The LPC presentation filed in January 2024 for a new single-family house at 4671 Grosvenor Avenue shows that new houses do go through this process here. Inside, the prewire should be planned before framing closes: the equipment closet, a sleeve from the service entry, conduit to the far end of the house and to any outbuilding, and spare pathways for what the family has not thought of yet. We work with your architect on both.

We own a co-op apartment on the Broadway edge. Can our cable use the building's old telephone riser?

Only with the board's agreement, and only if there is room. The riser belongs to the corporation, and in an older building like the 1930–31 co-op on Cayuga Avenue it may already hold decades of telephone, cable and provider wiring. If the board allows it, we trace what is in the riser first, identify abandoned cable that could be removed to make space, and pull only what your unit needs, sealed at each floor. Many boards prefer that shareholders stay inside their own units, in which case we work from wherever the provider's service already reaches your apartment. We send the managing agent a written scope so the board can decide quickly.

How do you protect floors, stair runners and paneling while cable is being pulled?

Our cellar has stone walls and gets a little damp in spring. Can the equipment still go down there?
+

Sometimes, but not in the damp corner. If no part of the cellar stays reliably dry, a ventilated closet on the first floor is the better home, with cable from the entry brought up to it.

Can a camera or access point be mounted on one of the big trees near the house?
+

We mount on the house, an outbuilding, a fence post or a gate pier instead, choosing positions that are discreet under the master plan and still see what you need.
