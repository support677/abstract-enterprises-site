New York School Camera Law, Applied From Kingston to the Camp Belt

Facial Recognition: Off Limits Everywhere

The State Education Department’s September 2023 determination covers every school in New York — district, charter, parish, independent, and communal alike: facial-recognition technology may not be purchased or operated. Kingston’s largest buildings and a one-room mountain program answer to the same sentence. Ordinary video was never restricted — cameras, recorders, and motion analytics stay entirely legal — and each system leaves our hands with face-matching switched off in firmware, a fact the commissioning file states plainly so the question never reopens.

Lawful Ground, Bunk Lines, and the Felony Rule

Recording in bathrooms, shower houses, changing areas, bunk and dormitory interiors, or any space carrying a reasonable expectation of privacy is a felony under Penal Law 250.45 — and neither schools nor camps receive an exception. The lawful map covers everything else: entrances, corridors, dining halls, gyms, gates, camp roads, waterfront approaches, fields, lots, studios, equipment buildings, perimeter. Classroom interiors route to your board or director rather than into our defaults; camp bunk lines get the strictest reading in our book — approaches and thresholds covered, interiors never, every position drawn so a director can hand the plan to any parent. Where rail trails or neighboring land touch the property, framing is composed onto your ground and photographed at survey.

When Footage Joins a Record — School Year or Camp Season

FERPA can pull identifiable video into a student’s education record once it informs a disciplinary decision, importing parental viewing rights and disclosure limits; New York’s Ed Law 2-d binds any outside vendor holding student-identifiable data to enforceable contract terms; and camps answer additionally to health-department oversight and their insurers’ own documentation expectations. The architecture that keeps all of it manageable is our default: recorder on premises, drives on the institution’s inventory, a written review-and-release procedure at handover, off-site copies only under a conforming agreement — so the hard conversation, whenever it comes, opens a binder instead of a scramble.

District buildings draw on the state’s $2 billion Smart Schools bond, which covers camera projects so long as facial recognition never enters scope. The county’s eligible nonpublic schools recover through NPSE, while the federal nonprofit-security and school-safety programs layer above it, each arriving with its §889 hardware exclusions attached.
