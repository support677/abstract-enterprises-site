The Neighborhood

Why Upper East Side Homes Need Sonos Installation

The Upper East Side is one of the largest neighbourhoods in the city, and one of the most settled. Wikipedia draws it from 59th Street up to 96th Street and from Fifth Avenue and Central Park across to the East River, taking in Lenox Hill, Carnegie Hill and Yorkville. The NYU Furman Center counted 224,027 residents in 2024, the fourth-largest population of the city’s 59 neighbourhoods, living in roughly 141,670 homes. Two of its other figures matter more to a Sonos plan than the headcount: 37.9 percent of households own their home, against 32.7 percent citywide, and 23.9 percent of residents are 65 or older. People here tend to stay, they tend to own, and many of them have spent years and real money getting their rooms exactly the way they want them. A speaker system is being added to something finished, not to a blank apartment.

That changes the brief. In a new rental the question is where the speakers sound best; in a Park Avenue living room with silk on the walls, a Fifth Avenue library lined with joinery, or a Treadwell Farm parlour that was restored inch by inch, the first question is what may not be touched. We treat the finished surface as the constraint and the sound as the thing we solve inside it. Often that points to products that need no opening at all — a soundbar on a console, a pair of Era speakers on a shelf, a Port feeding the stereo that is already there — and leaves built-in speakers for the rooms, and the season, where the owner and the building are both ready for them.

The second thing that shapes a job here is the building’s calendar. Many of the grand prewar co-ops along Fifth and Park have long limited renovation to the summer. Habitat magazine reported in September 2025 that at 1120 Fifth Avenue buyers “no longer had to undertake renovations from May 15 to Sept. 30, as had been the case for decades,” and can now work from April 15 to October 15; the same article describes other co-op boards rewriting rules so that owners can “tackle renovation projects in the fall and spring months.” Mansion Global, writing in 2019, described the classic version as a rule that “limits all major renovations to the three months from Memorial Day at the end of May to Labor Day at the start of September.” Rules like these are building by building, and they are the reason an Upper East Side Sonos plan is so often written in two halves: the part that goes in now, and the part that waits for the window.

The third thing is the floor plan. The classic six and classic seven — living room, formal dining room, kitchen, two or three bedrooms and a staff room by the kitchen — are, in CityRealty’s words, “closely associated with apartments built before World War II, particularly in the prewar cooperatives of the Upper East Side and Upper West Side,” with an entrance that “often leads through a foyer or gallery, with hallways separating the bedrooms from the entertaining rooms.” It is a layout designed to keep rooms apart, and it does that to Wi-Fi as well as to people. Sonos’s own interference guidance rates plaster and concrete as high obstructions; a long apartment with masonry partitions and the router in the foyer is a common reason a bedroom speaker keeps dropping out of the group.

Then there are the houses. The Upper East Side Historic District, designated in 1981 and enlarged by 74 buildings in 2010, runs along Fifth Avenue from 59th to 78th Street and reaches east to Third Avenue in places; the Metropolitan Museum Historic District (1977), the Park Avenue Historic District (2014, 64 properties from 79th to 91st), the Carnegie Hill Historic District (1974, expanded 1993, about 400 buildings) and the small Treadwell Farm and Henderson Place districts cover much of the rest of the old fabric. Inside those lines are townhouses several storeys tall, often carrying wiring left behind by earlier owners, alongside the apartment houses that replaced many of their neighbours. A Sonos system in a house like that is a vertical network problem before it is a speaker problem.

Upper East Side facts that shape a Sonos plan

  • 224,027 residents, 141,670 homes: the Furman Center’s 2024 population and housing-unit counts for the neighbourhood, fourth largest of 59 citywide.
  • 37.9% owners: the 2024 homeownership rate, five points above the city’s 32.7% (Furman Center) — more households here can approve built-in work themselves, subject to their board.
  • 23.9% aged 65 and over; 50.5% single-person households: Furman Center figures that explain why simple controls, clear dialogue and sensible volume limits come up on almost every visit.
  • May 15 to September 30: the decades-old renovation season at 1120 Fifth Avenue, now April 15 to October 15 (Habitat, September 2025) — one building’s example of the summer-work rules that govern cable and ceiling work.
  • Five landmark districts and two small ones: Upper East Side (1981), Metropolitan Museum (1977), Park Avenue (2014) and Carnegie Hill (1974/1993), plus Treadwell Farm (1967) and Henderson Place (1969), per Wikipedia and the Landmarks Preservation Commission’s designations.
  • January 1, 2017: the day the Second Avenue Subway opened its 72nd, 86th and 96th Street stations; the Furman Center counts 4,025 new homes on the Upper East Side between 2010 and 2025, many of them in the towers that followed the line.

Put together, the numbers describe a neighbourhood of owners and long-term residents, finished rooms and formal buildings, where a Sonos installer has to be as careful with the calendar and the plaster as with the sound. None of that makes a great system harder to own. It changes the order in which the work happens and the tools used to do it.

Most Upper East Side households pair Sonos with a TV move or a network fix, because the soundbar, the screen and the Wi-Fi tend to need attention at the same time. Building Specialists

Sonos Installation for the Upper East Side’s Architecture

Six building types account for nearly every Upper East Side Sonos job. They differ less in how they sound than in what can be opened, who has to agree, and where a cable can travel.

Park and Fifth Avenue Prewar Co-ops

The mid-rise apartment houses of the 1910s and ’20s that line Park Avenue above 79th Street. DNAinfo’s report on the 2014 Park Avenue Historic District names J.E.R. Carpenter and Rosario Candela among their architects. Expect plaster on masonry partitions, deep beamed ceilings, a formal board process and, often, a seasonal window for anything that opens a wall.

Side-Street Townhouses and Mansions

The brownstone and limestone houses of the East 60s, 70s and Carnegie Hill, many inside the Upper East Side or Carnegie Hill historic districts. One owner, several floors, original plaster ceilings in the parlour rooms and service floors that were rebuilt more than once — ideal for a wired backbone up the stair.

Treadwell Farm and Henderson Place Rows

Two of the city’s earliest historic districts: the Italianate rows of East 61st and 62nd Streets between Second and Third Avenues (designated 1967), and the Queen Anne houses Lamb & Rich designed for John C. Henderson at East End Avenue and 86th Street (designated 1969). Narrow rooms, small footprints, and a strong case for free-standing speakers.

Yorkville Walk-Ups and Tenements

Walk-up buildings east of Third Avenue, including the full-block First Avenue Estate and York Avenue Estate model tenements listed by Wikipedia among the neighbourhood’s historic districts. Mostly rentals, narrow rooms, brick party walls and the walk-up line on the price list above the second floor.

Postwar White-Brick Towers

The doorman buildings of Second, Third and York Avenues: typically concrete slab ceilings, block or metal-stud partitions, lower ceilings than the prewar houses, and management offices used to scheduled contractor work. In-ceiling speakers are possible only where there is a dropped soffit.

Second Avenue Condos and Ground-Floor Suites

The newer condominiums that followed the subway, often with a control system and network jacks already installed, and the ground-floor professional suites and maisonettes of Park and Lexington with their own street doors and their own reasons for wanting background music.

The Long Apartment

Getting Sonos From the Foyer to the Last Bedroom

A classic six or seven is a string of rooms along a gallery and a hall. The entertaining rooms sit near the front door; the bedrooms sit at the far end, often around a corner; the kitchen and the old staff room sit off to one side. The internet usually arrives where the building’s riser happened to be, which in many prewar houses means the kitchen or the foyer closet. From there to the main bedroom can mean several masonry partitions, a pair of heavy doors and a bathroom tiled floor to ceiling — exactly the materials Sonos lists as high obstructions.

The result is predictable. The living room speakers are rock solid; the bedroom speaker plays most of the time and drops out of the group when the house is busy; the kitchen speaker works when the door is open. Adding a range extender, which is the first thing many owners try, makes things worse, because Sonos does not support wireless extenders at all. The fix is to put a wired connection near the far end of the apartment and let everything else find its way to it.

Three ways to reach the far end

A wired access point at the bedroom end. One network cable from the router to a ceiling or wall access point in the bedroom hall gives the far rooms their own strong signal. In a prewar co-op the cable usually travels inside closets and behind the back of built-ins, which is quieter work than it sounds and sometimes fits a decoration agreement rather than a full alteration, though that is the building’s call.

A wired Sonos product in the middle. If the gallery has a console, a cable to a Sonos product sitting on it can anchor the system halfway along. Sonos’s own requirements note that in a mesh network with a wired setup, Sonos must be wired to the primary node — so a Sonos product cabled to a satellite does not help, and we check which node is which before we run anything.

Moving the router. Sometimes the simplest answer is to relocate the provider’s router from the kitchen to the gallery with one cable back to where the line enters. It costs a cable run and a visit, and it improves every device in the apartment, not just the speakers.

The staff room question

The small room by the kitchen is often the best place in the apartment for equipment: it is out of the way, it frequently has its own outlet and closet, and it sits near where the internet arrives. Whether the Amp lives there depends on the distance to the speakers it powers and on heat. An Amp needs breathing space around it, and a former staff room with the door closed and a steam riser in the corner can run warm in winter. We measure, and if the room is too warm we use a ventilated cabinet or a different closet.

Wherever the equipment ends up, it gets labelled. The router, any switch, the Amp and each cable end carry a plain tag saying what they are and which room they serve, because in a household with a housekeeper, a decorator, a building handyman and a provider’s technician all passing through over a year, the one thing that reliably breaks a working system is someone unplugging the wrong grey box. A labelled closet is also what lets your next installer, or ours, find the fault in minutes instead of an hour.

None of this is visible when it is done. The gallery stays the gallery; the bedroom speaker simply stops disappearing.

Working With the Building

Three Calendars Behind Every Upper East Side Sonos Plan

A Sonos plan here runs on three clocks at once: the household’s, the building’s and the trades’. Treating them as one is how jobs stall in October with a hole in the ceiling and no approval for the rest.

The household’s calendar

Many owners spend part of the year elsewhere, host the family for the holidays, or want the system ready for a particular dinner. We plan backwards from that date. If the deadline falls outside the building’s renovation season, everything that needs no opening goes in first, so the rooms have music on the day that matters and the built-in work follows later.

The building’s calendar

Co-op rules on the Upper East Side commonly split work into small jobs a managing agent can approve quickly and alterations that go to the board. Brick Underground explains that a decoration agreement “can sometimes be approved by the managing agent without a full board review, meaning you can have it authorized in less than a week,” while alteration approval takes at least a month and can take far longer. On top of that, some buildings still restrict the months in which alterations may happen, as the 1120 Fifth Avenue example shows. Which category a speaker opening falls into is a decision for your building, not for us; our job is to give the managing agent a clear description of exactly what we will and won’t touch.

The trades’ calendar

If a ceiling is being opened, a plasterer or painter has to follow us. If the speakers are the plaster-over invisible kind, the finishing trades are the critical path: Sonance’s installation manual allows 12 to 24 hours for each coat of compound to cure, with several coats needed. Booking those trades inside the same window as our work is what keeps the room from sitting half-finished.

Commercial Sonos

Business Sonos Installation on the Upper East Side

Commercial work here is small-scale and particular: a single waiting room, a gallery with two rooms and an office, a boutique on a side street, a restaurant with apartments directly overhead. Sonos markets installed systems to businesses, and in rooms this size it is often the right tool, provided the network, the music licence and the neighbours are thought about first.

Madison Avenue Boutiques

The Madison Avenue Business Improvement District runs from East 57th to East 86th Street and covers the side streets alongside. Shops here want music that sits under conversation and stays consistent from the front door to the fitting rooms, controlled from a counter tablet rather than a staff member’s phone.

Galleries Between 57th and 86th

The BID’s Fall Gallery Walk on October 3 listed 42 participating galleries along Madison and its side streets. Most of the year a gallery wants silence or something close to it; on opening nights it wants more. Grouping the rooms for the evening and a volume limit for the rest of the week handle both.

Park Avenue Professional Suites

Ground-floor doctors’, dentists’ and therapists’ offices with their own street entrance inside residential co-ops. Waiting-room music, a separate zone for staff areas, and nothing that leaks into the apartments above.

York Avenue Medical Corridor

The offices around Memorial Sloan Kettering’s hospital at 1275 York Avenue and NewYork-Presbyterian/Weill Cornell at 525 East 68th Street, where private practices and support offices fill nearby buildings. Small rooms, many of them, each needing its own quiet level.

East 86th Street and Lexington

The neighbourhood’s busiest shopping streets, once the heart of German Yorkville. Chain stores bring their own systems; the independent shops, salons and studios around them are where Sonos is usually the right size.

Second and First Avenue Restaurants

Dining rooms and bars along the avenues of Yorkville and Lenox Hill, most with residents above and beside them. Here the work is as much about where the sound goes as how good it is.

Compliance notes for business music on the Upper East Side

  • Noise into homes upstairs: the city’s noise code fact sheet says music from a commercial establishment “shall not exceed 42 dBA as measured in any dwelling unit receiving the noise.” In a building with apartments over the shop, we keep subwoofers off shared walls and floors, point ceiling speakers into the room rather than at the ceiling plane above the bar, and set Sonos volume limits per product.
  • Background-music rights: the account on your phone was sold for home listening. Practices, boutiques and dining rooms need a provider that licenses business playback; you choose and pay for it, and we connect it to the system without offering licensing advice.
  • Patient privacy: HHS guidance on HIPAA’s incidental-disclosure rule asks practices for “reasonable safeguards” and does not require that every conversation be soundproofed. Background music can be one part of that; it is not compliance on its own, and we say so.
  • Building rules: a commercial unit inside a residential co-op usually follows the same contractor hours, insurance certificate and alteration rules as the apartments upstairs.

We can provide a certificate of insurance naming the building and its managing agent for any of these jobs, free of charge, and schedule cable work outside opening hours where the building permits it.

Your Questions Answered

Sonos Installation on the Upper East Side: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from this neighbourhood in particular — its renovation calendars, its finished and furnished rooms, its long-time residents and its part-year households. Broader questions about Manhattan board approval, permits, parking and small-apartment sound are answered on our Manhattan Sonos installation page, and city-wide ones on the New York City Sonos hub.

The building’s renovation calendar

Our co-op only allows renovation work in the summer — which parts of a Sonos job can still happen in the winter?

Usually everything that doesn’t open a wall or ceiling. A summer-work rule restricts alterations; it rarely stops a contractor from setting up equipment you already own, placing a soundbar on a console, mounting a bracket into an existing wall or connecting a Port to your stereo. Those jobs typically need only the building’s ordinary contractor paperwork — an insurance certificate and a booked service elevator — though your managing agent has the final word, and we ask before we book.

What waits is the work that cuts: ceiling speaker openings, cable pulled through finished plaster, invisible speakers that need skim-coating. The sensible plan is to put the free-standing system in now, live with it through the winter, and use those months to decide exactly where built-in speakers would add something. By spring we can hand the managing agent a short, specific scope — which rooms, how many openings, where the Amp lives — so the alteration request is ready the moment the window opens. Rules do change; Habitat reported in September 2025 that some boards have widened their seasons, so it is worth asking whether yours has.

Rooms that are already finished

We have a Samsung Frame TV that hangs like a painting — can a Sonos soundbar join it without spoiling the look?

Yes, and the trick is the connection box rather than the screen. Samsung’s support pages explain that on models with a One Connect box the HDMI ARC port is on the box, not on the TV, and that most of those boxes carry it as the third HDMI input. That means the soundbar’s cable goes to wherever the box is hidden — a cabinet, a console drawer, a closet behind the wall — and nothing extra has to run up to the picture itself.

The design decision is whether a bar under the frame suits the room at all. Under a Frame hung low over a console, an Arc Ultra or Beam on the console top looks deliberate. Over a mantel, where a bar would look like an afterthought, the better answer is often a pair of Era 100 speakers on shelves either side, or in-ceiling speakers on an Amp, which can take the TV’s sound through its own HDMI ARC input. We bring the bracket and the cable lengths for whichever option you choose, and we test lip-sync with the art mode switched off before we leave.

Can speakers be plastered over so nothing shows, and will a Sonos Amp drive them?

Plaster-over speakers exist — Sonance’s Invisible Series is the best-known — and they can disappear completely, but they come with rules that matter in a prewar apartment. Sonance’s installation manual says the finish over the speaker must be a flexible material no thicker than 3 mm, ideally 1.0 to 1.5 mm, and specifically forbids “genuine Venetian plaster” and fast-setting “hot mud” over the speaker face. Each coat needs 12 to 24 hours to cure, and the manual asks for a listening test before and after finishing. They are a renovation-stage product, not a retrofit into an existing plaster wall.

On the amplifier: Sonance highly recommends its own DSP amplifiers, which carry correction curves for different finishes, and warns that an amplifier whose power exceeds the speaker’s rating can damage the speaker or the finish. A Sonos Amp can drive a passive speaker, but whether it suits a particular invisible model depends on that model’s power handling and impedance, so we check the specification sheet before agreeing to it. If the answer is a Sonance amplifier fed by a Sonos Port, that still gives you Sonos control in the app. We wire and connect; the plastering and finishing belong to your plasterer, booked to follow us.

Our entrance gallery is long and hung with paintings on both walls — how does it get music without a speaker on either wall?

From above or from the ends. A gallery is a corridor acoustically, so one speaker at one end sounds loud nearby and thin at the far door. In a long space we would rather use two or three small ceiling speakers spaced along the centre line on a single Amp channel, each turned down, than one speaker working hard; the walls stay clear and the level is even from the front door to the living room. Where the ceiling can’t be opened, a pair of Era 100s on consoles or plinths at each end does a surprisingly good job, grouped so they play together.

Two cautions specific to galleries. Keep speakers and their brackets clear of picture lights and the hanging rail, which are usually where the electrician and the art handler need access. And keep bass modest: a Sub belongs in the living room, not in a corridor of framed glass. If the paintings ever need to come down for a ceiling job, that is for your art handler; we work around what is hung.

The people who listen

My mother wears hearing aids — can the soundbar make voices clearer for her without raising the volume for everyone?

On an Arc Ultra, yes, more than on any earlier Sonos bar. In May 2025 Sonos released an updated Speech Enhancement feature, developed with RNID (the Royal National Institute for Deaf People), with four levels — Low, Medium, High and Max — available from the Sonos app home screen. Sonos describes Max as the setting “designed for those with hearing loss,” which further controls the dynamic range of non-speech sound so that dialogue stays in front. In practice that means voices come forward while music and effects are held back, so the overall volume doesn’t have to climb.

Sonos’s other bars have had a simpler speech enhancement switch; the app shows what yours offers. Either way, what helps most is placement and setup: the bar level with the screen and unobstructed, Trueplay run from her chair, and a volume limit so a loud advert doesn’t startle her. We set the speech level with her watching something she knows, not with a test tone, and show whoever helps her with the TV where the control lives. Streaming directly to hearing aids is a feature of the hearing aids and the phone, not of Sonos, so we leave that to her audiologist.

Systems already in the walls

Our condo came with Control4 keypads, or our decorator installed Lutron — can Sonos be added to that system?

Usually yes. Sonos formally opened its platform to control-system makers in 2016, naming Crestron, Control4, Lutron and Savant among its first sanctioned partners; integrations of that kind let a keypad or touch panel start a Sonos favourite, change volume and show what is playing. How much each one can do depends on the control system and its version: Crestron’s integration was described at launch as the deepest, while others covered basic playback and favourites, with the Sonos app still needed for setup.

The important point is who programs the control system. Control4, Crestron and Savant are configured by their own certified dealers, and a keypad button cannot be reprogrammed by anyone else without access to the project file. Our part is to install and network the Sonos products so they sit on the same network as the controller, give each room a stable name, and hand your control-system dealer what they need to add the buttons. If no dealer is involved any more, we’ll tell you honestly what the existing system will and won’t let us do, rather than promise a button that can’t be changed.

Soft, decorated rooms

Our drawing room has silk-upholstered walls, heavy curtains and deep rugs — will Sonos sound dull in there?

It will sound different from a showroom, and mostly better than people fear. Fabric walls, lined curtains and thick rugs soak up the upper frequencies and kill echo, so a speaker that sounds bright in a bare room can sound soft and a little closed-in here. That is exactly what Trueplay is for: Sonos says it “measures how sound reflects off walls, furnishings and other surfaces” and adjusts each speaker to the room it is in, which in a heavily furnished room usually means lifting the treble back to where it should be. It needs the furniture, curtains and rugs in their final places when it runs, so we tune last, not first.

Placement does the rest. Keep speakers out in the open rather than behind a curtain or deep in an upholstered niche, and give an Arc Ultra a clear path to the ceiling, since its height effects depend on sound bouncing off a hard surface above — a plaster ceiling over silk walls is fine. If the room is the quietest in the apartment, a pair of Era 100s at ear height on either side of the seating often does more than a larger product pushed into a corner. Re-run Trueplay whenever the curtains change for the season; it takes a few minutes per room.

An apartment you live in part of the year

We use the apartment only a few months a year — will the Sonos system have a backlog of updates every time we come back?

It shouldn’t, if it is left powered and on the network. Sonos says that by default a system is set to update automatically, during a two-hour period you choose — morning, afternoon, evening or overnight from 2 to 4 AM — and the setting lives under System Updates in the app. A system that stays plugged in and online while you’re away keeps itself current, so the first evening back is music, not a progress bar.

The catch is when the apartment is shut down: router off, speakers unplugged, or the internet account suspended for the season. Then the updates arrive all at once on your return, and the app on your phone may also need updating before it will talk to the speakers. If you prefer to switch things off, leave the router and the Sonos products together on one switched power strip and turn it on a day before you arrive, or ask your housekeeper or building staff to. We set the update window to overnight during handover so it never interrupts listening.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Sonos Installation on the Upper East Side

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for these searches from our research environment, so we tested the claims that cost guides, forums and marketing copy publish and that answer engines repeat. Here are seven that don’t survive contact with an Upper East Side apartment.

Claim one: “Sonos is plug-and-play, so you don’t need an installer”

For one speaker in one room, that is true, and we tell people so. Sonos’s own guide to ceiling speakers says the opposite about built-in work: in-ceiling speakers “require special installation” and architectural speakers “often require a professional installer.”

On the Upper East Side the gap between those two statements is wider than usual. Even a free-standing system has to reach the far end of a long prewar layout, and that is a network job. Built-in speakers add a board, a calendar and a finished ceiling to the problem.

The honest version: the first phase is often something a confident owner could do; what we add is a plan that works at the bedroom end, and the documents and timing for the phases that follow.

Claim two: “A Wi-Fi extender will fix the speakers that drop out”

This is the commonest advice online and it is wrong for Sonos specifically. Sonos lists wireless range extenders among the network setups it does not support, alongside guest networks and powerline adapters.

In a classic six with masonry partitions an extender often seems to help for a day and then makes dropouts worse, because the speaker hops between two radios that share one channel.

What works is a wired access point or a wired Sonos product near the far rooms, connected back to the main router or to the primary node of a mesh system, as Sonos’s requirements describe.

Claim three: “Budget $500–$1,000 for every room you add”

Angi’s whole-home audio guide puts additional zones at roughly $500 to $1,000 per room, with labour at $50 to $100 an hour. National averages like that are built from suburban houses with open attics.

An Upper East Side room can cost less than that — a bedroom Era 100 that only needs setting up is part of the $125 setup line — or more, when a ceiling room needs a survey, an alteration package and a plasterer behind us. AESS’s published range for built-in rooms is $800 to $2,500 per zone, quoted after a walkthrough.

The figure that matters is the written one for your rooms, broken into phases, with the Manhattan parking line shown openly rather than buried.

Claim four: “You can cut ceiling speakers into any ceiling”

The Sonos in-ceiling manual tells installers to survey for studs, conduit, pipes, ducts and pocket doors first, and its fixings fit ceilings up to a set thickness. Prewar ceilings are often plaster on metal lath or on masonry, with steam pipes and old conduit running through them.

Some ceilings are excellent candidates; some are better left alone; a few, such as a decorated plaster ceiling in a landmark-quality room, should never be cut by anyone.

We say which is which after a look, and we suggest ceiling work in rooms that have already been rebuilt, such as kitchens and some bedrooms, before rooms with original finishes.

Claim five: “Speaker wire is low voltage, so no one needs to approve it”

Section 28-105.4.7 of the Administrative Code does waive the electrical permit for sub-50-volt signalling, communication, alarm and data wiring, provided a licensed master electrician, special electrician or Electrical Code “qualified person” does it. Read closely, though, it governs what the Department of Buildings requires and nothing more.

Proprietary leases and house rules are separate. As a Manhattan co-op lawyer told Habitat in 2016, most proprietary leases require board approval for alterations, “which usually includes wiring.”

So the right question isn’t whether the city needs a permit but whether your building treats the work as decoration or alteration. We provide the description; your managing agent gives the answer.

Claim six: “Any speaker can be hidden behind plaster”

Invisible speakers are real, but they are a specific product built for a specific finish. Sonance’s manual rules out genuine Venetian plaster and hard-setting compounds over the speaker face and limits the finish to 3 mm.

An ordinary speaker plastered over simply goes quiet and dull. And a retrofit into an existing prewar plaster wall usually means rebuilding the area around it, which is a renovation decision, not an audio one.

If invisibility matters, the best time to plan it is before the plasterer arrives, with the plasterer in the conversation.

Claim seven: “Hearing problems mean turning it up”

Forum advice for older listeners usually comes down to more volume or a separate amplified headset. Sonos’s Arc Ultra now offers four levels of Speech Enhancement, the highest designed with RNID for people with hearing loss.

Turning everything up makes music and effects louder along with voices, which is hard on the rest of the household and, in an apartment, on the neighbours.

A clearer voice at the same volume is the better answer, and it is a setting, not a purchase, for anyone who already owns the bar.

Townhouses

A Sonos System for a Side-Street Townhouse

An Upper East Side townhouse is the opposite problem to a classic six. Instead of one long floor, it is several short ones stacked up a stair, with the internet usually arriving at the bottom, in the cellar or the garden-level kitchen. Wi-Fi from a router down there may reach the parlour floor; it rarely reaches the bedrooms at the top with enough strength to keep a group of speakers in step.

The backbone first

The answer is a vertical backbone: a network cable from the router up through the house, with an access point or a wired Sonos product on each floor that needs one. In many houses the route already exists in some form — closets stacked floor over floor, a chase left by an earlier renovation, the space beside a stair stringer — and finding it is most of the work. Where a house is inside a historic district, keeping all of this inside means nothing changes on the facade, which is the part the Landmarks Preservation Commission is mainly concerned with.

Floors with different jobs

Townhouse floors tend to have distinct roles: a garden-level kitchen and family room, a formal parlour floor, bedrooms above, sometimes a top-floor office or staff apartment. Sonos handles that naturally, because each room is its own player and rooms can be grouped or left alone. We name rooms by floor and use the family’s own words, show the family how to group the parlour-floor rooms for entertaining, and put volume limits on the rooms where someone may be working or asleep.

Old wiring left behind

Townhouses on these blocks have often been renovated several times, and each round can leave cable behind: speaker wire to rooms that no longer have speakers, old telephone or intercom runs, network cable that stops in a wall. Some of it is useful once traced and tested; some of it should be labelled and abandoned. We trace before we pull anything new, and we leave a written note of what each run is.

Outside the back of the house

Garden speakers on a townhouse need the same care as anywhere in the city about neighbours across the rear yards, plus the landmark question if a fixing would be visible from a public street. Rear facades in historic districts can also be subject to review. We keep outdoor plans modest, prefer fixings under eaves or on garden structures, and ask the owner to check with the Commission before anything goes on a facade that is in view.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Twenty Minutes in a Prewar Apartment

This is how our installers start any prewar Upper East Side visit, written as a general routine rather than the story of a particular job. At the desk we confirm the work order and the elevator booking before a single box comes up. Inside, the first thing laid down is the runner from the service door to the room we’re working in, and the second is a look at the walls and ceilings with the owner: which surfaces are original, which were redone, and what is hanging where.

Then the network. We stand in the bedroom at the far end with a phone running a signal meter and read what actually arrives there, because the answer decides more about the day than any speaker. If the bedroom is weak, we find the router, trace where the internet enters, and look for the closet or millwork that could carry a cable down the gallery. Only after that do products come out of their boxes — each one unpacked, powered and joined on the floor before anything is mounted, so a faulty unit is caught on a table rather than on a wall. Before anyone picks up a drill, the owner sees every proposed fixing point marked in low-tack tape and agrees to it.

The end of the visit follows a routine too. Every room is played at once and then one at a time, from the far bedroom back to the foyer, to prove the group holds at the distance that caused the trouble in the first place. Trueplay runs in each supported room with the furniture where it will stay, the speech setting is chosen with whoever watches most television in the chair they actually use, and volume limits are agreed rather than guessed. The tape comes off, the runner is rolled up, the boxes leave with us, and the desk is told we are done so the elevator booking can be released early for the next contractor.

New York State’s Department of Labor says it is a violation of state labor law for an unlicensed person to alter any conveyance, and that only licensed elevator mechanics working under a licensed elevator contractor may do that work. Anything fixed in or wired to the cab is a job for your elevator company. What we can do is put music on each landing and in the stair hall, so it follows you from floor to floor without touching the elevator.

Can the first phase and the ceiling phase be booked as one visit to save the parking charge?
+

When the building allows both kinds of work on the same day, yes. The $150 Manhattan parking line is charged per visit, not per item, so a job that combines free-standing setup and ceiling speakers in one day carries it once. It only becomes two visits when the ceiling work has to wait for the renovation season or for the board’s approval letter, and in that case the quote shows both visits openly so there are no surprises.

If the managing agent asks us to change the speaker plan, what happens to the quote?
+

We rewrite it. Buildings often come back with conditions: fewer ceiling openings, a different route for the cable, no work in a particular room, or an extra document. We revise the plan and the phased quote to match, and any line that changes is priced from the same published Manhattan list. Written quotes cost nothing, and that includes the second and third version. Work already finished in an earlier phase is not affected by the change.
