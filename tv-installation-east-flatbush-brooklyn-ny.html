The Neighborhood

Why East Flatbush Properties Need TV Installation

East Flatbush is one of the largest residential neighborhoods in Brooklyn and, by the numbers, one of the most uniform in age. Brooklyn Community District 17, which Wikipedia describes as consisting entirely of East Flatbush and its subsections, runs from Empire Boulevard and East New York Avenue in the north down toward the Bay Ridge branch of the Long Island Rail Road, and from the New York and Brooklyn Avenue edge across to East 98th Street. Inside those lines, the city's PLUTO records list about 16,998 residential lots holding 62,077 homes, and the median lot was built in 1930. Nearly 7,000 of those lots are two-family houses and about 5,500 are one-family homes, which together make up most of the ground. For a TV installer, those two facts, the age and the house type, decide more about the job than the brand of the TV ever will. A house that went up around 1930 was not built with drywall, and the wall behind the screen almost always tells that story the moment a bit goes in.

CityNeighborhoods sums up the housing as solid brick rowhouses, freestanding two-family homes and small apartment buildings, many from 1910 to 1940, with long rows of brick houses on blocks such as East 51st Street, Schenectady Avenue and Remsen Avenue. Developers carved the old farm estates into narrow lots, and the result is a living room that is often long and not very wide, with the best TV wall being either the party wall shared with the next house or an interior partition with the stair behind it. Those two walls behave completely differently. The party wall is masonry under its plaster and will hold a heavy screen for decades once the right anchor is set in sound brick. The partition is usually wood framing with plaster on lath, where a screw has to find the framing or use an anchor rated for a hollow plaster wall. A detached two-family adds a third option, an exterior wall that may be brick or may be framed with siding outside, and the only honest way to know is to scan it and drill one small test hole where the bracket will hide it.

Then there are the big complexes, which hold a large share of East Flatbush renters on a small share of its lots. Flatbush Gardens, the former Vanderveer Estates, is 59 six-story brick buildings with 2,469 apartments on 30 acres between Newkirk, Nostrand, Farragut and Brooklyn Avenues, built in 1949 on the site of the old Flatbush Water Works. Rutland Plaza on East 93rd Street is five buildings with 428 units, and Rutland Towers at 955 Rutland Road moved from NYCHA's direct management to a PACT partner in November 2023. Across the district, PLUTO counts 133 elevator-building lots holding about 10,200 apartments. Those buildings bring different questions from a house. Who has to be told before a contractor arrives? Is the elevator working? News 12 reported in February 2026 that Rutland Plaza tenants had gone weeks or months without elevators, which turns a 65-inch TV in its box into a nine-floor carry. And what are the walls? Postwar construction of that era tends to mean masonry outside and dense plaster or block inside, where a stud finder is useless and a masonry bit is the only tool that works.

The people matter as much as the plaster. Wikipedia counts 135,619 residents in the 2010 census and describes East Flatbush as the single largest West Indian neighborhood in New York City, with 51 percent of residents born abroad. The Languages of New York City map lists Haitian Creole, French, Jamaican Patwa, Guyanese Creole and the creoles of Barbados, St. Lucia, Grenada, Trinidad and St. Vincent among the languages spoken here. In practical terms that means a lot of multigenerational homes where the TV in the front room is shared by a grandparent who wants the news, a parent who wants the match and children who want a game console, sometimes with menus that are easier to read in French than in English. It also means a lot of households where someone is on a shift at Kings County Hospital, SUNY Downstate or one of the clinics around Clarkson Avenue. We ask about both when we book, because they change where the TV should go, how the remote should be set up and what time of day the drilling should happen.

The street calendar matters too. Empire Boulevard, the neighborhood's northern edge, is part of the J'Ouvert route on Labor Day morning; NY1 reported that in 2026 Empire Boulevard was closed from Flatbush Avenue to Nostrand Avenue from 11:30 p.m. on Sunday until 11 a.m. on Monday, September 7, and East New York Avenue was among the affected streets. Church Avenue, co-named Bob Marley Boulevard from Remsen Avenue to East 98th Street, and Utica Avenue are the two commercial spines, and both are busy enough that a van with a large TV box needs a plan for where to stop. None of this makes a TV job difficult. It simply means the job is planned around the building, the household and the block before anyone talks about which bracket to buy.

Same-Day Repair in East Flatbush

Need Repair in East Flatbush? A TV Sagging Off Old Plaster, Anchors Spinning in Brick Dust, or a Bracket Left Behind by the Last Tenant

The calls that cannot wait usually come in one of three shapes. The first is a screen that has dropped at one corner or started to tilt forward. In a 1920s or 1930s East Flatbush house, the cause is almost always a fixing that was right for drywall and wrong for plaster: a plastic plug or a small spring toggle that gripped the hard skin of the plaster but nothing behind it. Every time someone pulled the screen out on its arm, or a child hung on it, the plaster around the plug crushed a little more. The second shape is a bracket on a brick party wall that was screwed into the soft mortar joints rather than into the brick itself, and has begun to pull away as the mortar powders. The third is a leftover: an empty bracket from a tenant who moved out, four holes that do not line up with any bracket you own, and a power cord poking out of a hole where somebody fished it through the wall.

If a TV is tipping, do not push it back up and do not tighten the screws. Unplug it, and if two adults can lift it safely, take its weight off the bracket and lay it face up on a bed or a padded surface. Then call or text (347) 934-8335 with a photo of the bracket and the wall around it. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. When the same TV goes back up on a properly anchored bracket in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall type, so a 50-inch screen on plaster comes to $179.99 plus the $45 plaster line, with the $85 take-down added. If the wall itself has failed, a crack running across the plaster or a brick that has come loose, we will say so plainly rather than hang a TV on it, and you can bring in the landlord or a mason before we come back.

One more urgent case is specific to older houses: a TV power cord that somebody ran inside the wall to hide it. It may have worked for years, but the electrical code does not allow a flexible cord to be concealed in a wall, and an old cord pinched behind plaster is the kind of thing worth fixing before it fails. We replace it with an in-wall power and cable kit ($169), which puts a proper rated outlet behind the screen, or with a paintable cord cover ($59) on the surface if the wall cannot be opened.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for East Flatbush's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for almost every TV call we take in East Flatbush. They differ in three ways that matter more than any bracket: what the wall behind the screen is made of, how far and how high the TV has to travel from the curb, and who besides you gets a say in what gets drilled.

The attached brick row house

Two-story brick rows on blocks like East 51st Street, Schenectady Avenue and Remsen Avenue, with plaster on brick along the party walls and a long, narrow front room.

The detached two-family

The commonest lot in the district: two stacked apartments, often with a driveway, an owner on one floor, a tenant on the other and walls that can be brick, framing or both.

The six-family walk-up

Small brick apartment houses on the avenues and side streets, stairs only, with narrow turns at every landing and a mix of plaster partitions and masonry outer walls.

The postwar complex

Flatbush Gardens' 59 six-story buildings from 1949, Rutland Plaza's five buildings on East 93rd Street, and similar complexes with a management office and dense masonry walls.

The landmarked limestone row

The 56 houses of the East 25th Street Historic District, built 1909 to 1912 with rounded or angled bays, plaster interiors and front rooms shaped by the bay.

The new supportive or affordable building

Recent and planned buildings such as Sparrow Square at 681 Clarkson Avenue, an all-electric Passive House design, where drywall on metal framing and a property manager set the rules.

The attached brick row house: the party wall is the TV's best friend

In a typical East Flatbush row house the front room runs the full width of a narrow house, and the deepest wall is the one you share with the neighbor. That party wall is usually solid brick with plaster laid over it, sometimes straight onto the brick and sometimes onto furring strips with a narrow gap behind. Solid brick is the strongest wall in Brooklyn for a TV, and a properly anchored bracket on it will outlast the TV, the soundbar and probably the people who bought them. The catch is that brick has to be drilled with a hammer drill and carbide-tipped masonry bits, the anchors have to sit in the brick and not in the mortar between bricks, and plaster over brick tends to chip at the surface if the bit is pushed too hard. That is why the brick line on our list is $65 rather than the $45 for plaster on framing. It covers the slower drilling, the masonry anchors and the extra care at the plaster face.

The other wall in the front room is usually a partition, with the stair or a hallway behind it. That one is framed in wood, plastered on lath, and hollow. It can carry a TV well, but only if the fixings land in the framing or the anchor is rated for hollow plaster and the weight of the screen. We find out which wall is which before we choose a bracket: a scanner pass, a knock test and then one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it. The dust tells the story. Red powder means brick, white or grey means plaster, grey grit means concrete or block, and a bit that suddenly drops forward into nothing means lath with a cavity behind it.

The detached two-family: two households, one set of walls, and an owner who often lives downstairs

With nearly 7,000 two-family lots in the district, this is the house we see most. Many are freestanding brick or framed houses with a driveway down one side, a basement where the utilities come in, and two full apartments stacked above it. The upstairs living room often sits directly above the downstairs one, and the walls are frequently aligned, so a hammer drill on the second floor is heard plainly on the first. If you rent the upper unit and the owner lives below, a short text to the owner before the visit, naming the wall, roughly how many holes and whether any wire will go inside the wall, goes a long way. Owners who are asked first rarely object. Owners who learn about it from the noise sometimes do.

If you own the house, the TV question looks different. A bracket you choose and we install on a wall you pick, with the power and cable hidden in an in-wall kit, is a clean way to give the rental unit a TV wall that every tenant can use without anyone drilling new holes. It also means the cable from the provider can come up from the basement once, to one place, rather than in a new route for every tenant. The exterior walls of a detached two-family deserve one extra check: some are brick, some are framed with siding outside, and a few have been re-sided over older brick, so the pilot hole is not optional on an outside wall.

The six-family walk-up: the stairs come first

The small brick apartment houses of East Flatbush, the ones with four to six families and a single stair, are where the carry becomes part of the job. A 65-inch TV in its box is a little over five feet long and has to be kept upright, and the turn at each landing in an older walk-up is tight. We carry it boxed to the door of the apartment, unbox it inside on a blanket and keep the packaging until the screen is on the wall, so that if the wall turns out to need a different plan the TV is never left standing unprotected. If you are buying a TV for a walk-up, measure the stair turn before you order the biggest size the store has. It is the most common reason a screen ends up back in its box.

Inside, walk-up walls are mixed. The wall shared with the hallway or the next apartment is often masonry, the walls between rooms are often plaster on lath, and over the decades many apartments have had some walls covered in drywall during a renovation. That last case, drywall laid over old plaster, means longer fixings and a careful check of what the screw is actually biting into. It costs nothing to find out from your photos which case you have, and the quote reflects the wall we find, not the wall we hoped for.

The postwar complex: a building office, an elevator and a hard wall

Flatbush Gardens and Rutland Plaza are what most people picture when they think of East Flatbush apartment life, and they come with their own rules. Many large complexes ask residents to tell the management office before a contractor arrives, some want proof of insurance, and some restrict noisy work to certain hours. Ask your office before you book; if they want something in writing from us, tell us what and to whom. On the day, we check the elevator before we load the van, and if it is out we plan the carry by stairs rather than leaving the TV in the lobby. Tenants in Rutland Plaza told News 12 in 2026 about elevators out for weeks, so this is not a theoretical problem here.

The walls in buildings of this era are usually the hardest in the neighborhood. Expect masonry around the outside of the building and dense plaster or block on many interior walls; a stud finder gives nothing useful and a regular drill makes no headway. We bring a hammer drill and masonry bits to every complex apartment for that reason, and we use anchors rated for the specific wall we find. Brick is the $65 line on our list; block or poured concrete is quoted from your photos, because it varies more from building to building. If the wall the TV should go on is an outside wall with a radiator or a window air conditioner below it, we plan the height so the screen clears the heat and the bracket stays out of the way of both.

The landmarked limestone row: bays, plaster and a facade nobody touches

The 56 houses on East 25th Street between Clarendon Road and Avenue D, designated in 2020 as East Flatbush's first historic district, were built from 1909 to 1912 in the Renaissance Revival style with limestone or brownstone fronts and rounded or angled bays. The bay shapes the front room. It takes up much of the street wall, so the TV usually goes on a side wall, and the viewing distance across a narrow parlor is often shorter than people expect. That can argue for a slightly smaller screen or a tilting bracket rather than the largest TV that would physically fit.

The designation is about what the houses look like from the street. A bracket on an interior wall of your living room is interior work. What does call for a separate conversation is anything on the outside: a satellite dish, an antenna, or a new cable run on the facade from the provider. If a TV project involves any of those, ask the Landmarks Preservation Commission first. The plaster inside these houses is more than a century old, so we drill it with sharp bits at low speed and keep the bracket's fixings well away from any cornice or picture molding.

The new supportive or affordable building: light walls and a property manager

East Flatbush is also getting new buildings. 6sqft reported in June 2026 on Sparrow Square at 681 Clarkson Avenue, on part of the former Kingsboro Psychiatric Center campus: two 10-story, all-electric Passive House buildings with 261 apartments including 117 supportive homes, slated for completion in early 2029, alongside Utica Crescent, with 322 units including 89 for elderly residents with on-site services. Buildings like these, and the smaller new rentals that have gone up around the neighborhood, are more likely to have drywall over metal framing on interior walls. Metal studs look like wood on the surface and behave very differently under a screw, so we confirm the framing and use fasteners designed to hold in it.

In any new building with a property manager or on-site services, ask the office what it allows before booking. Some ask for the work to be limited to certain walls; some prefer surface cord covers to opening the wall; airtight construction makes some managers careful about any hole in an outside wall. We would much rather know that before the visit than discover it on the day.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in an East Flatbush Front Room Before the Drill Comes Out

This is the routine our technicians follow in a house of East Flatbush's age, written from the installer's side of the job. The first thing we look at is not the wall but the floor. Old Brooklyn floors settle, and a front room in a 1930s row house can be a quarter of an inch lower at one end than the other. Leveling a TV to the floor or the ceiling line would make it look crooked against the room, so we level to true and then step back to the sofa to make sure it reads straight to your eye.

Next comes the knock. On a party wall, a hard, dull knock that hurts the knuckles is brick under plaster. On a partition, a softer, slightly hollow knock with harder spots every foot or so is plaster on lath over framing. Then the scanner, which in an old house is good for finding pipes, cables and metal lath and not much else. Finally the pilot hole, small, placed where the bracket plate will hide it, and watched closely for the color of the dust and the feel of the bit. Only after that do we commit to an anchor.

Before any full-size hole, painter's tape goes on the wall in the outline of the screen and you sit where you actually watch. In a narrow front room that often moves the TV down a few inches from where people first imagine it. We also check what is under the window and along the baseboard, because radiators, window air conditioners and old capped pipes are common in East Flatbush houses, and they decide how high the screen sits and where the cables can run. If anything about the wall surprises us, we stop and tell you before drilling, with the price difference if there is one.

Wall by Wall

Brick, Plaster, Block or Drywall: What Holds a TV in an East Flatbush Wall and What Each One Adds

Drywall on wood studs, the starting price. The $179.99 line on our Brooklyn list assumes the easiest wall there is: drywall over wood studs, with a screen up to 55 inches on a bracket you supply or buy from us. In East Flatbush you mostly meet it in renovated rooms, finished basements and newer buildings. The lag bolts go into the center of two studs, the bracket is leveled, and the job moves quickly. Even here we scan for the studs and for anything inside the wall, because a renovated room in an old house can hide a pipe or a cable where you would not expect one.

Plaster, plus $45. Plaster is the wall we expect first in a house from the 1910s to the 1940s. It may be on wood lath, on metal lath or straight onto brick, and each holds differently. On lath over framing, we find the framing and fix into it, or where the framing is in the wrong place for your screen, we use anchors rated for a hollow plaster wall and the weight of the TV. Plaster is hard on the face and brittle underneath, so it is drilled with a sharp bit, at low speed, without hammer action until the bit is through the skin. That is what the $45 covers: the time, the bits and the anchors that keep the plaster in one piece.

Brick or stone, plus $65. The party walls of East Flatbush row houses and the outer walls of many two-families and walk-ups are solid brick, usually with plaster over it. A hammer drill and carbide masonry bits go through it, and the anchors are set in the brick itself, never in the mortar joints, which in a century-old wall can be soft enough to scratch with a key. A bracket in sound brick is the strongest TV mount you can have, and it is the wall we would choose for a large or heavy screen whenever the room allows.

Concrete, block and anything unusual, quoted first. Some walls in postwar complexes and some basement walls are concrete block or poured concrete. Hollow block needs a different anchor from solid block, and poured concrete needs a different bit again. Because the range is wider, we price these from your photos and the TV's model number before booking, so you know the number before the visit rather than on the day.

Above a mantel, plus $130. Many East Flatbush row houses and two-families have a fireplace or a decorative mantel in the front room, often long since closed. A TV above it sits high, so we talk through the viewing angle before drilling, and the chimney breast itself is usually brick, so the brick line normally applies as well. The $130 covers the extra height, the ladder work and the care needed around the mantel and any heat source below.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

Almost everyone wants the wires gone, and this is where a lot of online advice goes wrong. The National Electrical Code does not allow a flexible cord, which is what a TV's power cord is, to be run through a hole in a wall or concealed inside a wall. Electricians on the Mike Holt forum say it bluntly when the question comes up, and EC&M's code column makes the same point. The proper way to hide power is to put a rated outlet behind the TV. That is what an in-wall power and cable kit does: a recessed box behind the screen and a second one near the floor, joined inside the wall by in-wall-rated power cable, with a separate path for HDMI and coax. On our list it is $169.

Not every wall can take one. Solid brick cannot be fished like a hollow wall, and opening a party wall for a chase is a much bigger job than most people want. Many leases do not allow tenants to open walls at all. In those cases the honest answer is a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, which runs on the surface from the TV down to the outlet or the console, can be painted to match the wall and comes off cleanly when you move. Hoppler, a New York design-build firm, notes that mounting a TV by itself does not usually need a Department of Buildings permit, but opening a wall for an AV chase or moving electrical circuits does. We do not move circuits or add new ones; an in-wall kit uses the existing outlet below it, and where a job needs a new circuit, that is work for a licensed electrician.

Renters and Owners

Leases, Landlords and Building Offices: Mounting a TV When the Wall Belongs to Someone Else

Most East Flatbush households rent, whether from the owner downstairs in a two-family, from a small landlord with one walk-up, or from the management company of a complex with thousands of apartments. That makes the lease the first document in any TV job. Brick Underground, the New York real estate site, put the question to landlords and lawyers in 2022 and found that landlords can restrict hanging things on walls through the alteration clause in the lease. Arik Lifshitz of DSA Property Group told the site that "a nail or screw-sized hole from a picture frame or shelving system would fall within wear and tear." Attorney Justin C. Brasch drew the other line, warning that "careless drilling of the wall or drilling to hold heavy hangings" can be charged to the tenant. A TV bracket is a heavy hanging. Whether it costs you anything when you leave depends on what the lease says, whether you asked, and how carefully the holes were made and repaired.

The practical answer is simple. Before you book, read the alterations clause, and if it is vague or says no without consent, ask the landlord or managing agent in writing. Say which wall, what size TV, how many holes and whether any wire will go inside the wall. Keep the reply. In a two-family where the owner lives downstairs, that may be a text; in a large complex it may be a form from the office. If the answer is no, a TV on its stand ($119.99 on our list) with a soundbar placed in front of it ($79) involves no holes at all, and there is nothing to argue about at move-out.

When you do move out, the same source explains that New York landlords have 14 days to return a security deposit with an itemized list of any deductions. If the lease or the landlord wants the wall restored, the bracket comes off, the holes are filled and the patch is painted. We take photos of the wall before we start and after we finish, which helps if anyone ever asks what the wall looked like.

Ownership changes add their own wrinkle. News 12 reported in April 2026 on a four-story Linden Boulevard walk-up where tenants were unsure who their landlord even was after the owner died, until a new owner registered in December 2025. If you are in a building like that, the building's registration with HPD lists the owner and managing agent, and that is the address to write to for consent. In NYCHA buildings and in developments converted to PACT management, such as Rutland Towers on Rutland Road, the development's own rules apply. NYCHA's house rules, for example, say residents may only install TV antennas inside their apartments, and a PACT partner's property manager is the person who answers requests about walls. Ask them first; we will work to whatever they say.

Owners have the opposite question: what to allow. If you own a two-family or a small walk-up, letting each tenant choose their own wall and their own installer is how a rental ends up with four sets of holes in four places. Choosing one TV wall per unit, having a bracket and an in-wall kit installed once, and writing it into the lease as a fixture is cleaner for everyone. It also gives the next tenant a TV wall that works on day one.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in East Flatbush

Ask an AI assistant or a search summary how to mount a TV and you get advice written for a typical American house built in the last forty years. East Flatbush is not that house. Here are seven claims that come up often, and what they miss on these blocks.

"Locate two studs 16 inches apart and screw the bracket into them"

That instruction assumes drywall on a wood frame. In Community District 17 the median building dates from 1930, and the commonest TV wall in a row house is a brick party wall with plaster on it. There are no studs in a brick wall to find.

A stud finder on old plaster tends to beep everywhere or nowhere, because it is reading the density of the plaster, the lath and the metal lath rather than any framing. People who trust it end up drilling a line of holes looking for something that is not there.

The reliable method is a knock test, a scanner used to rule out pipes and cables, and one small pilot hole hidden behind the bracket plate. The dust tells you the wall, and the anchor follows from that.

"Mounting a TV costs about $255"

HomeAdvisor's national range is $155 to $350 with an average of $255, and Angi's Brooklyn figure averages $280. Those numbers are real, but they describe the average job, and the average job is drywall.

Bob Vila, summarizing Angi and HomeAdvisor data, puts brick, concrete or stone at $200 to $800 and a mount above a fireplace at $175 to $400. In East Flatbush, where brick and plaster are the norm, the wall line is the part of the quote that matters most.

Our list makes that visible: $179.99 for the base mount, plus $65 for brick or $45 for plaster, plus $130 above a mantel. You see each line before you book, and the total does not change on the day unless the wall turns out to be something different, in which case we tell you before drilling.

"Hide the power cord by running it inside the wall"

This is the most common bad advice on the subject. The National Electrical Code does not allow a flexible cord, which is what a TV's power cord is, to be concealed in a wall or run through a hole in one. Gizmodo pointed this out years ago, and electricians repeat it on trade forums whenever the question comes up.

A cord inside a wall can be pinched, overheated or chewed, and nobody can see it when it starts to fail. In a century-old East Flatbush house with old wiring already in the walls, that is not a risk worth adding.

The code-compliant way is an in-wall power kit, which puts a rated outlet behind the TV fed by in-wall-rated cable, at $169 on our list. Where the wall is solid brick or the lease forbids opening it, a paintable cord cover at $59 is the honest alternative.

"A handyman by the hour is always cheaper"

TaskRabbit lists Brooklyn TV mounting from $45 an hour, and some Taskers set a two-hour minimum. On a drywall job that can work out cheaply. On a brick party wall, the hours depend on whether the person arrives with a hammer drill and masonry anchors.

A Brooklyn customer's Thumbtack review describes an installer who "had to drill four extra holes in the dry wall the size of a quarter." On drywall that is an annoyance; on century-old plaster, extra holes can crack the wall around the bracket, and in a rental they can come out of a deposit.

A fixed price from a list, with the wall type agreed in advance, removes both the clock and the guesswork. HomeAdvisor's own advice is to hire a TV mounting specialist rather than a general handyperson for exactly this reason.

"Renters should never mount a TV, or they will lose the deposit"

The real picture is more specific. Brick Underground found that the lease's alteration clause is what decides it, and that small holes are generally treated as wear and tear while drilling for heavy hangings can be charged to the tenant.

That means the answer is to ask. A written yes from the landlord, a mount done carefully on the agreed wall and a clean patch at move-out are what protect a deposit. New York landlords have 14 days to return it with an itemized list of any deductions.

If the landlord says no, a TV on its stand at $119.99 with a placed soundbar at $79 gives most of the benefit with no holes at all. Renting in East Flatbush does not mean living with a TV on the floor.

"You can schedule an installer for any day that suits you"

In a house that is mostly true. In a complex it depends on the building. Large buildings may ask to be told before contractors arrive, and some limit noisy work to certain hours.

Elevators matter too. News 12 reported Rutland Plaza tenants waiting weeks or months for working elevators in 2026, and a 65-inch TV on the ninth floor is a very different job by stairs. We confirm the elevator the day before and plan a stair carry if needed.

And the street has its own calendar. J'Ouvert closes Empire Boulevard early on Labor Day Monday, and Church and Utica Avenues are busy enough that we plan where the van stops before we arrive.

"Mounting a TV in New York City needs a permit"

Usually it does not. Hoppler, a New York design-build firm, notes that mounting a TV by itself does not normally require a Department of Buildings permit.

What changes that is the scope. Opening a wall to build a recessed media chase, or moving or adding electrical circuits, is permit work, and new circuits belong to a licensed electrician. We do neither on a TV visit.

The approvals that do come up in East Flatbush are a landlord's consent, a building office's rules, a PACT or NYCHA development's house rules, and, on the 56 landmarked houses of East 25th Street, the Landmarks Preservation Commission for anything on the facade. A bracket on the inside wall is none of those.
