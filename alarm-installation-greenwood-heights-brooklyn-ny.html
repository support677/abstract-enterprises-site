Greenwood Heights is a neighborhood of edges. Its blocks of old wood-framed row houses run up the hill from Third Avenue and stop, one after another, at something that empties out after dark: the iron fence of Green-Wood Cemetery, the sunken Prospect Expressway and the little fenced parks along it, the warehouse and garage lots west of Fourth Avenue, or a row of storefronts that faces 478 acres of graves instead of apartments. A burglar alarm here is easier to get right if it is planned from the back fence inward. This page explains how we do that, and how a wood-framed house changes the way sensors go in.

The Neighborhood

Why Greenwood Heights Properties Need Alarm Installation

The name was a real-estate invention, borrowed from the cemetery next door. According to Wikipedia, the blocks between the Prospect Expressway and 39th Street were once simply part of South Brooklyn, were folded into Sunset Park after the area was designated a poverty area in 1966, and were being called Greenwood Heights again by the late 1980s. Its borders are, in Wikipedia’s words, “much-debated”: roughly the expressway on the north, the Gowanus Canal and the bay on the west, Eighth Avenue on the east and 39th Street on the south, where Green-Wood Cemetery and a subway yard meet. It shares Community District 7 with Windsor Terrace, Sunset Park and the South Slope, and its ZIP code is 11232. What matters for an alarm is less the name than the shape. Almost every Greenwood Heights block ends against something large and quiet.

The biggest of those things is Green-Wood itself: 478 acres, founded in 1838, a National Historic Landmark since 2006, with about 570,000 people buried there. An elaborate iron fence runs around the whole cemetery, and its main entrance, the Gothic arch at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street, sits at the neighborhood’s doorstep. Along Fifth Avenue between 24th and 34th Streets the cemetery is on one side of the street and Greenwood Heights houses and shops are on the other, as the Brooklyn Eagle described in a 2020 walk around its perimeter. North of 30th Street, two small 1922 private courts, Roosevelt Court and Woodrow Court, have back yards that face the cemetery; Forgotten New York wrote that the houses seem “turned away from the cemetery as if fearing it.” The cemetery is a beautiful neighbor and a peaceful one. It is also, at night, a neighbor with nobody in it.

The northern edge is a trench. The Prospect Expressway, which Robert Moses first envisioned in the 1940s and which was built as a sunken highway in the 1950s, cuts across the top of the neighborhood. Leftover land beside it became fifteen small parks, just under seven acres in all, and Untapped Cities noted in 2016 that “a chain link fence is usually all that separates the parks from neighboring homes’ backyards.” Those parks are used all day by dog walkers and neighbors and are mostly empty late at night. The western edge is industrial. The Department of City Planning’s 2005 rezoning summary describes large lots along Fourth Avenue holding one-story warehouses and commercial buildings, and west of Fourth Avenue the blocks still mix houses with garages, workshops and the edge of Industry City’s six million square feet of lofts. In September 2026 New York YIMBY reported permits for a three-story manufacturing building with four parking spaces at 156 21st Street, between Third and Fourth Avenues, on the site of a three-story residential building slated for demolition. A house on a block like that can end up with a warehouse wall beside it that is busy on weekdays and silent from Friday night to Monday.

And then there are the houses themselves. Wikipedia describes the neighborhood’s “architectural mix of wood frame, vinyl sided and brick homes,” and Brownstoner’s Suzanne Spellen wrote in 2014 that “Greenwood Heights is better known for its wood framed houses, both restored and covered with aluminum siding,” noting that wood-framed row houses dominated both sides of 18th Street between Fourth and Fifth Avenues on an 1888 map. Since the 2005 rezoning, owners have been gut-renovating some of those houses and carefully restoring others to their nineteenth-century look. A hollow frame wall lets hidden cable be fished through it, but a restored door is exactly what an owner does not want spoiled by a plastic sensor, and restoration leaves valuable material outside. A Brownstoner forum post from about 2015 described a house in the 20s between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, near the cemetery, whose stoop railings were taken from the front yard while the stoop was being rebuilt.

Burglaries here look much like burglaries anywhere in this part of Brooklyn: opportunistic, usually through a door or window that is out of sight. Just north of the expressway, the Brooklyn Eagle reported a store at Fourth Avenue and 17th Street broken into through its side door at about 2:10 a.m. in July 2019, and a home at 16th Street and Fourth Avenue burglarized at about 12:50 in the afternoon in September 2018. In August and September 2026 the NYPD looked for two men linked to nineteen commercial break-ins across sixteen Brooklyn neighborhoods, including neighboring Windsor Terrace and Park Slope, who got in by throwing something through a window. We do not claim Greenwood Heights is a high-crime neighborhood; the point is that a back or side door facing an empty edge gives a burglar time, and an alarm takes that time away.

Greenwood Heights at a glance

  • Green-Wood Cemetery: 478 acres, founded 1838, about 570,000 interments, surrounded by an iron fence; main gate at Fifth Avenue and 25th Street; Battle Hill, about 216 feet above sea level, is the highest point in Brooklyn (Wikipedia).
  • Housing: wood-framed row houses, restored or under aluminum and vinyl siding, beside brick and brownstone rows (Brownstoner, Wikipedia); the 2005 rezoning described the area just north of the cemetery as row houses of two to four stories built as worker housing (Department of City Planning).
  • Expressway parks: fifteen small parks along the sunken Prospect Expressway, just under seven acres in total, usually separated from back yards by a chain-link fence (Untapped Cities).
  • Industry City: between 32nd and 41st Streets west of Third Avenue, more than 650 tenants in about 6,000,000 square feet of early-twentieth-century warehouses and lofts (Wikipedia).
  • Police: the 72nd Precinct station is at 830 Fourth Avenue (Hoodline, citing the NYPD).

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Greenwood Heights

Each service is priced on its own line of the Brooklyn list. A house backing onto the cemetery or an expressway park usually takes the first three; a house being restored takes the fourth and fifth; a shop or workshop on the avenues takes the last three.

Whole-house alarms for frame and brick rows

Contacts on the street door, the yard door and any cellar entrance, motion detection on the main floors, glass-break where a rear window faces an empty edge, and a panel with cellular reporting and battery backup placed where a burglar coming through the back cannot reach it first.

Rear-boundary zones

Sensors on the back gate and yard door, outdoor detectors or beams set along the fence line, and the rule that decides what happens when they trip: chime only by day, alert by night, or call the monitoring station only if a second sensor confirms it.

Night-only and weekend schedules

Programming that arms yard sensors after dark and relaxes them in the morning, or that tightens the whole system on weekends when the warehouse or garage beside you is empty and nobody would notice noise through a shared wall.

Hidden contacts for restored houses

Recessed door and window contacts drilled into the frame where the wood allows it, so nothing shows on a restored door or sash, with cable fished through the hollow bays of a wood-framed wall instead of run along the trim.

Restoration-season protection

A temporary layer for the months when ironwork, salvaged doors, tools and building materials sit in the front yard or the cellar: tilt and shock sensors on stored pieces, an outdoor beam across the yard, and a camera view, removed when the work is done.

Apartment and condo alarms

Wireless, removable systems for rentals and for condos in the newer Fourth Avenue buildings, working beside the building’s own door entry without touching it, and coming out cleanly when you move.

Shop and restaurant alarms

Front, back and side-door contacts, roll-down gate and glass protection, motion inside, a panic button by the counter, opening and closing reports, and a siren set to stop on time, for stores on Fourth and Fifth Avenues.

Workshop, garage and unit alarms

Overhead-door contacts, heavy-duty contacts on steel doors, motion detectors high enough to ignore vehicles, and separate areas for an office and a bay, for the garages and small industrial units west of Fourth Avenue.

App control and optional monitoring

Arming, alerts and schedules on your phone carry no AESS monthly charge; a professional central station through our partners costs from $19.99 a month and can be switched on or off any month.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Greenwood Heights’ Architecture

The cards below describe the building types we meet most often in the neighborhood and what each one means for an alarm. The building descriptions come from the sources named on each card; the installation notes are our own practice.

Wood-framed row houses

The type the neighborhood is known for (Brownstoner). The walls are timber studs with hollow bays between them, so cable can usually be dropped from the attic or pulled up from the cellar inside a wall without opening it. Doors and frames are wood, which takes a recessed contact well. The weak points are usually the back: a yard door, a kitchen window and a cellar door under the rear stoop.

Frame houses under aluminum or vinyl siding

Many frame houses still wear the siding put on in the twentieth century (Wikipedia, Brownstoner). Behind it is the original clapboard and sheathing. We avoid cutting siding at all; when an outdoor siren or detector is needed it goes on a mounting block sealed into the siding, and cable enters from inside the house.

Brick and brownstone rows

Some blocks, such as 18th Street between Fourth and Fifth Avenues, mix brick and brownstone row houses in with frame ones (Brownstoner). Solid masonry walls do not let cable fall inside them, so a brick house leans toward wireless sensors on the upper floors and wiring only where floors or baseboards are already open.

The cemetery courts

Roosevelt Court and Woodrow Court, built in 1922 off Fifth Avenue north of 30th Street, face pedestrian paths in front and have fenced back yards toward Green-Wood (Forgotten New York, Brooklyn Eagle). Here the rear yard is the side nobody overlooks, and it is where we start.

Mixed buildings on Fifth Avenue

Three- and four-story buildings with stores on the ground floor and apartments above, many directly across from the cemetery (DCP, Brooklyn Eagle). The shop and the apartments need separate systems or separate areas, and the shop’s side and rear doors matter more than its glass front.

Newer buildings on Fourth Avenue and near the cemetery

The 2005 rezoning allowed larger apartment buildings with affordable housing on Fourth Avenue (DCP), and 11 townhouses at Seventh Avenue and 23rd Street were built under a 2006 agreement with the cemetery to keep the Battle Hill view open (Brooklyn Eagle). These are drywall-and-steel or new masonry buildings; wireless sensors and a small panel suit them best.

Garages, workshops and warehouse units

West of Fourth Avenue houses share blocks with one-story warehouses and garages (DCP, New York YIMBY). For the business inside, the alarm centers on overhead doors, steel man-doors and high motion detectors; for the house beside it, on the shared wall and the roof line.

How the materials change the method

  • Running cable in a frame wall. We find the stud bay that lines up with a door or window, drill a small hole through the top plate from the attic or the bottom plate from the cellar, and drop a flexible rod and cable inside the wall. The only marks are a hole the size of a pencil at the device and a cover plate where the cable meets the panel.
  • Wood that moves. Old wood doors and frames swell and shrink with the seasons. A recessed contact with a wider gap tolerance, or a surface contact on the hinge side where the movement is smallest, keeps the door from reporting open on a humid August night.
  • Siding. We never cut through aluminum or vinyl siding to hide cable. Exterior devices sit on a sealed mounting block, and the cable goes back through the wall from inside, so water has no new path behind the siding.
  • New construction. In newer Fourth Avenue buildings, the walls are usually metal studs and drywall and the building owns the corridor; everything we install stays inside the apartment or the store.

Backs to the Fence

Planning a Greenwood Heights Alarm From the Back Fence In

Most alarm plans start at the front door and work backward, because the front door is where people come and go and where the keypad usually lives. In Greenwood Heights we turn that around. The front of a house here faces a street with neighbors, parked cars and people walking to the R train. The back faces whatever the block ends against, and on a great many blocks that is something with nobody in it after dark. So the first question on a survey is not how many windows the house has. It is what lies beyond the rear fence, who can see the back of the house from there, and at what hours that changes.

We sort the answers into four kinds of edge. Each one leads to a slightly different alarm, and a house on a corner or at the end of a block can have two of them. The table below is our working practice, not a rule from any code or manufacturer; it is how we decide which sensors go on the back of the house, when they are armed, and who hears about it when they trip.

Schematic: the four edges in the table above. Outdoor zones (shaded) are worth considering only where the edge is empty.

Two ideas run through the whole table. The first is that sensors inside the house are far more reliable than sensors outside it. A door contact either opens or it does not; an outdoor detector has to decide whether something moving in the dark is a person, a raccoon, a branch or a plastic bag. Where the edge is empty, it is tempting to put detection out in the yard so the alarm reacts before anyone reaches the house. Sometimes that is right. More often, the better design is to make the house itself very hard to enter quietly and to use the yard sensors only as an early phone alert.

The second idea is that the right behavior changes with the clock. A yard sensor useful at three in the morning is a nuisance at three in the afternoon, so we use schedules, two-sensor confirmation and automatic bypass of a misbehaving sensor, and explain each at handover.

The Cemetery Side

Houses Facing or Backing Onto Green-Wood

Green-Wood is one of the reasons people choose this neighborhood. It is also an unusual neighbor for an alarm. Wikipedia describes an elaborate iron fence around the entire cemetery, and Forgotten New York called it “an incredibly lengthy fence running along its entire exterior.” Inside it there are hills, lakes, paths and thousands of trees. By day there are visitors, tour groups and staff. Once the gates are locked, there is nobody, and the houses that back onto it, or face it across Fifth Avenue, have a side that no one overlooks.

An iron fence is a boundary, not a barrier. The clearest public example is from the cemetery itself. In August 2012, as reported by Brooklyn Paper and the Brooklyn Eagle, someone damaged more than forty monuments, and surveillance video showed a man climbing the inside of the fence near Fifth Avenue and 25th Street at about seven in the morning, an hour before the grounds opened, and dropping onto the sidewalk. That was an act of vandalism inside the cemetery, not a burglary of a house, and we cite it only to make one point: the fence can be climbed in either direction, and it does not tell anyone when it is.

For a house on the cemetery side, that leads to a few practical decisions. The rear doors and ground-floor windows get contacts, always, because they are the openings nobody else will see being forced. Large rear panes get a glass-break detector, because a burglar with time and privacy may choose glass over a lock. An indoor motion detector covers the rear rooms so that anyone who gets in through an opening we did not expect is still caught. And the panel itself goes somewhere a person coming in from the back cannot reach in the entry delay, usually a front hall closet or an upstairs landing.

Outdoor detection along a cemetery fence is where we slow down. Trees overhang many of these yards, branches move in wind, and animals use the cemetery as a refuge. Wikipedia even records a colony of monk parakeets nesting in the spires of the main gate. An outdoor detector that sees all of that will trip often, and a system that trips often gets switched off. When an owner wants early warning in the yard, we use detectors with a narrow, downward field aimed along the inside of the fence rather than across it, set them to send a phone alert rather than sound the siren, and require a second sensor, usually a door or window contact or the indoor motion detector, before the monitoring station is called.

AESS practice, schematic: house sensors do most of the work; an optional outdoor detector aims along the fence, not across the yard.

The Expressway Side

Yards That Meet the Prospect Expressway Parks

Along the northern edge, the houses back onto a different kind of emptiness. The Prospect Expressway runs below street level in a trench, a “sunken six-lane freeway” in Wikipedia’s description, built in the 1950s and finished in the 1960s by these accounts. When it was built, the city was left with odd slivers of condemned land too awkward to build houses on, and those became a string of fifteen small parks, including a dog run and Detective Joseph Mayrose Park, the largest of the hidden South Slope parks, according to Untapped Cities. And between the parks and the back yards there is usually just a chain-link fence.

That edge behaves very differently from the cemetery. It is busy during the day, with dogs, children and neighbors who use the parks as a back garden. For an alarm, the result is that the yard side of these houses needs two personalities. During the day, nobody wants an alert every time someone walks a dog past the fence. At night, someone climbing that fence is exactly the event an alarm exists to report.

We handle that with a schedule. Sensors on the house itself, the doors and windows, behave the same way whenever the system is armed. Sensors that watch the yard are put in their own group, and that group arms itself at dusk and relaxes in the morning on a timetable you choose, with the option of overriding it from your phone. In summer we set the evening arming later than in winter. If the house also has a rear camera, its motion alerts follow the same schedule, so the phone stays quiet while the park is full.

The highway hum also masks sound, so on this edge we test each rear glass-break detector in place, windows closed, at the sensitivity we leave it on.

AESS practice, schematic. Black: door and window contacts follow the arm state at all hours. Red: the yard group, armed from dusk to morning, with a later evening start in summer. Times are examples set with each household, not fixed rules.

The Warehouse Side

Living Beside a Warehouse, Garage or Workshop West of Fourth Avenue

The third edge is the one that is changing fastest. West of Fourth Avenue, and on some lots along it, houses share blocks with garages, workshops, storage buildings and one-story warehouses. The 2005 rezoning summary from the Department of City Planning describes large Fourth Avenue lots with one-story warehouse and commercial buildings, and the September 2026 New York YIMBY report on 156 21st Street describes a three-story residential building between Third and Fourth Avenues due to be demolished for a 45-foot manufacturing building with enclosed parking. Neighbors like that keep business hours. On a weekday the building beside you is full of people and noise; from Friday evening to Monday morning it may be locked and dark.

For the house next door, that suggests a weekend profile. Most panels let you define more than one armed state. A weekday night state might cover the doors, the ground-floor windows and the rear rooms while the family sleeps upstairs. A weekend state, used when the building beside you is empty, can add the side windows facing the lot, a glass-break detector on the shared side and the cellar, without changing anything about how you come and go through the front door. Switching between them is one button or one tap, and the panel can remind you on Friday evening.

The shape of the neighbor matters as much as its hours. A one-story building beside a three-story house puts a flat roof at the height of your second-floor side windows. We look at the building beside you from your windows and your yard, and if its roof or wall makes one of your openings easier to reach, that opening gets a contact even if it sits on a floor we would normally leave to the motion detector. While a neighboring lot stands open during a demolition, we sometimes add a temporary sensor or two.

AESS practice, schematic: the weekend state adds the lot-side windows, a glass-break detector and the cellar.

Restoration Season

Protecting a Frame House While It Is Being Restored

Since the 2005 rezoning, Wikipedia notes, many Greenwood Heights owners have taken neglected wood-frame houses from around 1900 and restored them to their historical look, while others have gutted theirs. Either way there is a stretch of months, sometimes more than a year, when the house is not quite a house. Doors are off their hinges. Windows are out for repair. The stoop is being rebuilt. And things of real value sit outside or in the cellar: salvaged doors, cast-iron railings, newel posts, stacks of clapboard, tools, a dumpster that a passer-by might help themselves to.

The Brownstoner forum post we mentioned above, from a house in the 20s between Fourth and Fifth Avenues near the cemetery, described exactly that: the stoop railings were lying in the front yard while the stoop was rebuilt, and over two days they disappeared. We cannot say who took them or why, and the post had no replies. It is simply an honest picture of what restoration leaves exposed. Cast iron, old wood and architectural salvage are hard to replace and easy to carry off.

An alarm can help during that period, but it has to be designed for a house in pieces. We usually install the permanent panel early, in its final position, running on a battery and cellular communicator so it does not depend on the house’s wiring or internet. Then we add a temporary layer: tilt or shock sensors fixed to stored railings, doors or stacked materials so they report if lifted; a contact on whatever temporary door secures the house at night; an outdoor beam across the front yard if materials are stored there; and, often, one camera that can see the yard. As each part of the restoration finishes, the temporary sensors come off and the permanent ones go into the restored frames.

There is a second benefit. A frame house with its walls open is the easiest house to wire: if the carpenter leaves a string in each stud bay that lines up with a door or window, cable can follow later without an extra hole in finished work.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Greenwood Heights

Greenwood Heights’ businesses sit on a handful of corridors, each with its own pattern. The descriptions below come from the sources named; the alarm notes are our own practice. None of the places mentioned is an AESS client.

Fifth Avenue facing the cemetery

From 24th to 34th Street, shops and houses line one side and Green-Wood the other (Brooklyn Eagle). A store here has no apartments across the street to notice anything at night, so its alarm should report straight to the owner’s phone and, ideally, a monitoring station, with a camera view of the door to confirm what happened.

Fourth Avenue

The rezoned avenue of newer apartment buildings with stores on the ground floor, the 72nd Precinct station at No. 830, and older shops and garages (DCP, Hoodline). The city’s Fourth Avenue special district generally keeps new curb cuts on the side streets, so side and service doors on cross streets are common, and those are the doors we see left off alarms.

Third Avenue and the blocks below Fourth

Garages, auto shops, workshops and storage buildings among houses, where demolitions like the one at 156 21st Street are turning homes into industrial space (New York YIMBY). Alarms here center on overhead doors, steel doors and yards.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Greenwood Heights: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from the particular shape of this neighborhood. General questions about prices, ownership and monitoring are in the FAQ further down.

Backs to the fence

Our back yard ends at Green-Wood’s iron fence, and the cemetery is locked at night. Does having nobody behind us make the back of the house safer or riskier?

Both, in different ways. It is safer in that nobody can walk up to your back fence from a neighboring house or a street, and casual passers-by never see the back of your home. It is riskier in that anyone who does get into the yard, by climbing the cemetery fence or coming over a side fence, has privacy and time. The fence itself can be climbed: in 2012 a man was filmed climbing the inside of it near the main gate before opening hours. So we treat a cemetery-side back as a quiet but real way in. Every rear door and ground-floor window gets a contact, large rear glass gets a glass-break detector, the rear rooms get a motion detector, and the panel goes where nobody entering from the back can reach it during the entry delay.

Can the alarm wait for a second sensor to agree before it calls anyone about something at the back of the house?

Yes. Most current panels support what installers call cross-zoning or sequential verification: the panel treats a trip on one sensor as a warning and calls the monitoring station only if a second, different sensor trips within a set window, often a minute or two. We use it for outdoor sensors along an empty edge, pairing, for example, a yard detector with the rear door contact or the indoor motion detector. The first trip can still send you a phone alert and turn on a light, so you lose nothing by waiting for confirmation. The thing we never cross-zone is a door contact on its own: if a rear door opens while the system is armed, that is reason enough to alarm.

AESS practice, schematic: a lone yard trip only alerts you; a second sensor inside the window calls the monitoring station.

A pocket park beside the Prospect Expressway sits on the other side of our chain-link fence and is busy all day. Can the yard sensors stay quiet in daylight and arm only at night?

Yes. We put the yard sensors in their own group and give that group a schedule, for example armed from dusk to seven in the morning, with a later evening start in summer. The doors and windows of the house are not on that schedule; they follow whatever arming state you choose. You can override the yard group from your phone, so if you are away for a weekend you can arm it all day, and if you are having people over in the yard you can keep it off later. If you have a rear camera, its motion alerts can follow the same timetable, so your phone is not buzzing every time a dog walker passes the fence at lunchtime.

The warehouse beside us is empty from Friday evening until Monday morning. Should our alarm behave differently on weekends?

It can, and for some houses it should. We set up a second armed state for the weekend that adds the windows on the warehouse side, the cellar and, where it makes sense, a glass-break or shock sensor on the shared side, all of which would be unnecessary or noisy on weekdays when the building next door is full of people and machines. You switch to it with one button, and the panel or app can remind you on Friday evening. We also look at the building’s roof line from your windows; if a low warehouse roof brings one of your upper windows within reach, that window gets a contact regardless of the day of the week.

If one sensor at the back keeps tripping while we are away, will the siren go off again and again all night?

Not if the panel is set up properly. Panels have a feature usually called swinger shutdown or auto-bypass: after a sensor has caused a set number of alarms in one armed period, typically one or two, the panel stops responding to that sensor until the system is next disarmed, while every other sensor stays fully active. You get an alert telling you which sensor was bypassed. We turn this on for every system, and we keep the siren within the fifteen-minute limit in the city’s Administrative Code. A sensor that trips repeatedly is usually telling us something real, such as a gate that does not latch, a detector seeing branches or a door that has warped, and the event history shows us which.

Frame houses and restorations

We restored our wood doors and windows and want nothing visible on them. Can the contacts be recessed into the frames instead?

In most wood frames, yes. A recessed contact is a small cylinder that sits in a hole drilled into the frame, with a matching magnet drilled into the edge of the door or the sash, so that when the door or window is closed nothing can be seen. In a frame house we can usually bring the cable to it through the hollow wall from the attic or the cellar. There are limits: very thin sash, frames with weights and pulleys right where the hole would go, and doors whose edges have been stripped and filled may need a different approach, such as a slim surface contact on the hinge side or a wireless recessed contact. We show you exactly where each hole would be before drilling anything.

AESS practice, schematic: magnet in the door edge, contact in the frame, cable in the wall.

A neighbor on our block lost the stoop railings from the front yard while the stoop was being rebuilt. Can an alarm protect ironwork and materials left outside during a restoration?

It can help a good deal, as long as it is designed for that job. Tilt or shock sensors fixed to the stored railings, salvaged doors or stacked materials report as soon as something is lifted. An outdoor beam across the front yard, or a camera with a motion alert, covers the area where things are stored. These go on a temporary group that can be armed at night and over weekends while work is paused, and they come off when the restoration is finished. Moving the most valuable pieces into a locked cellar or a room with a contact on its door is still the simplest protection of all.

Our wood-framed row house shares walls with frame houses on both sides. Can noise from next door set off a glass-break detector?

It is possible with a poor detector or a careless setting, which is why we are careful with them in attached frame houses. A good acoustic glass-break detector listens for the combination of a low-frequency thud and the high-frequency sound of glass shattering, not just a loud noise. We place it facing the windows it protects, away from the shared wall, and test it with a glass-break simulator at the sensitivity we leave it on. If a household next door is unusually loud, or the wall is very thin, we can lower the sensitivity, move the detector, or protect a particular window with a contact instead. We would rather fit one fewer detector than leave you with one that cries wolf.

AESS practice, schematic: the detector faces your own windows, away from the shared walls, and is tested in place.

Avenues and family houses

A Fourth Avenue store was burglarized through its side door at two in the morning. Which doors do shops on the avenues tend to leave off the alarm?

In our experience the doors most often forgotten are the ones the owner thinks of as part of the building rather than the store: a side door onto the cross street, a back door into a shared hallway, a cellar hatch in the sidewalk, a stair door to the roof and a door into a neighboring unit that has been bolted for years. On Fourth Avenue, where the city’s special district rules generally keep new curb cuts on the side streets, service and side doors on the cross streets are common. Every one of those gets a heavy-duty contact. The front glass behind a roll-down gate is often the least important opening, because forcing a gate on an avenue is noisy and visible.

My late father’s house sits empty while the estate is settled, and three of us need to get in. How should the alarm be set up for that?

Give each person their own code and app login, so the history shows who came and went, and agree on one person who is the primary contact for alerts and decisions. Keep the system on cellular reporting with a battery backup, because utilities and internet at an empty house are often cut back. Add a temperature sensor or two to warn of a failed boiler in winter and a water sensor near the boiler or under the kitchen sink. If a buyer’s agent, appraiser or contractor needs access, a temporary code that works only on certain days can be created and deleted remotely. When the house is sold, everything can be reset to factory settings for the new owners or removed.

Each is paraphrased, then checked against what actually happens in this neighborhood.

“Start with the front door, because that is where most burglars enter”

Generic advice often leads with the front door, citing national surveys about entry points. That may be true of suburban houses set back from a quiet road. It is the wrong starting point for a Greenwood Heights row house, whose front door opens onto a sidewalk watched by neighbors and passers-by.

Here the back is where privacy is, and on many blocks the back faces a cemetery, an expressway park or a warehouse with nobody in it after dark. The 2019 store burglary at Fourth Avenue and 17th Street came through a side door, not the front.

So we plan from the back fence in. The front door still gets its contact and the keypad, but the money goes first to the openings nobody else can see.

“Outdoor motion sensors give the earliest warning, so put them everywhere”

Cost sites and product pages present outdoor sensors as a simple upgrade: catch the intruder in the yard before he reaches the house. It sounds sensible, and for the right yard it is.

Along Green-Wood, outdoor sensors face trees, moving branches and wildlife; along the expressway parks they face dog walkers by day. A sensor that trips often teaches the household to ignore it or switch it off, which is worse than not having it.

We use outdoor detection sparingly, aimed along the fence rather than across the yard, scheduled for night where the edge is busy by day, and set to need a second sensor before anyone is dispatched.

“A fence or a gate makes the yard secure”

AI answers about home security often list fencing as a deterrent and stop there. A fence marks a boundary and slows people down; it does not report anything.

Green-Wood is surrounded by an elaborate iron fence, and in 2012 a man was still filmed climbing it near the main gate before opening time. Behind many expressway-side houses, Untapped Cities found, a chain-link fence is all that separates the parks from the yards.

We treat the fence as the edge of the plan, not the end of it. What reports is a contact on the gate, a detector inside the yard where it is worth it, and above all the sensors on the house itself.

“Wireless is always easier than wired”

National guides say wired systems need walls opened and wireless systems do not, so wireless is presented as the obvious choice for older homes.

That is true of solid brick and brownstone. It is often not true of a wood-framed Greenwood Heights house, where hollow stud bays let cable drop from the attic or rise from the cellar to a recessed contact with only a pencil-sized hole to show for it. A wired sensor never needs a battery change.

We decide opening by opening. Frame walls with clear bays get wired, recessed contacts; brick walls, blocked bays and upper floors get wireless parts, all on one panel.

Some AI answers and older websites state that New York City requires an alarm permit and quote a fee. We looked for a primary source and did not find a citywide burglar-alarm permit rule.

What New York City does have is § 24-238 of the Administrative Code, which requires a building’s audible burglar alarm to shut off automatically within fifteen minutes. New York State requires the installing business to be licensed by the Department of State.

Can we arm and disarm the alarm with a voice assistant, and is that wise?
+

Many current panels link to voice assistants, and arming by voice is convenient and low risk. Disarming by voice is a different matter: we set it up only with a spoken PIN, or leave it off, because anyone who can speak near the device, or a voice heard through a window, could otherwise switch the system off. Most households keep voice for arming and checking status and use the keypad, a fob or the app to disarm.

Will the cellular communicator you install stop working when a phone network shuts down, the way older alarm radios did?
+

No communicator lasts forever, but we install current LTE communicators intended for the networks carriers are keeping, rather than older radios on networks already retired. If a carrier ever announces a shutdown that affects your panel, the communicator is usually a module that can be swapped without replacing the sensors. Your panel also reports trouble if it loses its cellular connection, so you would not find out by accident.

Can the monitoring operator talk to us through the panel before sending anyone?
+

Yes, if you choose a panel with two-way voice and a monitoring plan that supports it. When the alarm trips, the operator can speak through the panel’s built-in speaker and listen through its microphone, so a household member can answer and give the password, or the operator can hear what is happening. It shortens false alarms and gets help moving faster when something is wrong.

Is there a code that seems to switch the alarm off but quietly calls for help?
+

Yes. Most panels support a duress code: entering it disarms the system as usual, so it looks normal to anyone standing beside you, but the panel silently sends a duress signal to the monitoring station, which treats it as an emergency.
