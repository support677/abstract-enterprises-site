The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Properties Need Blink Camera Installation

Brooklyn has been building on the same ground for a long time. The Dutch West India Company authorized the village of Breuckelen in 1646, which made it the first municipality in what is now New York State. The consolidated City of Brooklyn absorbed Williamsburgh and the Town of Bushwick in 1854, and the whole city joined New York on January 1, 1898. By the 2020 census the borough held 2,736,074 people. Almost every phase of that history is still standing, and that matters to a Blink camera more than most people expect, because each era of construction leaves a different kind of wall, a different kind of door and a different place where the internet comes into the building.

The numbers tell the story plainly. The Census Bureau's 2023 American Community Survey counts 1,090,187 housing units in Kings County. The median one is in a building from 1943, and 513,629 of them are in buildings from 1939 or earlier. Only 52,359 are in detached one-family houses. Another 93,812 are in attached one-family houses, the rowhouses and townhouses that line so many side streets. Two-unit buildings account for 175,805 homes and three- to four-unit buildings for 170,655, so the typical Brooklyn front door is shared: one buzzer panel, one stoop, two or three households and often one landlord. And 709,570 of the occupied homes are rented, about seven in ten. A Blink system here has to fit a building that is old, brick and shared, and a household that frequently does not own the wall.

Those buildings are hard on the kind of radio link Blink uses. Blink's support pages tell owners to keep the Sync Module away from "thick walls or materials like concrete, brick, stone, or metal" and say its Sync Modules can reach cameras up to about 100 feet away. In open air that is plenty. Inside a brick rowhouse that Brownstoner describes as typically two to five stories tall, with the internet service landing in the cellar and the front door one floor up behind a masonry stoop, 100 feet of radio range can shrink to a single floor. Brownstoner's architecture writers also point out that most "brownstones" are really brick houses with a brownstone facing four to six inches thick on the street front, which means the camera is often fighting through two masonry layers at once. A doorbell on a steel-clad apartment door adds metal to the list, and a doorbell supply company notes that walls, brick and metal doors not only shorten the range but also drain the battery faster as the camera works harder to stay connected. This is why so many Brooklyn Blink owners call us after a system that worked on the kitchen table started dropping offline once it went on the door.

Then there are the reasons people buy a Blink in the first place. Package theft is the one we hear most, and Brooklyn has been in the news for it again and again. CBS New York showed a thief filmed near Montgomery Street and New York Avenue in Crown Heights, less than a block from a police station. In Park Slope, CBS reported thieves cutting open packages and taking what was inside while they were still in the lobby, which is a different problem from a box on an open stoop. PIX11 covered a Sheepshead Bay porch theft caught on a home camera in which the gifts taken were worth more than $700. ABC7 showed a suspect scooping up a handful of packages on State Street in Downtown Brooklyn back in 2017. News 12 Brooklyn repeated the city's figure in December 2025 that about 90,000 packages are stolen every day across New York City. The New York Attorney General's consumer alert tells New Yorkers to use a doorbell camera or video system to deter thieves or help police identify them. A Blink doorbell is one of the cheapest ways to do that, as long as it is placed where it actually sees the package and stays online long enough to send the clip.

Break-ins shape the plan too, and they do not always come through the front. The Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2018 that police were looking for a man linked to at least a dozen burglaries in Bensonhurst and Gravesend, and that in every known case he forced open a window at the rear of the house. CBS New York reported in 2019 that two people burglarized a Bushwick home near Central Avenue and George Street while the resident was in the backyard, and that the break-in was caught on surveillance footage. For a rowhouse or two-family, that means the camera on the back of the house, looking over the yard, the rear windows and the cellar door, can matter as much as the doorbell on the front. It is also the camera that has to cross the most brick to reach the router, which is why we plan the network before we plan the mounting.

Finally, cameras in Brooklyn come with neighbors and rules. Tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville fought their landlord's 2019 plan for facial recognition at the entrance, and tenants at 60 Turner Place in Kensington objected to their super wearing a body camera, so people here pay attention to who records whom. NYCHA allows a video doorbell on an apartment door only if it is wireless and fixed over the peephole or with adhesive. Brick Underground reminds renters that a landlord's permission is needed for equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside the door. Connextivity notes that many co-ops and condos have never written a rule at all and that audio is often switched off because the law on passive hallway recording has never been settled. And on a landmarked front, the Landmarks Preservation Commission has specific rules about where cameras go and how they attach. A good Blink install here respects all of that without making the system useless.

Same-Day Repair in Brooklyn

Need Repair in Brooklyn? A Doorbell That Keeps Going Offline, a Camera That Fell Off the Brick, or Old Wires Nobody Can Identify

Most of the Blink repair calls we take from Brooklyn fall into three groups. The first is a camera that worked when it was set up and now drops out, sends late alerts or eats batteries in a few weeks. In nearly every case the camera itself is fine. What changed is that it moved from beside the Sync Module to the far side of a brick wall, a brownstone front or a steel door, and it now spends its battery fighting a weak signal. Blink's support pages say plainly that a second Sync Module can improve weak signal strength for cameras with connectivity problems, and that the Sync Module should sit away from concrete, brick, stone and metal. So the repair is usually about moving or adding a Sync Module, or moving the router's coverage closer, not about buying new cameras.

The second group is a mount that has failed. Adhesive pads put on a cold, dusty steel door in January, a plastic plug pushed into crumbly plaster, a screw driven into the soft lime mortar of an old rear wall: all of them can let go. We clean and re-seat the mount, or replace it with a fixing that suits the material, and on a rented door we stay with adhesive and peephole mounts so nothing new gets drilled. The third group is wiring nobody can identify: an old doorbell button, a pair of wires sticking out of the frame, a transformer somewhere in the cellar, or an intercom line that someone hoped would power a doorbell. Before anything gets connected, we trace it.

If a doorbell or camera is hanging loose, take it down so it does not fall, keep it with its mount and screws, and call (347) 934-8335. A repair visit is billed as a setup-only visit on our list, $225 for the first hour and then 30-minute blocks, and the technician tells you what is wrong and what the fix involves before starting. If the problem turns out to be the building itself, a router in a flooded cellar, a buzzer system the landlord is responsible for, or loose brick that needs a mason, we say so plainly rather than mounting a camera on something that will not hold it.

Blink Camera Installation for Brooklyn's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Brooklyn Blink call we take. They differ in where the internet comes in, what the door is made of, how the signal has to travel and who owns the front. Those four things decide which Blink devices make sense and where they go.

The brownstone and brick rowhouse

Two to five stories on a narrow lot, often about 20 feet wide, with brick party walls, a stoop over a garden-level door and the internet landing in the cellar.

The two- and three-family house

The most common Brooklyn front door: an owner on one floor and tenants on the others, one entry, one stoop and sometimes one shared internet line.

The prewar walk-up

Four- and five-story brick buildings with a buzzer panel at the street, a vestibule with a glass inner door and steel-clad apartment doors that self-close.

NYCHA developments

Public housing towers and low-rises where the House Rules allow only wireless doorbells, fixed over the peephole or with adhesive, and no holes anywhere.

Elevator buildings, co-ops and condos

Prewar elevator buildings and new towers from Downtown Brooklyn to the Greenpoint waterfront, with management offices, boards and concrete walls.

The detached house with a driveway

Southern Brooklyn homes, such as the detached and semi-detached houses of Mill Basin, with side driveways, rear garages and more ground to cover.

The brownstone and brick rowhouse: the signal has to climb out of the cellar

A Brooklyn rowhouse is tall, narrow and walled in by brick. Most sit on lots close to the city's standard 20 by 100 feet, with each floor perhaps 20 feet wide and 40 to 50 feet deep. The internet usually enters in the cellar, where the cable or fiber company left its box, and the router often sits right there on a shelf or, worse, on the floor. The front door is a floor up, behind the masonry of the stoop and the four to six inches of brownstone facing that Brownstoner describes on the street front. The rear yard is behind another full-depth brick wall. A Blink doorbell on the parlor door and an Outdoor camera over the yard are, in radio terms, on opposite sides of the house and on different floors from the Sync Module. That arrangement works only if the Sync Module is placed to suit the cameras, not the router.

Our usual plan in a rowhouse puts the Sync Module on the parlor floor, near the middle of the house and away from the brick party walls, plugged in where it has a good Wi-Fi signal of its own. If the router's Wi-Fi does not reach the parlor floor well, the answer is to bring the network up to the Sync Module, through the router's own mesh or an access point, rather than moving the Sync Module down into the cellar where it cannot hear the cameras. For a house where the front and back cameras are both struggling, Blink's own guidance is that a second Sync Module can help, and we will tell you when that is the right call. We measure all of this with the cameras held in their real positions before anything is fixed to a wall.

Mounting on a rowhouse depends on which face of the house we are working on. The rear wall is usually common brick, which holds a small Blink Outdoor mount well with masonry anchors placed in the brick. The front is different, especially in a historic district: a brownstone facing is soft and expensive to repair, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission's guidebook says cameras should attach at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork rather than through decorative surfaces. Inside, most rowhouses have plaster on lath or plaster on brick, and an indoor Mini on a wall needs a plaster-safe fixing or, more often, just a shelf. Our list adds $65 for brick or stone and $45 for plaster where we fix to those walls.

The two- and three-family house: one front door, several households

The two-family house is the most common building in Brooklyn by number of homes, and it creates Blink questions that a single-family house never does. The front door, the stoop and the hallway are shared. The owner may live on the ground floor and rent the upper floors, or the other way round. The doorbell may be a button for each apartment wired to a buzzer, not a chime, which affects which Blink doorbell can be powered. And everyone walks past the camera several times a day. The technical part of the job is ordinary. The human part, deciding whose account the doorbell lives on, what it sees and who gets alerts, takes more thought.

We plan a two-family system with the owner first. The doorbell covers the stoop and the package spot, not the inside of the tenants' hallway. Motion sensitivity is set so it records visitors and deliveries rather than every trip a tenant makes. Audio is left off unless you decide otherwise. If the tenants want their own doorbell on their own apartment door, that is their device on their own account. Where there is a rear yard that only the ground-floor unit uses, the yard camera belongs to that unit and is aimed at the yard and the rear windows, not up at the floors above. We write down who controls each device so nobody is surprised later. Sunset Park's historic districts, which the Historic Districts Council describes as one of the city's largest concentrations of two-family masonry rowhouses, mostly built between 1885 and 1912, are a good example of where this comes up.

The prewar walk-up: buzzer panel at the street, steel door upstairs

Walk-up apartment buildings line the avenues in Bay Ridge, Bushwick, Crown Heights, Flatbush, Sunset Park and a dozen other neighborhoods. For a Blink owner, three features matter. The street door is controlled by a buzzer panel that belongs to the building, and Housing Maintenance Code rules require many multiple dwellings to have an intercommunication system and self-locking entrance doors, so the building's buzzer is the real doorbell for visitors. The apartment door is usually steel or steel-clad and must self-close, which is hard on adhesive mounts and harder on Wi-Fi. And the router is inside your apartment, which is good news: the signal only has to get through your own door, not through four floors of brick.

That makes the walk-up an apartment-door job more than a building job. A Blink battery doorbell or a small camera on the inside of the door, looking through or over the peephole area, avoids the hallway wall altogether. A no-drill door clamp mount can work on an inward-opening door if the thickness and gap fit the clamp's limits. The steel door shortens the range between the doorbell and the Sync Module, so we place the Sync Module close to the door inside the apartment, sometimes on a shelf in the entry hall, and check the signal with the door shut. The vestibule downstairs, with its tile floor and glass inner door, is not your space, and a camera there needs the landlord's agreement.

NYCHA developments: wireless, adhesive or over the peephole

NYCHA's House Rules are unusually clear. A tenant may put a camera or video doorbell on the apartment door only if it is wireless, and only if it is placed over the apartment's peephole or fixed to the door with adhesive. Tenants may not make holes in or alterations to the door, the frame or the walls above and around the door. NYCHA announced the policy on May 23, 2022. Blink's battery doorbells fit that rule well, because they need no wiring, and we fit them with adhesive on a cleaned door face or with a peephole-style bracket where the doorbell's design allows.

Two things make NYCHA installs harder than they look. The doors are heavy and metal, and the corridors are block and concrete, so the doorbell's signal to the Sync Module inside the apartment can be weak; the Sync Module goes as close to the door as an outlet allows. And adhesive on a painted steel door needs a clean, dry surface and time to set, which in winter means working on a door that has been warmed indoors. We do not drill, we do not mount anything on the corridor wall, and we leave the peephole usable if the bracket covers it. If your development's management asks what was installed, the device box and our paperwork show it.

Elevator buildings, co-ops and condos: concrete, management and the board

Brooklyn's elevator buildings range from prewar co-ops in Brooklyn Heights and along Ocean Parkway to the new towers of Downtown Brooklyn, where the 2004 rezoning has been followed by close to 27,000 new housing units, and the Greenpoint Landing master plan of about 5,500 units on the waterfront. In these buildings the challenge is rarely Wi-Fi range inside the apartment; it is the concrete between apartments and corridors, the steel doors, and the rules. Connextivity notes that some co-ops and condos require board approval in writing, others decide case by case, and many have never addressed doorbell cameras at all.

Inside the apartment, Blink Minis are often all a co-op or condo owner needs: one watching the entry, one in the living room, each on its own outlet and connected straight to Wi-Fi. On the door, the choice is the same as a walk-up: inside-the-door placement, adhesive, or a clamp where the door fits. Anything in the corridor is a common-area question for management. We send the certificate of insurance to the managing agent before the visit and work inside the building's contractor hours.

The detached house with a driveway: more ground, longer distances

Southern Brooklyn has a different kind of house. Mill Basin, Marine Park, Manhattan Beach, Bergen Beach and parts of Dyker Heights have detached and semi-detached homes with side driveways, rear garages and front lawns. Here the Blink job looks more like a suburban one: a doorbell at the front, an Outdoor camera covering the driveway, perhaps one on the garage. Distance matters more than brick, and the rule of thumb from Blink is that a Sync Module reaches cameras up to about 100 feet away, less through walls. A garage at the back of a long lot may need its own Sync Module or a stronger network at the rear of the house.

Many of these houses are also in low-lying areas, close to Jamaica Bay or the ocean, and the Health Department's flooding research flags southern Brooklyn as especially vulnerable. If your router lives in a basement that has taken water, we look for a higher place for the network equipment and the Sync Module before mounting cameras that depend on them. Exterior walls here are often brick, stucco or siding; brick and stone take the $65 add-on on our list.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Walks a Brooklyn House Before Unboxing a Blink

These notes describe how our technicians approach Brooklyn buildings. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Find the router first. The first question in any Brooklyn house is where the internet comes in. In rowhouses it is usually the cellar, often by the front wall where the cable company entered. We check whether the router is on the floor, near the boiler or under a water pipe, because a Sync Module is only as reliable as the network it plugs into.

Walk the signal. With a phone showing Wi-Fi strength, the technician walks from the router to every planned camera spot: up the cellar stairs, out to the parlor door, through to the rear windows, out onto the stoop and into the yard. Brick walls and the stoop's masonry show up as sudden drops. That walk decides where the Sync Module goes.

Set up beside the module. Blink's setup guide asks for each new camera to be added in the same room as the Sync Module, within about seven feet. We do exactly that, then carry each camera to its real position and hold it there while checking live view and a test clip. Only then do we decide on the mount.

Look at the front from the sidewalk. On a landmarked block, the technician stands on the sidewalk and looks at where the camera would be visible. Under the stoop, behind an areaway wall or beneath a band course usually beats a spot beside the door.

Ask whose door it is. Before any adhesive, screw or clamp, the technician confirms who controls the door: you, a landlord, a board or NYCHA. The mount follows the answer.

Signal and Power

Brick, Brownstone and Steel: Keeping a Blink System Online in a Brooklyn Building

Every Blink problem we are called to in Brooklyn comes back to two things: the link between each camera and the Sync Module, and the link between the Sync Module and the internet. Blink's cameras run on batteries or a plug-in adapter and talk to the Sync Module over a low-power radio. The Sync Module plugs into an outlet and joins your Wi-Fi. Blink Minis skip the Sync Module and join Wi-Fi directly. Each of those links has to cross the building, and in Brooklyn the building is mostly masonry.

Range is a ceiling, not a promise. Blink says its Sync Module (1st Gen), Sync Module 2 and Sync Module Core can communicate with cameras up to 100 feet away. One independent review that tested Blink in a house reported a strong connection only within about 70 to 80 feet once walls were in the way. In a Brooklyn rowhouse, a single brick party wall or the stoop between the parlor door and a cellar-level Sync Module can use up most of that margin. We plan for the real number, not the one on the box.

Materials matter more than distance. Blink's own advice names concrete, brick, stone and metal as the things to avoid between the Sync Module and the cameras. A doorbell supply company adds that metal doors and brick both cut range and increase battery drain. In practice, a doorbell on a steel apartment door 15 feet from the Sync Module can struggle more than an Outdoor camera 40 feet away across an open yard. That is why we place the Sync Module last, after we know where every camera has to be.

A second Sync Module is a legitimate tool. Blink states that a second Sync Module can help cameras with weak signal strength. In a tall house, one module near the front and one near the back can be the difference between a system that works and one that drains batteries. Each module needs its own outlet and good Wi-Fi, so we check both before suggesting it.

Keep the network off the cellar floor. Many Brooklyn routers sit wherever the installer left them, often low in the cellar. After Hurricane Ida, when the Health Department recorded rainfall of 3.5 inches an hour against sewer capacity of 1.75 and deadly flooding in basement apartments, that is not where we want the heart of a camera system. A Sync Module plugged in on the parlor floor, connected to a router or access point that is also up off the floor, survives a wet night that a cellar-floor setup does not. If the router has to stay in the cellar, we at least lift it onto a high shelf away from the boiler and the water heater.

Bring the network up, not the module down. The Brownstoner forum has a homeowner who put the fiber box in the cellar and used Cat6 to connect mesh access points around the house, and a homeowner on r/HomeNetworking described a four-floor brownstone where the router was set right but simply did not reach far enough. Both are the usual Brooklyn story. When the Wi-Fi does not reach the floor where the Sync Module belongs, the honest fix is a mesh node or a wired access point on that floor. We can set that up as part of the visit when you already own the equipment.

Power: what the doorbell button in a Brooklyn house can and cannot do

Blink sells doorbells that run on batteries and doorbells that can be wired. The wired option depends on what is behind your button. Blink's support pages say its Video Doorbell can connect to chime systems that provide 16 to 24 volts AC, at 50 to 60 Hz and 40 VA maximum. If the app shows "Wired power not supported" during wiring and the doorbell is not connected to a Sync Module, the existing chime wiring is not compatible and the doorbell falls back to Event Response mode. Doorbell systems with wireless chimes are not compatible with the Blink Video Doorbell, according to Best Buy's product answers. The newer Wired Doorbell 2K+ does not support in-home mechanical chimes at all, and where a home has no doorbell wiring Blink offers a plug-in adapter that gives 24-volt AC power from a standard indoor outlet, though it has no battery backup and needs continuous power.

In a Brooklyn building with a buzzer panel, the button at your apartment is usually part of the building's intercom, not a household chime circuit, and it belongs to the landlord. We do not tap a building's intercom to power a doorbell. In a one-family rowhouse with an old bell and transformer in the cellar, we trace the wires and measure the transformer before connecting anything. And in most rental apartments the right answer is simply a battery doorbell, which needs no wiring and no permission to open the wall.

Rules and Permissions

NYCHA, Landlords, Boards and Landmarks: Who Has a Say in Your Brooklyn Blink Camera

With most Brooklyn households renting, and whole neighborhoods landmarked, the question of permission comes up on a large share of our calls. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is the published guidance we work from, so you can ask the right person before booking.

NYCHA tenants. The NYCHA House Rules let you put a camera or video doorbell on your apartment door if it is wireless and placed over the peephole or fixed with adhesive. You may not make holes in or change the door, the frame or the walls above and around it. That is the whole rule, and a Blink battery doorbell installed with adhesive meets it. We do not drill NYCHA doors or walls, and we do not put anything in the corridor.

Private rentals. Brick Underground's guidance is that you need your landlord's permission to install any equipment in the common areas of your building, including the hallway outside your apartment. One New York security firm calls door-mounted video doorbells a gray area: generally tolerated by most landlords but technically subject to the lease's alteration clause. Our practice is to keep the camera on your side of the door where possible, use no-drill mounts on rentals, and suggest a short written note to the landlord when the device will be visible from the hallway. Blink's own renter guide suggests a plug-in Mini for living areas, a battery doorbell for the front door and a battery Outdoor camera for a balcony or patio, which matches what we usually fit.

Co-ops and condos. Connextivity reports that some boards require written approval for door cameras, others decide each request on its own, and many have never addressed it. A hallway wall is a common area. A device on your own door is usually the board's question too. Ask the managing agent first, send us whatever paperwork they need, and we send a certificate of insurance before the visit.

Two-family owners with tenants. When you own the house, the front door and stoop are yours, but your tenants still live there. Aim the doorbell at the stoop and the package spot, not into their hall or windows. Keep audio off unless you have a clear reason. Tell tenants the camera exists and what it covers. That avoids the kind of dispute the Kensington body-camera story describes.

Landmarked fronts. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook has a section on security cameras. Staff can approve cameras by permit when the installation meets its criteria. Cameras should be well spaced to avoid a cluttered look; small residential and commercial buildings are limited to two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage. Cameras should not go on a primary facade unless there is no feasible alternative, and when they do, they belong in discreet places such as beneath a band course, underneath a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence, or behind plantings. They should attach at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, not through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and be as small as possible and painted to blend. The Commission's general guidance is that most exterior changes to front and rear facades in historic districts need its review. One installer advises that visible camera mounts on landmarked brownstones may need a Certificate of No Effect or a Permit to Alter. We tell you when your address is in a district and plan the mount to the guidebook's criteria; the permit, where needed, comes from the Commission.

Audio. New York is generally treated as a one-party consent state for recording conversations, but whether a camera passively capturing hallway audio counts as eavesdropping has never been settled cleanly, which is why many buildings and many installers leave audio off on doors facing common areas. We set audio to your choice after explaining that.

Compliance notes for Brooklyn businesses and buildings

  • Video is not biometric data under the city's signage rule. NYC's biometric identifier law requires certain commercial establishments that collect biometric identifiers from customers to post notices, but its disclosure provision generally does not apply to information collected through photographs or video recordings such as security cameras. Blink cameras used as ordinary cameras fall on that side of the line.
  • Common areas need the owner's consent. In a tenant-occupied commercial or mixed-use building, a camera on a shared corridor or a shared street door needs the landlord's written approval.
  • Landmarked storefronts follow LPC. In a historic district, a camera on a storefront follows the Commission's criteria on spacing, location and attachment, including the limit of two cameras per 25 feet of frontage on small buildings and three on large ones.
  • Wired floodlights need a licensed electrician. Blink's own setup guide warns that permits and professional installation may be required by law for its Wired Floodlight. Our license covers the camera, network and app side; new 120-volt wiring is work for a licensed master electrician under DOB rules.
  • Private spaces stay off camera. New York's Penal Law makes a camera recording inside a bathroom, changing room or similar private space without consent a felony. We never place cameras in those rooms, in homes or businesses.

Blink Camera Installation in Brooklyn: Questions Answered

These are the situations Brooklyn tenants, owners and shopkeepers describe when they call: public-housing doors, steel-clad apartment doors, boards that have never written a rule, tall brick houses, two-family stoops, lobbies where packages disappear and old doorbell wiring. Doors, public housing and the people who run the building

We live in a NYCHA development in Brooklyn. Which Blink doorbell setup actually fits the Housing Authority's rules?

A battery-powered Blink doorbell fixed to your apartment door with adhesive, with its Sync Module plugged in inside the apartment, fits the rule as written. NYCHA's House Rules say a camera or video doorbell must be wireless and must be placed over the peephole or fixed to the door with adhesive, and that tenants may not make holes in or alterations to the door, frame or the walls around it. A battery doorbell needs no wiring, so nothing is opened. We clean and dry the door face, fit the adhesive mount, let it set, and test the doorbell with the door closed, because the metal door and block corridor can weaken its link to the Sync Module. Nothing goes on the corridor wall, and if a bracket covers the peephole we make sure you can still see out or have a working view on your phone.

Will a no-drill clamp mount fit the steel-clad door on our apartment?

It depends on three measurements, and we check them before you buy one. One anti-theft Blink door mount sold online is designed for inward-opening doors only, needs a door between 0.82 and 2.08 inches thick, and needs a gap between the door and the frame of more than 0.063 inches. Many Brooklyn apartment doors open inward and fall within that thickness, but some steel-clad doors are thicker, some have weatherstripping that closes the gap, and apartment doors that open into a common hallway must be self-closing, which puts the clamp under stress every time the door slams. If your door fits, a clamp is a good renter option because it leaves no mark at all. If it does not, adhesive on the door face is the usual alternative. Measure the door's edge with a ruler and send us a photo, and we will tell you which fits.

Can the camera go on the hallway wall beside our door instead of on the door itself?

Usually not without the landlord's or board's agreement, and often it is not worth asking. Brick Underground's guidance is that the hallway and the area outside your apartment are common areas, and equipment there needs your landlord's permission. In a co-op or condo, Connextivity notes, a camera mounted on a hallway wall typically needs building approval. A device on your own door, or a small camera inside the apartment looking out through a peephole kit, avoids the common-area question almost entirely and gives the same view of whoever is at the door. If you do get permission for a wall mount, get it in writing, and we will fix it with the smallest possible hardware and keep the view on your door rather than your neighbors'.

Our Park Slope co-op board has never written a rule about doorbell cameras. How should we raise it before installing?

Write a short request to the managing agent that answers the questions a board will ask, and wait for the reply before booking. Say which device it is, where it will sit (on your door or inside it), whether anything will be drilled, what it will see, and that audio will be off. Mention that the installer is licensed and insured and can send a certificate of insurance. Connextivity describes Brooklyn and Manhattan co-ops as falling into three groups: those with a written approval policy, those that decide case by case and those that have never addressed it, and a board in the last group usually appreciates being asked rather than discovering a camera on the door. Many boards are more comfortable with a camera that sits inside the door than one on its face.

Rowhouses, brownstones and two-family houses

How many Blink cameras does a typical Brooklyn rowhouse need, and which spots matter most?

Most rowhouses are well covered by three or four devices: a doorbell at the main entrance, an Outdoor camera covering the garden-level door under the stoop, an Outdoor camera over the rear yard and rear windows, and sometimes an indoor Mini at the cellar door or the parlor hall. The narrow lot helps: a 20-foot front can be covered by one well-placed doorbell and one camera under the stoop, and the rear yard by one camera high on the back wall. The burglary reports from Bensonhurst, Gravesend and Bushwick are a reminder that the rear deserves its own camera. On a landmarked front, the Commission's limit of two cameras per 25 feet of frontage for small buildings caps the street side anyway. We plan from the doors and windows people actually use, not from a number.

Burglaries in parts of Brooklyn have come through rear windows. Should our first camera face the back yard rather than the front door?

If packages are not your main worry, a rear camera is often the more useful first purchase. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that in every known case in a 2018 string of Bensonhurst and Gravesend burglaries, the suspect forced open a rear window, and CBS New York covered a Bushwick burglary that happened while the resident was out in the yard. The back of a rowhouse is out of sight from the street, which is exactly why it matters. The challenge is that the rear wall is solid brick and furthest from a cellar router, so the yard camera is the one most likely to drop offline. We usually put the Sync Module toward the back of the parlor floor for this reason and test the yard camera's signal at night with the windows closed before mounting it.

We own a two-family and rent the top floor. How do we keep the tenant's daily comings and goings out of our clips?

Aim and tune the doorbell for visitors and deliveries rather than residents. The camera can face the stoop and the package spot, not the inside of the shared hallway or the tenant's stair. Motion sensitivity and the retrigger time can be set so routine trips in and out do not flood your phone, and on models that offer activity zones you can leave the tenant's mailbox or stair out of the area that triggers a clip. Tell the tenant the camera exists and what it covers, and agree whether they get notifications too. That respects their privacy, keeps your alerts useful and avoids the kind of resentment that follows a camera nobody mentioned.

Would a second Sync Module help the camera at the garden-level door of our four-story house?

Quite possibly. Blink's own support guidance says a second Sync Module can help cameras with weak signal strength, and in a four-story Brooklyn house the garden-level door is often the weakest spot: below the parlor floor, behind the stoop's masonry and close to the cellar's brick walls. One module on the parlor floor for the doorbell and rear camera and a second near the garden level can stop a camera that keeps dropping out from draining its batteries. Each module needs an outlet and a good Wi-Fi signal, so if the Wi-Fi on the garden floor is poor, that has to be fixed too. We measure both before suggesting it, so you only buy the second module if it will solve the problem.

Doorbells, chimes and power

We have a plug-in wireless chime from a previous doorbell. Will a Blink doorbell ring it?

No. Best Buy's product answers for the Blink Video Doorbell say doorbell systems with wireless chimes are not compatible with it, and Blink's own support pages say the Wired Doorbell 2K+ does not support in-home mechanical chimes either. A Blink doorbell announces visitors through the Blink app on your phone, and through any compatible smart speaker you link to your account. If you want a sound in the house as well as on your phone, tell us at booking, and we will explain the options for your model rather than leave the old chime plugged in and silent.

There is an outlet just inside our front door. Can the Wired Doorbell 2K+ run from it?

Yes, with Blink's plug-in adapter, which is sold separately. Blink's support pages say the adapter gives 24-volt AC power from a standard indoor outlet for homes without doorbell wiring. The trade-off is that the Wired Doorbell 2K+ has no battery backup and needs continuous power, so if the adapter is unplugged, a power strip is switched off or the power goes out, the doorbell stops. The adapter's thin cable has to reach from the outlet through or around the door frame to the doorbell, which on a rented apartment door may count as an alteration. In a one-family house with an outlet near the door, it is a neat way to avoid batteries; we hide the cable with a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, where the run is visible.

Can you put a Blink Wired Floodlight over the rear yard of our rowhouse?

We can set up and aim the camera, but the power side needs an electrician. Blink's Wired Floodlight setup guide says it needs a 4-inch round, UL-listed, weatherproof electrical box, and warns that permits and professional installation may be required by law. Most rear walls of Brooklyn rowhouses have no outdoor fixture at all, and a Brownstoner forum homeowner who wanted a floodlight camera on the second floor of a brownstone asked exactly what it would cost to run a new wire. Our license covers low-voltage work; new 120-volt wiring is for a licensed master electrician. If you already have a suitable box on the back wall, or an electrician is putting one in, we handle the Blink side: mounting, app setup, motion and light settings, and aiming the light so it lights your yard without shining into the windows of the next row.

Packages, lobbies and footage

Our packages disappear inside the lobby of our walk-up, not on the stoop. Where would a Blink camera even go?

That is a building problem, and the camera that would help most belongs to the building, not to you. CBS New York's Park Slope story showed thieves cutting open packages inside a lobby, which means they were already past the street door. A camera in the lobby or vestibule is a common-area device and needs the landlord's agreement; if the landlord agrees, a Blink Outdoor or Mini placed high, covering the mail area and the inner door, is a quick way to start. What you control is your own door: a doorbell there sees deliveries brought up to you and anyone tampering with them. Many tenants also ask carriers to deliver to a pickup point. If you are the owner of a small building, we can plan lobby coverage with you directly.

After a package theft, how do we save a Blink clip and get it to the precinct?

Save the clip in the Blink app straight away and keep a copy on your phone, rather than relying on the cloud to hold it. Security.org notes that Blink's plans include 60 days of cloud storage, after which clips go. Download the clip, note the time and date, and take it to your local precinct or share it when you file a report. The New York Attorney General's package-theft alert says doorbell cameras can help law enforcement identify thieves, but the clip is only useful if it shows a face and is kept. During setup we place the doorbell low enough to see faces and packages, not just the tops of heads, and at hand-over we show you exactly where the download and share buttons are.

Can a Blink Mini watch the stoop through our parlor window?

In daylight it can give a reasonable view, but at night glass is a problem. Infrared night vision reflects off window glass, so a camera pressed against a window often shows its own glare instead of the stoop. A competitor's Brooklyn page makes the same point about vestibules, where tile floors and glass inset doors cause night-vision glare. If the window gets streetlight, turning off the Mini's infrared and relying on that light can help, and pressing the lens flat against the glass reduces reflection. For a reliable night view of a stoop, an Outdoor camera mounted outside, under the stoop or beside the door within landmark rules, works far better. We test both in your window before you decide.

FAQ

Brooklyn Blink Camera Installation FAQ

Do we need a paid Blink plan just to talk to someone at the door?
+

Shoppers ask this often in retailer Q and A threads, and Blink's own doorbell guide covers what works with and without a plan. During setup we show you which features on your exact doorbell model need a plan and which do not, including live view and two-way talk, so you can decide before paying for anything.

We already bought a Blink bundle online. Will you install the cameras we have?
+

Yes. Most Brooklyn Blink jobs use cameras the customer already owns. We install them at the labor prices on our list, check the bundle includes the Sync Module your battery devices need, and tell you if any device will not suit its planned spot.

Can the same technician hang our TV while setting up the Blink system?
+

Yes. TV wall mounting on drywall over wood studs, up to 55 inches, starts at $209.99 on our list with the bracket separate, and a TV placed on its stand is $137.99. Tell us at booking so we plan the time.

Can the Mini's power cord be hidden along the wall in our apartment?
+

Yes. A paintable cord cover is $59 on our list. It runs neatly on the wall surface from the Mini to the outlet, can be painted to match, and is easy to remove and patch when you move out.

Our front door sits behind an iron gate under the stoop. Can the doorbell go on the gate?
+

Often it can, using a mount clamped or fixed to a solid section of the ironwork, which also keeps it off the stone. We check the signal from the gate to the Sync Module first, because the iron and the stoop's masonry can weaken it.

The house came with an old camera and doorbell. Can you take them down?
+

Yes. We remove old devices as part of the setup visit time, patch small holes where needed, and leave the old equipment with you. If old wiring is left behind, we trace and cap or label it so nobody mistakes it for a live doorbell circuit later.

Does someone have to be home for the whole visit?
+

Someone needs to be there at the start to show us the router and agree the camera spots, and at the end for the app hand-over on your phone. In between, the account holder should be reachable to approve sign-in codes.

Our management office wants proof of insurance before any contractor comes in. Can you send it?
+

Yes. Send us the exact names and address the managing agent, board or office wants on the certificate, and we send it before the visit so the building can clear the appointment.

Is a Mini fixed to an old plaster wall priced differently from one on a shelf?
+

Yes. Fixing to plaster adds $45 on our list because old plaster needs slow pilot holes and the right anchor. A Mini that simply sits on a shelf or console has no wall add-on at all.

If we want a camera moved after the job is finished, what does that involve?
+

Moving or re-aiming a camera after the job is complete is a new setup visit, $225 for the first hour on our list, because readjustments after completion are not covered by the warranty. We agree every position with you on the day to avoid that.
