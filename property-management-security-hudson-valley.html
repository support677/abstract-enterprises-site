The Region

Why Hudson Valley Property Managers Hand the Whole Portfolio to One Security Contractor

A management office in White Plains or Pearl River rarely runs a neat set of identical buildings. The list on the whiteboard tends to read like a tour of the region: a 1960s six-story elevator rental near a Metro-North station, a block of brick walk-ups over storefronts on a village main street, a newly delivered downtown tower with a leasing office and a fob for every amenity door, a garden complex spread over twenty acres off a state route, and a row of townhouse rentals where each unit has its own front door. Every one of those properties came with a different intercom, a different camera recorder, and usually a different contractor who installed it and then stopped answering the phone.

Distance makes that worse than it is in the city. A super in the Bronx can walk to the corner hardware store; a resident manager at a complex outside Middletown may be forty minutes from the nearest supply house, and the management office may be an hour away in the other direction. When a gate reader or lobby panel fails, the cost is not just the repair. It is the drive, the second drive when the first contractor did not bring the right part, and the tenant complaints that pile up in between. Property management security in this region is mostly about cutting those trips: one contractor who knows every property on the list, carries the parts that fit them, and keeps records so the next visit starts where the last one ended.

Growth adds pressure. A development report prepared by RM Friedland and published through the Business Council of Westchester counts roughly 12,500 residential units delivered across Westchester since late 2021, with another 15,000 under construction, and says Yonkers, New Rochelle, White Plains and Mount Vernon together delivered 9,413 units with 13,114 more underway. Most of those units are rentals in elevator buildings with fob-controlled lobbies, parking garages, package rooms and amenity floors. A management company that picks up even one of those buildings inherits a security platform someone else chose, often with a subscription in a developer's name.

The rules are also different once you leave the five boroughs. The state's Multiple Dwelling Law covers cities of 325,000 people or more, so apartment buildings in Yonkers, New Rochelle and every village up the river fall under the Multiple Residence Law instead, which applies to cities under that size and to all towns and villages. There is no HPD north of the city line; code enforcement comes from the city, town or village building department under the state's Uniform Code and Property Maintenance Code. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act for smart-access systems is a city law and does not follow a fob reader into Westchester. We are not lawyers and nothing on this page is legal advice, but we plan each job knowing which set of rules a property sits under, and we cite the source so the owner's counsel can check it.

Behind the name is one owner who has been installing and repairing entry systems since 2000 and running this company since 2002. Our vans head north each morning from 460 East Fordham Road in the Bronx, and every job is done under New York State low-voltage license #12000287431 with general liability coverage in place. For a Hudson Valley manager, that means one vendor record, one number to call for every address, and a technician who books into the next open early-day window at whichever property needs one, seven days a week.

Numbers and rules we work from north of the city line

  • Which housing law applies. Multiple Residence Law §3 says the chapter applies to "all cities of less than three hundred twenty-five thousand population" and to all towns and villages. Yonkers, the region's largest city at 211,569 residents in the 2020 Census, is below that line, so the city-only Multiple Dwelling Law does not govern its apartment buildings.
  • Building security is a maintenance item. The 2025 Property Maintenance Code of New York State, §304.18, requires doors, windows and hatchways of dwelling units to have "devices designed to provide security for the occupants and property within," and §304.18.1 sets rules for deadbolts on rented units, including a one-inch minimum throw and opening from the inside without a key.
  • The warranty of habitability is statewide. Real Property Law §235-b treats every residential lease as a promise that the apartment and the areas used in connection with it are fit to live in and free of conditions dangerous to life, health or safety. Broken entry doors and dead intercoms are the kind of complaint that lands there.
  • Rent stabilization reaches the suburbs. Westchester County's housing department lists eighteen municipalities that adopted the Emergency Tenant Protection Act, including Yonkers, New Rochelle, Mount Vernon, White Plains, Port Chester, Tarrytown and Sleepy Hollow. The state housing agency reports that Kingston adopted it on July 28, 2022 for buildings of six or more units built before January 1, 1974.
  • Licensing is county by county. Westchester requires plumbers and electricians to hold a county trade license and licenses alarm businesses under County Code Chapter 863; Rockland's Chapter 250 defines electrical contractors by work on systems of 50 volts or more. Statewide, General Business Law §69-m governs security and fire alarm installers.
  • Weather takes systems down. Orange & Rockland Utilities reported on July 6, 2026 that more than 47,000 customers had been hit by extreme heat, thunderstorms and heavy rain since July 1. Central Hudson reported more than 19,000 homes and businesses out after a September 2026 nor'easter, and Con Edison reported more than 5,000 Westchester customers out after a June 2026 storm.
  • Package theft follows the rentals. Daily Voice reported in April 2026 that Haverstraw police recovered packages taken from several buildings inside one apartment complex after serving a search warrant.

What breaks most often at suburban and small-city rentals

The list differs from the city in ways that matter for planning. Exterior doors on garden buildings take far more weather than a vestibule door in Manhattan, so strikes freeze, closers lose tension in the cold and latches stop catching after a summer of swelling. Outdoor cameras on long cable runs pick up surges every thunderstorm season. Entry panels mounted on posts at a complex entrance get hit by snowplows and moving trucks. Recorders sit in a maintenance garage or a laundry building with no climate control, and drives fail in the heat. Wireless links between buildings fade when the trees leaf out in May. And because the office is far away, small failures sit unreported for weeks: a camera that went dark in March is often discovered in June, the day someone needs the footage.

Who we answer to at a managed property, and how

The property manager or managing agent approves the work, so every quote, appointment and invoice goes through the office. We do not agree dates or prices with tenants on our own, and when a resident reaches us first, the report goes back to the manager who decides what happens.

The owner wants a number before work begins and a system the next manager can still run. Our written scopes list equipment, labor and any platform fee separately, and if there is a chance an old cable will fail its test and add cost, we say so before anything is ordered, because ordered equipment cannot be returned.

The resident manager or super is often the only person on site at a large complex. They need to know where the controller and power supply are, what each light on the panel means, and when to call. We walk them through it at handover and leave labels and an equipment map where they will find them.

HOA management firms work for a board rather than an owner, and the board votes on spending. For those communities we write scopes the board can approve at one meeting, and the condo and HOA security page for the Hudson Valley covers the board side in more depth.

Residents judge the whole system by two moments: getting home with groceries and getting a visitor upstairs. When they report a problem, it comes to us through the office, which keeps the work authorized and the paperwork in one place.

Entry panels and intercoms: where most resident complaints start

"Nobody can buzz me in" is still the most common call a management office gets, and north of the city the reasons are varied. In a downtown Westchester rental it is usually a cloud panel whose internet or subscription lapsed. In a 1920s village walk-up it is an audio system whose transformer has been cooking in an unvented closet for decades. At a garden complex it may be twelve separate entry panels, one per building, each installed by a different contractor at a different time. Our approach is the same in all three: test the wiring before recommending anything, keep what still works, and replace only the part that has failed or cannot be supported.

When the cable between the entrance and the apartments tests clean, a new panel can usually go in on the existing wires, which keeps the job inside the lobby and the closet and spares the tenants a hallway full of ladders. When it does not, we price a new run per building so the owner can decide one address at a time. Panels that ring residents' phones avoid apartment wiring altogether, but a share of tenants in most buildings do not want an app, so we keep in-unit stations available. New entry systems and building-wide upgrades are covered on our intercom installation page for the Hudson Valley, where multi-unit video systems are published at $1,200–$5,000; single faults in an existing system are handled on the Hudson Valley intercom repair page. Managers weighing repair against replacement for a board-run building can also read our guide to co-op board intercom upgrades and our condo intercom replacement guide; the decision steps are the same ones an owner faces.

Buzzers and door releases on older buildings

A large part of the region's rental stock still runs on simple buzzer systems: a button at the door, a buzzer in each apartment, and a release that pulls an electric strike. They are cheap to keep running when someone who understands them does the work. The usual faults are a burned-out transformer, a strike knocked out of alignment by a settling door frame, a button corroded by road salt, or a broken pair somewhere in the riser. Published Hudson Valley prices for that work sit on our door buzzer repair page for the Hudson Valley: an apartment station swap at $250–$525, a transformer at $325–$650, a strike with alignment at $450–$950, a lobby panel or faceplate at $650–$1,800 and riser work at $850–$2,400. If a buzzer building is due for video, the existing pairs can often carry a two-wire video system, which keeps the project small.

Fobs, readers and access control across many doors

Suburban properties have more doors per resident than city buildings. A garden complex can have a front and rear door on every building, plus a laundry room, a clubhouse, a fitness room, a pool gate, a maintenance garage and a dumpster enclosure. Putting all of those on one fob system lets the office add a new tenant once and have them open every door they should, and cancel a moved-out tenant once and lock them out of all of them. The cloud-managed version also lets a manager in White Plains change a credential at a property in Orange County without anyone driving there.

The hardware side matters just as much. Exterior doors need strikes and maglocks rated for outdoor temperatures, readers that tolerate snow and direct sun, request-to-exit devices that keep egress free, and power supplies with battery backup that hold the doors in the right state when the power drops. Locks that release when the fire alarm activates, and maglocks that release from the door hardware, are part of a correct installation, not an extra. Single-door systems start at $1,500 on our Hudson Valley access control installation page; multi-door and multi-building systems are quoted after the walk-through.

One point on privacy. Outside the city there is no equivalent of New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act, but a fob system still records when each resident comes and goes. We set the system to keep only what the office needs, give each staff member a separate login with only the permissions their job requires, and remove former residents' credentials at move-out, and we recommend the owner's counsel review any written privacy notice to tenants.

Elevator access for downtown rental towers

The newer elevator buildings in Yonkers, White Plains and New Rochelle often have amenity floors, roof decks, garage levels and leasing offices that residents should reach only with a credential, and some owners want floors locked off during lease-up. Elevator access control ties a reader in the cab to the floor buttons through the elevator controller, which means the elevator company has to be part of the job. We coordinate that work with the building's elevator contractor rather than touching the controller ourselves. Published prices begin at $1,875 per car in Westchester for a card, fob or code reader, rising by county, on our elevator access control page for the Hudson Valley.

Cameras for grounds, lots and common areas (the short version)

Cameras get their own pages, so this is the manager's summary. At suburban properties the hard part is rarely the lobby; it is the parking lot two hundred feet from the nearest building, the mailbox kiosk at the entrance road, and the path between buildings that has no wall to mount on. We plan coverage around the incidents an office actually deals with, usually package theft, car break-ins, dumping, damaged gates and slip-and-fall claims, and we keep cameras away from apartment windows and doors. Recording stays local by default, on a recorder in a locked room, with remote viewing for the office.

For the details, our apartment building camera installation page for the Hudson Valley covers rental buildings and complexes, and the general security camera installation page for the Hudson Valley lists four-camera systems from $1,099. Board-run communities have their own camera pages for co-op camera systems in the Hudson Valley and condo and HOA camera systems in the Hudson Valley. We do not sell a monitored video service on this page; cameras record and alert, and the office or the owner decides who watches.

Package rooms, mail kiosks and delivery doors

Garden complexes were not designed for the volume of parcels that arrive now. Boxes end up stacked in every vestibule, on every stoop and against the cluster mailboxes at the entrance, and anyone driving through can take them. The fixes are straightforward: a single lockable package room or a converted storage room that residents open with their own fob, a courier code or reader on that door so drivers can drop parcels without access to the hallways, and one camera inside the room aimed at the shelves rather than the door. In downtown towers, where a package room usually exists, the problem is more often an old reader, a door that does not close on its own, or a camera that stopped recording. Electronic parcel lockers come from the locker companies themselves; we do not sell them.

Intrusion alarms where nobody lives

Most rental buildings do not need an intrusion alarm on the residential floors, but many managed properties have spaces that do: the leasing office, a maintenance garage full of tools and equipment, a clubhouse with furniture and electronics, and units sitting empty between seasons at a rural rental. Central-station monitoring through our provider runs $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, as published on our Hudson Valley alarm installation page. Some towns require an alarm permit and penalize repeated false alarms, and the Town of Cortlandt, for example, ties its residential permit to a county-licensed alarm business and can revoke it after more than four false alarms in six months, so we check the local rule when we set up the account.

Fire alarm systems

Fire alarm work is in our scope in the Hudson Valley. Outside the city there is no FDNY plan exam; approval runs through the local code enforcement office or fire prevention bureau under the New York State Uniform Code, starting with drawings from an engineer or architect, then installation, then an acceptance test witnessed by the authority. We take a managed property through that sequence on our fire alarm installation page for the Hudson Valley. We do not publish fire alarm prices; every system is priced per building from the approved drawings. Where a building has electric locks, the fire alarm has to release them, so we plan both systems together.

Cabling, networks and surge protection

Everything above depends on cable and power. We run Cat6 and riser cable, mount racks, install PoE switches and battery backup, and set up a separate network for the security systems so the leasing office Wi-Fi and the laundry card machines never compete with the cameras. At complexes with several buildings, we run cable between them through existing conduit where it exists, or link them with point-to-point wireless where new underground work is not practical. Long outdoor runs get surge protection at both ends, because the region's summer storms reliably find unprotected camera cable. Per-drop pricing of $150–$300 is published on our Hudson Valley structured cabling page. We do not do electrical work; a new 120-volt circuit for a gate operator or a rack goes to the building's electrician, and we coordinate with them.

Internet and cell service at remote properties

A downtown rental usually has fiber in the street. A rural townhouse community in Ulster or the far side of Orange County may have one cable provider, weak cell signal at the entrance, and a leasing office connection that drops in every storm. Before we recommend a cloud panel or a phone-dialing gate box, we test the signal and the connection where the equipment will sit. Where internet is unreliable, we lean toward systems that keep working locally when the connection drops, such as access control that stores credentials at the door and recorders that keep recording without the cloud, and use cellular backup or cellular cameras only where the signal is strong enough to support them.

Settings we put in place for privacy and footage

At handover we set retention on each recorder to a period the owner chooses, give every staff user a separate account, limit exports to named people, and turn off audio recording on common-area cameras unless the owner's counsel approves it, since New York's eavesdropping law can apply to recorded conversations. We record the settings in the building file so a new manager knows what the system keeps and for how long.

One contact for the whole list

The office gets one number for every property, one vendor record, and one technician team that already knows the buildings. When a call comes in about a property we have worked at before, the technician arrives with the notes, the equipment model and, where possible, the replacement part. That is how a two-trip repair becomes a one-trip repair when the property is an hour from the office.

Making separate systems behave like one

Integration is the step most portfolios skip. When the entry panel, the fob system and the cameras come from compatible platforms, an office manager can see the clip from the door that a credential just opened, cancel a resident across every door at once, and check whether the panel at a distant property is online without driving there. We do not force a single brand onto every building. We standardize where it pays, usually starting with the buildings that need replacement anyway, and leave working systems in place until they are due.

Staffed lobby services and remote watching

We do not run a virtual doorman, remote concierge or monitored video service. Those are staffed services sold by companies that watch the cameras and answer the door for the building. What we do is install and maintain the entry panels, cameras, readers and network such a provider needs, and connect to one the owner already uses. If an owner wants a staffed service, we will wire the building for it; the service contract stays between the owner and the provider.

When the resident would rather not use an app

Senior rental communities and older tenants in long-held buildings often prefer an in-unit station, a large-button handset or a keypad code over an app. We keep those options in every design, mount panels at a height a seated person can reach, choose readers that work with a card held anywhere near them, and check that the door opens wide enough and long enough for a walker or wheelchair after a release.

Commercial properties in the same portfolio

Many Hudson Valley firms manage office parks, medical buildings, retail strips and storefront space alongside the apartments. The same contractor can handle them: access control for tenant suites, cameras for lots and loading areas, alarms for stores and storage, and cabling for small offices. We keep commercial work on the same vendor record so the office does not onboard a second security company for the retail side of the list.

Seasonal and short-term rentals

Owners with furnished or seasonal units in the Catskill and mid-Hudson towns can have keypad smart locks and an entry camera installed so codes change between stays.

Building Types

Property Management Security for the Hudson Valley's Building Stock

The region's rental housing comes in shapes the city barely has. The same management firm can run a twenty-story tower and a cluster of two-story garden buildings in the same week, and each one needs a different plan for doors, wiring and cameras.

Downtown elevator rentals

New towers near the Metro-North stations in Yonkers, White Plains and New Rochelle: fob lobbies, garage gates, package rooms, amenity floors and leasing offices, often on a developer-chosen cloud platform.

Village apartment houses

Early-1900s brick and stone buildings on main streets from Port Chester to Kingston: audio intercoms, narrow vestibules and old risers inside plaster walls.

Garden complexes

Two- and three-story buildings spread across lawns and lots, each with its own entrances, plus a laundry building, pool and maintenance garage.

Townhouse rentals

Attached homes with private front doors, shared roads and parking, and sometimes an entrance gate or a clubhouse.

Rent-stabilized buildings

Pre-1974 buildings of six or more units in the municipalities that adopted the ETPA, where service changes need care and documentation.

Affordable and senior housing

Newer tax-credit and senior rentals with staffed offices during the day, accessibility needs and funder reporting.

Mixed-use main-street buildings

Apartments over storefronts, with separate residential and commercial entrances that must not share keys.

HOA-managed communities

Condominium and homeowners' association properties run by the same management firms, governed by a board rather than one owner.

Downtown towers: taking over a platform someone else picked

When a newly built rental changes hands or a new management firm is hired, the first problem is usually ownership of the software. The entry panel, the fob platform and the camera system were set up by the developer's contractor, the administrator account sits in an email address nobody controls, and the subscription renews on a card that belongs to the developer. Before anything else we help the office get the administrator rights moved into the owner's name, document which doors and floors are on which system, and find what was never finished during construction. Those buildings often have amenity doors, bike rooms or storage cages that were left on mechanical keys because the fob work ran out of budget.

Village walk-ups: working with what is in the walls

A four-story building over storefronts in Ossining, Peekskill, Nyack or Beacon may have the same intercom pairs it got after the Second World War. Those wires are often fine. The failures are at the ends: a corroded button plate, a handset a tenant removed, a strike that no longer meets the keeper because the door frame moved. Repairs at that end are cheap. Replacing the whole system makes sense only when the riser fails its test or the owner wants video, and even then a two-wire video system can often reuse the pairs. We test before quoting so the owner never pays to rewire a building that did not need it.

Garden complexes: many small doors instead of one big one

A garden complex has no single front door to protect. Instead it has building entrances spread over acres, connected by paths and parking. That changes three things. Credentials need to work across all the doors at once, so a cloud-managed or networked fob system usually beats separate keypads. The network between buildings is the hardest part of the job, because every building needs a path back to the controller and the recorder. And the outside matters more than the inside: lighting, sight lines across the parking area and the entrance road are where most incidents happen, so camera planning starts outdoors.

Townhouse rentals: private doors, shared grounds

Townhouse rentals sit between a single-family house and an apartment building. Each unit's front door is private, so a shared entry panel rarely fits, and the owner usually wants a wired video doorbell on each unit that the tenant controls, with credentials reset at every turnover. The shared parts of the property, such as the road, mailboxes, dumpster area and any clubhouse or pool, belong to the owner, and that is where management cameras and readers go. Keeping those two layers separate avoids the common complaint that management can see a tenant's front door.

Rent-stabilized buildings under the ETPA

Many pre-1974 buildings of six or more units in Yonkers, Mount Vernon, New Rochelle, White Plains and the other ETPA municipalities are rent-stabilized, and since 2022 so are qualifying buildings in Kingston. In those buildings, the services tenants have, including the intercom and the way the front door works, are part of what the owner must maintain, and changes are best planned with the owner's counsel and the state housing agency's rules in view. Our part is practical: we keep the existing level of service running during any upgrade, avoid switching tenants to a system that needs a smartphone without keeping another option, and give the owner dated, itemized records of what was repaired or replaced.

Affordable, supportive and senior rentals

Newer affordable and senior buildings in the region often have a staffed front office during the day and nobody at night. The entry system has to work for both: a desk that can see and release the door during office hours, and a panel that rings residents or an on-call manager after hours. Accessibility drives hardware choices here, and funders and agencies sometimes ask for documentation of the systems in place, so we write up what we install in plain language the office can file.

Mixed-use buildings on main streets

Apartments over a pharmacy, a deli or a salon need a hard line between the residential entrance and the commercial space. The store's staff should not have a key to the apartment stair, and residents should not be able to reach the shop's back room through a shared cellar. We put those boundaries in the access plan, keep the store's own alarm and cameras separate from the building's unless the owner wants them combined, and make sure a camera aimed at the residential door is the building's, not the tenant's.

HOA and condominium communities run by the same firm

Many Hudson Valley management firms carry HOA and condominium contracts next to their rentals. Our method is the same; the approval path is different, since the board votes and the owners pay through common charges. We write those scopes so the board can approve them at one meeting and keep the community's records separate from the firm's rental properties.

Working in occupied rentals

We work in common areas first, keep the old system running until the new one is ready, and switch over in one short window. For any work that needs access to apartments, the office schedules those appointments, and we ask for access to every affected unit on the same visit, because intercom faults in one apartment are often caused by wiring in another.

Turnover, lost fobs and seasonal residents

We set up systems the office can run itself: adding a new resident, cancelling a former one, replacing a lost fob, editing the entry directory and issuing temporary codes for movers. Where an office wants us to handle the programming instead, we do it on a scheduled visit. For communities where residents leave for the winter, access can be suspended and restored on dates rather than deleted.

Inheriting another installer's equipment

Most of our first visits for a management firm are at buildings someone else wired. We document what is there, recover or reset administrator access with the owner's authorization, test the riser and cameras, and tell the office what is worth keeping. We will repair a system we did not install, and we are clear about which parts we cannot get.

Renovations and capital work

When an owner is renovating a lobby, replacing exterior doors or repaving a lot, that is the cheapest time to change the security system. Walls are open, doors are off their hinges and trenches are already dug. We like to be brought in before the general contractor closes up, so conduit, back boxes and door preps go in at the right time and the security work does not have to undo new finishes.

Fixing the old system versus starting over

We recommend repair when the failure is isolated, parts are available and the cable tests well. We recommend replacement when the same fault keeps coming back, parts are discontinued, the system cannot do something the owner now needs, or a renovation is opening the walls anyway. In between, we give the owner both numbers and let them decide.

Seasons: freeze, thaw and storm

Winter brings frozen strikes, salt-corroded panels and snowplow damage at gate posts. Spring thaw shifts door frames, so strikes that worked in February stop catching in April. Summer brings the thunderstorm season, when surge damage to outdoor cameras and gate equipment peaks, and the leaf-out that blocks wireless links between buildings. Fall is the window to fix doors and replace backup batteries before cold weather. Package theft rises through the holiday weeks, which is why package-room projects are best finished before November. We plan maintenance visits around that calendar, and we do not run seasonal discounts or specials.

Incident records and insurance

Camera footage and door logs are only useful if someone can retrieve them later. We set clear export permissions, show office staff how to save a clip, and make sure the recorder's clock is correct, which matters more than most people realize when footage is used in a claim. We do not promise that a system will lower an insurance premium; that is up to the carrier.

RFPs, bids and scopes of work

We respond to RFPs and bid requests from management firms and owners. A scope that lets bids be compared lists each property and door, states whether existing wiring is to be reused, names the platform or asks bidders to name theirs, separates equipment, labor and recurring fees, and says who owns the administrator accounts at the end. We bid as the licensed contractor and provide the insurance certificate once the work is awarded. We do not hand out client references or building addresses; our Google reviews are public.

Small walk-up or sprawling complex: same method, different scale

A 12-unit walk-up usually needs one entry panel, one controlled door, a handful of cameras and a small recorder, and the whole job can be done in a day. A 300-unit garden complex needs a credential system across dozens of doors, a network across the grounds, cameras planned around roads and lots, a recorder sized for weeks of footage, and a schedule that sets up one building at a time so residents are never locked out. The approach is the same; the planning effort scales with the number of doors and buildings rather than the number of units.

Rules a Hudson Valley property manager should know

  • Multiple Residence Law, not the Multiple Dwelling Law. Outside New York City, apartment buildings fall under the Multiple Residence Law (§3) and local codes.
  • Property Maintenance Code. §304.18 requires security devices on doors, windows and hatchways of dwelling units, and §304.18.1 governs deadbolts on rented units.
  • Warranty of habitability. Real Property Law §235-b applies to every residential lease in the state, including the areas used in connection with the apartment.
  • ETPA municipalities. Rent stabilization applies in the eighteen Westchester municipalities on the county's list and in Kingston for qualifying buildings.
  • Licensing. Electrical work requires a licensed electrician; alarm businesses are licensed by the state under GBL §69-m and, in Westchester, by the county.
  • Alarm permits. Some towns require permits for monitored alarms and penalize repeated false alarms.
  • Fire alarm approval. Local code enforcement or the fire prevention bureau approves fire alarm systems under the Uniform Code; there is no city-style plan exam.
Your Questions Answered

Property Management Security in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

Complexes, townhouses and grounds

A garden complex has a dozen separate entrances and no lobby. Where should the first fob readers go?

Start with the doors that let a stranger reach shared space without passing anyone: the main entrance of each building, then the laundry and package rooms, then rear doors that face parking or woods. Amenity doors like the pool gate and fitness room can follow in a second phase. Put every door on the same system from the first day, even if only a few get readers at first, so the office never ends up running two fob platforms on one property.

Our townhouse rentals each have their own front door. Should every unit get a video doorbell, or should the complex share one entry system?

Usually a doorbell per unit for the private door, plus a shared system only where the community has a shared entrance such as a gate or clubhouse. A shared panel at a cluster of private front doors confuses visitors and delivery drivers. The doorbell should be wired to the unit's existing transformer, and the office resets its account at every move-out so the next tenant does not inherit the previous tenant's recordings.

When should a camera go on a pole instead of a building wall?

When the thing you need to see is far from any wall. A camera on the corner of a building gets a sharp picture of the first thirty or forty feet and a poor one of the far end of a long lot. A camera on a pole in the middle of the lot sees both ends at a useful size. Poles also help at entrance roads and mailbox kiosks. The catch is power and cable, so a pole that already carries the property's lighting is the easiest place to start, as long as the owner, not the utility, owns it.

A parking lot sits far from any building with power. How do you get a camera out there?

There are three options, in order of reliability. A cable in conduit to the nearest building carries both power and video and works for years without attention. A wireless link from a building to the pole can carry video when a cable run is not practical, but it needs a clear line of sight. A solar camera with a cellular connection needs neither, but it depends on sun and signal, and it records less than a wired camera. We test the signal and sun exposure at the spot before recommending the third option.

Our complex pool opens on Memorial Day weekend and closes after Labor Day. Can pool access turn on and off with the season?

Yes. The pool gate reader can follow a schedule with a start and end date as well as daily hours, so it opens for residents only during the season and only during posted hours. If the owner requires a signed pool rule acknowledgment, the office can enable each resident's access as their form comes in. Out of season, the reader stays locked without anyone remembering to change it.

Weather, wiring and older equipment

After the utility restores power to a building, why do some cameras come back and others stay dark?

Usually because the power did not come back cleanly. A surge or a series of flickers at restoration can damage a network switch port, a camera's power circuit or the recorder's power supply, while other devices on protected circuits survive. A recorder sometimes restarts before the switch and loses the cameras until it is rebooted. If cameras stay dark after an outage, call it in rather than resetting equipment yourself, and ask us about battery backup and surge protection for the closet so the next outage is shorter.

Older two-story garden buildings have intercom wire stapled along the outside. Can it be replaced or protected?

Yes, and it usually should be. Exposed cable on a facade gets brittle in the sun, cracked by ice and cut by landscapers. Where there is a path inside the building, we move the run inside. Where there isn't, we replace it with outdoor-rated cable in a paintable raceway or conduit so it is protected and neater. If the old run has caused intermittent faults, replacing it often fixes problems that were blamed on the panel.

Can the coax cable from an old analog camera system be reused when the cameras are replaced?

Often, yes. Modern HD-over-coax cameras and recorders run on the same coax at much higher resolution, and adapters can carry network cameras over coax where a new cable would be hard to pull. Whether it is worth it depends on the condition of the cable and the connectors, which we test. On short runs that are easy to replace, new Cat6 is usually the better long-term choice.

Rules, records and residents

Our Westchester building is rent-stabilized under the ETPA. Does that change how we plan a lobby upgrade?

It changes how carefully you plan it. In a stabilized building, the intercom and door are services tenants already have, so the upgrade should keep every apartment's ability to be called and let visitors in, including tenants who will not use an app. Ask the owner's counsel about any filing or notice the state housing agency expects before starting, and keep our dated invoices and work descriptions with the building records. We handle the installation side so service is not interrupted while the new system goes in.

How do you keep outdoor camera cable from being cut by landscaping crews and string trimmers?

By keeping it out of their reach. Outdoor runs at ground level go in conduit or under the edge of a walkway, cable on walls is mounted high or inside raceway, and the last few feet to a camera rise straight up the wall rather than across a planting bed. We also tell the site manager where every outdoor run is, so the landscaping contract can note it.

The property maintenance code mentions deadbolts on apartment doors. Is that your work or a locksmith's?

A locksmith's. Mechanical deadbolts and unit door locks are outside our scope. We handle the electronic side of a door: strikes, maglocks, readers, keypads, door position switches and request-to-exit devices. If an electronic lock is going on an apartment door, we coordinate with the locksmith so the electronic and mechanical hardware work together and the door still opens from inside without a key.

Our new downtown rental opened with only some of the amenity doors on the fob system. Can the rest be added later?

Almost always. Most platforms used in new buildings add doors by installing a reader and a controller or a door module and enrolling them on the existing account. The questions are whether there is a cable path to each door, whether the doors were prepped for electric hardware during construction, and whether the platform charges per door. We check all three and price each door separately so the owner can pick which ones come first.

Plowed snow buries the view from our entrance camera every winter. Where should it be mounted instead?

Higher, and back from where the plow throws snow. A camera low on a post or a short wall at the entrance road ends up behind a snowbank by January. Moving it to a taller post, the gatehouse eave or a building corner that looks down the road keeps the view clear, and a longer lens can make up the distance. When we plan entrance cameras we ask the site manager where the piles end up each year and design around them.

Our complex's maintenance garage holds tools, equipment and spare parts. What does it need?

Usually an intrusion alarm with a contact on every door and a motion sensor inside, a camera covering the overhead door from the outside, and a fob or keypad on the walk-in door so the office knows which staff member went in and when. Central-station monitoring is optional; many owners prefer alerts to the property manager's phone for a building like this.

What AI Gets Wrong About Property Management Security in the Hudson Valley

Managers north of the city often arrive with an answer they found online, and the answer was usually written for a Manhattan high-rise or a house in the suburbs of somewhere else. Here are the ones we correct most.

1. "The intercom rule people quote applies up here too"

The intercom requirement people usually mean is in the Multiple Dwelling Law, which covers cities of 325,000 or more. Outside New York City, apartment buildings fall under the Multiple Residence Law and local codes. That does not make a working entry system optional, because the Property Maintenance Code and the warranty of habitability still apply, but the specific rule people quote is a city rule.

2. "Securing a rental means paying for guards"

For most managed rentals it means working doors, an entry system that reaches residents, credentials the office controls and cameras that actually record. Guards and remote monitoring are staffed services some owners add. We do not sell either; we install and maintain the systems they depend on.

3. "A Wi-Fi camera is fine for a parking lot"

Building Wi-Fi rarely reaches a lot more than a short distance from the wall, and it gets weaker as trees fill in. A camera that loses its connection stops sending video, often without anyone noticing. Wired cameras, wireless links built for distance or cellular cameras tested at the spot are the options that work.

4. "Security wiring is just electrical work"

Electricians handle line-voltage circuits; security systems are low-voltage work with their own licensing, and in New York alarm installation is licensed by the state under General Business Law §69-m. The two trades work together on most projects. We do the low-voltage side and coordinate with the building's electrician for anything that needs a new circuit.

5. "Keyless entry in New York requires tenant consent under the privacy law"

The Tenant Data Privacy Act is a New York City law. It does not apply in Westchester or the rest of the valley. A careful owner still limits what a fob system collects and who can see it, and has counsel review the notice to tenants, but the city statute is not the rule here.

6. "Budget about two grand for an apartment intercom"

National cost guides quote a range for a house. For a multi-unit building, our published Hudson Valley range for a video intercom is $1,200–$5,000, and a garden complex with many buildings is priced per building after the survey. A single fault in an existing system is often a few hundred dollars, which is why we test before recommending replacement.

7. "Solar cameras need no maintenance"

Solar panels collect snow, pollen and leaves, batteries lose capacity in the cold, and short winter days mean less charging. In shaded spots, a solar camera can run out of power by midwinter. They are useful in the right place, and we check them on maintenance visits like any other camera.

The five things behind a Hudson Valley quote

Five factors account for most of the difference between two similar-looking quotes. First, whether existing cable can be reused, which can cut a project in half. Hudson Valley Property Management Security FAQ

Is a service call at a Hudson Valley property priced the same as one in the city?
+

Yes. A repair visit anywhere in Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess or Ulster starts with the same $250 service call, which covers the diagnosis and simple repairs. Anything larger is quoted before we do it.

Can one proposal cover properties in several different counties?
+

Yes. A portfolio proposal lists each property with its own scope and price, grouped by county so visits can be routed together. Each owner or board can approve its own lines without approving the rest of the list.

Does the town need to issue a permit before cameras or an intercom go in?
+

It depends on the city, town or village. Permit rules for low-voltage work differ across the region, so we check with the local building department, especially when a job involves new door openings or structural changes. Fire alarm systems always go through local code enforcement, and some towns require permits for monitored alarms.

Do you install wireless links between buildings in winter, or wait for spring?
+

We install them in any season as long as the mounting points can be reached safely. What winter hides is the summer view: a link aimed across bare trees in January can be blocked by leaves in June, so we plan the path for full foliage and mount high enough to clear it.

Do cellular cameras need a data plan, and who pays for it?
+

Yes. A cellular camera needs a data plan from a mobile carrier, and that plan is in the owner's name and billed by the carrier, not by us. We test signal strength at the mounting spot before recommending one and set the camera to record on events so data use stays reasonable.

Can a camera be mounted on the utility company's pole in our parking lot?
+

Generally not. Poles owned by the electric or phone company belong to the utility, and private equipment cannot go on them without the utility's permission. We mount on poles the property owns, such as lot lights, on a new post the owner installs, or on the nearest building with a lens that reaches the area.

+
