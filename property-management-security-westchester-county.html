The County

Why Westchester Managing Agents Want a Single Security Vendor for the Whole List

Ask a managing agent in Westchester to describe the buildings on the books and the answer rarely fits one category. A typical list might hold a five-story brick walk-up off South Broadway in Yonkers with an audio intercom older than anyone on staff, a pair of 1960s elevator buildings in Mount Vernon with a fob lobby added by a previous owner, a downtown New Rochelle rental that opened two years ago on a cloud platform the developer picked, a garden complex in Greenburgh with eight separate entrances and a laundry building, and a mixed-use building on a Port Chester or Ossining main street with apartments over a storefront. Each property arrived with its own installer, its own login and its own idea of who was supposed to fix it.

That patchwork is the real cost of security in a Westchester portfolio. The hardware is rarely the expensive part; the expensive part is the office chasing a different contractor for every building, paying a second service call when the first one arrived without the right part, and losing a week while nobody can find the administrator password for a panel in a building the firm took over last spring. Property management security in this county is mostly a records problem dressed up as an equipment problem. Our work for a management firm starts by putting every building on one vendor record, documenting what is installed where, and making sure the next visit begins with notes instead of guesswork.

The numbers explain why Westchester needs that discipline more than most suburbs. The county health department's September 2025 housing brief, built on American Community Survey data for 2019 through 2023, counts 139,962 renter-occupied homes in Westchester against 230,294 owner-occupied ones, so nearly two in five households rent. The same brief shows that 27.7 percent of the county's homes were built in 1939 or earlier, which is why so many lobbies still run on cloth-insulated intercom pairs and strikes set into frames that have been repainted for eighty years. At the other end of the timeline, a development report from RM Friedland published through the Business Council of Westchester counts roughly 12,500 residential units delivered countywide since late 2021, with Yonkers, New Rochelle, White Plains and Mount Vernon accounting for 9,413 delivered units and 13,114 more under construction. A firm can be managing a 1920s walk-up and a building that opened last year on the same afternoon.

The legal setting is also different from the city a few miles south. Westchester apartment buildings fall under the state's Multiple Residence Law, which section 3 applies to cities of fewer than 325,000 people and to every town and village, rather than the Multiple Dwelling Law that governs New York City. There is no HPD here; complaints go to the city, town or village building department, which enforces the state Uniform Code and Property Maintenance Code. Eighteen Westchester municipalities have adopted the Emergency Tenant Protection Act, so many pre-1974 buildings of six or more apartments are rent-stabilized. What fails most often in Westchester rental buildings

Across the calls we take from Westchester management offices, the failures sort into a short list. The commonest is a front door that stops latching: a strike pushed out of line as an old wood or steel frame settles, a closer that lost its adjustment, or a keeper worn by decades of slamming. Next comes an audio intercom where one floor cannot hear the lobby, usually a broken pair in the riser or a handset a tenant removed. Third is the cloud panel in a newer building that went offline because the building's internet account changed hands or a subscription lapsed in the developer's name. Fourth is the camera recorder in a boiler room or a closet with no ventilation, where the hard drive gave up in August. And fifth is the garage gate or overhead door reader that stopped talking to the access system after a power event. Every one of those starts with the $250 service call, and most are finished on the first visit when the technician has the building's history in hand.

The people we answer to on a Westchester job

The managing agent authorizes the work, so every quote, every appointment and every invoice runs through the office. A tenant who calls us directly is pointed back to the office, and nothing is scheduled with a resident on our own.

The owner or asset manager wants a figure before anything is ordered and a system that survives a change of management. Our scopes list hardware, labor and any platform fee on separate lines, and when there is a real chance that an old cable will fail testing and add cost, the owner hears about it before the equipment is ordered, because ordered equipment cannot be sent back.

The superintendent is the person who meets us at the door and calls when something breaks at night. Supers in Westchester often cover two or three buildings, so we label the controller, the power supply and the recorder, and leave a one-page map in the closet showing what each light means and when to call.

Residents experience the whole system in two moments: getting in with their hands full and letting a visitor up. Their complaints are the best early warning a management office gets, and when they reach us they go back to the office, which keeps the work authorized and the paperwork in one place.

Lobby panels and apartment intercoms

"My buzzer doesn't work" is the call every Westchester management office knows by heart, and the cause depends on the building's age. In a prewar Yonkers or Mount Vernon walk-up it is usually the low-voltage transformer in the cellar, a corroded button plate at the street door, or one broken conductor in a riser that runs through plaster walls. In a 1960s or 1970s elevator building it is often a lobby amplifier that has been patched for decades and can no longer be repaired. In a building that opened since 2020 it is almost always the network: the panel is fine, but the internet account it depends on was cancelled, moved or throttled. We test before we recommend anything. If the riser is sound and only an end has failed, we fix the end. If the old panel cannot be supported any longer, a new one can often run on the existing pairs, which keeps the job in the lobby and the closet instead of in every apartment.

Repairs to existing systems are covered on our Westchester intercom repair page and our door buzzer repair page for Westchester County, which publishes $250–$525 to replace an apartment station, $325–$650 for a transformer, $450–$950 for a strike with alignment, $650–$1,800 for lobby panel or faceplate work and $850–$2,400 for riser pair repair or re-termination. New and replacement systems are on our intercom installation page for Westchester County, which lists video intercoms for four- to eight-unit buildings at $1,200–$3,500 and app-connected systems for larger buildings at $2,500–$8,000 and up. Agents weighing repair against replacement can also use our guide to intercom upgrades for co-op boards and the condo intercom replacement guide; the decision steps for an owner are much the same as those for a board.

One habit of ours matters in rent-stabilized and older buildings: we never move a building to a phone-only entry system without keeping a way to reach tenants who don't use smartphones. A panel that rings residents' phones is a sensible upgrade for a building of commuters, but a building with long-term tenants in their seventies needs in-unit stations or a landline option, and the owner should hear that before choosing a platform.

Strikes, maglocks and the door itself

The best intercom in the county is worthless if the door it releases doesn't close and latch. A large share of our Westchester service calls are really door calls: a strike whose keeper no longer meets the latch because the frame moved, a maglock that holds unevenly because its armature plate came loose, a closer that slams in winter and creeps in summer, or a door that has been propped so often its hinges sag. We set the electrified hardware, adjust it to the latch and test the release from every apartment station we can reach. Where the door itself needs a closer, a hinge or a new frame, we tell the office so a door contractor or locksmith can handle that part; mechanical locks and deadbolts on apartment doors stay with the locksmith.

Exit devices matter as much as entry devices. A maglock on an exit route needs a way out that works without the power, a request-to-exit sensor or button, and a connection that releases it when the fire alarm activates. A strike on a front door must still let people leave with a turn of the handle. We install those pieces as part of the job, not as extras, and if a building's existing hardware does not meet that standard, we say so in writing.

Fobs, readers and multi-door access control

A fob system is how a management office stops cutting keys. Done right, the office adds a new tenant once and that tenant opens every door they should: the street door, the garage, the laundry room, the package room, the gym on the amenity floor. When the tenant moves out, one click locks them out everywhere. In a Westchester portfolio the hard part is not the reader; it is consolidation. Many firms inherit buildings with three different fob platforms, each with its own fob format, its own dashboard and its own monthly bill. We document what each building has, tell the owner which platforms are worth keeping, and plan the moves so a building changes system only when it makes financial sense.

Our access control installation page for Westchester County publishes $1,500–$2,500 for a single-door keypad or fob reader, $4,000–$15,000 for multi-door systems covering a lobby, service door and basement, and $15,000–$40,000 and up for a full building with elevator restriction. Those are installed figures; recurring platform fees, where a platform has them, are shown on a separate line so the owner can compare the long-term cost of each option. New York City's Tenant Data Privacy Act does not reach Westchester, but a fob system still logs every entry by every tenant, so we set retention to what the office actually needs, give each staff member a separate login with only the permissions their job calls for, and remove former tenants' credentials at move-out.

Garage gates, overhead doors and parking

Westchester rental buildings park cars in ways the city rarely does: underground garages under downtown towers in White Plains and New Rochelle, surface lots behind Mount Vernon elevator buildings, carports and open lots at garden complexes. Each one has an entry point that should respond to the same credential as the front door. We add readers or long-range credentials at garage gates and overhead doors, connect them to the building's access system, and coordinate with the gate or door company that services the operator itself, because the motor and the safety edges belong to them. Cameras at the garage entry and along the lot are covered on our Westchester parking lot camera page.

Elevator access in downtown towers

Newer rental towers often lock amenity floors, roof decks and garage levels to residents with a credential, and some owners restrict floors during lease-up or renovation. A reader in the cab tied to the floor buttons does that, but it connects through the elevator controller, so the building's elevator maintenance company has to be part of the job. We schedule that work with them rather than touching their equipment. Our elevator access control page for Westchester County publishes $1,875 per car for a Level 1 card, fob, code or intercom reader and $3,125 per car for a Level 2 biometric reader.

Cameras for lobbies, halls and lots (the short version)

Cameras have their own pages, so here is the management summary. In Westchester rental buildings the incidents that actually reach the office are package theft from vestibules and lobbies, car break-ins in lots, dumping by the trash area, damage to doors and panels, and slip-and-fall claims on stairs and walks. We plan coverage around those events, keep cameras out of apartment doorways and windows, and record locally on a recorder in a locked room, with remote viewing for the office. Our apartment building security camera installation page for Westchester County covers rental buildings in detail, and the general Westchester security camera installation page lists four-camera systems from $1,099, with the full model range on our Westchester camera pricing page. Package theft deserves a line of its own because it drives so many calls. The pattern we see is consistent with that: a camera inside the vestibule aimed at where parcels are left, a door that actually latches, and a package room or locker area reachable only with a credential do more than any single gadget. We install the doors, readers and cameras; electronic parcel lockers are sold by the locker companies, not by us.

Intrusion alarms for leasing offices, storage and empty units

Residential floors rarely need an intrusion alarm, but a managed property usually has spaces that do: the leasing office with computers and files, a super's workshop full of tools, a storage room of appliances waiting for turnovers, and apartments sitting empty during a gut renovation. Central-station monitoring through our provider is $30 a month keypad-only or $45 a month with the phone app, as published on our alarm installation page for Westchester County. Local rules matter here: the City of White Plains alarm permit rules charge $25 for the first false alarm police or fire respond to in a calendar year, then $50, $75, $100 and finally $250 per false alarm as the count climbs, so we set entry delays, sensor placement and user codes to keep a busy leasing office from running up that bill.

Fire alarm systems for managed buildings

Fire alarm work is in our scope in Westchester. Outside New York City there is no FDNY plan review; approval runs through the city, town or village code enforcement office or fire prevention bureau under the state Uniform Code. The sequence is drawings from an engineer or architect, approval, installation and an acceptance test witnessed by the authority, and our fire alarm installation page for Westchester County walks a managed property through it. We do not publish fire alarm prices; each system is priced from the approved drawings. Where a building has electric locks, the fire alarm must release them, which is one more reason to plan both systems together.

Cabling, risers and networks

Everything above rides on cable. We pull Cat6 and riser cable, set racks and PoE switches, add battery backup, and build a separate network for security equipment so the cameras, readers and panels never compete with the leasing office Wi-Fi or a laundry vendor's card machines. In older buildings the route is the hard part: plaster walls, abandoned risers full of dead cable and cellars with no ceiling to hide anything in. Our structured cabling page for Westchester County lists Cat6 data drops at $175 and up per drop. We do not do electrical work; a new 120-volt circuit for a gate operator, a rack or a panel goes to the building's licensed electrician, and we coordinate with them.

Power, outages and surge protection

Southern Westchester sits on Con Edison's grid, and the utility's own 2026 updates show how often storms reach these buildings: more than 5,000 Westchester customers out after a June storm, and a July round of storms in which, by Con Edison's account, Westchester took the greatest impact, led by Greenburgh, Yonkers, Elmsford, Dobbs Ferry and Rye. When power flickers on and off at restoration, the equipment that dies first is the equipment without protection: a PoE switch, a panel power supply, a recorder that boots before its cameras and loses them. We put battery backup on the access controller and the network switch, surge protection on long outdoor runs, and a note in the building file explaining what to restart in what order. Doors on fail-secure strikes stay locked from outside during an outage and still open from inside; maglocks release, which is why buildings that depend on them need backup power sized for more than a few minutes.

Video doorbells and smart locks for small buildings

Not every building on a Westchester list needs a lobby panel. Two- and three-family rentals and small houses converted to apartments often do better with a wired video doorbell for each entrance, set up so each tenant controls their own and the owner resets the accounts at turnover. Our Westchester doorbell camera installation page covers that work. For leasing offices, model units and furnished rentals, code locks avoid handing keys back and forth, and our smart lock installation page for Westchester County explains the options.

Who owns the logins

The quiet failure in many Westchester buildings is not hardware at all; it is an administrator account in the name of a developer, a former super or a vendor who has since disappeared. When the account is lost, nothing can be changed, and the owner pays to recover access or to replace working equipment. We put administrator rights in the owner's name, give the managing agent a separate administrator account, and give staff limited accounts. When management changes, the owner removes the old firm's accounts and adds the new one's without anyone rebuilding the system.

Keeping unknown devices out of the entry system

Habitat magazine reported in February 2022 that at a large Westchester co-op in Yonkers, delivery-access devices had been installed inside intercoms without the board's authorization by a local vendor, under agreements signed by someone with no authority, and that the building's own intercom company found them. The building was a co-op, but the lesson applies to every rental on a manager's list: anyone who opens a lobby panel can add something to it. We record what is inside each panel and controller when we first service a building, flag anything nobody can account for, and recommend that the office approve in writing any device added to an entry system, whoever offers it.

Staffed services we do not sell

We do not run a virtual doorman, a remote concierge or a monitored video service, and we do not supply guards. Those are staffed services sold by companies that watch cameras and answer doors for a building. What we do is install and maintain the panels, readers, cameras and network such a provider needs, and connect to one the owner already uses. The service contract stays between the owner and that provider.

New downtown rentals

Garden apartments

Two- and three-story buildings spread across a campus in Greenburgh, Elmsford, the Sound Shore and the northern towns, each with its own entrances.

Prewar walk-ups: the wiring is usually better than its reputation

Owners of old Yonkers and Mount Vernon walk-ups are often told the intercom must be ripped out because the wiring is "too old." Sometimes that is true. More often the copper pairs in the riser are fine and the failures are at the ends: a station a tenant painted over, a lobby button plate eaten by road salt, a transformer that has been running hot for thirty years, or a strike no longer meeting the latch. We test each pair before we write a scope. When the riser tests well, a new panel and new stations go in on the existing wires, and a two-wire video system can often use the same pairs if the owner wants video. When the riser fails, we price new cable per line so the owner can decide floor by floor. Either way, the owner pays for the job the building actually needs.

Walk-ups also have the most abused doors in the county. The street door takes deliveries, strollers, bicycles and moving dollies every day, and many frames are wood that has been patched and painted for a century. A strike set true in a solid frame with a properly adjusted closer is the single most valuable fix in these buildings, and it costs far less than a new intercom.

Postwar elevator buildings: the lobby is the whole system

A 1960s elevator building in Mount Vernon or southwest Yonkers usually has one lobby, one mailroom, one laundry room and a rear door to a lot or a trash area. That simplicity is useful. A video panel at the vestibule, a reader on the inner lobby door, a credential on the rear door and the laundry room, and cameras on the vestibule, the mailroom, the elevator landing and the rear door cover most of the incidents an office deals with. The complications are age and regulation: many of these buildings are rent-stabilized under the ETPA, so the change must keep every tenant's ability to be called and to let visitors in, and the original wiring often runs through a single riser that also carries the building's old TV and phone cable.

New downtown rentals: inheriting someone else's choices

The towers that have gone up in downtown New Rochelle, White Plains and the Yonkers waterfront were built fast, and their security systems show it. The developer's contractor picked a platform, enrolled the doors that were finished before opening, and left. The management firm that arrives a year later often finds the administrator account tied to a developer's email address, a subscription billed to a card the owner does not hold, bike rooms and storage cages still on mechanical keys because the fob budget ran out, and a camera recorder that nobody has logged into since the ribbon cutting. Our first visit at a building like this is an inventory: which doors and floors are on which system, who holds the logins, what is billed monthly and to whom, and what was left unfinished. Then the owner decides what to keep, what to finish and what to replace.

Garage levels in these towers deserve extra attention. The garage door or gate is usually the busiest entrance in the building, it opens onto the elevator lobby, and it is the one most often tailgated. A credential at the gate that matches the lobby fob, a camera covering the gate from inside the garage, and a reader at the garage elevator lobby close the gap that most new buildings leave open.

Garden apartments: many entrances, wide grounds

A garden complex in Greenburgh or Elmsford has no single front door. It has a dozen building entrances spread across lawns and lots, often a laundry building and a maintenance shop, and sometimes a pool. The work here is about reach. Every entrance should take the same credential so the office manages one list. Every building needs a network path back to the controller and the recorder, which means existing conduit, new trenching or wireless links between rooftops. And the outdoor areas, parking, paths, trash enclosures and mailbox clusters, matter as much as the doors because that is where most incidents happen. We survey the grounds before we price, and we phase the work building by building so no resident is ever without a working entrance.

Mixed-use buildings on Westchester main streets

Apartments over a pizzeria in Port Chester or a salon in Ossining need a hard boundary between the stair and the store. The shop's staff should not have a credential to the residential entrance, residents should not be able to walk through a shared cellar into the shop's storage, and a camera covering the apartment door should belong to the building, not to the commercial tenant. We draw that boundary into the access plan, keep the store's alarm and cameras on its own account unless the owner wants them combined, and make sure the cellar door between the two uses is controlled from the residential side.

Two- to four-family rentals

Plenty of Westchester management lists include small rental houses, often for owners who hand a firm a handful of buildings rather than a tower. A shared panel rarely makes sense when each apartment has its own entrance. We usually recommend a wired doorbell camera at each entrance on the house's existing doorbell transformer, an owner-controlled camera on the driveway or rear yard, and code locks where keys keep going missing. Each tenant's doorbell is set up in the tenant's account and reset at move-out, while the owner's cameras stay in the owner's account, so nobody watches anybody else's front door.

Affordable and senior rental buildings

Newer regulated buildings across Westchester often run a staffed office or front desk during business hours and nobody overnight. The entry system has to serve both shifts: a desk station that can see and release the door in the daytime, and a panel that rings residents or an on-call manager after the desk closes. Accessibility drives the hardware. Panels go at a height a seated visitor can reach, readers work with a card held anywhere close to them, and doors stay open long enough for a walker or wheelchair. Agencies and funders sometimes ask what systems are in place, so we describe what we install in plain language the office can file.

Commercial properties on the same management list

Rules a Westchester property manager should keep in view

  • Multiple Residence Law. Westchester apartment buildings fall under the Multiple Residence Law (§3) and local codes, not the city's Multiple Dwelling Law.
  • Property Maintenance Code. §304.18 requires security devices on dwelling-unit doors, windows and hatchways; §304.18.1 governs deadbolts on rented units.
  • Warranty of habitability. Real Property Law §235-b applies to every residential lease and to the areas used with the apartment.
  • ETPA buildings. Rent stabilization applies in the eighteen municipalities on the county housing department's list for qualifying buildings.
  • Lead Rental Registry. Pre-1980 buildings with two or more units in ZIP codes 10550, 10801, 10701 and 10705 must register and schedule inspections every three years, per the county health department.
  • Licensing. The county requires plumbers and electricians to hold a county trade license; alarm installers are licensed by the state under General Business Law §69-m, and the county licenses alarm businesses under its consumer protection code.
  • Alarm permits. Cities such as White Plains require alarm permits and charge escalating fees for repeated false alarms.
  • Fire alarm approval. Local code enforcement or the fire prevention bureau approves fire alarm systems under the Uniform Code.
Your Questions Answered

Property Management Security in Westchester County: Questions Answered

New towers and takeovers

The developer's contractor still holds the warranty on our new tower's entry hardware. Will your work void it?

Not if it's planned. Read the warranty terms first, because some require that only the original installer open the equipment during the warranty period. If they do, we limit our work to what the warranty does not cover, such as adding doors or cameras that were never installed, and we document the condition of the existing equipment before touching anything. If the warranty allows other licensed contractors, we service it normally and keep records the owner can show the developer if a claim comes up.

Our New Rochelle building's garage gate and lobby panel come from different platforms. Can residents use one credential for both?

Usually, yes. The simplest route is a reader at the garage that reads the same fob format as the lobby and reports to the lobby's access platform, leaving the gate operator itself untouched. If the garage system is tied to a parking vendor's billing, the two can sometimes share a credential through an integration, or the garage keeps its own tag and the lobby keeps its fob. We look at both systems before recommending one path, and the gate company stays responsible for the motor and the safety devices.

Can a prospect touring with a leasing agent get a code that stops working when the tour ends?

On most current platforms, yes. The leasing agent can issue a code or a phone credential that opens the lobby and the amenity floors for a set window, such as one hour, and expires on its own. The log records which agent issued it and which doors it opened. That beats lending out a resident fob or a master key, both of which tend to disappear during a busy leasing season.

Our building's internet provider changed and the cloud intercom went offline. Why did that happen, and how do we stop it next time?

A cloud panel needs a working internet connection with the right settings, and a provider change usually brings a new router with a different address, different firewall rules or a modem placed somewhere else. The panel keeps looking for the old network and drops off. The fix is to put security equipment on its own network segment with a fixed configuration, document it in the building file, and make sure anyone who changes the internet service calls us first. Where a building's connection is shaky, a cellular backup for the panel is worth considering.

Older buildings and their quirks

Our Mount Vernon building is in a lead-registry ZIP code. Can intercom work be booked on the same days the inspectors are in the apartments?

Yes, and it saves your office a second round of tenant notices. Send us the inspection schedule and the list of apartments with intercom problems. We plan station replacements and in-unit checks for the same days and keep our work out of the inspectors' way. We do not perform or certify lead inspections; we only use the access the owner has already arranged.

A Yonkers walk-up has a sidewalk cellar entrance that the oil company uses. How do we secure it without blocking deliveries?

The common answer is to control the inner door between the cellar and the building, not the sidewalk doors. A reader or keypad on that inner door gives the super, the oil company and other vendors their own codes, each logged, and a door contact alerts the office if it is left open. The sidewalk doors stay locked mechanically from inside. If the delivery driver only needs the fill pipe outside, nobody needs to enter the cellar at all, which is worth confirming before anything is installed.

Our Port Chester building is two blocks from Long Island Sound. Does the salt air change what goes on the front door?

It does. Salt air pits ordinary steel faceplates, swells cheap enclosures and corrodes screw terminals inside panels within a few seasons. Near the water we use stainless or marine-grade faceplates and enclosures, sealed connections and hardware rated for coastal exposure, and we mount the panel under the entrance overhang where possible. Our Westchester door buzzer repair page prices a marine-grade enclosure as an add-on for Sound Shore buildings for exactly this reason.

Which entrance should get the visitor panel when a building on a hill has a street door and a garage-level door?

The one visitors actually use. In the river towns and parts of Yonkers, many buildings have a street entrance on the uphill side and a garage or lot entrance a floor or two below. Delivery drivers and guests go to the street door, so the main panel belongs there. The lower entrance usually gets a reader for residents and, if it is the only way in from the lot, a single-button station that calls the office or rings a resident.

Garden complexes and suburbs

Our Greenburgh garden complex has a separate laundry building. Should it be on the same system as the apartment doors?

Yes. Put the laundry building on the same credential list as the residential doors, with its own hours, so residents can use it and nobody else can. It is also one of the best places for a camera, because disputes over machines and missing clothes are common and the room is often out of sight of every apartment. If the laundry vendor's card machines share the building's internet, keep them off the security network.

Deer and wind keep setting off motion alerts at our northern Westchester complex. How do we cut down the false alerts?

Use detection that recognizes people and vehicles rather than any motion, draw detection zones that exclude tree lines and lawns, and aim cameras so branches don't sweep across the frame. Turning sensitivity down alone usually misses real events, so we change what the camera looks for rather than how hard it looks. After the changes, we review a week of alerts with the office and adjust again.

Staff, residents and daily operations

A tenant's home health aide comes every morning. How do we give access without a fob that never expires?

Issue the aide a credential with a schedule and an end date: valid only during the visit hours, only on the street door and the tenant's floor, and set to expire when the care arrangement is up for renewal. The tenant's own fob stays separate, and the log shows the aide's entries. If the agency changes aides, the office cancels one credential and issues another without changing anything else.

Our building has a doorman until midnight. What should the entry system do after the desk closes?

Switch to unattended mode on a schedule. While the desk is staffed, the panel calls the desk station first and the doorman releases the door. After midnight the panel rings residents directly, courier codes stop working, and the package room switches to residents only. The switch happens automatically at set times, so nobody has to remember it at the end of a shift.

What should happen to the superintendent's credentials and app logins when the super leaves the job?

Cancel them the same day, from the office. A super usually holds a master credential, an app login with door release rights, often a camera account, and sometimes the alarm codes. Each one should be in the super's own name so it can be removed without affecting anyone else, and the replacement super gets new ones. If the departing super was the only person who knew a password, that is the moment to move every administrator account into the owner's name.

Can one dashboard show which entry panels across our Westchester buildings are offline right now?

On cloud platforms, yes: the portfolio view shows each building's panels and readers with their online status, and can email the office when one drops off. Older local systems don't report on their own, so we either connect them to a small monitoring device or add them to the maintenance check list. Knowing that a panel in Mount Vernon went offline at 3 a.m. lets the office call before the tenants do.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Property Management Security in Westchester

Managing agents increasingly arrive with an answer from a chatbot or a search summary. Most of those answers were written for Manhattan or for a single-family house somewhere else. These are the ones we correct most often in Westchester.

1. "HPD rules decide how you can enter apartments"

HPD is a New York City agency, and its access-notice rule is a city rule. In Westchester, access is governed by the lease, state law and local code, and the management office sets the notice. We follow what the office gives tenants.

2. "The Tenant Data Privacy Act limits fob systems"

That law is New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021 and applies to class A multiple dwellings in the city. It does not apply in Yonkers or White Plains. A careful owner still limits what a fob system keeps and who can see it, and has counsel review any notice to tenants, but the city statute is not the rule here.

3. "A new building's system belongs to whoever manages it"

The system belongs to the owner, but the logins often belong to whoever set it up. Until administrator rights are moved into the owner's name, a developer or a former vendor can still control the doors. That transfer should be the first item on any takeover.

4. "A rental building's security means a guard at the desk"

For most Westchester rentals it means doors that latch, an entry system that reaches every tenant, credentials the office controls and cameras that actually record. Guards and remote monitoring are staffed services some owners add on top; we do not sell either.

5. "An apartment intercom costs about two thousand dollars"

National cost guides quote single-family prices. For a four- to eight-unit Westchester building our published video intercom range is $1,200–$3,500, larger buildings run $2,500–$8,000 and up, and a single fault in an existing system is often a few hundred dollars. That is why we test before recommending replacement.

6. "Package theft is out of control everywhere"

News 12 Westchester's June 2026 report showed package thefts falling in Yonkers's First Precinct and in White Plains year over year. Thefts still happen, and they cluster where parcels sit in an open vestibule, so a latching door, a package room and a camera on the drop spot remain the practical fix.

7. "Security wiring is part of the electrician's job"

Electricians handle line-voltage power; security systems are low-voltage work, and alarm installers in New York are licensed by the state under General Business Law §69-m. The two trades work side by side. We handle the low-voltage side and coordinate with the building's electrician for any new circuit.

Field notes: what a hot summer does to a cellar equipment room

Many Westchester walk-ups and elevator buildings keep their recorder and intercom power supply in a boiler room or a closed closet. A recorder moved to a cooler room usually lasts years longer than one left beside the boiler.

What moves a Westchester quote up or down

Five things explain most of the gap between two quotes for similar-looking buildings. The first is the riser: if the existing pairs test clean, a new panel and stations can reuse them, and if not, new cable runs through every floor. The second is the number of controlled openings, because each one carries its own reader, hardware and wiring. The third is the door itself, since an electrified lock on a warped frame needs frame work before it will hold. The fourth is recurring fees, which range from nothing after installation to a monthly charge per door or per unit. The fifth is access, meaning how easily we can reach the equipment room, the riser and the apartments, which in an occupied rent-stabilized building can set the schedule.

Budgeting across several years

Few owners replace everything at once, and few should. After the buildings are surveyed, the office can sort them: open code complaints and the loudest tenant problems first, then equipment with no available parts, then working systems that carry heavy monthly fees. Each budget year funds the next group, with repairs covering the rest. Owners who would rather spread a project's cost have financing available through Wisetack, subject to approval.

Thinking per unit, per door or per building

A per-apartment figure is handy for comparing buildings after they are surveyed, but it is a poor way to price one in advance, because a small walk-up with a failed riser can cost more than a larger building with good wiring. Access control prices best per door, cameras by the area covered, and intercoms by the riser and the panel. In the end the owner approves a number per building, which is how our scopes are written.

Full terms — including 50% deposit, one-year parts-only warranty on Abstract Enterprises Security Systems-supplied products, and the late-fee schedule — are set out in the service agreement.

FAQ

Westchester County Property Management Security FAQ

Do you charge more to send a technician to northern Westchester towns such as Somers or Bedford?
+

No. The $250 service call is the same anywhere in Westchester County, from the Yonkers city line to the northern towns. Visits to the northern towns are booked into the same early-day arrival windows as everywhere else in the county.

Is sales tax added to security work in Westchester?
+

Yes. Sales tax applies to the work and is shown as its own line on each building's invoice. The rate depends on the municipality where the building sits, so two buildings on the same list can carry different tax amounts.

Can our office look up which door a tenant's fob opened on a particular night?
+

Yes, on any networked access system. The event log lists each credential, the door it opened and the time, and staff with the right permission can search it by tenant, door or date. How long those records are kept is a setting the owner chooses, so ask us to set it to match the owner's records policy.

Can a reader be added to the garage roll-up door at a rental building?
+

Yes. A reader or a long-range credential at the overhead door can trigger the opener through a dry contact, so residents use the same credential as the lobby. The garage door company keeps responsibility for the motor, the safety edges and the photo eyes, and we coordinate the connection with them.

Do you replace batteries in wireless door sensors and keypads during maintenance visits?
+

Yes. Wireless contacts, motion sensors, keypads and battery-powered locks are checked on each maintenance visit and their batteries replaced before they run low. The building file records the battery type and the date it was changed, so the next visit knows what to bring.

How do new tenants get their fobs when they move in on a weekend?
+

The office can program and hand out fobs itself, so a weekend move-in does not have to wait for a technician. We set the office up with a stock of blank fobs and show staff how to enroll them; a temporary code for the move-in day covers anyone who arrives before the fob is ready.

Can our cameras send video straight to the police when something happens?
+

We do not set up a direct feed to any police department. When police ask for footage, the office exports the clip from the recorder or the app and decides what to hand over. We show staff how to save a clip with the correct time so it is ready when needed.

Can the lobby panel call a concierge desk instead of ringing apartments directly?
+

Yes. A desk station or a desk app can take the first call from the lobby panel during staffed hours, see the visitor and release the door, then pass the call to the resident. Outside desk hours the panel can ring residents directly on a schedule.

Can different people at our firm get different alerts from the same building?
+

Yes. Most platforms let each user choose which alerts they receive. The super might get door-held-open and offline alerts, the property manager a daily summary, and the asset manager nothing at all. Each person gets their own login, so alerts follow the person, not a shared account.

Can every exterior door at a building be locked at once if there is an incident nearby?
+

On many access platforms, yes. An authorized user can send a lockdown command that locks all controlled exterior doors to credentials, while every door still opens from inside for exit. We set who can trigger it and test it at handover so staff know how it behaves.
