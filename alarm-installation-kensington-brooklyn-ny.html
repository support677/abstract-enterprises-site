The Neighborhood

Why Kensington Properties Need Alarm Installation

Kensington sits south of Prospect Park and Green-Wood Cemetery, between Borough Park to the west and the Prospect Park South and Ditmas Park sections of Flatbush to the east, with Windsor Terrace above it and Midwood below. Wikipedia describes it as mostly residential, with brick row houses, detached one-family Victorians and apartment buildings, and notes that pre-war brick apartment buildings, many of them co-ops, dominate the Ocean Parkway and Coney Island Avenue frontage. The shopping streets are Coney Island Avenue, Church Avenue, Ditmas Avenue and McDonald Avenue, the ZIP code is 11218, and the NYPD's 66th Precinct covers it.

What does make it different is who lives inside those buildings. The same Wikipedia article lists South Asian (Bangladeshi and Pakistani), Orthodox Jewish, Uzbek, Latin American, Polish and Ukrainian communities, and the city's 2020 census figures put the White and Asian populations at roughly equal sizes. Brownstoner's report on the 2022 co-naming quoted Assembly Member Robert Carroll saying that "for years everyone knew this was the heart of Brooklyn's Bangladeshi community". The Endangered Language Alliance's map marks Church Avenue as a major hub for Bengali and records Urdu, Punjabi and several regional languages of Bangladesh on the same blocks. In practice that means a Kensington household is often three generations under one roof, with the grandparents most comfortable in one language, the parents switching between two, and the children doing the paperwork in English.

An alarm installer who ignores that ends up with a familiar failure. The system goes in, the person who signed the contract learns the keypad, and everyone else learns to avoid it. Then one evening a grandmother opens the side door before anyone has disarmed, the siren goes off, the monitoring center rings the house, and the person who picks up cannot follow a fast conversation in English or remember the passcode word. Industry research cited by the Security Industry Association, as reported by Security Info Watch in 2009, attributed about 70 percent of false alarms to user error. In a multilingual house the risk of that kind of mistake is higher, and the cost of it is more than a fine: families who get two or three embarrassing false alarms stop arming the system at all, and an unarmed alarm protects nothing.

Kensington is not short of reasons to arm one, either. In April 2024, News 12 Brooklyn reported that police were looking for a man who broke into three homes and one commercial building in less than three hours across Midwood, Kensington and Borough Park, with every entry between 5 p.m. and 8 p.m., the hours when many houses are empty and not yet lit. In August 2024 the same outlet reported residents of a Kensington building watching on camera as a man walked in through a back door, which the landlord said could not be kept shut because of garbage removal, and took packages from their mailboxes. Neither story calls for a complicated system. Both call for an alarm that the family actually turns on every day, which is what this page is about.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Kensington

Each of these is its own line on a written quote, so you can see what you are paying for and leave out what you do not need.

A household arming routine

Before any device goes up, we agree with the family on one routine: how each person arms when leaving, how the last person up arms for the night, and what anyone does if the siren starts. We program the panel to match it and leave it on a printed card by the door.

Door and window contacts

Contacts on the apartment or house door, every window reachable from the ground, a porch roof or a fire escape, the cellar door and any door onto a back yard or lane. Steel casement windows in older Ocean Parkway buildings get slim surface contacts.

Spoken and named zones

Each sensor gets a short, plain name that the panel can speak aloud and the app can show, such as "kitchen door" or "back bedroom window", so a family member who does not read the screen can still hear which door is open before arming.

Two households, two areas

For duplex-porch two-family houses, the panel is split so each family has its own keypad, codes and alerts, and the shared front hall or cellar is covered by both or by whichever family the owners choose.

Key fobs and simple keypads

Fobs with large, separate buttons for people who mix up numbers, and keypads placed where the family really comes in, often the side door off the driveway or the kitchen door rather than the formal front door.

Monitoring set-up and call lists

Central-station monitoring billed month to month from $19.99, or self-monitoring through the app. Either way we set the call order around who can answer quickly and calmly, and we test the call with the family before we leave.

Shop and restaurant alarms

Glass-break behind the front window, motion in the sales floor, contacts on the back door and roll-down gate, and a closing routine short enough that every member of staff can learn it regardless of which language they speak.

Takeovers of old systems

Panels left by previous owners or by alarm companies long gone, tested, re-coded and moved to cellular reporting where the old wiring is sound. Codes nobody remembers are cleared and replaced.

Repairs and false alarms

Beeping keypads, trouble lights, motion sensors that react to a radiator or a pet, and doors that will not show closed. We also review the event history with the family to see which mistakes keep setting the system off.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Kensington's Architecture

Kensington's housing was built in waves, and each wave left a building type that needs a slightly different alarm. These are the ones we meet most often.

Pre-war elevator buildings on Ocean Parkway

Six-story brick apartment houses from the late 1920s to 1941, many converted to co-ops in the late 20th century. The alarm stays inside the apartment: the entry door, any window on a fire escape or a low courtyard, and motion in the hall. The board's alteration rules come first.

Post-war towers

Taller buildings put up on and near the parkway after the Prospect Expressway arrived, among them Caton Towers and Americana Towers, which Wikipedia names. Upper-floor apartments rarely need window contacts; the door, a balcony door and a smoke detector tied to the alarm usually matter most.

Duplex-porch two-family houses

A porch and an entrance for each household, stacked one above the other. Each family gets its own area of the panel, the upper porch door is treated as a real entry point, and the shared hall is assigned deliberately rather than left to chance.

American Foursquares and detached Victorians

Square, two-and-a-half-story houses on the numbered streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, and older detached houses near the parkway. Windows on all four sides, a full cellar and an attic with dormers mean more openings to choose from, and a hybrid of wired and wireless sensors.

Row houses on the lanes

The short streets Forgotten New York walked in 2012, such as Kermit, Caton, Friel, Turner and Hinckley Places, carry rows of attached houses and two-story brick buildings with one apartment per floor. Front and back doors plus the ground-floor rear windows are the core of the job.

Apartments over stores

Walk-ups above shops on Church, McDonald and Coney Island Avenues, where the street door is shared with the store's customers or staff. The alarm protects the apartment door and any window over a roof or awning, and is kept separate from the shop's system.

Method notes by building type

Co-op paperwork. Almost every Ocean Parkway co-op asks for an alteration agreement or at least an insurance certificate before a contractor works in an apartment. A wireless alarm needs no new wiring through walls or common areas, which makes approval simpler, but we still send the board our certificate, the license number and a one-page description of what will be mounted and where, and we work only within the building's contractor hours.

Two-families with a shared hall. In a duplex-porch house the lower family's door, the upper family's door and the cellar door may all open off one hall. We map who uses which door before we program anything, because the most common two-family complaint is an alarm that one household cannot arm without the other.

Foursquares and cellars. Detached houses have cellar windows on several sides and often a separate outside cellar entrance. These get contacts or glass-break detection first, because they are low, out of sight and far from the bedrooms. Attic windows are covered only when a porch roof or a neighboring roof puts them within reach.

Kensington owners who are already opening walls for an alarm often add data cabling to each floor in the same visit; we can quote structured cabling in Kensington alongside the alarm so the work is done once.

One House, Several Languages

Why a Kensington Alarm Starts With Who Lives in the House

Most alarm quotes start with a floor plan. Ours, in Kensington, start with a list of people. The reason is simple: the devices on the doors and windows do the same job in every house, but the part of the system that fails most often is the conversation between the alarm and the person standing in front of it. The keypad asks a question, the panel counts down, the monitoring center rings, and somebody has to respond correctly within a minute or two. If that somebody cannot read the keypad's words or follow the operator's English, the best sensors in the world end up as a nuisance that the family switches off.

So we design four things around the household before we choose a single sensor: what the panel shows and says, how each person arms and disarms, who the monitoring center calls and in what order, and what word each caller gives to prove they live there. None of this requires a special product. It requires asking the right questions at the walkthrough and spending an extra half hour at handover with the people who will actually use the system, not just the person paying for it.

What the panel and the app can and cannot do with language

It helps to be honest about the starting point. An Alarm.com dealer's support page, published by Alert360, says the customer app follows the language of the phone it is installed on and lists English, Spanish, Spanish (Spain), French, Icelandic, Dutch, Norwegian, Swedish, Portuguese, Turkish and Hebrew. Alarm Grid's guide to the Qolsys IQ Panel 5 says the touchscreen ships with English and Español as its two quick-toggle languages and can be switched to others, naming Français, Deutsch, Italiano, Nederlands, Português and Polskie among them. Neither list we checked names Bengali, Urdu, Punjabi, Yiddish or Uzbek. A Polish-speaking grandmother may get her own screen; a Bengali-speaking one, as far as those lists show, will not.

What the hardware can do helps more than the menus. On the Qolsys panel the language toggle needs no code, so a family can leave the screen in whichever of its two languages most people read and flip it when needed. Resideo's ProSeries panels, according to Alarm Grid's March 2022 note on Release 4, can speak a custom word descriptor for each zone through text-to-speech rather than choosing from a fixed vocabulary. That lets us name a sensor in plain, short English that a non-English speaker can learn by sound, such as "side door" or "kitchen", and the panel will say it out loud when that door opens. Most families learn the five or six spoken names in a day, even when they never learn to read the screen.

The monitoring call: who answers, and what they say

When a monitored alarm trips, the center does not send police straight away. Frontpoint's help pages describe a typical sequence: the station first calls the household to verify, works down two or three pre-dispatch numbers in the order on file, asks whoever answers for the account passcode, and dispatches if nobody answers, the passcode is wrong or missing, or the person asks for help. Other centers differ in detail, but almost all follow this shape. Two parts of it matter in a Kensington house.

The first is the order of the call list. Families usually put the account holder first, which is often the person least likely to be home. We suggest the opposite: first the adult who is most often in the house and can talk calmly with an operator in English, then the account holder, then a relative nearby. If the person usually home does not speak English comfortably, the first number should belong to someone who does and who can phone home in the family's language within seconds.

The second is the passcode. Many families pick an English word that one person chose at signup and nobody else remembers. Pick something every member of the house can say clearly over a bad phone line: a short number, a family name, a place everyone knows. Some centers have bilingual staff or interpreters. Security Systems News reported in 2015 that 80 percent of the operators at Emergency Response USA's station in Freeport, Long Island, spoke English and Spanish and that the company could reach a translation service for more than 150 languages. Ask any monitoring provider, ours included, whether it uses an interpreter line and how long that takes to connect before you rely on it, because an operator waiting for an interpreter is still counting toward dispatch.

Codes, fobs and apps: who gets which

Every person who uses the alarm should have their own way in, so that the history shows who armed and disarmed and so that one person can be removed without changing everyone's habits. The tool depends on the person. Adults who are comfortable with phones usually use the app and a personal code. Older relatives usually do better with a fob, because pressing a marked button is easier than remembering four digits under pressure. A home aide or a cleaner gets a code that works only during their hours. Children who come home from school get a code that can disarm but not change settings.

The handover we do in a multilingual house

Our standard handover takes about thirty minutes. In Kensington we plan for an hour and ask for everyone who will use the system to be home, or on a video call, at the same time. We walk each person through the same three actions on the card: leaving, going to bed, and what to do if the siren starts. Each person then does them once with their own code or fob while the rest watch. We trigger a test signal to the monitoring center so the first number on the list receives a real call and hears what the operator says. If the family wants a relative to translate during the handover, that is welcome and usual; we would rather explain everything twice than leave a person in the house who is afraid of the keypad.

A week later we call back. The event history shows whether everyone has been arming, whether any false alarm came close to happening and which door caused it. Most problems at that stage are a delay that is too short for a slower walker, or a door name the panel pronounces badly. Both are fixed by changing a setting, not by adding hardware.

Ocean Parkway

Apartments Along the Parkway: Service Roads, Co-op Boards and the Door You Actually Control

Ocean Parkway was laid out by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux and built between 1874 and 1876, and Wikipedia credits its completion with setting off Kensington's development in 1885. It was designated a scenic landmark on January 28, 1975, and its bicycle path, opened in 1894, is often called the first in the country. The parkway is about 210 feet wide: a 70-foot central roadway, two 20-foot landscaped malls with paths, two 25-foot service roads and two 15-foot sidewalks. Above Church Avenue the original roadway was replaced in the 1950s by the Prospect Expressway, which runs in a trench, while the service roads continue north to Park Circle.

The buildings that face that width are mostly apartments. Wikipedia's Kensington history describes six-story elevator houses spreading along upper Ocean Parkway from the late 1920s to 1941, then taller post-war buildings, among them the Marlene J., later Caton Towers, and the Americana Towers. Its Ocean Parkway article counts 26 apartment buildings built on the parkway itself between 1960 and 1965, and notes co-ops and rentals of up to 20 stories in Kensington. Many converted to co-ops in the late 20th century.

For an alarm, that history means two things. First, the building's perimeter belongs to the building, not to you. The lobby door, the back service door and the basement are the board's or the landlord's responsibility, and the August 2024 News 12 story, where a back door stayed open for garbage collection, is a reminder that a shared entrance can be wide open while every apartment door is locked. Second, what you can control is your own apartment door, any window you can be reached through, and what happens inside. A good apartment alarm in Kensington is therefore small, quiet and precise: a contact on the door, contacts on fire-escape and courtyard windows on low floors, motion in the entry hall, a smoke detector the panel watches, and an indoor siren loud enough to wake you but not to start an argument with neighbors.

How an Ocean Parkway apartment alarm is usually laid out

The apartment door. A slim contact on the frame and a keypad or a fob, never a keypad mounted outside in the public hallway. The entry delay is set to the time it takes the slowest person in the household to reach the keypad, often 30 to 45 seconds rather than the factory default.

Fire-escape and courtyard windows. On the first three or four floors, any window onto a fire escape, a courtyard, a low roof or a setback gets a contact. Above that, we usually leave windows alone and rely on the door and interior motion.

Steel casements. Many pre-war buildings still have their original steel casement windows, or steel replacements with narrow frames. Recessed contacts do not fit them, so we use small surface contacts placed where the sash and frame meet, and check them with the window cranked fully open and fully closed.

Interior motion. One sensor covering the entry hall, which every route through the apartment crosses. In "night" mode it stays off, so people can walk to the kitchen; in "away" mode it is on. Pet-tolerant sensors are used where there is a cat or a small dog.

Siren and neighbors. The siren lives inside the apartment, never in the corridor. The city caps an audible building alarm at fifteen minutes before it must shut itself off, and an apartment siren in a building of a hundred units should be set for a small fraction of that.

The Side Streets

Stacked Porches, Foursquares and Parkville's Angled Blocks

Walk east of Ocean Parkway, or onto the numbered East streets off Beverley Road and Ditmas Avenue, and the apartment buildings give way to houses. Untapped New York's 2023 guide points to a significant number of American Foursquares on those streets, the square, two-and-a-half-story style that was popular from the 1890s to the 1930s. Forgotten New York's 2012 tour of the lanes between Ocean Parkway, Coney Island Avenue and Church Avenue, including Kermit Place, Caton Place, Friel Place, Turner Place, Hinckley Place, Lewis Place, Matthews Court and Slocum Place, found attached rows from about the 1910s, two-story brick buildings with one apartment per floor, a few large private houses, and on Turner Place a duplex porch that the author called "almost the signature style of the neighborhood".

The duplex porch is the clearest example of why a Kensington house alarm has to be designed around people. Two families, often related, live one above the other, each with its own porch and its own door, sometimes sharing a front hall and a cellar. A single alarm that one household arms and the other cannot see is the source of most of the two-family complaints we hear: the upstairs family comes home late and sets off the downstairs alarm, or the downstairs family arms "away" while a cousin is asleep upstairs.

What changes on a Foursquare or a detached house

A Foursquare has no party walls, so it has windows on every side, a full cellar with windows at ground level, and an attic, usually with a dormer on one or more faces. The porch across the front is often deep enough to hide someone standing at the front windows. We cover the cellar windows and any cellar door first, then the first-floor windows on the sides and back, which neighbors cannot see, then the front windows behind the porch. Second-floor windows are added where a porch roof, a back extension or a garage roof puts them in reach. Attic dormers are covered only when something below makes them reachable, which on most Foursquares means the front porch roof.

Detached houses also have longer distances from the panel to the far sensors, and brick or stucco walls in between. We often combine wired contacts where old alarm wiring is still in the walls with wireless sensors elsewhere, and we test every wireless device from the panel's final spot before it is fixed in place.

Parkville

Between 18th Avenue and Foster Avenue, the southern end of the neighborhood, the streets run on a slight diagonal. Wikipedia and Untapped New York trace this to Greenville, a settlement whose land the Freeman's Association bought in 1852 after the Coney Island Plank Road was built, later known as Parkville. Public School 92, later P.S. 134, and the Church of St. Rose of Lima were built for it in 1870. The angled blocks leave some lots triangular or deeper on one side, and some houses have side windows that face a neighbor's back yard instead of another wall. On a Parkville walkthrough we look at every side of the house, not just the front and back, before deciding which windows count as reachable.

What a Kensington shop alarm needs

A closing routine anyone can learn. Gate down, back door locked, one button or code, and a confirmation the closer can see. We write it on a card in the languages your staff read, using words you supply, and we program the system to text the owner when the store is armed and when it was not armed by a set time.

Glass and gates. Glass-break detectors behind the display windows, contacts on the back door, and a contact on the roll-down gate where the motor housing allows one. Motion sensors cover the sales floor and the stockroom, aimed away from refrigerated cases and heaters that confuse them.

Staff codes that come and go. Each employee gets a personal code, removed the day they leave. The event history shows by name who opened and closed.

Separate from upstairs. When there are apartments above, the store's alarm and siren belong only to the store. Tenants should never be woken by the shop's system, and the shop should never depend on a tenant answering a call.

Rules a Kensington business alarm has to respect

Sirens that end on their own. Under § 24-238 of the city's Administrative Code, a building burglar alarm that makes noise must cut itself off within fifteen minutes. A shop siren with tenants sleeping above should run for far less, and we program it that way.

Who may install it. Anyone installing alarms for paying customers in New York State needs a Department of State license under General Business Law Article 6-D, which means completed coursework, a passed exam and fingerprinted staff. Fire alarms are separate. A burglar alarm with a smoke detector is not a fire alarm system. Buildings with stores and apartments, or larger multiple dwellings, may be required to have a fire alarm system that meets the Building and Fire Codes; that is designed and filed separately. Our fire alarm installation for Kensington buildings covers it.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Kensington: Questions Answered

When not everyone in the house reads the keypad

When the monitoring center rings our house after the alarm goes off, my mother usually answers, and she speaks only Bengali. What actually happens on that call?

The operator will try to verify the alarm in English, ask for the passcode, and, if the answer is unclear, move on to the next number or send the police. Some centers can bring an interpreter onto the line, but that takes time, and the clock keeps running. The fix is in the setup, not the call: put a family member who speaks English and can phone your mother immediately first on the list, and choose a passcode she can say clearly even if the operator's other questions are lost on her. Then ask your monitoring company, in writing, whether it uses an interpreter line, for which languages, and how long it usually takes to connect.

Are there alarm panels that show pictures and colors instead of English words for the basic steps?

Touchscreen panels such as the Qolsys IQ series use large on-screen icons for arming, disarming and the panic screen, so the three everyday actions can be learned largely by picture. Key fobs go further: separate buttons, each with a symbol, and nothing to read at all. For relatives who will never read the screen, we usually pair a fob with spoken door names and a printed card that uses the same colors as the buttons, so the card, the fob and the screen all tell the same story.

My grandmother cannot read the screen, but she listens carefully. Can the panel say out loud which door is still open before she arms it?

Yes, on most current panels. Each sensor can be given a short spoken name, and Resideo's ProSeries panels, for example, can turn a typed name into speech rather than choosing from a fixed word list. When a door opens, or when she tries to arm with a window open, the panel speaks its name: "kitchen door", "back bedroom window". We keep the names to one or two simple English words she can learn by sound, and we test each one at handover with her standing next to the panel.

Industry research blames users for most false alarms. Which mistakes do you see most in houses where several languages are spoken?

Three, in order. Someone who did not know the alarm was armed opens a door and freezes when the keypad starts beeping. Someone enters a code too slowly or wrongly under pressure and gives up as the siren starts. And the monitoring call goes unanswered or the passcode is forgotten. We design against all three: a longer entry delay at the door older relatives use, fobs instead of codes for anyone who struggles with numbers, a passcode every caller can say, and a trial week with self-monitoring before the central station goes live, so mistakes cost nothing while everyone learns.

Our monitoring passcode is an English word my father cannot pronounce clearly. Can the passcode be a number, or a word from our own language?

Usually yes. Most monitoring centers accept any word or short number you register, and the operator simply checks that what is said matches the account. A number is often the safest choice, because digits are easy to say and hear in any accent. If you choose a word from Bengali, Urdu or another language, spell it for the center exactly as your family says it, and have each person on the call list say it to the operator during the test call at handover, so everyone knows it will be recognized.

Ocean Parkway apartment buildings

We live high up in one of the post-war towers on the parkway, with a balcony off the living room. Does a balcony door fifteen floors up need a sensor at all?

Rarely for burglary. Nobody climbs fifteen stories, and on upper floors we normally leave balcony doors and windows off the intrusion zones and concentrate on the apartment door and hall motion. A contact on the balcony door can still earn its place in two cases: as a chime when small children are home, so you hear the door open, and as a reminder in the app if the door was left open before a storm or a long trip. Lower balconies near a setback, a neighboring roof or a terrace are a different matter and get treated like any reachable opening.

In one Kensington building a man walked in through a back door that stayed open for garbage pickup and took packages from the mailboxes. If our building's doors cannot be kept shut, what should our own apartment alarm cover first?

Assume the hallway is public and start at your own door. The apartment door gets a contact and the entry hall gets a motion sensor, so anyone who gets past the door is detected within a step or two. Any window you can reach from the fire escape or a low courtyard comes next. A video doorbell or a small peephole camera at your door, if the board allows it, shows who knocked while you were out. The building's back door and mailboxes are the board's or landlord's to fix; a letter from shareholders or tenants about the August 2024 incident is more likely to get that door repaired than any device you mount yourself.

Two-family houses, Foursquares and Parkville

Our two-family has stacked front porches, one for each family. Does the door onto the upper porch really need a sensor?

Yes. It is the upstairs family's main entrance, and it is reached by the porch stair or, often, by climbing the lower porch's railing and roof. We treat it exactly like a front door: a contact, its own entry delay and a keypad or fob inside. Windows that open onto the upper porch or over the lower porch roof get contacts too.

Our Parkville house sits on one of the angled blocks, so a side window looks straight into a neighbor's back yard instead of at another wall. Does that window need a contact?

Usually yes. A side window that faces open yard rather than a narrow gap between houses can be reached from behind a fence, out of sight of the street and often of the neighbor too. We give it a contact, and if it is a large or double window we may add glass-break detection for the room. Before deciding, we walk the outside of the lot, because on the diagonal Parkville blocks the reachable sides of a house are not always the ones you would expect from the street.

The grandparents downstairs go to bed at nine and we stay up past midnight. Can their floor be in night mode while ours stays disarmed?

Yes, if the house is set up as two areas on one panel, which we recommend for most two-family houses. The grandparents' fob puts their floor into night mode: their doors and windows are armed, their hall motion is off so they can walk to the bathroom. Your floor stays disarmed until you arm it. The shared front hall can be set to arm only when both floors are armed, so you can still come and go without setting off their alarm.

In 2024 police were looking for a man who broke into homes in Kensington and nearby between 5 and 8 p.m. We rarely get home before dark. Should the alarm treat those early-evening hours any differently?

Mostly it should simply be armed in away mode until the first person is back, which is why the morning routine matters. Two settings help during those hours. A lamp or porch light on a smart plug can come on at sunset whenever the system is armed, so the house does not sit dark while the street is still busy. And the app can send the account holder a reminder at a set hour if the system was left disarmed, with a one-tap button to arm it from work. For anyone who comes home first, a slightly longer entry delay at the door they use avoids a false alarm in the rush.

Shops on the avenues

Keep the routine short and identical for everyone: gate down, back door locked, enter your own code, watch for the armed light. What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Kensington

We could not open Google's AI Overview from our research environment, so the statements below are not quoted from it. They are the claims we hear most often from customers who have read a summary online or a listing on Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr, and each is followed by what we see in Kensington homes.

"Any modern alarm app works in your language."

Apps do follow the phone's language, but only for the languages the developer has translated. The Alarm.com app list published by one dealer covers English, Spanish, French, Hebrew, Turkish, Portuguese and several Northern European languages. It does not include Bengali, Urdu or Punjabi, which are spoken on the blocks around Church and Coney Island Avenues.

That gap is not a reason to avoid an alarm. It is a reason to rely on things that need no translation: fobs with symbols, a panel that speaks short door names, a printed card in the family's own words, and a call list built around who can talk to an operator.

If an article or a salesperson tells you language does not matter, ask them to arm and disarm the system while reading only the words on the screen in a language they do not know.

"False alarms are mostly caused by faulty equipment."

Equipment does fail: worn door contacts, sensors that see a heater, batteries that run down. But SIA-backed research reported by Security Info Watch in 2009 attributed about 70 percent of false alarms to user error, and that matches what we see when we read event histories.

In a Kensington house the user errors cluster around the same moments: someone opening a door they did not know was armed, someone entering a code wrongly under pressure, and an unanswered or failed monitoring call.

Better equipment helps a little. Better setup helps a lot: longer entry delays where they are needed, fobs for people who mix up numbers, and a week of self-monitoring before the central station goes live.

"Put the keypad by the front door."

Put it by the door the family actually uses. In a duplex-porch two-family that may be two keypads at two porch doors. In a Foursquare it is often the side or kitchen door. In an apartment it is just inside the entry, never in the public hallway.

A keypad in the wrong place stretches the entry delay, because people have to walk past sensors to reach it, and it confuses relatives who come in the way they always have.

We walk each entrance with the family before choosing the spot, and we ask who comes in where, because that answer varies from house to house even on the same block.

"An apartment in a doorman or co-op building doesn't need an alarm."

Some Kensington buildings have staff; many pre-war and post-war buildings on Ocean Parkway do not, and even staffed buildings have service doors, basements and fire escapes. The August 2024 News 12 story about a back door left open for garbage collection shows how a shared entrance can be wide open while every resident assumes it is shut.

A small apartment alarm, a door contact, hall motion, a smoke detector and an indoor siren, costs less than most house systems and protects the one opening you control.

It also does something building staff cannot: it tells you on your phone, while you are away, that your own door opened.

"Alarm installation costs $1,000 to $2,000, wherever you live."

National averages hide the parts that change a price: how many openings, whether old wiring can be reused, whether the house is split into two areas, and whether monitoring is included.

On our Brooklyn list, which Kensington uses without any surcharge, a basic wireless system starts at $600 and typically runs $600 to $1,200, a complete-home system starts at $2,500, and a panel replacement on existing wiring runs $800 to $1,500.

Two-family houses usually land in between, because they share one panel but need devices for two households. We put each line on the written quote so you can see where the money goes.

"You can bring any alarm kit from abroad and use it here."

Not always. Wireless alarm devices are built for the radio bands allowed in the country where they are sold. Ajax's published specifications for one of its transmitters, for example, list several bands, from 866 and 868 MHz up to 905–926.5 MHz, and say the band depends on the sales region.

A kit bought for one country may use a band that is not authorized in the United States, or its monitoring and app features may be tied to a provider that does not operate here.

Before you install a kit a relative brought from overseas, send us the model number from the label. We will tell you whether it is a US version, whether we can add to it, or whether replacing it would be cheaper than fighting it.

"Anyone handy can install an alarm."

A homeowner can put a kit in their own home. A business that installs alarms for others in New York needs a security or fire alarm installer license from the Department of State, with qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting of employees.

Licensing matters for more than paperwork. Co-op boards and insurers ask for it, and a licensed installer carries insurance that covers work inside an apartment above someone else's ceiling.

Field Notes: Count the People Before the Windows

This describes how we run a Kensington survey; it is our method, not an account of a particular job. The technician arrives, introduces himself, and before taking out a tape measure asks who lives in the house and who comes and goes in a normal week. He writes the list on the survey sheet with a column for the language each person reads and whether they carry a smartphone. If a relative is translating by phone, he puts the call on speaker and waits for each answer.

Then he asks each person to show him how they come in. Not the formal front door unless they really use it: the side door off the driveway, the upper porch, the kitchen door from the yard. The last thing on the sheet, before the price, is the call list and the passcode, agreed with the family in the room. In our experience that page, more than the device count, decides whether the alarm is still armed every night a year later.

Are key fobs easier than keypad codes for someone who mixes up numbers?
+

Usually, yes. A fob has separate buttons for arming, disarming and help, so there is nothing to remember and nothing to enter in a hurry. Each fob shows by name in the history, and if one is lost it is deleted from the panel without changing anyone else's fob or code.

A relative brought an alarm kit from overseas. Can you install it here?
+

Send us the model number from the label first. Wireless alarm kits are built for the radio bands of the country they were sold in, and their apps or monitoring may depend on a provider that does not operate in the United States. If it is a US version we can install it at our labor-only rate; if not, we will tell you plainly before anyone visits.

A relative is staying with us for a few months. How do we give them access and take it away when they leave?
+

Give them their own user code or fob rather than sharing yours. Many panels let a user be set up with an end date, so the code simply stops working on the day the visit ends; if yours does not, we delete it then. Add them to the app only if they need alerts, and leave them off the monitoring call list unless they will be the one at home most often.

Can the monitoring center call two of us at the same moment instead of one after the other?
+

Most centers work down the list in order, but the app sends a push alert to every user it is set to notify at the same instant the alarm trips. In practice that means the first caller hears the phone ring while everyone else already sees the alert, which is usually as fast as a simultaneous call.

Should the super of our Ocean Parkway building have a code to our apartment alarm?
+

Only if you want him to be able to enter while the alarm is armed, for example for a leak while you are traveling. If you do, give him his own code rather than yours, limited to disarming, so the history shows when he used it and you can delete it without changing your own. Many residents give the super a key but no code and simply tell him to call first.

If the person who opened the alarm account moves out, can the account be moved to another family member?
+

Yes. The panel and sensors stay in the house; only the account holder, the master code and the call list change. We update the monitoring account, transfer app ownership to the new holder, delete the old master code and fob, and test the call list again with the new first caller.

If a smoke detector on our alarm goes off and nobody at home speaks English, will the fire department still be sent?
+

Fire signals are handled differently from burglar signals. With monitored smoke detectors, a central station typically treats a fire alarm as urgent and does not wait for a long verification conversation before contacting the fire department. Ask your monitoring provider to confirm its fire procedure in writing, and keep the battery smoke alarms your building requires as well.

What happens if someone types the wrong code several times in a row?
+

Most panels lock the keypad for a short period after a set number of wrong attempts, and some send a tamper or failed-code alert to the account holder. That protects you from someone guessing, but it can trap a nervous relative. For anyone who struggles with codes, a fob avoids the problem altogether.
