The Neighborhood

Why Laconia Properties Need Access Control

Laconia is a name people use more than they define. Wikipedia treats it as the northern section of Allerton, bounded by Boston Road to the northwest, East Gun Hill Road to the northeast, the Dyre Avenue Line of the 5 train to the east, Allerton Avenue to the south and Bronxwood Avenue to the west, and notes that it contains a public housing campus and lies mostly in ZIP code 10469. One neighborhood guide draws the western edge further east, at Williamsbridge Road, and local papers stretch the name north of Gun Hill Road: a 2022 Norwood News report placed an incident on Boston Road above Gun Hill in “the Laconia section,” in the 47th Precinct, while the city’s own housing authority describes the public housing on Burke Avenue as being in Eastchester. Wikipedia itself admits that the names in this part of the Bronx “are highly disputed.” We describe streets instead: if your property sits around Laconia, Wilson, Bouck, Givan, Seymour, Burke or Adee Avenues, between Allerton Avenue and Gun Hill Road, this page is written for you.

Civically the picture is clearer. Bronx Community Board 11 describes its district as running north to Adee Avenue, Boston Road and East Gun Hill Road, and as coterminous with the NYPD’s 49th Precinct, whose page lists Allerton and Eastchester Gardens among the areas it serves from 2121 Eastchester Road. The confusion with the 47th Precinct is understandable: that station house sits at 4111 Laconia Avenue, but far to the north, where the avenue runs on into Williamsbridge and Edenwald.

The housing is a mix that has been changing for twenty years. Wikipedia describes Allerton as “dominated by multi-unit homes of various types” and says the majority of residents rent, and the neighborhood guide describes detached brick homes, two- and three-family houses and low-rise apartments, many from the 1920s to the 1950s. In 2005 the City Council approved a rezoning of 163 blocks covering parts of Pelham Gardens, Laconia and Baychester, which CityLand reported was meant to stop rowhouse and multifamily development that the Department of City Planning found out of scale with the existing homes. The same plan lowered permitted density on many blocks but slightly raised it along Gun Hill Road and near the 5 train station, and changed the commercial zoning along Gun Hill Road to allow more retail and encourage mixed-use buildings. That is the logic behind what is being built today.

The building numbers have turned sharply upward. The NYU Furman Center’s profile of Morris Park/Bronxdale, the community district that contains Laconia, last updated May 6, 2026, counts new residential building permits authorizing zero units in 2006 and 16 in 2010, against 253 in 2023, 194 in 2024 and 295 in 2025, and 148 units receiving new certificates of occupancy in 2025 alone. Between 2010 and 2025 the district added about 900 units in buildings of four or more apartments. In 2026 New York YIMBY reported three filings it placed in Laconia: a 13-story mixed-use building with 97 residences, about 20,600 square feet of commercial space and 55 parking spaces at Boston Road and Bouck Avenue; a six-story mixed-use building with 60 residences, a cellar and 28 enclosed parking spaces on Wilson Avenue near Givan Avenue; and a five-story, 12-apartment building on Wilson Avenue between Adee and Burke Avenues.

Every one of those buildings will need a self-locking street door, an intercom, entry for couriers and staff, a garage or service entrance, and doors that release when the fire alarm sounds. The general contractor, the electrician, the door supplier and the fire-alarm contractor each touch access control without owning it, and when nobody owns it the gaps show up late: frames without strike preparation, a reader box on the wrong side of the door, conduit that stops at the ceiling, a fire-alarm relay never wired. Our work in Laconia starts with making sure somebody owns it.

The same discipline matters on a smaller scale. The Department of Buildings’ rules for ancillary dwelling units, adopted in December 2024 with the City of Yes for Housing Opportunity, let the owner of a one- or two-family house add one more unit, and require that unit to have a separate entrance. Bronx Community Districts 11 and 12 are both inside the pilot area for legalizing existing basement and cellar apartments, outside flood-risk zones.

Finally, the neighborhood is watching a very public example of doing it once and doing it right. NYCHA’s March 2025 announcement of the $391 million Eastchester Gardens renovation lists “entryway repairs,” “new security installations” and lobby and hallway upgrades among the work, and News 12 reported in August 2026 that residents are being paid to inspect the doors, stairs, elevators and roofs as the work goes in. We do not work for NYCHA or its PACT partners, but checking every door against a written standard before signing off is exactly what we bring to private buildings around it.

Repairs

New Building Door Not Releasing or a Lobby Reader Offline in Laconia?

New buildings fail in their own particular ways, usually in the first few months. A strike that buzzes but will not release because the door was hung a little low and the latch now binds. A reader that went dark because a painter unplugged the power supply in the low-voltage room. A fire-alarm release that was never wired, discovered when the alarm sounds and a door stays locked. Ring (646) 490-0629 and describe what the opening is doing; most single faults are cleared within an hour or two of the technician arriving, and anything touching egress is made safe first.

Older Laconia buildings bring the classic faults: a buzzer panel whose wiring has been spliced by three different supers, a street door closer that no longer pulls the door shut against the wind, a keypad deadbolt on a two-family whose batteries quietly died. We work on systems that other companies installed, including systems a developer’s subcontractor put in and nobody can log into. Tenants should contact their landlord or managing agent first; we work with the owner’s authorization.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Laconia’s Architecture

Laconia’s blocks put buildings from very different decades side by side, and each one takes entry hardware differently. These are the types we plan for, described from the sources above and from what any installer sees on the street; none of the addresses mentioned on this page is an AESS client.

Brick and stucco houses, 1920s to 1950s

Detached and semi-detached one- and two-family houses with front steps, side doors and basements, the stock that neighborhood guides describe as the core of Laconia. Solid wood or steel entry doors in wood frames, old mortise or cylinder locks, and no conduit anywhere. Work here is careful retrofit, with the door repaired first.

Attached houses and small multifamily from before 2005

The rowhouses and small apartment buildings that went up under the older zoning CityLand describes, often with a shared stoop door, a separate basement entrance and a buzzer panel that serves two to six apartments. Construction varies from block to frame, and what is inside the walls is only known once a cover plate comes off, so we open one box before quoting a whole building.

New five- and six-story buildings

Concrete-framed walk-ups and mixed-use buildings like the 12- and 60-apartment filings on Wilson Avenue. The structure is poured, so every sleeve and box in a slab or concrete wall has to be placed before the pour. Interior partitions are usually metal stud and drywall, doors are hollow metal in hollow metal frames, and the entrance is often an aluminum storefront system.

Mid-rise mixed-use with parking

Buildings like the 13-story filing on Boston Road: retail below, apartments above, open and enclosed parking, elevators and a community facility room. The access system becomes a small campus of lobby, garage, service, stair, elevator and retail doors planned together.

A mid-century garden-apartment campus

Eastchester Gardens, ten seven- and eight-story buildings on shared grounds, established in 1950 and now being renovated under rules that preserve the original campus design. It is named here as a landmark and an example only; its renovation is managed by NYCHA’s PACT partners, and AESS has no role in it.

Storefront buildings on the commercial streets

One- and two-story commercial buildings and corner buildings with apartments above along Gun Hill Road, Boston Road and Allerton Avenue: glass storefront doors with narrow aluminum stiles, roll-down gates, back doors onto alleys or yards, and upstairs apartments reached by a separate street door that is easy to forget.

How the materials change the method

Poured concrete. In a concrete building there is no fishing a cable later. A reader beside a garage entry or a door position switch in a concrete wall needs its box and sleeve set on the formwork before the pour, which means the device location has to be known weeks before the doors exist. We give the concrete contractor a sleeve and box list keyed to opening numbers and check it before the pour.

Metal stud and drywall. Corridors and apartment demising walls in new buildings are usually framed in steel studs, which is forgiving: boxes go in at framing, conduit or cable runs through the stud punch-outs, and changes can be made until the board goes up. The risk here is the opposite of concrete: other trades fill the ceiling space quickly, so the route from each door up to the corridor ceiling and back to the low-voltage room has to be reserved on the coordination drawings.

Hollow metal doors and frames. Electric strikes, electrified locks, door position switches and power transfers all need cut-outs and reinforcement in the frame or door. Ordered correctly, the frames arrive prepared and the hardware drops in. Ordered without the access schedule, the frame has to be cut and reinforced on site, sometimes after it has been grouted solid in a masonry wall.

Aluminum storefront entrances. Many new lobbies and almost all retail fronts use narrow-stile aluminum doors. They take electrified exit devices, narrow-stile strikes or electric latch retraction well, but not every lock fits the profile, and the cable has to pass through the frame or a power transfer hinge.

Old masonry and wood frames. In the 1920s to 1950s houses and older walk-ups, the walls are brick, block or plaster on lath, and the frames are wood. Strikes go into frames only after we check that the jamb is sound and the door hangs true; cable goes through the basement and up an interior wall where possible, or in a painted raceway where it is not. Every hole through an exterior wall is sealed against water.

Two of those rows deserve emphasis because they are where most Laconia projects either save or lose money. The door and frame submittal is the moment the hollow metal frames are ordered; if the access schedule is not attached, the frames arrive blank and every controlled opening needs field cutting. The rough-in walk is the last moment the walls are open; if nobody checks every box and conduit against the schedule before the drywall goes up, the missing pieces are found during trim-out, when fixing them means opening finished walls.

For gut renovations of older Laconia buildings the sequence is shorter but the same logic applies: decide the openings while the walls are open, prepare the doors before they are rehung, and test before the building is reoccupied. The worst outcome in a renovation is a beautiful new lobby with a reader screwed to the face of new tile because nobody remembered to leave a box.

The rules a Laconia entry system has to meet

These are summaries for planning, not legal advice. The architect or engineer of record, the Department of Buildings and FDNY have the final word.

  • Self-closing, self-locking entrances. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires the entrance doors of class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 to be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and requires an intercom with voice communication and a remote door release for buildings with eight or more apartments. Roof doors are self-closing but must not be self-locking.
  • Free exit from electrified doors. The NYC Building Code, sections 1010.1.9.8 and 1010.1.9.9, sets out how electrically locked egress doors and magnetic locks must release: on loss of power, on activation of the fire alarm or sprinkler system where present, by a sensor or by hardware on the door, and with a manual release that cuts power directly where required.
  • Apartment door hardware. Whatever happens at the street door, each apartment door in a class A building still needs its own keyed lock, heavy-duty latch set, heavy-duty deadbolt and chain guard under Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043.
  • Tenant data. Once fobs, apps or biometrics are used in a building of three or more apartments, the Local Law 63 of 2021 rules follow: a written policy, consent, as little data as possible, a 90-day limit on keeping authentication records unless they are anonymized, and keys or another option at each apartment door.
  • Low-voltage permits. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 exempts most low-voltage signaling and access wiring under 50 volts from electrical permits, but the 120-volt circuits that feed power supplies are licensed electrical work, and anything tied into the fire alarm is coordinated with the fire-alarm contractor’s work.
  • New units in houses. Under Local Laws 126 and 127 of 2024, an ancillary dwelling unit needs a separate entrance, and a two-family house that adds one becomes subject to the Multiple Dwelling Law as a three-family unless the unit is separated by a fire wall.
  • Vehicle gates and doors. Motorized garage gates and doors are built to UL 325, with monitored entrapment protection, as DASMA’s technical data sheet explains; a reader added to a gate never bypasses those devices.
  • Elevators. Firefighters rely on Phase I recall and Phase II in-car operation, which NFPA describes; any card control in the cars is designed with the elevator contractor so it drops out when the elevator goes into emergency service.
A Lesson Next Door

What the Eastchester Gardens Renovation Shows About Doing It Once

The largest construction project in Laconia right now is not a new building at all. NYCHA’s Eastchester Gardens page describes a development established in 1950, one of the oldest public housing developments in the city and the second oldest in the Bronx, with more than 1,850 residents in ten residential buildings plus a senior center and a community center. On March 27, 2025 it converted to Project-Based Section 8 through NYCHA’s PACT program, and a partner team of MDG Design + Construction, Infinite Horizons and Wavecrest Management began a renovation of all 877 apartments that NYCHA expects to finish at the end of 2028. NYCHA continues to own the land and buildings.

Two details in the public record are directly relevant to anyone planning doors in the neighborhood. The first is the scope: NYCHA’s announcement lists “new façade, envelope, and entryway repairs,” modernized elevators, “new security installations” and lobby and hallway upgrades, and says the development will be listed on the National Register of Historic Places, so the exteriors and common spaces must be restored to the extent the original designs are intact. In other words, new entry hardware has to fit doors and facades that are being preserved.

The second is the inspection. News 12 reported in August 2026 that residents are being paid through a Resident Inspector Program to walk the buildings and common areas, checking staircases, elevators, doors, roofs and grounds, photographing what they find and reporting it to management and the construction team, and that a first-of-its-kind “Little Lamb Agreement” gives the tenant association a formal way to hold the private partners to the quality and timing of the work.

We have no role in that renovation, and nothing on this page should be read as describing its equipment or its contractors. The lesson for private owners on the surrounding blocks is the method. Write down what each door is supposed to do. Check it before it is closed up. Test it against the fire alarm and against a power failure. Have someone who did not install it walk through and try every opening. Fix what fails before the final payment.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Laconia: Questions Answered

These are the questions developers, architects, owners doing gut renovations and house owners adding a unit raise when we talk about Laconia projects. They are grouped by the stage of the work they belong to. Answers that depend on law are planning summaries, not legal advice.

On Paper: Decisions Before Anything Is Built

Our building is still in design. When does access control need to be decided, and who should draw it?

The openings and the low-voltage room need to be decided by the time the architect starts the construction documents, and the details need to be on the drawings before the doors and frames are ordered. That is earlier than most owners expect: a conversation at schematic design about how residents, visitors, couriers, retail staff and drivers get in, then a written scope the architect can draw from.

Who draws it depends on the size of the project. On a small walk-up the architect usually adds our device locations to their set; on a larger building a hardware consultant or electrical engineer may carry it. What should not happen is leaving it to “the security vendor” to be hired after the walls are up; by then the decisions that cost nothing on paper have become field changes.

What belongs on the door hardware schedule for an opening that will get a reader?

Everything the door supplier, the electrician and the fire-alarm contractor need to order and wire that single opening, written in one row. For each controlled door we list: the lock function, such as an electric strike, an electrified mortise lock or an exit device with electric latch retraction; whether it is fail-safe or fail-secure; the frame and door preparations, including strike cut-outs, a raceway or power transfer hinge and a door position switch; the request-to-exit method; the closer; and whether the fire alarm must release it. We add the reader model, its mounting height and side, and the opening number used on the cable label and in the software.

Which trade installs the empty conduit and back boxes before the walls close, and how do we stop them being missed?

Usually the electrician, who is already setting boxes during framing. What matters is that one contract names the work, that the boxes and conduit are drawn on a plan keyed to opening numbers, and that someone walks every opening before the walls are closed.

Our habit is to give the electrician a single-page rough-in list per floor, with the opening number, box type, height, conduit size and destination for each controlled door, and then to walk the floor with that list before close-up. We photograph each opening at rough-in so the owner has a record of what is inside the wall.

Our lender’s budget has one line for “security.” What should that allowance actually cover for a twelve-unit building?

A single number hides the decisions that drive the cost, so we suggest breaking it into its parts before the budget is fixed. For a typical five-story, twelve-apartment Laconia walk-up the access part covers a reader and video intercom panel on the street door with a self-closing, self-locking door that meets the Multiple Dwelling Law; a controlled inner vestibule door where the plan has one; a contact or reader on the cellar and service doors; the controller, power supplies and batteries; the rough-in described above; and commissioning with the fire-alarm contractor.

Our published prices give the scale: a single controlled door at $1,500 to $2,500, a multi-door system at $4,000 to $15,000, and a video intercom panel with built-in fob access at $1,500 to $3,000, before tax. Writing it this way also ends the dispute over whether the strike was in the door package or the security package.

The general contractor wants to swap the specified readers and strikes for cheaper ones. What should never be value-engineered out?

Some substitutions are harmless and some cost the owner for the life of the building. What we advise owners never to give up is the infrastructure and the safety parts: the rough-in at every planned opening, even where no reader is going in yet; door position switches, without which the building never knows a door was propped; fail-safe or fail-secure behavior as specified, because a cheaper strike with the wrong function can trap people or leave a door open in an outage; the fire-alarm release; battery backup sized for the locks; and encrypted credentials rather than the older proximity fobs that can be copied with an inexpensive device.

The other substitution to watch for is the controller. A low-cost standalone keypad on each door looks cheaper than a networked controller, but it gives the owner no central list of who can open what, no log and no way to delete a lost credential from every door at once.

Mixed-Use Buildings: Shops, Parking and a Community Room

Diagram drawn by AESS: a generic building, not any filed project.

Our retail tenants and our residents will share a cellar corridor and a refuse room. Where should the line between the two sides fall?

At the doors that lead into anything residential, not at the shared spaces themselves. In most new mixed-use buildings the retail tenants need their own storefront entrance, a back door for deliveries, and a route to the refuse room and perhaps a meter room in the cellar. Residents need the lobby, the elevators or stairs, the mailroom and the same refuse room. The shared corridor and refuse room can be open to both groups at agreed hours; the doors from that shared zone into the residential stair, the elevator lobby and the residential corridors are the ones that must stay closed to retail staff at all times.

We set that up with access groups and schedules in the controller and, just as important, with a door plan that does not force retail staff through a residential door to reach their own back-of-house. Retail leases should say which doors the tenant may hold credentials for.

Should we pre-wire the empty storefronts for readers before we know who the retail tenants will be?

For the back door of each storefront and for any door from the retail space into shared areas, yes. Those are the openings the landlord will want controlled no matter who the tenant is, and they are usually in concrete or block where adding conduit later is disruptive. A box, an empty conduit back to the low-voltage room and a frame prepared for a strike cost little during construction. The tenant’s own interior doors and storefront entrance can be left to their fit-out.

What we suggest writing into the lease is simple: the landlord controls the back door and any door to shared space on the landlord’s system, and the tenant may connect their own alarm or camera to the pre-wired conduit with the landlord’s approval.

The plans include a small community facility room off the lobby, and nobody knows yet who will use it. How should its door be set up?

As its own access group, on its own schedule, under the owner’s control. A community facility room in a residential building might become an office for a nonprofit, a meeting room, a small program space or something else, and its users will change. If its door sits on the residential access group, every change of occupant means touching residents’ credentials. If it has its own group, a new occupant can be given credentials that open the room’s street door, or the room door off the lobby, during their hours and nothing else.

Physically, we prefer a route to the room that does not pass through the residential lobby, or a second controlled door between them.

The door from our enclosed parking into the building is the one nobody watches. What should it have?

The same treatment as the street door, because for anyone already inside the garage it is the front door. That door should have a reader, a door position switch, a strong closer and a held-open alert, and it should be covered by a camera on both sides.

The vehicle entry itself is a separate decision. Whatever opens it, whether a resident’s credential at a reader on a post, a remote or a phone, the overhead door or gate operator keeps its own safety devices, and the reader never bypasses them. We coordinate the reader location with the operator installer and with the driveway layout, and we set the garage door and the interior door on the same resident access group so a credential deleted for one is deleted for both.

Code, Fire and the Certificate of Occupancy

Which doors in a new five- or six-story apartment building have to close and lock by themselves?

Under Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, the entrance doors of a class A multiple dwelling built after January 1, 1968, which covers every new apartment building in Laconia, must be self-closing and self-locking, and kept locked except when an attendant is on duty. The law speaks of entrances from the street, passageways, courts, yards and cellars, so in a typical new building that means the main entrance, any side or rear entrance from a yard or court, and the cellar entrance. Roof doors are the exception: they must close by themselves but must not lock by themselves, and must open from inside without a key.

Beyond the law’s list, fire-rated doors such as stair doors and corridor doors have their own self-closing requirements under the building code, and apartment entrance doors carry the Housing Maintenance Code’s latch, deadbolt and chain requirements. We mark each category on the hardware schedule.

Before the certificate of occupancy, who proves the fire alarm actually releases the electrified doors?

Both installers, together, with the results written down. The fire-alarm contractor owns the relay or control module that signals the access system, and the access installer owns the door hardware that has to respond. On a properly run job the two sit down with the hardware schedule, put the fire-alarm panel into test, trigger an alarm, and walk every door marked for release to confirm it unlocks and stays unlocked until the alarm is reset.

We record the result for every opening with the date and both technicians’ names. When each installer assumes the other tested it, buildings open with doors that stay locked during an alarm.

Should card readers for the elevators be bought from the elevator contractor or from the access installer?

Usually the reader and controller come from the access installer and the interface inside the elevator controller comes from the elevator company, under a scope both have agreed to in writing. The elevator contractor is responsible for anything inside the car, the hoistway and the elevator controller, including how a security feature behaves when the car is taken over for firefighters’ emergency operation. The access installer provides the reader in the car or lobby, the cable in the traveling cable or a dedicated conduit, and the logic that says which credentials may call which floors.

The common mistake is ordering the elevators without telling the elevator company that card control is planned. If a building might ever restrict floors, put the interface and cable in the elevator contract from the start.

Houses Adding Units and Owners Next to Construction

Laconia is inside the city’s basement-apartment pilot area. If we legalize our cellar unit, what does its entrance door need before an electric lock goes on it?

First, everything the legalization itself requires, because an electric lock cannot fix a door that does not meet the rules. The Department of Buildings’ pilot program for existing basement and cellar units covers Bronx Community Districts 11 and 12, among others, for one- and two-family houses outside flood-risk areas, and its detailed rules were still being finalized when we last checked in 2026. Units that are legalized must meet the program’s fire and safety milestones, and an ancillary dwelling unit needs a separate entrance. Your architect or expediter defines that entrance; we work from their drawings.

Once the door itself is right, the electric part is straightforward. The unit gets its own credentials, the door must open from inside with one motion and no key or special knowledge, and any electric lock must fail in a way that never traps the occupant. We usually recommend a keypad or reader with a fail-secure strike on the outside and ordinary free-egress hardware inside, a door position switch so the owner knows if the door is left open, and battery backup. If the house becomes a three-family under the Multiple Dwelling Law, the tenant data rules and the Housing Maintenance Code’s door hardware requirements come with it.

A six-story building is going up on the lot behind our house. What should we change on our own doors while the construction lasts?

Mostly the back of the house, for the length of the job. A construction site next door brings scaffolding, a sidewalk shed, temporary fencing, deliveries at odd hours and a lot of people who have a reason to be near your fence. Rear doors, basement doors and yard gates that were safe because nobody could reach them unseen may become reachable from the scaffold or the site. The answer is usually temporary and inexpensive: check that the rear and basement doors latch and deadbolt properly, add door contacts with phone alerts on the openings that face the site, add a motion light on the yard, and consider a camera covering the rear for the duration.

Problems with fences or scaffolds touching your property are a matter for the contractor named on the site’s permit board and the Department of Buildings, not for us.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Laconia

Google’s AI Overview and question boxes did not load in the environment we researched from, so nothing below is quoted from them. It answers claims we hear repeated by owners who have read quick online answers, cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and general advice that does not fit buildings under construction in this part of the Bronx.

“Access control is something you add once the building is finished.”

This is the most expensive belief on the list. Readers and keypads can be added at the end; the things they depend on cannot, at least not cheaply. Boxes in concrete, conduit to the ceiling, frames prepared for strikes, power transfer hinges, a 120-volt circuit in the low-voltage room and a fire-alarm release relay all belong to earlier stages of construction.

Laconia’s current filings show why this matters at scale. A building with 60 or 97 apartments, retail, a community room and enclosed parking has dozens of openings that need decisions, and each decision touches the concrete, the doors, the electrical and the fire alarm. The Furman Center’s count of 295 units authorized by new building permits in the district in 2025 is a lot of openings to get right.

The fix costs almost nothing: a written access scope at design, the hardware schedule attached to the door submittal, and a rough-in walk before close-up.

“Wireless locks mean a new building doesn’t need any conduit.”

Wireless and battery locks have real uses, especially on apartment doors and in retrofits where opening walls is impractical. But a building’s street door, garage entry and service doors carry the heaviest traffic, the code requirements for release on fire alarm and power loss, and the expectation that they keep working through a long outage. Those doors are best wired, with power from a supply that has battery backup, and with a door position switch that reports reliably.

Wireless locks also need gateways, and gateways need power, network cable and a signal path through concrete.

Our usual plan for a new Laconia building wires every common-area door and leaves the choice of wired or wireless locks on apartment doors to the owner, with the Housing Maintenance Code’s mechanical latch, deadbolt and chain kept regardless.

“Any electrician can install the access control.”

An electrician is essential to the job, and many are skilled at low-voltage work. But New York State licenses security and fire alarm installers separately under General Business Law §69-m, and programming a controller, setting fail-safe and fail-secure behavior, integrating the fire-alarm release and handing over a working credential system are a different trade from pulling power.

The city’s low-voltage rules point the same way from the other side. Under Administrative Code §28-105.4.7, most signaling and access wiring under 50 volts does not need an electrical permit, while the 120-volt circuits that feed the power supplies are licensed electrical work. The sensible division is the one we use: the electrician provides circuits, boxes and conduit; we provide the cable, devices, programming and testing.

What matters most is that one party owns the result. Otherwise the owner inherits a system that is present but untested.

“A magnetic lock on every door is the most secure choice.”

Magnetic locks are strong and simple, which is why they appear on so many value-engineered drawings. They are also fail-safe by nature: they release when the power goes, and the building code requires them to release on fire alarm and to have a sensor or door-mounted release and, in many cases, a manual button. A building full of maglocks is a building whose doors all unlock at once in a blackout.

For most Laconia openings, an electric strike or an electrified lock in a properly prepared frame gives better security, keeps a mechanical latch on the door, and lets each door fail the right way for its purpose.

We specify door by door on the hardware schedule and explain what each door does when the power fails, the alarm sounds or someone pushes from inside.

“Access control costs $1,500 to $5,000 a door, whatever the building.”

Per-door figures from cost guides are a reasonable starting point for a single retrofit door. One published guide puts wired access control at $1,500 to $5,000 per door including cabling and a panel, and our own single-door price is $1,500 to $2,500 installed in the Bronx. But per-door figures say little about a new building, where the cost is driven by the number of openings, how many share one controller, whether the rough-in was done during construction and how much coordination the job needs.

Some cost guides also mix up security doors, smart locks and electronic access control.

For new Laconia buildings we price from the drawings: a count of controlled openings, the controller and intercom, rough-in support, commissioning and the elevator or garage interfaces.

“Phone apps make the building intercom unnecessary.”

Phone-based entry is excellent, and many new Laconia buildings will use it, but it does not cancel the legal requirement. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires buildings with eight or more apartments to have an intercom with voice communication and a remote door release. Many modern intercom panels meet that by calling residents’ phones, but the panel, the call and the release still have to work, including for residents without a smartphone.

An app-only approach depends on phones, accounts and the building’s internet. A good design keeps a fallback for each: a credential or code that works without the internet, an in-unit station or a phone call for residents who prefer it, and a battery that carries the panel and the lock through an outage.

The Tenant Data Privacy Law adds one more rule: an owner cannot require a tenant to use smart access to enter their own apartment, so the building has to support keys or another option at the apartment door.

“The tenant data rules only matter for big landlords.”

The Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to owners of multiple dwellings, which in New York means buildings with three or more apartments, that use keyless entry. That includes a new 12-unit walk-up on Wilson Avenue, and it can include a two-family house that becomes a three-family by adding a unit. Owners must give tenants a written privacy and retention policy, get express consent, collect only the minimum data and destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized.

For a new building the easy moment to comply is lease-up: the policy goes out with the lease, consent is collected at signing, and the system is configured from the start to keep only what the policy allows.

We set the retention settings in the controller to match the owner’s policy at commissioning and give the owner the policy template to adapt with their own lawyer.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

A new mid-rise and a single new unit’s entrance follow the same eight steps; on a small job some of them take minutes rather than weeks. On an existing walk-up the drawings step becomes a survey.

1. Drawings and opening count

We read the architectural, electrical and door drawings, number every opening that people pass through, and mark which of them need a reader, a keypad, a contact only or ordinary hardware.

2. Hardware schedule and riser

For each controlled opening we write the lock function, frame preparation, power transfer, position switch, request-to-exit device and fire-alarm release, and we draw a one-line riser showing where every cable goes.

3. Coordination with the trades

One meeting with the general contractor, the electrician, the door and hardware supplier, the fire-alarm contractor and, in an elevator building, the elevator company. Each leaves knowing what they supply: conduit, back boxes and 120-volt circuits from the electrician; cable and devices from us; release relays from the fire-alarm side.

4. Rough-in walk before close-up

Before drywall or block is closed we walk every opening with the schedule in hand: box at the right height, conduit to the ceiling, a pull string in every pipe, the frame prepared for the strike or the power transfer.

5. Cable, devices and the head-end

Cable is pulled and labeled with the opening number at both ends, readers, strikes and contacts are trimmed out after paint, and the controller, power supplies and intercom gateway go into the low-voltage room with battery backup sized for the locks they carry.

6. Fire-alarm and egress testing

With the fire-alarm contractor present we put the alarm into test, confirm every electrified door that must release does release, push every exit button, cut the power to prove the battery and fail-safe behavior, and record the results opening by opening.

7. Programming and lease-up

Schedules, access groups and administrator accounts are set up; the owner gets a tenant data privacy policy to issue under Local Law 63 of 2021; and credentials are issued as each household signs a lease rather than all at once on opening day.

8. Handover and follow-up

The owner receives as-built drawings, the completed hardware schedule, the test record, a controller database backup and the administrator credentials in their own name. We return about a month after occupancy to adjust closers and clear anything early use has shown.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Door That Was Not on the Drawings

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular building. When we review a set of drawings for a new building, the first thing we do is walk it the way people will use it once it is occupied, not the way it is drawn. A resident comes home from the station with shopping. A courier arrives with a cart. A retail worker opens up at six in the morning. The porter takes the bags out on collection night. A driver parks in the garage and heads upstairs. A firefighter arrives with the alarm sounding.

Each of those people passes through a sequence of doors, and nearly every set of drawings has at least one door in those sequences that nobody has scheduled: the door from the garage into the stair, the door from the retail corridor into the refuse room, the gate in the rear yard fence, the door from the mailroom into the lobby. Those doors are not missing from the plans; they are missing from the access scope, because each one sits at the edge of somebody else’s package.

Finding them on paper costs an afternoon; finding them at trim-out costs a cut frame and a patched wall. So our scope always begins with that walk, and with a list of every opening each kind of person passes through, numbered before anything is drawn.

FAQ

Laconia Access Control FAQ

How much cheaper is it to put access control in while a building is under construction than after the walls are closed?
+

Much cheaper per door, though the exact gap depends on the wall. In new construction the electrician sets a box and an empty conduit at each controlled door for little more than the cost of the materials, the door supplier prepares the frame at the factory, and we pull cable through open pipe. After the walls are closed the same door needs the frame cut in the field, a channel opened or a surface raceway run, and the wall patched and painted, often by three trades instead of one. Our installed prices on this page assume normal access; retrofit conditions are priced after the survey.

Our budget covers readers on only a few doors when the building opens. Which openings should be roughed in and left blank for later?
+

Every opening that is likely to need control within the life of the building, because that part cannot be added cheaply later. Boxes, conduit with pull strings, frame preparation and capacity on the controller are inexpensive during construction; readers and locks can follow. In a typical Laconia mixed-use building we would control the street door, the garage entry and the garage-to-building door on day one, and rough in the storefront back doors, the community room, the refuse and service doors and the roof contact, each capped with a blank plate and labeled with its opening number. Adding them later then takes a day of trim-out rather than a construction project.

How much spare capacity should a new building’s controller and power supplies have?
+

We plan for roughly a quarter more doors than the building opens with, and for power supplies loaded well below their rating. A controller that is full on opening day means the first retail tenant who wants a reader on a back door forces a second controller. The extra capacity is inexpensive when it is bought with the system; we write the spare door count and the battery runtime on the riser drawing so the next owner or manager can see what is left.

Should the low-voltage contractor work for the general contractor or be hired directly by the owner?
+

Either can work, as long as the scope is written down. Working under the general contractor keeps one schedule; hiring directly gives the owner a straight line to the people who will support the system later. What matters most is that the access scope, the hardware schedule and the handover documents are in whichever contract applies, and that the administrator accounts end up in the owner’s name rather than the builder’s.

What should an owner test at each door before signing off on a new installation?
+

Five things, at every controlled opening. Let the door close on its own and confirm it latches without a push. Present a valid credential and a deleted one, and confirm the first opens and the second is refused. From the inside, open the door the way a person leaving in a hurry would, with one motion and no key. Prop the door and confirm the held-open alert reaches whoever is supposed to receive it. Then ask to see the fire-alarm test record for that door. If any of the five fails, it goes on the punch list before final payment.

Tenants keep propping open the refuse room door that leads to the street on collection nights. How should that door be designed?
+

Design for the habit instead of fighting it. A refuse room door to the street is used by the super, the porter and sometimes the sanitation crew, usually with both hands full. We put it on a schedule that allows a free exit at all times, a credential for staff on collection nights, a strong closer and a held-open alert that waits long enough for a load of bags to go out before it complains.

Does AESS also install access control in Laconia’s older houses and walk-ups, or only in new buildings?
+

Both. New construction is the focus of this page because so much of it is being filed near the Gun Hill Road station, but most of our Laconia calls still come from owners of existing two-family houses and small walk-ups. Those jobs are retrofits under the same rules about free exit, tenant data and battery backup. The price table further up applies to both.

Can a reader go on a fire-rated stair door in a new building, and what does that do to re-entry from the stairs?
+

Sometimes, with care. A stair door is a fire door, so anything fitted to it has to be listed for use on fire-rated doors, and an electric strike on a fire door is normally a fail-secure type so the door stays latched in a fire. Exit into the stair must always be free. The harder question is re-entry: the building code has rules for taller buildings about letting people leave a stair at the floors, typically by unlocking stair-side hardware when the fire alarm sounds or by keeping certain floors open for re-entry. Which rule applies depends on the building’s height and the architect’s code analysis, so we design stair-door access from their notes rather than guessing, and test the re-entry behavior with the fire-alarm contractor.

Can AESS provide drawings or a written scope for an architect or expediter’s filing set?
+

We provide a written access control scope, a door hardware schedule for the controlled openings, a one-line riser and device locations marked on the owner’s floor plans, which the architect can incorporate into their set. We do not seal drawings or act as the architect or engineer of record, and anything that needs a licensed electrician or a fire-alarm filing is coordinated with those trades.

Can the entry system work for the first tenants if only some floors are finished when a temporary certificate of occupancy is issued?
+

Yes, if the system is planned for partial occupancy. The street door, the mailroom route and the doors on the finished floors are commissioned and tested first, with their fire-alarm releases proven, while the unfinished floors stay locked out in the software and physically separated by the builder. Credentials for residents only open what is occupied. What does not work is leaving the whole system off until the last floor is done and propping the street door in the meantime.
