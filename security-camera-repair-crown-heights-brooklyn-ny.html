The Neighborhood

Why Crown Heights Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Crown Heights is a neighborhood of front doors that a great many people pass through. The big prewar apartment buildings on Crown Street, Montgomery Street, Eastern Parkway and the blocks off Nostrand Avenue have lobbies where dozens of households share one entrance, one mail area and, increasingly, one pile of packages on the floor. The limestone and brownstone row houses north of Eastern Parkway have stoops, areaways and foyers between an outer and an inner door. The avenues are lined with stores that open onto the sidewalk under apartments. In every one of those places, the camera over the door is expected to answer the same question after something goes wrong: who came in, and when. A camera system that has quietly stopped saving video, or one whose picture of the doorway is a white glare, cannot answer it.

The local news makes the point over and over. In August 2025, News 12 Brooklyn reported that at 240 Crown Street, a building it described as having 135 units, security camera footage appeared to show a man with a small child in a stroller getting someone to open the lobby door, checking that the lobby was clear, and putting two packages from a pile on the floor into a bag on the stroller. One resident told the station, "I ship them to my job and I just collect them there." In January 2026 News 12 reported a string of thefts that police said began on December 12, 2025, the first in the foyer of 1492 Union Street, with surveillance footage showing the suspect riding off on a bicycle with a stolen package and an e-scooter. "Of course, that makes me scared," a nearby shop owner said. And in July 2025, on Pacific Street, video showed three teenage boys appearing to break into parked cars, two smashing windows while a third kept watch on a scooter. In each case the recording became the record. That only works when the camera was aimed at the right spot, the recorder was actually writing, and the clock on the clip was right.

Power is the second thing that shapes repairs here, because a camera system is only as reliable as the electricity it runs on. Just before midnight on May 6, 2025, a manhole fire on Albany Avenue cut power to a block of Crown Heights; Con Edison confirmed that "the fire that started in the manhole caused the outages," reported 200 customers out of service, and News 12 found some residents still without power late the next night. Soon after the manhole fire, firefighters were called to a separate fire in the cellar of a home on Lincoln Place between Albany and Troy Avenues. In the June 2025 heat wave, Anash.org reported outages affecting more than 6,000 Brooklyn customers, and Con Edison said it had shut off almost 900 customers in Central Brooklyn on purpose to prevent longer outages, after "an electric problem in Central Brooklyn." Stores on Kingston Avenue closed or never opened, traffic lights went out at Crown Street and Kingston and Albany Avenues, and News 12 heard residents describe power that came and went, taking air conditioning, refrigerators and building elevators with it. Each of those events is hard on recorders, hard drives, network switches and the small power boxes that feed older cameras, and the cellar, where most Crown Heights recorders live, is exactly where a fire, a flood or soot does the most damage.

The third thing is that Crown Heights has more cameras on the street than most Brooklyn neighborhoods, and they are not all owned by the people who look at them. In March 2020 CrownHeights.info reported that the first of the cameras in the city's plan for 100 new cameras in Williamsburg, Crown Heights and Borough Park went up on Kingston Avenue at the corner of Union Street, and noted that "the cameras are also not monitored 24/7, and officers are required to retrieve the footage from another location." Crown Heights Shmira, working with a local security company, has offered subsidized camera installations for homes, shuls and businesses, described as "a discount of up to 40% off the full security camera installation," with Shmira "having access only to the exterior cameras." Many buildings therefore carry cameras that serve two audiences: the owner who paid for them and a patrol or neighbors who rely on them. When one of those cameras goes dark, the repair has to respect both.

The fourth thing is the neighbors. On r/crownheights, residents regularly ask whether anyone has footage after an incident on their block. One poster looking for video of a hit-and-run wrote, "I have tried to contact businesses / buildings in the area for cameras but have not had much luck," and another, after a man set fire to a building's trash can, wrote that "we have security cameras set up and got him on cam." Footage that can be found quickly, exported cleanly and handed over with the right time on it is worth far more to a block than footage that exists only in theory on a drive nobody can search.

Finally, the buildings. The Crown Heights North Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission in three phases in 2007, 2011 and 2015, takes in row houses, two-family houses, flats and apartment buildings put up between about 1853 and 1942, and the small Park Place Historic District between Franklin and Bedford Avenues protects a row from the 1890s. South of Eastern Parkway the stock shifts toward apartment buildings from the first decades of the twentieth century and row houses with lower stoops. Brooklyn's landlords and tenants live side by side in all of it, and Brick Underground, putting the question to New York attorneys, reported that city buildings are not required to have security cameras and that a landlord is not obliged to show tenants footage outside a legal process. A dead lobby camera stays dead until whoever controls the recorder decides to pay for the repair, and we work with owners, managing agents and boards to make that decision easy.

If the recording never happened because the drive had stopped writing weeks before, we say so straight away, and then we set the system to tell you the next time it stops.

For everything else the path is the same. The $150 service call is booked within 2 hours of your request, and the visit comes in a two-hour arrival window. The large prewar lobby building: one door, a hundred households and a pile of packages

The big apartment buildings on Crown Street, Montgomery Street, Carroll Street and the blocks between Nostrand and Kingston Avenues are where Crown Heights camera repairs are most consequential, because so many people depend on one system. These buildings typically have a camera outside the main door, one or two in the lobby, one by the mailboxes, one in each elevator, and a few at the cellar door, the trash area, the courtyard and the roof, all running back to one recorder. That recorder is usually in the cellar beside the boiler room, or on a shelf in the super's office, and it has often been there for years. The cameras were added in rounds, so a lobby camera from one installer may sit beside a mailroom camera from another, with two recorders in the same closet.

What usually fails in these buildings is not a camera but coverage and recording. The 240 Crown Street video News 12 reported in 2025 shows what a lobby camera can do when it is aimed at the pile, but in many lobbies the cameras were placed to watch the door when packages were still taken upstairs, and they now see the pile only at the edge of the frame or not at all. In others, the recorder kept recording but its drive filled years ago and it was never set to overwrite, so it stopped saving on the day it ran out of space. A repair here starts with an inventory: every camera, which recorder it feeds, how many days each recorder actually holds, and whether the clock is right. Then the camera that failed, or the camera that is pointing at the wrong thing, is dealt with.

The Eastern Parkway apartment house: wide sidewalks, benches and light from every side

Eastern Parkway was laid out by Olmsted and Vaux in 1866 with a central drive between pedestrian malls and rows of trees, and the apartment houses along it face a view that is unusually hard on cameras. The entrances look out over a service road, a mall with benches and trees, and the main roadway beyond, so a camera aimed outward sees a deep scene with bright sky and headlights in the background and a doorway in shade in the foreground. Trees that were bare when a camera was aimed in winter fill the frame by June. Many of these buildings also have side or courtyard entrances on the cross streets that see far more daily traffic than the formal front door.

The limestone or brownstone single-family house: stoop, areaway and the space between two doors

On Dean Street, Bergen Street, Pacific Street, Park Place and St. Marks Avenue, a single-family row house usually has three places a camera matters: the top of the stoop, the areaway gate under it, and the foyer between the street door and the inner door. Many of these houses had cameras put up a decade or more ago, with the recorder in the cellar and the cable run up the front of the house or through the foyer ceiling. On houses inside the Crown Heights North or Park Place districts, how that equipment is fixed matters. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook asks that equipment be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and that conduit be concealed, while it treats ordinary maintenance and repair of safety equipment as work that does not need a permit.

For a repair, that means we keep the replacement camera in the same position and on sound existing fixings wherever we can, so the job stays maintenance. Where a stoop camera's cable has been stapled across carved stone or run through a cornice, we tell the owner what a better route would be, but we price that separately, and the camera repair does not depend on it. The foyer is the other half of the house. The late-2025 theft from the foyer of 1492 Union Street is a reminder that a camera outside the street door does not see what happens once the door closes behind someone.

The row house cut into apartments: one recorder, several households, cable through other people's rooms

A great many Crown Heights row houses were divided into floor-through apartments long ago, and some have been joined back together since. Where the owner lives on one floor and rents the others, the camera system is the owner's, but the cameras watch a stoop, a hall and a yard everyone shares, and the original wiring may climb through a tenant's closet or across a tenant's ceiling. We plan these repairs from the recorder end first, test each cable from there, and use a tone generator to find which run leads where, so we enter as few apartments as possible. If we do need a unit, the owner gives notice and we say in advance which one and for roughly how long.

These systems also tend to lean on someone else's internet. If the recorder reaches the owner's phone through a router in a tenant's apartment, the cameras disappear from the phone the day that tenant moves out, while they carry on recording perfectly well in the cellar. We check that before touching any camera, because it is the cheapest fix in the house.

The newer building near Atlantic Avenue: network cameras and a contractor who has gone

The newer buildings going up on and near Atlantic Avenue usually have network cameras, a managed PoE switch in a telecom closet, a video intercom with its own camera and a recorder that may be in a rack or a cabinet. The equipment is modern, but the handover often was not. We are called to buildings where the developer's low-voltage contractor finished, left, and took the only admin password and the only port map with them. A repair here starts by regaining control of the recorder and the switch with the owner's proof of ownership, then mapping which port serves which camera, and only then fixing the camera that started the call.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Taking Stock of a Crown Heights Lobby Camera System Before Fixing Anything

These notes describe how our technicians approach a camera call in a large Crown Heights apartment building, the kind with a busy lobby, a mail area and a recorder in the cellar. They set out standard practice, not the story of any one address.

Start at the recorder screen and photograph everything. Before touching anything, the technician photographs the live view, the recording status for each channel, the drive status page and the recorder's clock. A recorder that shows every camera live can still be recording nothing, and that is the first thing to establish.

Count the days. Next comes playback: the oldest recording on the drive, a spot check of each channel from last night, and the overwrite setting. In a building with many cameras, a drive that holds only a few days, or one that stopped when it filled, explains more "the camera missed it" complaints than any broken camera.

Walk the lobby with the screen in hand. The technician stands where packages are left, where people wait to be let in and where the mail is sorted, and checks each spot on the monitor. If a spot does not appear on any camera, that goes on the job record whether or not it was part of the complaint.

Then test the dark cameras from the recorder outward. Power at the switch port or the power box, the cable, the plug at the camera, and the camera last. Only when power and cable are proven good is the camera blamed. Every cable traced on the way is labeled, the clock is set to update itself, failure alerts are switched on and tested, and the super or managing agent sees a playback from after the repair before the technician leaves.

Fault by Fault

Saving, Power and Reach: Three Ways Crown Heights Camera Systems Let People Down

Most of the repairs we do in Crown Heights fall under one of three headings, alongside the aiming and coverage problems covered above. Each has its own signs, and recognizing which one you have is what keeps a repair to the part that actually failed, instead of a new system nobody needed.

Saving: a live picture and an empty playback

The most expensive surprise in camera ownership is discovering, the morning after an incident, that the camera showed a perfect live picture all night and saved none of it. It happens more often than people think, and in larger buildings it can go unnoticed for months because everyone looks at live view and nobody opens playback. Camera repair companies across the city list the same short set of causes: a failed hard drive, a recording schedule that was changed or never set, storage settings that stop the recorder when the disk fills instead of overwriting the oldest video, and a disk too small for the number of cameras. NYE Technical Services puts it bluntly: "Running out of storage is the most common reason."

The repair depends on which it is. A schedule or overwrite setting is fixed in minutes, usually within the $150 service call. A worn drive is replaced, $200 to $450 by capacity on our Brooklyn list. A recorder that is fine but too small for the building's camera count can often take a second or larger drive. Whatever the cause, we set the recorder to send an alert when a channel stops recording or the drive reports a problem, so the next failure is a notification rather than a discovery.

Power: outages, shutoffs and the cellar

When the block loses power, as it did around Albany Avenue in May 2025, or when Con Edison cuts power to part of Central Brooklyn on purpose, as it did to almost 900 customers in June 2025, every part of the system goes down at once and they do not all come back the same. The recorder is the most fragile: if it was writing when the power went, its drive can come back damaged, and the recorder may ask to initialize it, quietly record nothing, or lose its settings. A PoE switch can return with ports that no longer power their cameras. A central power box for older cameras can fail outright when the current returns, and a battery backup can turn out to have had a dead battery for years.

The cellar adds its own risks. Most Crown Heights recorders sit near the boiler, the meters or the water main, and the cellar fire on Lincoln Place in 2025 is a reminder that heat and smoke reach a recorder there before anyone upstairs knows. After an outage or an incident in the cellar, our order is fixed: protect any footage, test the drive, test the switch ports and power box, check the battery backup under load, and only then look at cameras. A PoE port swap runs $150 to $350, power supply repair $150 to $400, and a drive swap $200 to $450. Where the recorder itself is damaged, a DVR repair averages $300 to $700 and an NVR $400 to $900.

Reach: the phone, the app and the people who need to see

The third kind of failure is about who can see the cameras. In Crown Heights more people need access than in most neighborhoods: an owner, a super, a managing agent, sometimes a store tenant, and, on systems put in through Shmira's program, a patrol that sees the exterior cameras. Access breaks in predictable ways. The building changes internet provider and the recorder vanishes from every phone. A super leaves and takes the only admin password. A relative who set up the app moves away. The patrol's access stops after a recorder is replaced, or worse, an outside viewer ends up with access to interior cameras nobody meant to share.

These are repairs of accounts and settings far more often than of hardware, and they are usually covered within the service call. We check the recorder's own screen first, because if the cameras are live there, the fault is the network or the app. Then we set up separate accounts for each person or organization, with the cameras each one should see and no more, and write down who holds the main password.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Crown Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations Crown Heights owners, boards, supers, tenants and shopkeepers describe when they call us about their cameras. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Lobbies, foyers and packages

Couriers leave everything in a heap on our lobby floor, and the lobby camera only catches the edge of it. Can the camera we have be turned to cover both the heap and the front door, or does that take a second camera?

Sometimes one camera can do both, but often it cannot do both well. A wide view that takes in the door and the pile shows everything small, so you see that someone took a box but not who. The common answer is to keep the existing camera on the door, where it identifies people coming in, and add a second camera aimed down at the place the packages actually land, which is usually near the mailboxes. If the building would rather move the packages than add a camera, the cheapest fix of all is to agree on one spot for deliveries and aim the existing camera there. We look at the lobby on the visit, show you what each option would look like on the screen, and price the second camera separately if you want one.

Thieves keep taking parcels from the foyer between our street door and inner door. The only camera we have is outside on the stoop. Is there a way to watch the foyer without running a new cable all the way up from the cellar?

Often, yes. The stoop camera's cable already reaches the front of the house, and depending on the system it can sometimes feed a second camera through a small splitter or switch at that end, or the foyer camera can draw power from the same source and send its picture over a short run back to the stoop camera's cable. On older analog systems that is harder, and a separate run may be the honest answer, though it can usually follow the stoop camera's existing path. A small camera on the foyer ceiling, aimed at the inside of the street door, shows who comes in and what they carry out. We check what your system can do before recommending a route.

Outages, fires and the cellar

There was a small fire in our cellar after a manhole fire on the block. The recorder down there still turns on, but it is coated in soot. Can it be cleaned and kept, or should it be replaced?

It depends on what is inside it. Soot is conductive and slightly acidic, and when it gets into a recorder it settles on the circuit board, the fan and the hard drive's vent, where it can cause faults months later. A recorder that only has soot on its case and still records normally can often be cleaned and kept, with its drive tested and its fan replaced. One that has soot inside, a noisy fan, or a drive reporting errors should be treated as near the end of its life, and its footage should be copied off first. If an insurance claim is involved, photograph the recorder before anything is cleaned, and we write down what we found so the adjuster has it. Water from the firefighting is the bigger risk for the cameras and cable in the cellar, so we test those too.

Our recorder still shows every camera live, but it has stopped saving anything new and the screen says the disk is full. Did it break, or is something set wrong?

Almost certainly something is set wrong, though the drive is worth testing while we are there. Recorders are meant to overwrite their oldest video when the disk fills, so they keep running forever. If that setting was switched off, or the drive was set up as something other than a normal recording disk, the recorder stops saving the day it runs out of space and simply carries on showing a live picture. The fix is to switch overwrite back on, check every channel's schedule, and confirm playback the next day. While we are in the menu we look at the drive's health report, how many days it now holds, and whether the camera count needs a larger or second drive. If the drive filled because recording was set far higher than it needs to be, we can lower it on cameras that do not need the detail.

Cars and the street

After a night of car break-ins on our street, our camera that faces the curb showed nothing but glare off windshields and streetlights. Can it be set up to actually show the cars and the people around them?

Usually, yes, and often without a new camera. A camera set for a lit doorway close by is badly set for a row of cars across the sidewalk at night: the streetlight and passing headlights push it to darken the whole frame, and the windshields turn into white patches. Turning on wide dynamic range or backlight compensation, setting exposure for the curb rather than the whole scene, narrowing the view to the part of the street you care about, and turning off infrared that bounces off the nearest car usually bring the cars and the people back. Where the camera has a lens too wide for the distance, a replacement with a narrower view is the fix, priced from our Brooklyn list. Expect to see people and their actions clearly; reading a plate on a moving car at night is a different job and needs a camera made for it.

A window air conditioner on the floor above drips across our facade camera's view all summer, and the picture is streaked whenever it runs. Is the camera ruined, or can it be moved?

The camera is very likely fine; the problem is where it sits. Condensate from an air conditioner above runs down the wall and across the camera's window, leaving drops and mineral streaks that the camera's infrared lights up at night. Cleaning helps for a day. The lasting fix is to move the camera to one side of the unit's drip line, add a small hood or shield over it, or ask the neighbor upstairs to fit a drain line that carries the water clear of the wall. If the camera's window is etched by years of mineral water, the cover or the camera may need replacing. We look at the wall on the visit and price the move; on a landmarked facade we keep the new position to plain masonry or a mortar joint.

Neighbors and community camera programs

Our cameras went in through Shmira's discount program, and Shmira can see the outside ones. If you repair or replace the recorder, does that access stay, and who decides?

You decide, because the cameras and the recorder are yours. Under that program, Shmira was described as having access only to the exterior cameras, which normally means a separate viewer account on your recorder or app limited to those channels. When a recorder is replaced or reset, that account usually disappears with it, so access stops until someone sets it up again. Before a repair we ask whether any outside organization has viewing access and whether you want it kept. Afterward we recreate the viewer account limited to the exterior cameras, or leave it off if you prefer, and we never give an outside viewer access to interior cameras unless you ask in writing. If the original installer still services the system under the program, check with them too, so two companies are not changing the same recorder.

A neighbor posted online asking if anyone has video of a hit-and-run on our corner from two nights ago. How do we check our recorder and send her a clip, and should we?

Check quickly, because many recorders overwrite in a few days. Open playback on the camera that faces the corner, jump to the time she gave, and use the motion search or timeline to find the moment. Then export that clip, ideally a few minutes either side, to a USB stick from the recorder itself, rather than recording your phone screen, so the original quality and timestamp survive. Whether to share is your call. A sensible approach many people take is to save the clip, then offer it to the investigating precinct directly, the 77th or the 71st depending on the block, and let the neighbor know you have done so. If your recorder will not export or the playback is missing, that is a repair we can book with the clip as the first task.

Stores and restaurants on the avenues

The string lights in our restaurant's back garden turn the yard camera's night picture into bright blobs, and after we switch them off at closing the camera sees nothing at all. Can one camera handle both?

Often, with the right settings and position, but the hours you care about should decide. Point-like lights in the frame make a camera darken everything else, so during service the faces between the bulbs go dark; after closing, with the lights off, a camera that relies on its own infrared may be facing a fence or foliage that bounces the light straight back. Moving the camera so the strings run across the top of the frame instead of through the middle, switching on wide dynamic range, and setting the night mode to take over at the time you close usually gives you both. If the yard is fully dark after closing and you need detail, a low-level light on a timer or a camera made for low light does better than any setting. We look at it in the evening when we can, because that is when the problem is visible.

The camera over our register on Nostrand Avenue shows the cash drawer clearly but only the top of a customer's head. After a robbery, the police could not use it. What should change?

The camera is doing what it was aimed to do, which is watch the money. To identify people, you need a camera at roughly head height or a little above, aimed at the customer side of the counter, ideally from behind or beside the clerk so it sees faces as people approach. Many stores keep the overhead camera for the drawer and add a second, lower one for faces, and a third near the door so everyone who comes in is seen once from the front. We check whether your recorder has a free channel, where cable can reach without opening the ceiling, and whether the existing camera can be lowered a little instead. The written price lists each option so you can do one now and one later.

Analog and HD-over-coax cameras

Cameras on round BNC plugs, from old analog to HD-TVI, HD-CVI and AHD, still common in prewar lobbies and older stores. They fail at the twist-on plug, the plug-in adapter and the central power box after an outage.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras that take power and data over one cable from a PoE switch, standard in newer buildings near Atlantic Avenue. Faults: a dead switch port after a shutoff, a loose plug, an address clash or firmware out of step with the recorder.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrid recorders

The box that keeps the video, usually in a cellar, a super's office or a back room. Faults: drives worn by heat and outages, overwrite switched off, clocks that drift and passwords that left with a former super.

PoE switches and network closets

Switches and routers in telecom closets and cellars, where one failed port, a hot closet or a tripped battery backup takes a group of cameras down together.

Dome, turret and bullet housings

Cracked domes in lobbies, fogged windows on stoops, streaked covers under air conditioners and brackets loosened on fences and pergolas.

Wi-Fi, battery and doorbell cameras

Consumer cameras on doors, foyers and yards that drop off behind brick and limestone, lose the app after a router change, or stop saving clips when a cloud plan lapses.

Elevator and corridor cameras

Cameras in passenger elevators and long hallways of large buildings, fed by traveling cable or long runs, and taken down with everything else when an outage stops the elevator.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, plug-in adapters and battery backups, tested under load and replaced when they cannot carry the recorder and switch through a short outage.

Lobby monitors, viewer accounts and apps

Monitors at the super's desk or behind the counter, and phone or computer access for owners, staff, family and any outside viewer limited to exterior cameras.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Crown Heights

The claims below turn up in AI-generated answers and in national cost and how-to guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is paraphrased, then checked against what repairs actually look like in Crown Heights lobbies, row houses and stores.

"If you can see the camera live, it is recording"

Quick troubleshooting answers usually treat a live picture as proof the system works, and move straight on to the camera when something is missed.

Live view and recording are separate jobs inside the recorder. A recorder whose drive has filled with overwrite switched off, whose schedule was changed, or whose drive has quietly failed will still show every camera live. Repair companies across the city list a failed drive, a wrong schedule and storage settings among the most common reasons cameras show a picture but save nothing, and in a large Crown Heights building where everyone watches live view, that gap can last for months.

Open playback for last night on every camera once a month. If anything is missing, the repair is usually a setting or a drive, not a camera, and it is worth catching before the night you need it.

"Once the power comes back, the cameras will come back with it"

Many answers assume a camera system is like a lamp: switch the power back on and it works again.

After the Albany Avenue manhole outage in May 2025 and Con Edison's deliberate Central Brooklyn shutoff in June 2025, the parts of a camera system did not all come back the same way. Recorders that lost power while writing can return with a damaged drive, a lost configuration or a prompt to initialize, PoE switches can return with dead ports, and small power boxes can fail when the current returns. The cameras on the wall are often the healthiest part of the system.

After any outage, check playback from the hour after the power returned, not just the live screen. If the recorder asks to initialize or format anything, cancel and call before answering, because that prompt erases the drive.

"Your block already has police and community cameras, so yours matter less"

Some answers suggest that city or community cameras on the corner cover what a private camera would.

The NYPD camera that went up at Kingston Avenue and Union Street in 2020 was described by CrownHeights.info as not monitored around the clock, with footage retrieved from another location, and it watches a corner, not your lobby, your foyer or your yard. Community programs such as Shmira's rely on exterior cameras that residents and businesses pay for and maintain. When neighbors ask on r/crownheights for footage after a hit-and-run, they are asking the private cameras.

The cameras on your building cover the places public cameras never will: inside the door, at the mailboxes, along the side of the house. They are only useful if somebody keeps them recording, and that somebody is the owner.

"A bigger hard drive fixes recording problems"

AI answers often recommend a larger drive whenever a recorder is missing video or keeps too few days.

A bigger drive helps when the recorder is working correctly and simply needs more room for the number of cameras. It does nothing for a recorder whose overwrite is switched off, whose schedule is wrong, or whose cameras are recording at a resolution and frame rate far higher than they need. In those cases a new drive fills and stops, or keeps the same few days, just as the old one did.

The right order is to check the settings, test the drive's health, and work out how many days the building actually needs. Then, if more storage is the answer, a drive swap on our Brooklyn list runs $200 to $450 by capacity.

"Older camera systems are not worth repairing"

Generic advice often treats any system more than a few years old as due for replacement.

Many Crown Heights lobby and store systems are older coax systems with cable already run through prewar walls and ceilings, and that cable is often the most valuable part of the system. A hybrid recorder can keep those cameras working, add network cameras where they are needed, and avoid pulling new cable through plaster. A single failed camera on a working system is a single repair. Replacement makes sense when the recorder can no longer be supported, the cameras cannot produce a usable picture, or the repairs keep stacking up.

On our Brooklyn list a hybrid recorder swap runs $700 to $1,200 installed, with the channels set up again. We will tell you honestly when a system has reached the point where repairing it is money poorly spent.

"Any camera change in a historic district needs a Landmarks permit first"

Some answers warn that owners in landmarked areas need commission approval before touching a camera.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook treats ordinary maintenance, repair and retrofitting of health, safety and utility equipment as work that does not need a permit, while it sets rules for new equipment on facades: attachment at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, nothing through cast iron or decorative surfaces, and concealed conduit. In the Crown Heights North and Park Place districts, replacing a failed camera in the same position on sound fixings is normally that kind of maintenance.

Adding new cameras to a landmarked front, or moving one to a new spot on the facade, is a different matter, and we tell you when a change crosses that line. A repair on the existing mount does not have to wait for paperwork.

"Camera repair in Brooklyn costs about $1,400"

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page for surveillance camera installation or repair gives an average of $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145, and AI answers sometimes repeat it as the price of a repair.

That figure mixes new installations with repairs. Most Crown Heights repair calls are one fault: a drive, an overwrite setting, a switch port, a power box, a lobby camera re-aimed, one camera replaced. On our Brooklyn list those land between $150 and $700, and only a new recorder, a partial rewire or several cameras at once reach $700 to $1,500.

The realistic budget for one problem is the $150 service call plus the one failed part, and you see that price in writing before work begins. Owners who plan around the big average sometimes replace a whole system that needed one drive.

FAQ

Crown Heights Security Camera Repair FAQ

Should our building post a sign saying the lobby is on camera?
+

There is no general city rule requiring one for a residential lobby, but many owners post a small sign because it discourages theft and tells residents the cameras are there for them. Employers who monitor staff have separate notice duties under state law. If you want a sign, put it at the door where people see it before they enter.

Should the recorder be plugged into its own outlet rather than a shared power strip?
+

It is better on a battery backup plugged into an outlet that nothing else heavy shares, such as a boiler control, a sump pump or a space heater. That keeps it from being switched off by accident and lets it ride out short outages. If the outlet itself is faulty, that is a job for a licensed electrician, since we do not do electrical work.

Someone deleted video from our recorder on purpose. Can it be recovered?
+

Sometimes, if little has been recorded since, but it is never guaranteed. Stop recording on that drive as soon as you can so nothing overwrites the space, and do not run any repair tools on it. We can check the recorder's log for who deleted what and when, and tell you whether specialist recovery from the drive is worth trying.

Can the old intercom wiring in our row house be used for a new camera?
+

Occasionally, but usually not well. Old intercom wire is thin and was made for audio and a door release, not for video or for powering a camera. It can sometimes help pull a proper cable through the same path, which saves opening walls. We test what is there and tell you whether it can carry a camera or only guide a new run.

Do you repair cameras that people mount inside their cars?
+

No. Car cameras are self-contained consumer devices that the maker supports, and we work on building systems. What we can do is aim and set up a camera on your house or building so it covers the stretch of curb where you park, which records whether or not the car's own camera is running.

Is it all right for our house camera to record the sidewalk and the street?
+

In general, recording what anyone could see from the sidewalk is common practice, and most stoop and curb cameras do it. The care points are not aiming into a neighbor's windows, keeping audio off unless you have checked the rules, and handling footage responsibly. We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice; for a dispute, ask an attorney.

How will we know the technician at our door is from your company?
+

Your booking comes with a confirmation text giving the date and the two-hour arrival window, and the technician arrives inside that window, not before it. If anyone turns up outside the window or without that booking, call our office line before letting them in.

Our camera's built-in siren and flashing light keep going off in the middle of the night. Can that be switched off?
+

Yes. Cameras with deterrence features can sound a siren or flash a light when they detect a person, and the default settings often trigger on passers-by, cats or headlights. We can turn those features off, limit them to certain hours, or restrict them to a small zone such as your gate, while the camera keeps recording normally.

Can the recorder show who logged in and downloaded video?
+

Most current recorders keep a log of logins, playback, exports and setting changes, tied to each user account. That log is only useful if each person has their own account instead of everyone sharing the main password, so on a repair we set up separate accounts for the owner, the super and anyone else who needs access.

Can our facade camera be moved inside, behind the parlor window, instead of on the stone?
+

It can, and it keeps the facade untouched, but glass brings its own problems. At night the camera's infrared reflects off the window and blinds it, and dirt or condensation on the glass softens the picture. A camera pressed close to the glass with its infrared turned off, relying on street and porch light, works best. We test the view before committing to the move.

Can every clip we export show the date and time on the picture itself?
+

Yes. Recorders can stamp the date, time and camera name onto the video, so the information travels with the clip even when it is played on another computer. We switch that on, place it where it does not cover faces, and set the clock to update itself automatically so the stamp is right.
