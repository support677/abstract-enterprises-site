The Neighborhood

Why Long Island Properties Need Reolink Camera Installation

This is our hub page for Reolink work across Nassau and Suffolk, and we have anchored much of it in one place: the Nassau Hub around the Coliseum in Uniondale. The LI Herald defines the Hub simply as the area centered immediately near Nassau Coliseum, and its history explains a lot about Long Island as a whole. The land was part of the Hempstead Plains, which, according to Metro Airport News, served as an Army enlistment center during the Revolutionary War. In 1918 it became Hazelhurst Aviation Field #2, renamed Mitchel Field later that year, according to Wikipedia. When the base was decommissioned in 1961, the LI Herald notes, the ground went to the Mitchel Field sports complex, the Cradle of Aviation Museum, Hofstra University, Nassau Coliseum and Nassau Community College. Around that old airfield grew the pattern you see from Valley Stream to Riverhead: a commercial core of offices, stores and venues, surrounded by street after street of single-family houses with yards, driveways and garages. A Reolink system has to work in both worlds, and the two need very different installs.

Start with the houses, because they are most of what we are called to. The Census Bureau's 2023 five-year American Community Survey counts 477,860 housing units in Nassau, 364,529 of them one-unit detached homes, with a median year built of 1955. Suffolk has 579,790 units, 463,095 of them one-unit detached, and a median year built of 1970. Newsday, in an analysis of nearly 12,000 home sales in 2026, found that more than 5,000 of the houses sold were built in the 1950s and 1960s. In Levittown alone, according to Kerri Murray Architecture, 17,447 homes went up between 1947 and 1951, ranch houses built on concrete slabs with an expandable attic, no garage and hot-water radiant heating in the floor. That housing stock shapes every Reolink job on the Island. There is usually one owner who can approve every hole. There is usually an attic, a basement or a garage that can carry cable. And there is almost always an outbuilding, a long driveway or a side yard that sits farther from the router than a WiFi signal comfortably reaches.

The streets around the Hub show the variety in a small area. NeighborhoodScout describes Uniondale's homes as mostly capes and ranches, modest in size, and reports that 55.45 percent of its housing was built between the 1940s and the 1960s. Homes.com describes the area as divided by Hempstead Turnpike, with East Garden City to the north busy with commercial activity and holding condos, townhouses and apartments, and the southern part mostly residential, with Cape Cods and minimal traditional houses that often have detached garages and private driveways. A Hempstead real estate guide on hometsair.com says the homes near Hofstra and the Coliseum are mostly 1950s and 1960s split-levels and hi-ranches. A Uniondale restoration contractor, whose description we treat as one firm's view rather than an independent survey, says most local homes are Cape Cods and ranches on concrete-block foundations with finished basements added in the 1960s and 1970s. For a camera installer, each of those details is a fact about cable. A detached garage means a run across the yard or a camera that has to look back at the garage from the house. A split-level means cable climbing between levels that do not stack neatly. A block foundation means a masonry bit, not a wood bit, where the cable enters.

Ownership matters too. NeighborhoodScout puts Uniondale at 62.02 percent owner-occupied and 37.98 percent renter-occupied. That is a large renting population for a neighborhood of houses, and it means a good share of the people looking for cameras are tenants in a house or an apartment who cannot drill without the owner's agreement. Reolink's own guidance for renters says most landlords do not allow drilling for cameras or wiring, and points renters toward battery and WiFi models. We handle those calls differently from owner calls, and we cover both below.

Why do Long Islanders want cameras in the first place? The local news gives a clear answer, and most of it involves driveways and doors. In January 2024, ABC7 reported that Nassau and Suffolk police, both district attorneys, the NYPD, the State Police and the FBI had formed a task force on residential burglaries and stolen cars, and that police believed the burglars were attracted by cars in people's driveways. Daily Voice later reported a crew that allegedly entered Long Island homes searching for key fobs before stealing high-end vehicles; the towns named in that case did not include Uniondale. Patch reported a car theft in Uniondale itself, where a man left a gray 2008 Honda Element running with the keys inside on Uniondale Avenue near Waverly Place, and police released a screenshot of the suspects. Daily Voice reported a robbery at the Pump & Stuff gas station on Jerusalem Avenue, where police released surveillance footage of the suspects. CBS New York reported burglars breaking through the back door of a home on Walton Avenue in Uniondale shortly after 9 a.m. And in September 2026, News 12 Long Island reported that the owner of Morning Delights, a Uniondale business, received a motion alert from his security camera, went to the restaurant and found a burglary suspect there. We have no connection to any of those properties and do not claim any of them as customers. We mention them because they show what Long Island cameras are expected to do: see the driveway and the back door clearly, send an alert the moment something moves, and keep footage good enough for police to use.

Suffolk's biggest recent case was about packages. In November 2025, CBS New York reported that the Suffolk district attorney had charged 14 men in a porch-theft ring that worked 31 communities, including Brentwood, Central Islip, Shirley and Deer Park, between October 2023 and February 2025. Prosecutors said the group hacked into systems to learn when Verizon and AT&T devices were being delivered, stole more than 200 phones and tablets, and faced 50 felony charges. A front-door camera does not stop a theft like that on its own. It does put a face and a time on the doorstep, which is what investigators asked residents for.

Then there is the weather. When Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, ABC7 reported that almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers were in the dark at the peak. During Sandy, according to Wikipedia, Nassau called voluntary evacuations for the South Shore surge area south of Sunrise Highway. In August 2024, the state Department of Financial Services described the first flash flood emergency ever declared for Suffolk County, the failure of the Mill Pond dam in Stony Brook, and more than 2,000 residents and business owners with flood damage. A camera system on Long Island has to be mounted for wind, sealed against driven rain, kept out of basements that flood where possible, and backed up so the recorder keeps running when the street loses power. Most self-installed kits we hear about miss at least one of those.

That last point is why Reolink in particular comes up so often here. Reolink sells complete kits through big retailers; a Slickdeals thread from 2024 discussed a Costco Reolink 4K system with a 16-channel NVR, a 4 TB drive and eight wired cameras. Reviewers describe Reolink's battery and WiFi cameras as DIY-friendly. Plenty of Long Island homeowners buy a kit, mount the easy cameras themselves, and then stall at the detached garage, the second-story eave or the run through a block foundation. A Slickdeals commenter's tip for wired kits, putting an outdoor PoE switch box outside and pulling a single Ethernet cable indoors to the NVR, is the kind of shortcut that works well when it is weatherproofed properly and causes trouble when it is not. Another commenter on the Costco thread warned that the kit's cameras "will only work on their nvr," which is worth knowing before you mix brands. We do new Reolink systems from scratch, finish half-done kits, and take over systems someone else put in.

The slab ranches of Levittown and its neighbors

The Levittown ranch is the house that taught Long Island what a starter home looks like, and it is an unusual house for camera wiring. According to Kerri Murray Architecture, the original houses sat on concrete slabs and were heated with hot-water radiant pipes running through the floor. There is no basement to hide an NVR in and no crawlspace to pull cable through. Drilling down is not an option at all: a bit through a slab that carries heating pipes is a repair bill, not a shortcut. So every cable in a slab ranch goes up. We put the NVR in a closet, a hall cabinet or the converted garage many owners have added, run the cable up into the attic, and drop it out through the soffit to each camera. Many Levittown houses have been expanded and re-roofed since 1951, so the attic you have may not be the attic the house was built with. We look before we plan, and we tell you if a run needs a short exterior raceway instead.

The Capes and ranches south of Hempstead Turnpike

The Cape Cods and ranches that Homes.com and NeighborhoodScout describe around Uniondale are the most common Long Island job we expect. A Cape usually gives us an attic over the main floor, often with knee walls and finished rooms in the half-story, and a basement below. Front and side cameras feed from the attic out through the eaves. The NVR goes in the basement near the router or the utility area, where it stays cool. The tricky camera is usually the one facing the back yard and the detached garage. A Cape with finished second-floor rooms may leave little attic space at the back, and a cable to a garage forty or fifty feet away has to cross the yard. We plan that run as its own decision: an overhead aerial run, a cable along a fence, a buried conduit, or a camera on the back of the house looking at the garage instead of a camera on the garage itself. The restoration contractor's description of concrete-block foundations matters here too, because a cable entering the basement through block needs a masonry bit and proper sealing, or it becomes the place water comes in.

The split-levels and hi-ranches near Hofstra and the Coliseum

Split-levels and hi-ranches are the houses that make installers earn their pay. Their half-floors do not stack, so a wall on the upper level may sit over a garage ceiling, over a half-height crawlspace, or over nothing at all. Getting cable from an NVR on the lower level to cameras under the upper-level eaves means finding a vertical path: a closet that stacks, a chase beside a chimney, the gap behind a garage wall, or an exterior run hidden at a downspout or inside corner. A hi-ranch usually has its main living floor above a partly sunk lower floor, which often makes the attic a straightforward route for front cameras and the garage ceiling a good route to the side. We plan these houses room by room from your photos and confirm on site before drilling. The other quirk is camera height. The ground often falls away at the back of a split-level, so a camera mounted under the rear eave may sit much higher than the front ones and need a different lens angle to see faces.

The 1970s Suffolk colonials and raised ranches

With a median build year of 1970, Suffolk's typical house is a generation younger than Nassau's. Colonials and raised ranches of that period usually have an attached garage, a full second floor and a roomier attic, which tends to make camera cabling easier than in a Cape. The longer distances are the challenge. Suffolk lots are often larger, driveways longer, and the front of the property farther from the house. A camera covering the end of a long driveway may be well beyond the reach of a cable run from the house, which is when we look at a pole-mounted camera with its own power, a solar battery model, or a pan-tilt-zoom camera that can watch the far end from the house. On the East End, the job is often a seasonal house that sits empty for months; there the priority is remote alerts and recording that keeps going through a winter of storms with nobody home to reset anything.

East Garden City condos and townhouses

Homes.com describes condos, townhouses and apartments north of Hempstead Turnpike in East Garden City. Attached homes add a layer of approval. The exterior walls, roofs and common areas usually belong to the association, not to you, so a camera on the outside wall or a cable through the siding needs written approval first. Inside your unit, a Reolink camera looking out a window or a doorbell at your own door may be allowed where an exterior wall camera is not. We ask for the association's rules before we book, and we plan to them.

The office towers and the old airfield buildings

The commercial side of the Hub is covered in its own section below, but two building types matter here. LoopNet describes RXR Plaza's twin towers as built of virtually column-free concrete slabs with four-inch raised access floors, which makes cable routing for a tenant's cameras much easier than in a building with no floor void. And the Historical Marker Database notes that Mitchel Field's 1929 to 1932 construction added brick barracks, officers' clubs, housing, warehouses, operations buildings and eight massive steel and concrete hangars, much of which remains in place today. Patch reports that the main 108 acres of the Mitchel Field complex in Uniondale were added to the National Register of Historic Places. We found no source saying that listing restricts where cameras can go, so we do not claim it does; we ask the building's owner what they allow on the brick before we drill.

Decide what each camera has to do

The most useful thing you can tell us is not how many cameras you want but what each one is for. "See the faces of anyone at the front door." "See the whole driveway, including both cars, at night." "Watch the side gate." "See the back of the detached garage, where the bikes are." Each of those is a different camera choice. A face at the door needs a camera close and at a modest height. A whole driveway needs a wide view. A car's license plate at the curb needs a narrow view aimed at one spot. Write a sentence for each spot you care about, and we will tell you how many cameras it takes and which models suit each job. Often the count goes down once the purpose is clear.

Look at the neighbors' side too

On Long Island's close-set lots, a camera on your side yard will usually see some of the neighbor's yard and windows as well. New York Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to install a camera where people have a reasonable expectation of privacy without their consent. We aim cameras at your property, use the Reolink privacy masking feature where a neighbor's window falls in the frame, and will tell you if a position you want points somewhere it should not. It is also simply good relations on a block where everybody knows each other.

Reolink Camera Installation for Long Island's Architecture

Long Island's housing looks uniform from the expressway and varies a great deal up close. The type of building changes three things about a Reolink job: where the cable can run, what the camera is fixed to, and who has to agree before anything goes on the outside wall. These are the types the public record describes across Nassau, Suffolk and the Hub, and how we approach each.

Slab Ranches

The Levittown type: no basement, radiant heat in the slab, an attic that may have been rebuilt in a later expansion. Every cable goes up and out through the soffit, and the NVR lives in a closet or converted garage.

Cape Cods

The most common Uniondale house. An attic over the main floor, a half-story with knee walls, a basement on block, and very often a detached garage at the end of a private driveway.

Split-Levels and Hi-Ranches

The houses near Hofstra and the Coliseum. Half-floors that do not stack, garage ceilings that become cable routes, and ground that drops at the back and raises the rear cameras.

Colonials and Raised Ranches

Licensing, permits and practical limits for business cameras

  • State license for the installer. Your Questions Answered

Reolink Camera Installation in Long Island: Questions Answered

Cost and planning

What actually decides the price of a professional Reolink install on a Long Island house?

Three things do most of the work: how many cameras, how far each cable travels, and how hard each mounting point is to reach. Reolink's own store pages describe professional installation labor as a per-camera cost, and that is how installers on the Island tend to think, but the per-camera figure moves with the house. A camera on the front eave of a hi-ranch fed straight from the attic is a short, quick run. A camera on a detached garage fifty feet across the yard needs a separate plan and more material. A camera on a second-story gable needs a taller ladder and more time. The county matters too: Nassau addresses are priced at 1.15 times our Brooklyn base and Suffolk at 1.20 times. Our published Long Island Reolink price list sets out each line, and with photos of your lot we can tell you which ones apply before you book.

Is a four-camera kit enough for a Cape with a detached garage, or should we plan for eight?

For most Capes and ranches, four cameras cover the essentials and eight cover the house properly. A typical four-camera layout watches the front door, the driveway, the back door and the back yard. What it usually misses is the far side yard, the garage itself and the view of the curb. That is why many owners start with four cameras on an eight-channel recorder, leaving room to add the garage and side views later without replacing anything. Reolink sells kits in both sizes, and the Costco kit discussed on Slickdeals paired eight cameras with a 16-channel recorder. Before you buy, write down what each camera must see and send it to us; if two of your spots can be covered by one well-placed wide camera, we will tell you, and if one spot needs two, we will tell you that too.

Placement and coverage

How high should the cameras go on a split-level where the ground drops away at the back?

High enough to be out of easy reach, low enough to see faces. On the front of a split-level or hi-ranch, an eave camera often sits at a sensible height already. At the back, where the ground falls to a lower patio or yard, the same eave may be far higher, and a camera up there sees the tops of heads. We look at each position separately. Sometimes the answer is a camera on the lower level's wall instead, sometimes a narrower lens angled down, sometimes a second camera at the back door. Doorbells have their own rule: Reolink's support guidance recommends mounting its video doorbells about 1.2 meters, or 48 inches, off the ground, and we follow that unless your step or porch layout makes a different height better for faces.

Can cameras sit under the eaves of a 1950s Cape, and will the soffit actually hold them?

Under the eaves is usually the best place, because the overhang keeps rain and sun off the lens and the attic gives a hidden cable route. The soffit itself is the question. Many Long Island houses have vented vinyl or aluminum soffit panels that are thin and flexible, and a camera fixed only to the panel will sag and shake in the wind. We look for the solid parts: the fascia board, the framing behind the soffit, or a section where we can fit a solid mounting plate fixed back to wood. A Reolink junction box or mounting block between the camera and the house gives the screws something to bite and hides the cable connection from the weather. On an older wood soffit that has softened, we tell you and move the camera rather than trust it.

Our driveway runs a long way back from the street. Should we use a pan-tilt-zoom camera or fixed ones?

It depends on whether you need to see everything at once or one thing in detail. A fixed wide camera records the whole driveway all the time, so whatever happens is on the recording, but distant details are small. A pan-tilt-zoom camera can zoom into the far end of a long Suffolk driveway or the street, and Reolink's tracking models can follow a moving car, but while it looks one way it is not looking the other. For most long driveways we pair a fixed wide camera covering the whole drive with a second camera aimed at one choke point, usually where cars turn in. A PTZ makes sense when the property is large and you want to look around remotely. We will show you what each option sees from your actual mounting point before you commit.

Wiring and the house

How does the cable get from the house out to a detached garage at the back of the lot?

There are four honest options, and the lot decides. An aerial run on a support wire between the house and the garage is quick and clean where both buildings are tall enough. A cable along a fence or wall in protective conduit works where the fence connects the two. A buried run in conduit is the most permanent and the least visible, but takes the most work and needs care around any utilities. The fourth option is not to run cable at all: put the camera on the back of the house, looking at the garage. If the garage has its own power and you want several cameras there, a weatherproof PoE switch inside the garage fed by one cable from the house, the approach Slickdeals commenters describe, can make sense, as long as it is housed and sealed properly. Outdoor cable runs between buildings also need surge protection where they enter, which we include.

Our 1950s house may have older materials in the attic and basement. What happens before you drill?

We look first and plan around anything questionable. A Uniondale restoration contractor warns that postwar Cape Cods and ranches frequently contain asbestos materials; we cannot say that about your house without testing, and we are not a testing company. Our practice is to inspect attic insulation, pipe wrap, old ceiling finishes and flooring before we route a cable near them, to avoid drilling or cutting through anything that looks like an older material, and to stop and tell you if we find something we would rather not disturb. In almost every house there is another path, through the attic over a different room, through the garage, or along the outside in a neat raceway. If you already know from a home inspection that certain areas contain older materials, tell us when you book and we will plan around them from the start.

The recorder will live in the finished basement. How does cable reach cameras on the upper floor of a split-level?

By finding the one vertical path the house already has. Finished basements from the 1960s and 1970s often have dropped or drywall ceilings that hide joists running toward the garage or the front wall. From there we look for a stacked closet, the space behind a garage wall, or a chase beside the chimney to carry the cable up to the attic over the upper level. Where nothing stacks, the cleanest route is often outside: a single cable bundle running up an inside corner or beside a downspout in a painted raceway, from a sealed entry low on the wall to the soffit. We plan the route from your photos, confirm it on site, and tell you before we open anything inside a finished room.

Storms and power

How do Reolink cameras ride out a hurricane or a nor'easter on the South Shore?

The cameras themselves are weatherproof outdoor units; what usually fails is the installation around them. Wind works loose a camera fixed only to thin siding, driven rain finds an unsealed cable entry, and water sits in a connector that was left outside without a box. We mount every camera to solid framing or masonry, house the cable connections in junction boxes, use drip loops so water runs off the cable before it reaches the camera or the wall, and seal every penetration. In flood-prone areas, including the South Shore surge zone south of Sunrise Highway that Nassau evacuated during Sandy, we keep the recorder out of a basement that has taken water. After a big storm, a quick look at each camera in the app tells you whether any has moved.

Will the system keep recording when PSEG power goes out?

Only if something keeps the recorder powered. A PoE system takes all its camera power from the NVR or switch, so when those lose power, every camera goes dark together. When Isaias came through, almost half of PSEG Long Island's 1.1 million customers lost power at the peak, according to ABC7, and outages that long will outlast any small battery. What a battery backup does well is carry the system through the short cuts and flickers that happen far more often, and give the recorder time to shut down cleanly in a long outage instead of crashing. We put the NVR, the PoE switch and the modem on the same battery backup, so recording continues and alerts can still reach your phone while the battery lasts. If your house has a generator, the system can be on a circuit it feeds.

Theft and the police

Can a driveway camera actually help after a car theft or a break-in for car keys?

It can help investigators, which is what most residents need. In the Uniondale Avenue car theft Patch reported, police released a screenshot of the suspects; in the Jerusalem Avenue robbery Daily Voice reported, they released surveillance footage. Footage like that is useful when the camera sees faces and vehicles clearly at night, which depends on placement and lighting more than resolution. We set one camera wide enough to see the whole driveway and every door to the house, and a second aimed at the point where a car enters or leaves, where it is close enough to show the vehicle's shape and color. We also check the night view with your porch and street lights on, because glare from a bright fixture can wash out the very spot you need.

Is it worth registering our cameras with the Suffolk police or the Garden City police?

It can be, and it does not hand anyone access to your system. The Suffolk County Police Department's camera registry lets owners register where their cameras are, so detectives can ask participants to check video for a particular date and time, with retrieval at the owner's convenience. The Garden City Police Department runs a voluntary private camera registration program and states plainly that it does not give police access to privately owned systems; detectives may ask to inspect video for a set period. We found no county-wide registry for Nassau County police. If you register, we can show you how to find and export a clip from the Reolink app so you are ready if a detective calls.

Existing systems and kits

We put up a Reolink kit ourselves and some cameras keep dropping offline. Can you fix it?

Yes, and it is one of the most common calls. Cameras that drop in and out on a DIY PoE system usually come down to a few causes: a connector crimped badly or left out in the rain, a cable run too long or kinked, a cable pinched under a gutter or a window, a WiFi camera too far from the router, or an outdoor switch that gets too hot or wet. We test each cable, look at every outdoor connection, check the NVR's network settings and firmware, and fix what is wrong rather than replacing what works. Often we reroute a few cables that were left running along the siding and move the recorder to a better spot while we are there. You keep everything you bought that is sound.

Can we add another brand's camera to the NVR from our Reolink kit?

Sometimes, but plan on staying with Reolink. A commenter on the Slickdeals thread about the Costco Reolink kit warned that the cameras "will only work on their nvr," and the reverse often applies as well: Reolink's recorders work best, and sometimes only fully, with Reolink cameras. Some features, such as smart detection of people and vehicles, may not carry across brands even when a basic picture does. If you already own another camera you want to use, tell us the model before we visit and we will check what the recorder supports. In most Long Island houses the simplest answer is to add Reolink cameras to the free channels, and keep the other camera on its own app if you want it.

FAQ

Long Island Reolink Camera Installation FAQ

If our internet goes down, do the wired Reolink cameras stop recording?
+

No. Wired PoE cameras record to the NVR's hard drive inside your house, and that recording does not depend on the internet. What stops when the internet drops is remote viewing and push alerts on your phone. Once the connection returns, the app can play back everything recorded in the meantime. WiFi cameras are different, because they need your home WiFi to reach the recorder even when the internet is fine.

Can a Reolink doorbell record all day and night without paying for a subscription?
+

A Reolink PoE doorbell wired to a Reolink NVR can record continuously to the recorder's drive, with no monthly plan needed for that local recording. The WiFi doorbell's recording options depend on how it is set up and what it records to, so we confirm the exact model's recording modes with you before you buy. Cloud storage is optional rather than required for a recorder-based system.

Should we choose the PoE doorbell or the WiFi version for our front door?
+

If a cable can reach the front door from the attic, basement or garage, the PoE version is usually the better choice, because power and video arrive on one cable and it does not depend on WiFi. If running a cable would mean opening finished walls, the WiFi version can be powered by the existing doorbell transformer instead. Owners on doorbell forums say much the same thing, and a renter there noted needing a generic bracket to fit it to the door.

I rent a house in Uniondale. Can I still put up cameras?
+

Usually, with the owner's agreement for anything that drills. Reolink's own guidance notes that most landlords do not allow drilling for cameras or wiring, and suggests battery and WiFi models for renters. We can set up battery cameras on brackets that come off cleanly, or a wired system if the owner approves it in writing. Ask the owner first, and send us their approval with the booking.

Can we view our cameras remotely through a VPN instead of the Reolink cloud connection?
+

Yes, if your router supports a VPN. With a VPN set up on the home network, your phone connects securely to the house and then to the NVR, so the recorder is not reachable from the open internet. It takes more setup than the standard app connection and works best with a router that handles it well. Tell us your router model when booking and we will say whether it is a good fit.

Is it legal for our outdoor cameras to record sound?
+

Sound is where camera law gets stricter. New York's eavesdropping rules under Penal Law section 250.00 are based on one-party consent, and a camera recording conversations that nobody in your household is part of, such as people talking on the sidewalk, can fall outside that. We leave audio recording off on outdoor cameras by default. If you want it on, take legal advice first; this is general information, not legal advice.

We rent out the downstairs of our house. What should we tell the tenants about the cameras?
+

Tell them in writing where the cameras are and what they cover, before they go up. New York Penal Law section 250.45 makes it a crime to put a camera where someone has a reasonable expectation of privacy without consent, so no camera should look into a tenant's unit or through their windows. Cameras on the driveway, the front walk and shared entrances are the usual choice. Check your lease and take legal advice for anything unusual.

How long does a Reolink installation take at a typical Long Island house?
+

It depends on the number of cameras and the cable routes, so we give you a time estimate in the written quote after seeing photos of the lot. A system fed from an open attic and basement goes much faster than one needing a run to a detached garage or work on a second-story gable. Larger systems may be booked as a longer visit or two technicians. Either way, you know the plan before we arrive.

Do you install Reolink systems on the North Fork and the South Fork?
+

Yes. East End addresses are part of Suffolk County and are priced from the Suffolk list. Because of the distance, East End jobs are scheduled into planned windows rather than charged a separate surcharge. For seasonal houses that sit empty in winter, we focus on remote alerts, battery backup and cameras that can be checked from anywhere.

Can a solar or cellular camera watch our gate or boat slip where there is no power or WiFi?
+

Yes, that is what those models are for. A Reolink battery camera with a solar panel needs no cable, and a cellular model such as the Go Plus sends video over a mobile data plan instead of WiFi. They record on motion rather than continuously, to save the battery, and the solar panel needs a spot with daylight. We check the sun and the mobile signal at the location before recommending one.

Will we need a building permit for cameras in the Town of Hempstead?
+

Usually not for a typical low-voltage camera system, but it depends on the work and the municipality. The Town of Hempstead code requires a building permit for work that must conform to the Uniform Code or the Energy Code, and incorporated villages inside the town have their own building departments. We check the rules for your exact address and tell you before the job if anything is needed.

Can the cameras at my store send an alert to my phone when someone moves inside after closing?
+

Yes. We set Reolink motion alerts on the indoor cameras and schedule them for the hours the business is closed, so your phone stays quiet during trading hours. Street-facing cameras are usually left recording without alerts, because passing traffic would set them off all night. We test the alerts with you before we leave, so you know what one looks like when it arrives.
