The Neighborhood

Why Edgewater Park Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Edgewater Park is a private, gated cooperative of small houses on the Throggs Neck peninsula, and almost everything about camera work here follows from that. It sits on the eastern edge of the Bronx, bounded by the Throgs Neck Expressway to the south and west, Weir Creek to the north and Eastchester Bay to the east, inside ZIP code 10465 and the 45th Precinct. The City Planning report on the community puts it at about fifty-five acres with 675 single-family homes and roughly 3,000 residents; Wikipedia gives sixty acres. The land once held a mansion that George T. Adee built in 1856. In 1923 it was leased to Richard W. Shaw Sr., who let church youth camp on the grounds, and the tents of Edgewater Camp slowly gave way to bungalows. After the First World War the camp was split into sections A, B and C, named after Army camps, with D and E added later, which is why addresses here still read like 33C or 67E. Residents bought the land from the primary leaseholder and formed the co-op in 1988.

The second thing to understand is the gate. The Architect's Newspaper described Edgewater Park and neighboring Silver Beach Gardens as private cooperatives with closed-off streets, a single point of entry, security guards and signs forbidding loitering, trespassing and soliciting. Recent sale listings mention a friendly security guard, surveillance cameras and security walks through the community. Residents have also paid for that security themselves and seen it attacked: News 12 Bronx reported, roughly nine years ago, that residents banded together to put up a new guard booth, that vandals destroyed it, and that an after-hours guard went on duty in its replacement. So a home camera here is not a substitute for anything the community already runs. It is the piece the community cannot provide: a record of what happens at your own door, your own alley and your own yard, held on a recorder that you control.

The third thing is how close everything is. City Planning describes structures sitting immediately next to one another on narrow streets, and a resident on a public forum put it more plainly: houses and lots are small, and usually all that separates you from a neighbor is an alley. A camera that would be harmless on a suburban lot can, on these blocks, look straight through the window of the house next door. New York's 2017 change to the Civil Rights Law lets homeowners and tenants sue someone who records them in their backyard without consent when the intent is to harass or threaten, and legal guides note that video-only recording of public-facing areas such as sidewalks and front porches does not need consent. Edgewater Park's lanes are private, not city sidewalks, and its yards are back to back. That is why every view we plan here is drawn to stop at the property line, tested with the neighbor's windows in mind, and masked in the recorder where the frame cannot be narrowed any further.

The fourth thing is water. City Limits reported that 278 of the community's 675 homes sit in a flood zone; Bronx Times, summarizing City Planning, gave a figure of about 111 homes. The sources disagree, and we will not pick one for you, but every source agrees that a large part of Edgewater Park lies in mapped flood zones, including A zones with a 13-foot base flood elevation and Coastal A and V zones along the bay. The city's flood resilience rules require certain buildings to raise their lowest floor and mechanical equipment above the design flood elevation, and the co-op voted to measure building height from that elevation, keeping its 25-foot limit, so homes could lift. For cameras, the lesson is simple. The recorder, the network switch, the internet equipment and the battery backup belong on an upper floor or a high shelf, and the cable that serves cameras near the ground is planned so a flood does not take the whole system with it.

The fifth thing is the wider Throggs Neck picture. The news that reaches this corner of the Bronx is mostly about cameras that recorded something on nearby streets. In October 2025, News 12 reported that a Ring doorbell camera caught a man stealing from mailboxes at a home near Clarence and Randall Avenues. Around November 2025, News 12 reported that a Throggs Neck family filed a report with the 45th Precinct and shared door-camera video of teens breaking down their door, and that neighbors said the same teens had targeted other homes. Those addresses are outside the gate, and we will not pretend they describe your lane. What they show is how most cases on this peninsula now start: with a clear, time-stamped clip from a private camera that a family can hand to the precinct. A camera that shows only a shape in the dark, or one that was knocked out of aim weeks earlier, does not start anything.

The last thing is ownership. City Planning explains that residents own their homes outright but lease the land, divided into shares, from the cooperative, and pay dues that maintain the communal areas and infrastructure. The co-op sets architectural and construction guidelines and, in the report's words, strictly enforces them. We found no camera-specific rule in any public source, so we do not guess at one. Before we fix a camera, a mounting pole or a run of conduit to the outside of a house, we ask the homeowner to check with the co-op office at 1 Edgewater Park, and we put the plan in writing so it can be shown to whoever needs to see it.

Edgewater Park facts that shape a camera plan

  • One road in: the community has a single point of entry with a guard booth, so the gate already sees arrivals by car; home cameras cover the lanes, alleys and yards the gate cannot.
  • Houses an alley apart: City Planning describes homes immediately next to one another on narrow streets, so every view is aimed down your own lot and masked where it reaches a neighbor.
  • Flood maps on many lots: between about 111 and 278 homes are counted in flood zones depending on the source, with a 13-foot base flood elevation in the A zone, so recorders go upstairs.
  • Homes that were lifted: the co-op measures height from the design flood elevation and keeps a 25-foot limit, so raised houses gain stairs, landings and open space underneath that need their own views.
  • Lanes too tight for big trucks: NYS Homes and Community Renewal notes that many FDNY vehicles are too big for the community's small streets, which also shapes how an install crew arrives and unloads.
  • Land under shares: residents own the house and lease the land through the co-op, which enforces its own construction guidelines, so outdoor mounts are checked before work starts.

What Edgewater Park households often add next. Once the front step and alley are covered, the next question is usually about the door itself: whether a video doorbell can ring the same phone that gets camera alerts, or whether a door contact can tell the system when the back door opens while the house is empty. We leave a spare labeled port on the recorder and a pull string in the cable path for that kind of addition, so the second job does not mean opening the same wall twice.

What the Community's Cameras Cover and What Only Your Own Camera Can

A camera plan for an Edgewater Park house starts by sorting the places around it into three groups. Some are already watched by the community: the road in, the booth, and whatever the co-op's own cameras cover. Some belong to the community but are watched by nobody in particular at the hour something happens: the lanes, the beaches and the shared buildings after dark. And some are yours alone: the step, the alley, the yard, the deck and the space under the house. The gate sees who drives in; nobody sees who walks between houses

A single point of entry is a strong arrangement for cars. It is weaker for anyone already inside, anyone on foot, and anyone who comes along the shoreline. Edgewater Park's lanes branch off into alleys and gaps that a guard at the booth cannot see, and many of the community's houses face each other closely enough that a person can walk from one lane to the next without passing anything that records. That is not a criticism of the community's security; it is simply the limit of what a gate can do. A home camera fills that gap where it matters to you. The one at the front step is framed so that anyone who comes to your door walks toward it and shows a face. The one in the alley looks down your own side of the gap, from the front corner of the house toward the yard, so it sees whoever turns in. The one over the yard covers the back door and anything stored outside. Three well-aimed cameras often answer more questions than eight scattered ones.

Your recordings, the co-op's recordings and the 45th Precinct

Because the community has its own cameras and guards, people sometimes assume footage will simply be there when needed. In practice there are three separate record-keepers. The co-op holds whatever its cameras record, under rules set by its board. The NYPD runs its own cameras on public streets nearby; Bronx Times reported Argus cameras along East Tremont Avenue in Throggs Neck, and PIX11 reported that they are watched in real time by the 45th Precinct and police headquarters. And you hold what your own cameras record. When something happens at your house, your own recorder is the one with the view of your step, and it is the only one you can export from yourself, the same evening, without asking anyone. We set the recorder's clock to sync automatically, so a clip you hand to a detective lines up with the time on any other footage they collect, and we show you how to export a short clip without taking the recorder out of the house.

Planning for a neighbor six feet away

On most lots here, the most important decision is not which camera to buy but which way it faces. A side camera mounted at the back corner of the house and aimed toward the lane sees the faces of people walking in from the lane; mounted at the front and aimed toward the yard, it sees backs. Either way, the frame should run along your own wall, not across the alley into the next house. Where a sliver of a neighbor's window still appears, the recorder can black out that part of the picture permanently, so it is never recorded at all. Infrared night light also bounces off a wall a few feet away and can wash out the picture, so we check every side view after dark and change the angle, the light or the camera if the night image turns white. If a neighbor asks what your camera sees, the honest answer should be easy to show on your phone.

One-story bungalows: finding the path before choosing the camera

A cottage that started life as a summer bungalow rarely has the neat attic and full basement that camera kits are designed around. Before we suggest a single position, we look for the route the cable can take: a crawl space with enough room to work, a closet that sits against an outside wall, the space above a dropped ceiling in a kitchen, or a chase where an old chimney or vent once ran. The recorder goes on a high shelf in a closet or a bedroom corner where it is dry, ventilated and out of sight from the windows, with a small battery unit underneath. From there, each camera gets its own cable, which also carries its power. Where a run has to cross a finished room with no hidden path, a slim paintable raceway along the baseboard or ceiling line is tidier than opening a wall. Every hole through the outside wall is drilled sloping slightly downward toward the outside and sealed, so rain driven off the bay cannot follow the cable in.

Raised houses: cameras above the line, cable inside the floor

A home lifted onto a new foundation changes in three ways that matter for cameras. The front door is now a full flight up, so a camera at the old height sees the top of the stair, not faces at the bottom. The space under the living floor becomes open storage for bikes, tools and outdoor furniture, and it is exactly the part of the house that floodwater will reach. And the cable that once ran through a basement now has nowhere to go but the floor of the raised house itself. We mount the step camera on the wall beside the door or under the landing roof, aimed down the stair, so a visitor climbing toward the door is framed face-first. The under-house camera hangs from the underside of the floor framing, above the design flood elevation, looking down into the storage space. All cable stays inside the living floor wherever possible; where a short stretch has to run below, it goes in sealed conduit and the camera on the end is the only thing that could ever get wet.

Bay-facing homes: wind, weather and the shared shoreline

Houses along Eastchester Bay get the full weather off the water. A camera on a flimsy bracket at the end of a deck will shake in every gust, blur its own recordings and set off motion alerts all night. We mount yard and deck cameras on solid structure near the house rather than on railings, use stainless or corrosion-resistant screws and brackets, and seal every cable entry. The other issue on the bay side is the shoreline itself. The community's beaches are shared, and people use them in swimwear, so a back-yard camera should end at your own yard and deck. Where the beach still shows at the top of the frame, it is masked in the recorder. Bright light off the water can also leave the yard looking dark in daytime clips, so we test bay-side views in the afternoon, when the glare is strongest, and choose cameras that handle mixed light well.

Community buildings: block walls and shared decisions

The community's shared buildings are a different kind of job from a cottage. NYS Homes and Community Renewal announced a $2.5 million resiliency construction project for the volunteer firehouse and community center, which also serves as a polling place, so that the building can act as a storm shelter. Buildings like this tend to have masonry or block walls, a mix of public rooms and private ones, and a board, not a single owner, making decisions. Cable through a block wall goes through one planned, sealed opening per path rather than a scatter of holes. Cameras cover doors, the apparatus or storage areas and the parking or approach, never restrooms or changing areas. The recorder sits in a locked room above flood level, and the people who can watch, export or delete footage are named in writing before we finish.

Cameras the community owns: who decides and who watches

A camera on a private cottage is the household's business. A camera on the firehouse, the community center, the booth or the co-op office belongs to the community, and that changes how it is planned. The board, or whoever it delegates, decides where each camera goes, who can watch live, who can export, and who can delete. Those are four different powers, and keeping them apart is the main way a shared system avoids arguments later. A volunteer on duty at the firehouse might see the apparatus bay and the doors live, without being able to export. The co-op office might be able to export clips for a specific request. Only one or two named people should hold the administrator account that can change settings or delete recordings, and the password for it should be held in a way that survives a change of officers. We write all of this into the proposal the board votes on, list every camera position with a still image of its view, and leave a record at the office of every device, label and login once the work is done.

A small shop with one counter and one door

The deli, and the small shops on the roads outside the gate, share one problem: the person at the counter cannot also watch the door, the cooler, the stock room and the back entrance. A camera at head height just inside the front door, set back so customers walk toward it, gives one clear face per person; that single camera does most of the identifying work. A second camera above and slightly in front of the register shows the customer, their hands and the drawer. A third covers the back door or delivery entrance, and a fourth the stock area. Alerts are scheduled for the hours the shop is closed, and stay quiet while it is open. The recorder goes somewhere other than behind the counter, in a locked cabinet or a back room above floor level, on a battery backup. For a shop in a flood-prone part of the peninsula, the same rule as a house applies: keep the recorder and the internet equipment high.

Rules and limits for camera work on community and business property

  • The co-op decides about co-op property. Buildings, lanes, beaches and the booth belong to the community; cameras there are approved by the board or office in writing before we install anything.
  • Private places stay off camera. Recording where people expect privacy, such as restrooms, changing areas and showers, is a crime under New York Penal Law 250.45, and we will not aim a camera into one, on a cottage or a community building.
  • Neighbors' yards are not the camera's business. New York's 2017 backyard recording law lets people sue over harassing recording of their yards, so every view is aimed and masked to stay on the property being protected.
  • Video only, as a rule. We set community and business systems to record video, not sound, unless a lawyer has advised otherwise, because recording conversations raises separate legal questions in New York.
  • Gear above the flood line. City flood rules require certain buildings to keep mechanical equipment above the design flood elevation; we treat recorders and switches the same way on every job here.
  • Openings stay sealed. Every hole through an outside wall or a rated wall in a community building is sealed with the right product before we leave.
  • The installer must be licensed. The NYS Department of State licenses businesses that install, service or maintain security systems; AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, which the co-op office or a board may ask to see before work starts.

We do not publish stories about a particular house or family, so this is our routine rather than an anecdote. It is how we look at a cottage inside the gate before we suggest a single camera.

We start on the lane, not at the door. Standing where a visitor would stand, we look at the house the way a stranger would: which door is obvious, whether the side alley is visible from the lane, how far the next house is, and where light from the neighbors falls at night. Then we walk the alley slowly, measuring roughly how many feet separate the two walls, and note every window on the neighbor's side that a camera could catch. In the yard we look at what is stored outside, where the back door is, and how much of the shared shoreline or the next yard shows from the deck. If the house has been raised, we go under it and check how high the living floor sits above the ground and where the water came to last time, if the homeowner knows. Only then do we go inside to find the route: a crawl space, a closet on an outside wall, an attic hatch, a path down from a second floor. The recorder's spot is chosen last, as high and dry as the house allows. If the homeowner has photos of the house after a storm, or the co-op's guidelines in hand, those come out at the kitchen table before we write anything.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Edgewater Park: Questions Answered

The gate, the co-op and the shared land

The guard booth at the entrance was wrecked by vandals once before. Can cameras protect the booth itself?

Yes, and the camera that protects the booth should not be on the booth alone. News 12 Bronx reported that the residents' new guard booth was destroyed by vandals and that an after-hours guard went on duty in the replacement. A camera on the booth can be knocked down with it, so the better plan is one camera on the booth covering the window and road, plus a second on a separate structure the co-op controls, aimed back at the booth. The recorder sits somewhere other than the booth. This is co-op property, so the board approves the plan and decides who watches.

Should the camera on the single road into the community try to read plates, or just show each car clearly?

For most gated communities, a clear record of each car's shape, color and time is the realistic goal, with a separate, tightly framed camera for plates only where the road narrows and cars slow down. Reading plates reliably at night needs a dedicated camera with the right lens, shutter settings and lighting, aimed at one spot in the lane. Because Edgewater Park has one way in, that spot exists. Whether to record plates at all is the board's decision, and it should decide in advance who may search those recordings and why.

After a board election, how do the shared camera logins pass to the new officers without losing footage?

Plan for it before the first election comes around. The administrator account should belong to the co-op, not to a person, with its credentials kept in a sealed record at the office and changed after every handover. Officers get their own named accounts with the permissions their role needs, removed when they leave. Changing accounts does not touch the recordings, so nothing is lost. Edgewater Park's elections have been contested at times, which is one more reason to write the rule down. We can come back for a short handover visit when officers change.

We own our house but lease the land through the co-op. Can a camera go on our fence post or at the edge of the lane?

Possibly, but check first. City Planning explains that residents own their homes outright and lease the land as shares from the cooperative, which sets and strictly enforces construction guidelines. A camera on the house wall is the simplest case. A pole, a camera on a fence at the lane, or anything on the lane itself touches land the co-op maintains, so ask the office before you buy anything. We give you a written plan, with photos of each position, to bring to that conversation.

Can the co-op put a camera over the playground or the basketball court without upsetting parents?

It can, if the plan is open about it. The camera should cover the play area and its entrances from a height, recording what happens rather than zooming in on children, with no audio. A visible sign at the entrance tells families the area is recorded. Viewing should be limited to a small number of named people, with exports only for a specific reason, such as damage or an injury. Sharing the plan with residents before the camera goes up usually heads off most objections.

Raised houses and flood ground

Our house was lifted and the front door is now a full flight up. Will a door camera still see faces at the foot of the stairs?

Not if it sits where an ordinary doorbell would. From the top of a tall stair, a camera looking out sees the tops of heads and the lane beyond. We place it on the wall beside the door or under the landing roof and angle it down the stair, so anyone climbing toward you is framed face-first for most of the climb. A second, lower view of the bottom of the stair, mounted above flood level, catches people who come to the stair but never climb. The doorbell stays for talking to visitors; the camera does the identifying.

The open space under our raised house holds bikes and the mower, and water can get in there. Can a camera watch it?

Yes, as long as the camera is above the water, not in it. We mount it to the underside of the floor framing, above the design flood elevation, looking down across the storage space. Its cable goes up into the living floor straight away rather than along the ground. If the space is latticed, the camera sits behind the lattice line so it is not easy to reach from the lane. Motion alerts there can be set for the hours nobody should be under the house, such as overnight.

Lanes, lettered addresses and the bay

Our lane is too narrow for a work van. How does the install day run?

State housing officials have noted that many FDNY vehicles are too big for the community's small streets, so we plan for the same problem. We tell the guard booth when to expect us, ask where a vehicle can stop without blocking the lane, and carry ladders, cable and equipment the last stretch by hand if needed. Most cottage jobs need a ladder, hand tools and a few boxes, not a large truck. We ask you about any lane rules for contractors when we book.

Drivers keep leaving our boxes at the wrong lettered cottage. Can our door camera show whether a delivery ever reached us?

It can show whether anyone came to your step, when, and what they left or took. Addresses here run by number and section letter, such as 64A or 88D, and drivers unfamiliar with the community can misread them. A step camera framed to show the doorway and the ground in front of it records each arrival and each box. When a delivery app says a package arrived and your camera shows nobody at that time, you have the time-stamped proof the shipper will ask for. A clear house number near the door, readable on the camera, helps too.

Lanes here are too tight for two cars to pass, and parked cars get clipped. Which view shows the vehicle that did it?

A camera aimed along the lane, rather than straight across it, shows a vehicle approaching and passing your parked car over several seconds, which is when its color, shape and markings can be seen. Straight-across views catch only a smear. Because the lane is shared, we keep the frame to the stretch in front of your house, mask the houses opposite, and switch off motion alerts for the lane so ordinary traffic does not ring your phone. The recording is still there when you need to look.

Our house faces the community beach. Can our camera look out over the sand?

We advise against it. The beach is shared community land used by neighbors and their children in swimwear, and a camera that records it from a private house invites the kind of complaint New York's backyard recording law was written for. Aim the camera at your own deck, yard and back door, and mask the beach where it shows at the top of the frame. If the community wants the beach covered, that is a decision for the co-op, with its own signs and rules.

We keep a small boat at the community mooring. Can a camera on our house keep an eye on it?

Only if the mooring is close and directly in front of your own yard, and even then the picture at night will be limited. A boat on the water is a long, dark target, and infrared light rarely reaches it. If you can see your boat clearly from your deck in daylight, a camera on solid structure at the back of the house can record it by day and show movement at night. Anything mounted on the docks or the shore is co-op property and needs the community's permission.

The community's shared buildings

The firehouse and community center are meant to serve as a storm shelter. Should their cameras keep running when the power goes out?

Yes, because a shelter is when people need to know who is coming and going. The state project for the building includes resiliency upgrades, and the community's own records list generators among them. Whether the camera system rides on a generator-backed circuit is a question for the building's electrician. Our part is a battery backup that covers the recorder, switch and internet equipment through the seconds or minutes before a generator takes over, with the recorder kept well above flood level.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Edgewater Park

These are claims that appear again and again in quick online answers, cost guides and installer pages about cameras in the Bronx and in gated or waterfront communities, checked against what the sources say about this neighborhood. Cost pages on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; they help with a rough sense of scale, but none of them has stood in an Edgewater Park alley.

"Edgewater Park is just part of Throggs Neck, so a Throggs Neck camera plan fits."

Quick answers often fold Edgewater Park into Throggs Neck, and on a map that is understandable. It shares the peninsula, the ZIP code and the 45th Precinct with the surrounding neighborhood.

But it is a private cooperative with its own gate, guards, lanes and rules. Its streets are mostly private and narrow, its houses sit an alley apart, and residents lease the land under their homes. A plan written for a Throggs Neck house on a city street, with a driveway and a public sidewalk, does not account for any of that.

An Edgewater Park plan begins with what the gate already covers, keeps every view on the household's own lot, and checks the co-op's guidelines before anything goes on the outside of the house.

"A gated community with guards doesn't need home cameras."

This claim turns up in neighborhood guides and resident forums alike: the gate is there, the guards walk the lanes, so why bother?

The community's own history answers part of it. Residents paid for a guard booth that vandals then destroyed, according to News 12 Bronx. A gate controls cars on one road; it does not see who walks between houses, who comes along the shoreline, or who is already inside. And the community's cameras are recorded and controlled by the co-op, not by you.

A home camera is not a rival to the community's security. It covers the step, alley and yard that no guard can watch, on a recorder you can export from the same evening.

"Battery cameras are the easy answer for a small cottage."

Product pages make battery cameras look ideal for a small house: no cable, no drilling, stick it up and go.

On a cottage in a busy summer community, battery cameras run down quickly when every passerby wakes them, and many save clips only to the maker's cloud, which usually means a subscription. In winter, cold shortens battery life further. Cameras on a cottage's alley side are also the ones least likely to get checked, so a dead battery can go unnoticed for weeks.

For anything you rely on, a wired camera that takes its power and picture over one cable is steadier. Battery models still suit a temporary spot, such as watching a shed while a house is being lifted.

"Put the recorder in the basement, where it's out of the way."

Installation guides written for dry inland houses routinely recommend the basement or utility room for the recorder, because that is where the internet line and the power panel already are.

In a community where many homes sit in mapped flood zones, with an A zone base flood elevation of 13 feet, the basement or crawl space is the worst place for it. City flood rules require certain buildings to keep mechanical equipment above the design flood elevation, and a recorder sitting low takes every recording with it if water comes in.

We put the recorder, switch and battery backup on an upper floor or high shelf, and run the cable for low cameras so the camera, not the recorder, is the only part that could ever get wet.

"You own your house, so you can mount whatever you like on it."

Home security articles often tell homeowners they need nobody's permission to put cameras on their own house, and in much of the city that is broadly true.

Edgewater Park works differently. City Planning explains that residents own their homes but lease the land as shares from the co-op, which sets architectural and construction guidelines and strictly enforces them. We found no public rule about cameras specifically, which is exactly why we do not promise anything before the office has been asked.

Separately, owning the house does not give a camera the right to look into the house next door. New York's backyard recording law and simple neighborliness both point the same way: aim along your own walls.

"National cost guides tell you what an Edgewater Park install should cost."

AI answers frequently quote a per-camera range drawn from Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr, then present it as the going rate for any address.

Those averages do not know that the van may have to stop at the end of the lane, that a raised house needs cameras positioned for a tall stair and an open space below, that cable in a converted bungalow has to find its own path, or that a written plan may be needed for the co-op office. Each of those changes the work, sometimes up and sometimes down.

So we do not publish a per-camera figure for the neighborhood. Every Edgewater Park job is priced on our Bronx rate, quoted line by line after photos or a visit, and booked within 2 hours.

"The police cameras on East Tremont Avenue cover the neighborhood."

News about NYPD Argus cameras in Throggs Neck sometimes gets summarized as though the whole peninsula is now under police video.

Bronx Times reported that the Argus cameras were installed along East Tremont Avenue, and PIX11 reported they are monitored by the 45th Precinct and police headquarters. FAQ

Edgewater Park Security Camera Installation FAQ

Will the guard at the entrance let your crew in, and what should we arrange before the install day?
+

Tell the booth the day and rough time we are coming, and our company name, the same way you would for any contractor. Ask the co-op office whether it has rules about contractor hours, parking on the lanes or work on the outside of houses, and have any written guidance ready. We confirm the visit with you the day before, call when we reach the gate, and keep the vehicle out of the lane as far as possible. If something on the outside of the house needs the office's agreement, we bring the written plan.

Our door camera caught someone trying the handle at night. Should we post the clip on a neighborhood app?
+

Report it to the 45th Precinct first and keep the original recording on the recorder, so the clip police receive is complete and unedited. News 12 reported a nearby Throggs Neck family that did both, and neighbors then said the same people had targeted other homes. Posting can help neighbors, but it can also wrongly point at someone innocent and can tip off the person involved. If you post, share only the moment that matters, avoid naming anyone, and keep the full footage for the police.

Beach season and the Labor Day Olympics bring crowds past our house. Can the alerts be toned down for those weeks?
+

Yes. The recorder keeps recording either way, but alerts can follow a schedule. For busy summer weekends and the long-running Labor Day Olympics, we can switch the step and lane cameras to alert only overnight, or only when a person comes onto your own step rather than passing on the lane, while yard and under-house alerts stay on. Changing the schedule back takes a minute in the app, and we show you how before we leave.

Can one camera at the corner of a cottage watch both the lane in front and the alley at the side?
+

Sometimes, with a wide or dual-lens camera, but it usually does neither job well. A corner camera sees the lane and the mouth of the alley at an angle, so faces are small and turned away. On lots this tight, two narrower cameras, one framed for the step and one running along your own side wall, give far clearer pictures and are easier to keep off the neighbor's windows. A corner camera can still be useful as an extra overview where both sides meet.

Children cut through the alleys between houses all summer. Is it a problem that our side camera records other families' kids?
+

Recording people passing along your own side of the house is a normal part of a camera's job, and video of your own property is not illegal. Keep the frame on your side of the alley, record video only without sound, and do not share clips of other families' children unless something actually happened. Setting the alley camera's alerts for overnight hours means the daytime traffic of kids running through is recorded quietly without anyone watching it live. If a parent asks, show them the view.

We have an outdoor shower behind the cottage for beach days. Where should the yard camera stop?
+

Before the shower. Recording someone in a place where they expect privacy, such as a shower or changing area, is a crime under New York Penal Law 250.45, even on your own property. We aim the yard camera so the shower enclosure is fully outside the frame, then mask it in the recorder as a second safeguard, and check the view with the shower door both open and closed. The yard, the back door and the shed stay covered; the shower never appears.

During a storm, can a camera show us whether the water is rising toward the house while we are away?
+

Yes, as a rough guide. A camera aimed at a fixed reference, such as the bottom of the stair or a fence post, lets you see from your phone how far water has come. It works only while the camera, recorder and internet have power, so the battery backup matters, and the camera itself must sit above the flood line. It is not a warning system and does not replace official evacuation notices, but it can help you decide whether a trip back is worth it.

If flooding is our biggest worry, is it safer to keep footage in the cloud than on a recorder in the house?
+

A recorder kept on an upper floor already avoids the flood problem, and it records without a monthly fee or depending on the internet. Cloud storage protects against a different risk: the recorder being damaged or carried off. Many households use both, recording everything locally and sending key clips, such as front-step motion, to the cloud. If the internet goes down in a storm, the local recorder keeps working while cloud uploads pause, which is why we never rely on cloud alone.

Should the internet router go on the same battery backup as the camera recorder?
+

Yes, if you want to watch the cameras on your phone during an outage. The battery keeps the recorder recording either way, but without the router and modem running, the cameras cannot reach your phone. We size the battery for the recorder, the switch and the internet equipment together, and place them on the same high shelf where possible. The battery buys minutes rather than days, but that is often enough to cover the short cuts that follow a storm.

Is a video doorbell by itself enough for an Edgewater Park cottage, or do we need a recorder system?
+
