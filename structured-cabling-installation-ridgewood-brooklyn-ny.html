The Neighborhood

Why Ridgewood Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Ridgewood is a Queens neighborhood, ZIP code 11385, that runs right up to the Brooklyn line at Bushwick and East Williamsburg, and much of it was built in one burst. Most of its housing went up between about 1905 and 1915, in brick, after a 1905 rule required masonry construction, and by the First World War there were more than 5,000 buildings. Near the Brooklyn border the typical building is a six-family flat; deeper into the neighborhood the rows become two-family and one-family houses. That history is the first reason a Ridgewood network needs planning. These buildings have brick walls, plaster inside, and a cellar that dates from a time when many of them were cold-water flats, without hot water or central heat, so heating, hot water and heavier electric service were all added later, one system at a time. Nothing about them was laid out for a cable to every flat. A network gets through them by the routes the building already offers, or it ends up as wire stapled across the front.

The second reason is the shape of the six-family itself. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's report on the Ridgewood North Historic District describes the Mathews Model Flats as three-story buildings over a basement on lots about 27.5 feet wide, with two apartments on each floor, and notes that more than 300 of them were sold between 1909 and 1912. The commission called them "easier to manage by owner-occupants," and that is still how many work: the owner lives in one flat and rents the other five. For cabling, that means one cellar, one stair, six households and an owner who is both the landlord and the person whose own internet goes down when something in the cellar fails. It also means the flats stack directly above one another, so a route that works for the first floor usually works for the second and third.

The third reason is that Ridgewood also works for a living, mostly north of Myrtle Avenue and toward the Bushwick line. Old factory buildings there are being turned into offices and studios. The Box Factory at 1519 Decatur Street, a three-story, 63,000-square-foot building from 1929 that once housed a box and packaging maker, was redeveloped on a budget of about $10 million into some 56,000 square feet of loft-style offices with new elevators and mechanical systems. "It was just a mess," architect Chris Fogarty told Commercial Observer about the building before the work. Its first-floor suites were built with 14-foot ceilings, and on the second floor the drywall came off to show the original wood joists. That is a very different cabling job from a six-family cellar, and a very common one in this part of the neighborhood. The lots on the industrial blocks are changing too: in January 2026 QNS reported a rezoning application for two apartment buildings, of six and seven stories with 55 units, on manufacturing-zoned lots at 10-86 and 11-01 Cypress Avenue, where a furniture warehouse closed in 2025.

The fourth reason is that fast service at the curb does not mean a building is ready for it. One broadband directory says "about 100.00%, or 77,740 people, have Verizon fiber internet available to them in the neighborhood," and that fiber is the fastest connection type for 98.73% of Ridgewood homes. In practice, a provider still has to get into each building, find a dry place for its terminal and power supply, and get a line from the cellar to the flat that ordered it. A Verizon community thread about a fiber install under a strict lease shows how that goes: the basement wall needs the landlord's approval, the power outlet has to be the subscriber's own, and Ethernet "would then need to be routed through the walls to the unit." Multiply that by six flats, five different providers over twenty years and a few tenants who ordered service without telling the owner, and you get the typical Ridgewood cellar wall. Sorting that wall out, once, is usually where our work begins.

Put those together and a Ridgewood cabling job is usually one of two things. It is either a residential building job, where an owner wants every flat wired properly from one place in the cellar and the old clutter gone, or a commercial job in a converted loft or an avenue storefront, where a business needs desks, registers, cameras and access points on cable that will not be pulled loose. Both depend on the same thing: knowing where the line enters, where the cabinet belongs and how cable climbs through a brick building without being stapled across its face. Both also tend to grow. An owner who starts with internet in six flats soon wants a camera at the cellar door and a new door station, and a loft tenant who starts with eight desks takes the suite next door a year later. A good plan leaves room for both.

Ridgewood facts that change a cabling plan

  • Fiber reaches almost every block. BestNeighborhood lists Verizon fiber and EarthLink fiber as available to about 100% of the neighborhood, Spectrum fiber to about 8.83% and Optimum fiber to about 5.50%. Which providers can serve a particular building still depends on the building.
  • The six-family is the standard building. The LPC counts more than 300 Mathews Model Flats sold between 1909 and 1912, each with two flats on each of three floors over a basement, and the type was copied across the neighborhood near the Brooklyn line.
  • Much of Ridgewood is landmarked. The city has designated four historic districts here, Stockholm Street, Ridgewood North, Ridgewood South and Central Ridgewood, and the Central Ridgewood district alone covers 990 buildings. Conduit added to a designated facade has to be concealed.
  • Old factories are becoming offices. The Box Factory at 1519 Decatur Street turned a 1929 packaging plant into about 56,000 square feet of offices, and coworking and loft offices are listed on Centre Street and Metropolitan Avenue.
  • The tallest building in the area is still waiting. New York YIMBY reported in October 2024 that construction was on hold at 3-50 St. Nicholas Avenue, a 17-story building with 133 rentals and 97,000 square feet of retail beside the Myrtle–Wyckoff station, which topped out in December 2022.
  • Power on the shopping street has failed before. On July 5, 2016, a failed underground feeder cable cut power to 50 buildings on Myrtle Avenue from 12:23 p.m. until 8:35 p.m., QNS reported. Battery backup for a store's network is a real decision here, not an upsell.
  • No electrical permit for data cable. Under Local Law 128 of 2024, wiring that operates below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data no longer needs a DOB electrical permit, from December 21, 2025.

Ridgewood owners who call us about cabling usually end up asking about the front door and the cellar door as well, because the same cabinet and the same route up the stair can carry a camera cable and a door-station cable. We plan the cellar so those can be added later without a second set of holes.

Same-Day Repair in Ridgewood

Three Flats Lost Internet After the Plumber Left, a Store's Register Is Offline, or Nobody Can Tell Which Wire Is Whose: Same-Day Estimates in Ridgewood

Most network failures in Ridgewood buildings are caused by other work in the cellar. A plumber replacing a section of heating pipe pushes a cable out of the way and pulls its connector loose. A boiler contractor needs room and unscrews a box from the wall. A provider's technician installing service for a new tenant finds no free port and unplugs whatever looks unused, which turns out to be the second-floor flat on the left. A store owner reorganizing the cellar stockroom sets a shelf against the bundle that feeds the register. None of that is unusual, and almost none of it means the building needs to be rewired. It means somebody has to find the one connection that changed, put it back, and make sure the next contractor can tell what it is.

When one flat or one device has gone quiet, we start at the cabinet and work outward: power, the provider's equipment, the switch, then the patch panel port for that flat, then the cable itself. A damaged length is replaced or re-terminated, and the repaired run is tested so you know it is as good as the rest. When the cellar has no labels at all, we tone out every cable from the wall to the flat or device it serves, label both ends and leave a port map taped inside the cabinet door. Most of the time the fault turns up in the first hour, and the price for the fix is given on the spot.

What helps most is knowing what happened just before the trouble began. Tell us who was in the cellar last, whether a tenant moved in or out, or whether the power went off on the block. A photo of the cellar wall as it looks now saves more time than any test equipment. If a business is down and cannot wait, say so when you call, and we will tell you honestly how soon we can be there.

Building Specialists

Structured Cabling for Ridgewood's Architecture

Ridgewood looks uniform from the sidewalk, and in one sense it is: whole blocks were built by the same few builders in the same few years. Inside, the buildings fall into six kinds, and each decides where cable can go before anyone chooses a cable. The cards give the short version; the sections after them explain the method for each.

The six-family flat

Three floors over a cellar on a lot about 27.5 feet wide, two flats per floor off a central stair, often with the owner living in one of the six.

The two-family brick rowhouse

Two households over a cellar in long, matching rows from builders such as Paul Stier and Louis Berger and Co., with masonry party walls on both sides.

The corner building with a store

Flats above a shop on Fresh Pond Road, Forest Avenue, Fairview Avenue or a side-street corner, with a cellar the store and the building share.

The converted factory or loft

Brick, timber and steel buildings north of Myrtle Avenue and toward the Bushwick line, turned into offices, studios and workshops with high ceilings.

The newer mid-rise rental

Concrete-floored buildings on former industrial and mill sites, such as the seven-story building at 1626 Madison Street, with a telecom room the management controls.

The frame house and the later single-family

Older wood-frame houses that predate the 1905 masonry rule and later one-family homes with garages, most often on the blocks farther from the Brooklyn line.

The six-family flat: one cellar, one stair and six home runs

The six-family is the job we do most in Ridgewood, and it rewards a simple plan. Every flat gets its own cable, or a pair of cables, running all the way back to one cabinet in the cellar. No flat is fed through another flat, no cable is chained from the first floor to the second, and nothing depends on a tenant's router staying where it was. That is what "home run" means, and in a building where households come and go it is the difference between a network that keeps working and one that breaks every time a lease ends. In the cabinet, each flat's cable lands on a numbered port, the provider's equipment for each subscriber connects to the right port with a short patch cord, and changing a flat from one provider to another becomes a cord swap instead of a new hole.

The cable has to climb about three floors, and in these buildings there are only a few ways up that do not involve the front of the building or the walls of a tenant's living room. We look first for something that lines up floor to floor: closets stacked above one another, a chase beside the stair, the space behind the stair stringer, or an old pathway left by earlier service. Where none exists, a single small sleeve through each floor in a closet corner, sealed afterward, is cleaner than any surface route. Inside each flat, the outlet goes where the tenant needs it, usually by the window wall of the front room or in a bedroom used as an office, and the cable reaches it inside a closet or along the trim, so nothing crosses plaster that does not have to be opened.

The cellar decides the rest. The cabinet goes on the wall nearest the stair, high and dry, away from the boiler, the water meter and any part of the floor that has seen water. If the owner lives in the building, we talk about whether the building's own equipment, such as cameras and a door station, should sit in the same cabinet on a separate network, and who holds the key. A six-family cabinet is small: a wall-mount enclosure with a patch panel, a switch, room for the providers' terminals and a battery backup fits on one section of wall.

The two-family brick rowhouse: two households and two masonry party walls

Ridgewood's two-family rows, many by Paul Stier, whose firm built more than 2,000 houses according to the LPC's Central Ridgewood report, are narrower jobs than the six-family but not simpler. Both side walls are masonry shared with the neighbors, so nothing goes through them, and the route upstairs has to stay inside the house. The usual answer is a closet stack or the wall beside the stair, with the cable starting from a small cabinet in the cellar or, if the cellar is damp, a ground-floor closet with the provider's line brought up to it.

These houses usually have an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, sometimes with different providers. We keep the two households on separate cables and separate ports from the start, so the owner never has to troubleshoot the tenant's connection and neither household can see the other's devices. When a family occupies both floors, the same plan gives them one network with access points on each floor, wired back to the cellar, which works far better through brick and plaster than a wireless signal relayed from floor to floor.

The corner building with a store: the shop's network and the building's network

On Fresh Pond Road, Forest Avenue, Fairview and Grandview Avenues and many side-street corners, the ground floor is a store and the upper floors are flats. The store usually uses the cellar for stock, and the provider lines for the whole building usually come in there too. That works until the store needs registers at two counters, a printer in the back, a computer in the office, cameras and guest Wi-Fi, all of which should be on cable to a switch the staff cannot unplug by accident.

We keep the store and the flats physically apart: separate cabinets or at least separate panels, labels that say which is which, and no shared switch. The store's cabinet can be locked and its key held by the store; the building's cabinet belongs to the owner. Where the store's lease says it must remove its equipment at the end of the term, we plan its cabling on accessible routes so it can come out without damaging the building, and we tell both the owner and the tenant who owns what.

The converted factory or loft: timber, steel and a landlord's riser

North of Myrtle Avenue, from Cypress Avenue toward the Bushwick line and up toward Flushing and Metropolitan Avenues, Ridgewood still has factory and warehouse buildings. Some remain industrial; the city set up Industrial Business Zones in 2006 to keep manufacturers from being priced out, and Ridgewood's zone has been watched closely as housing moved in. Others are now offices, studios and workshops. In a converted building like the Box Factory, the landlord typically provides the base building: the provider's entrance, a telecom room, risers and sometimes cable to each suite. The tenant builds the network inside its own space.

Those suites often keep their structure on show. When the ceiling is exposed timber joists, cable cannot be hidden in it, so it has to be carried in a way that looks intended: a straight lane along one beam line on its own supports, dressed bundles with hook-and-loop straps, drops to desks at the same spacing as the lights, and nothing stapled to the wood. When the ceiling is 14 feet high, the access points go on the structure or on short drops rather than on the walls, and the work is done from a lift. We confirm the landlord's rules for which shafts and sleeves tenants may use, and we keep every opening through a rated floor or wall sealed.

The newer mid-rise rental: a management office and a telecom room

Ridgewood's newer buildings sit mostly on former industrial land. The seven-story building at 1626 Madison Street, opened in 2017 on the site of the Philru Knitting Mills with 89 apartments, a conference room and a lounge, is typical of the type. In buildings like these, the provider fiber, the managed building network and the entry system live in a telecom room that management controls, and some properties provide residents' internet through a managed network. Optimum's page for such buildings tells residents the service is "wired only," so a resident needs a wireless router of their own.

Our work in those buildings stays inside the apartment or the commercial unit, with management's approval. A resident who works from home usually wants a cable from the outlet the building provides to a desk or a television in another room. A ground-floor business usually needs its own service and its own small cabinet, separate from the residential network. We send the certificate of insurance and a short scope letter first, and we do not touch the building's own equipment.

The frame house and the later single-family: wood walls and a garage out back

Ridgewood has a scattering of wood-frame houses older than the 1905 masonry rule, and later single-family homes with garages, mostly on the blocks farther from the Brooklyn line. These are the easiest buildings in the neighborhood to cable, because their interior walls are hollow and the cable can be fished from the cellar to the upstairs rooms inside the walls. The questions are about where the provider's line lands, often at the side or back of the house, and whether a garage or a shed at the end of the lot needs a connection for a camera or a workshop. A cable to an outbuilding is run in protected conduit, and where it leaves one building and enters another, fiber or proper surge protection keeps a lightning strike or a ground fault from travelling between them.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Count the Boxes on the Cellar Wall Before Pricing a Ridgewood Six-Family

The first thing our technician does in a Ridgewood six-family is stand at the cellar wall where the lines come in and count. How many boxes are there, which providers put them there, which have power cords, and which have anything plugged into them at all? A wall with two live providers and four dead boxes tells us one story; a wall where every box has a cable leaving it in a different direction tells another. Then we follow each live cable as far as it goes. Some climb the stair; some leave the cellar through the front wall and climb the outside of the building to a window; some end at a tenant's router sitting on a shelf in the cellar because the installer had nowhere else to put it.

Next we go up the stair and look for the route. We open the closet nearest the stair on each floor, with the tenants' agreement arranged in advance, and check whether they line up. We look behind the radiator pipes where they pass through the floors, at the space under the stair, and at the top floor's ceiling below the roof. We ask the owner which flat names the building uses, because the tenants, the mailboxes and the rent roll do not always agree. And we ask who in the building is allowed to move a patch cord, because the answer decides whether the cabinet is locked and who gets the port map. Only after that do we price anything, because those three answers decide most of the cost.

The Cellar Wall

Five Providers, Six Flats and One Brick Wall: Rebuilding the Place Where a Ridgewood Building's Network Starts

If the inside of a Ridgewood building is where cabling gets done, the cellar wall is where it goes wrong. Each provider that has served the building drilled its own hole through the front or side wall, mounted its own box beside it and ran its own wire to whichever flat ordered service that year. When the tenant left, the box usually stayed. Over a few decades that leaves a wall with coax splitters, phone blocks, a fiber terminal or two, power supplies plugged into whatever outlet was closest, and cables leaving in every direction, some live, most not. Nobody planned it. It accumulated, one install at a time, and every new install makes the next one harder, because the technician who arrives has to guess which wire is free.

The cure is not complicated, but it has to be done once and done properly. We identify every live service and the flat it serves, tag it, and leave it working. We remove what is dead, with the owner's agreement and without touching anything a provider still uses. Then we give the building one place for providers to land: a backboard on the cellar wall near the existing entry, a single sleeve or conduit through the wall with spare room and a pull string in it, and a cabinet beside the backboard with a patch panel that has a port for every flat. From then on, a provider's technician mounts the terminal on the backboard, connects it to the patch panel port marked with the subscriber's flat, and leaves. Nothing new climbs the facade and nobody has to guess.

Power for the providers' equipment

Fiber terminals need power, and that raises a question unique to multi-family buildings: whose outlet? The Verizon community thread on installs under a strict lease points out that the power outlet for the provider's equipment has to be the subscriber's own. In a Ridgewood six-family, the cellar outlets are almost always on the owner's house meter, not on any tenant's. Owners handle that in different ways, from simply absorbing the small cost of powering a few terminals to having the provider place its equipment inside the flat. We do not install outlets ourselves; electrical work belongs to a licensed electrician. What we do is lay out the backboard so the owner's electrician knows exactly where the outlets are needed and how many, and we size the battery backup for whatever the owner decides to put on it.

When the line has to reach a flat directly

Sometimes a tenant's provider wants its equipment in the flat, not in the cellar, and the building has no path for it. That is where the home runs pay off. If every flat already has a cable from the cellar panel, the provider's line can be connected to that flat's port in the cellar and reach the flat over the building's cable, instead of a new wire being pulled up the outside. Where a provider insists on running its own cable to the flat, the owner can point its technician to the sleeves and closet route we left, with room for one more cable.

Landmark blocks and the outside of the building

A great deal of Ridgewood lies in city historic districts. The LPC designated the Stockholm Street district in 2000, Ridgewood North in 2009, Ridgewood South in 2010 and Central Ridgewood, with 990 buildings, in 2014. The commission's permit guidebook allows small health, safety and utility equipment on designated buildings but says that if an installation needs conduit, the conduit must be concealed. For cabling, that is another reason to keep providers off the facade entirely: one planned entrance, usually through the cellar wall below the stoop or at the side where it cannot be seen from the street, and everything else inside. If any work would change the outside of a designated building, we tell the owner before we quote and plan the entrance so it can be approved, and nothing is drilled until the paperwork is in order.

Rules and Approvals

Permits, Tenants' Flats, Ratings and Landlords: What Governs a Ridgewood Cabling Job

The city's rules on data cabling changed at the end of 2025, and many guides online still describe the old position. Local Law 128 of 2024 added section 28-105.4.7 to the Administrative Code, which says that an electrical permit is not required for the installation, alteration, maintenance or repair of wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data. It took effect with the 2025 New York City Electrical Code on December 21, 2025. For a typical Ridgewood job, home runs to six flats, desks in a loft suite, registers and access points in a shop, there is no DOB electrical permit to file.

That does not make the job unregulated. Cable that runs between floors in a chase or a sleeve needs at least a riser rating. Cable in a ceiling space that carries return air for heating or cooling, common in offices and restaurants with drop ceilings, has to be plenum rated. Every hole through a fire-rated wall or floor has to be sealed with a listed firestop, which matters in multiple dwellings where the floors and the stair enclosure separate households. Fire alarm wiring is its own licensed trade and is never part of a data job. And a new outlet or circuit for a cabinet is electrical work for a licensed electrician; we do not do electrical work and will tell you when the job needs one.

Working in occupied flats

Wiring a six-family means entering homes. The owner arranges access with each tenant, in writing and with notice, and we keep to the times agreed. Inside a flat the work is short: a closet corner, an outlet, sometimes a sleeve through the floor. We lay down drop cloths, keep dust to the closet and the outlet, and leave the flat as we found it. Any cable that has to cross a hallway or a stair runs inside the wall or in a closet, never across the floor or under a door. Apartment entrance doors in a multiple dwelling are meant to close and latch on their own, and a cable under a door or a tenant's cord stretched down the stair works against that and is a tripping hazard on an exit route.

Landlords, tenants and who pays

In a building the owner controls, the building's wiring belongs to the building. A tenant may want a jack in a back bedroom, and many owners are happy to have it, but the owner should approve the route and keep the record. Landlord forums show how quickly this becomes a dispute when it is not discussed: in one BiggerPockets thread, an owner found that a tenant's internet installer had drilled from outside and mounted equipment on the side of his house without asking, and the replies split between "not a big deal" and "no changes without permission." The simple way to avoid that is for the owner to wire every flat once, tell tenants that providers connect at the cellar panel, and let new service land there.

In loft buildings and stores, the tenant and the owner are usually different people, and the lease sets the rules: who may work in the building, the insurance they carry, where cable may run and whether it comes out at the end. We ask to see those clauses before we quote, because a lease that requires removal argues for surface pathways and accessible routes, while a lease that leaves improvements behind argues for doing it permanently and well.

Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling in Ridgewood: Questions Answered

These are the situations Ridgewood owners, tenants and businesses describe when they call: running a six-family as both landlord and neighbor, fitting out offices in old factory buildings, and keeping a store connected on a busy avenue. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Six-family owners

I live in one of the six flats in our building. If the cabinet that serves every flat ends up inside my apartment, what happens when I am away and a tenant's connection drops?

That is the strongest argument for keeping the cabinet out of your flat. Equipment that serves all six households belongs in a common space the owner controls, which in a Ridgewood six-family almost always means the cellar near the stair. You can lock it, a super or a trusted neighbor can be given a key, and a provider's technician can reach it without anyone opening your front door. If you are away and a tenant's service fails, the most common fixes, power-cycling the provider's terminal or moving a patch cord, can be talked through over the phone by whoever holds the key.

Owners sometimes want the cabinet upstairs because the cellar is damp or because their own office is up there. In that case we still put the provider entrance and the patch panel in the cellar, high and dry, and run one cable from it up to your flat for your own network. Your tenants' ports never leave the cellar, and nobody needs to be in your home for their internet to work.

Our six tenants pay for six separate internet plans. Could I buy one business connection, include internet in the rent and feed all six flats from the cellar instead?

It can be done, and some small-building owners do it, but it changes your role. You become the person tenants call when the internet is slow at 11 p.m., and the connection you buy has to carry six households' evenings of streaming and video calls at once. The cabling side is straightforward: the home runs to each flat are exactly what the plan needs, and a managed switch in the cellar can give each flat its own separate network, so no tenant can see another's devices. Each flat then gets its own Wi-Fi from an access point or a router inside the flat, wired back to the cellar.

What we cannot settle for you are the parts that are not cabling: the provider's terms for sharing a connection among residents, how internet is described in your leases, and whether a single line is reliable enough for tenants who work from home. Many owners find the better middle ground is to wire every flat to the cellar panel and let each tenant choose a provider. The building's cable then serves either arrangement, and switching later costs nothing more than a few patch cords.

A tenant on the third floor asked for a wired jack at her desk in the back bedroom. Should I pay for it, or should she?

There is no fixed rule, and owners split the cost in different ways. A useful way to decide is to ask who keeps the benefit. A jack wired back to the building's cellar panel stays with the flat after the tenant leaves and makes the flat more attractive to the next one, so many owners treat it as an improvement to the building and pay for it, or share the cost. A jack that only connects two rooms inside her flat, from her router to her desk, mostly serves her, and tenants often pay for that themselves.

Either way, the owner should approve the route and keep the record, because it is the owner's wall. Our minimum job on the Brooklyn list is $500, which covers a small job of two or three drops with termination, testing and documentation, so if two tenants want the same thing, doing them on the same visit is the better value for whoever pays.

A tenant ran a flat Ethernet cable under her door and down the hall stair to plug into her friend's router in the flat below. Is that allowed, and what is the proper fix?

It should come out. A cable under an apartment door keeps the door from closing and latching properly, and apartment entrance doors in multiple dwellings are meant to close on their own to keep smoke out of the stair. A cord along the stair is a tripping hazard on the building's only way out. It also means one household's internet depends on another's, which ends badly when either moves.

The proper fix is the same home run every flat should have. If her flat has a cable to the cellar panel, her friend's provider, or her own, can be connected to her port in the cellar in a minute. If her flat does not have one yet, it is a good moment to wire it, and the others with it, so the hall stays clear for good.

Factory and loft buildings north of Myrtle

Our office suite in a converted factory had its drywall stripped to show the original wood joists, and the landlord wants them left bare. How does cable cross a timber ceiling without spoiling it?

By being designed into the ceiling instead of attached to it afterward. We pick one lane, usually along a beam or parallel to the joists on the corridor side, and carry the cable on supports fixed at regular intervals, with the bundle dressed straight and held with hook-and-loop straps rather than staples or tight ties. Drops to desks come down at the same spacing as the light fixtures, in a short pole or along a column, so they read as part of the fit-out. Access points mount on a small bracket on the joist face or on a short drop at a consistent height.

Before we fix anything to old timber, we confirm with the landlord where fasteners are allowed, because some owners prefer clamps to screws in original structure. We also leave spare capacity in the lane, so when the tenant adds desks next year the new cables follow the same line instead of starting a second one across the ceiling.

We are opening a small coworking space on Centre Street with twenty desks and two private offices. How many cables per desk, and how do members' devices stay apart?

For open desks, one cable per desk is the usual minimum and two is safer, because members bring a laptop dock and sometimes a second device, and adding a cable later across a finished floor costs more than pulling it now. Private offices get two or three outlets each, plus one for a printer or display. Ceiling access points, wired back to the same cabinet, carry most members' traffic, since many will never plug in at all.

Keeping members apart is mostly a switch and firewall job rather than a cabling one, but the cabling has to make it possible. Every desk and office lands on its own labeled port, so you can put private-office tenants on their own network, keep the front desk and payment terminal separate, and give open-desk members a guest network that cannot see anyone else's computer. The cabinet goes in a locked room or closet, not under the reception counter, so a member can never move a patch cord.

Our company leases two suites on different floors of the same loft building and the landlord controls the riser. Can the two suites run as one network?

Yes, with the landlord's approval for the path between them. In most converted buildings the landlord's riser or telecom room already links the floors, and the building rules say whether tenants may run their own cable through it. If they may, we pull a backbone between your two suites, copper if the run is short enough, fiber if it is long or passes heavy equipment, terminated in your cabinet at each end. If the landlord does not allow tenant cable in the riser, the building may provide a connection between floors for a fee, or each suite can have its own provider service joined by a secure link over the internet.

Ask the landlord early which of those is possible, because it changes where each suite's cabinet should go. We put the answer in the quote, along with whose approval each section of the route depends on.

Stores on the avenues

Our store is on the Ridgewood side of Wyckoff Avenue and our stockroom is across the street in Bushwick. Can one cable connect the two buildings?

Not across a public street. Running cable over or under a city street is work for franchised providers with the city's permission, not for a private owner or a cabling contractor. The two buildings are also in two boroughs and are almost certainly on different electrical services, which is another reason not to join them with copper. What works is a separate internet service at the stockroom, wired inside that building to a small cabinet, with the two locations joined by a secure connection over the internet, so the stockroom's scanners and computers see the store's systems as if they were next door.

We cable both buildings to the same standard and label them so your IT provider or the vendor that runs your point-of-sale system can set up the link between them. If the two spaces are in the same building or adjoining buildings you own, a direct backbone through the shared wall or cellar becomes possible, and we will tell you when that is the case.

A six- and seven-story building is planned on the old furniture warehouse lot beside our Cypress Avenue shop. What should we do about our internet line before demolition and excavation start next door?

Find out exactly how your service reaches you, and write it down, before the site goes up. If your line comes from a pole or crosses the lot next door, the provider may need to move it, and it is far better to learn that from a planned call than from a dead connection on the day the demolition crew arrives. We locate the entry, photograph and label the line inside and out, and tell you which provider to call about any outside plant that crosses the site.

Inside, we make sure your cabinet is not on the shared wall where vibration, dust and any repair work to that wall will reach it, and that the provider's terminal and your router are on battery backup. City code makes the builder responsible for protecting adjoining property during construction, so keep the photos with your records. When the new building's ground floor fills with retail, the same block will have new provider equipment arriving too, and a prepared entrance on your side makes any later upgrade a short visit.

After the July 2016 outage, when 50 buildings on Myrtle Avenue lost power for about eight hours, we wondered what a battery backup is really for. What should ours keep running, and for how long?

No affordable battery runs a shop through eight hours without power, and it should not try to. Battery backup for a network is meant to ride through the short dips and brief outages that are far more common, and to give equipment time to shut down safely in a long one. On a battery sized for the purpose, the provider's terminal or modem, the router or firewall and the switch can stay up for a meaningful stretch, keeping card terminals that run on cellular backup or a phone hotspot working, and keeping cameras recording if they are powered from the switch.

What should not go on it are the things that draw heavy power for little benefit: monitors, printers, heaters, the coffee machine. We list exactly which devices the battery carries in the quote, and we label the outlets on it so nobody plugs a space heater into the backup during the next outage. Con Edison's spokesperson said after the 2016 outage that "a few customers in the area may experience short disruptions in their service" as repairs continued, and short disruptions are precisely what a network battery handles well.

Cat6, Cat6A or fiber in a Ridgewood building

A copper link is limited to 100 meters, including the patch cords at each end. In a six-family that limit never comes into play: the longest run, from the cellar to the back room of a third-floor flat, is a small fraction of it. For those runs Cat6 is the sensible cable, and Cat6A earns its extra cost and bulk only where a flat or office needs 10-gigabit speeds or an access point with a heavy power draw. In loft buildings and warehouses the distance can matter, and a suite at the far end of a long floor may need a fiber link or a second small cabinet partway along. Fiber is also the right way to reach a garage, a rear building or a stockroom with its own electrical service, because it carries no current between buildings.

One detail matters more than the category on the box: every part of a link has to match. A cable channel performs at the level of its weakest component, so Cat6A cable punched down on Cat6 jacks behaves like Cat6, and a cheap patch cord at the end can cost a run its certification. We use matched cable, jacks and panels from one category and certify the finished link, not the spool.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Ridgewood

Ask an AI assistant or a cost-guide site such as Angi, HomeAdvisor or Fixr about network cabling in Ridgewood and you will get confident, general answers. Some are right in general and wrong for these buildings. These are the seven we hear most, and what the work here actually shows.

"Fiber is available at every Ridgewood address, so you are all set"

The availability figures are real. BestNeighborhood lists Verizon fiber as available to about 100% of the neighborhood, roughly 77,740 people, and fiber as the fastest connection for 98.73% of homes. AI answers take that and conclude that any building is ready for it.

Availability describes the street. The building still has to let the provider in, find a place for its terminal and power, and get a line from the cellar to the flat that ordered service. The Verizon community thread on installs under a strict lease shows each of those steps needing the landlord's approval, and in a six-family they repeat for every tenant who signs up.

The correction is to prepare the building once: one entrance, a backboard for providers, and a cable from the cellar panel to every flat. Then "fiber is available" becomes "fiber is connected" in a single short visit.

"Ridgewood is in Brooklyn"

Some directory listings and AI summaries place Ridgewood in Brooklyn, partly because the name was first used on the Kings County side and partly because the neighborhood's mail went through Brooklyn until it received the 11385 ZIP code in 1980.

Ridgewood is in Queens, in Community District 5, and borders Bushwick and East Williamsburg in Brooklyn. For cabling the borough does not change the rules, since the city's code applies in both, but it matters for listings, for addresses on a provider order and for the stores and stockrooms that straddle Wyckoff Avenue.

"Low-voltage cabling in New York City needs a DOB permit"

Many cabling guides still say so, and some list skipping a low-voltage permit as a common mistake. For data cabling that is out of date. Local Law 128 of 2024 says an electrical permit is not required for wiring below 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, effective December 21, 2025.

The opposite error is to read that as "no rules." Riser ratings between floors, plenum ratings in air-handling ceilings and listed firestop at every rated floor still apply, and a six-family's floors and stair enclosure separate households for a reason.

So a Ridgewood data job usually has no DOB filing, but it still has to be built to code, and the owner, the loft landlord or the store's landlord will have approvals of their own.

"Mesh Wi-Fi solves coverage in any home"

Mesh systems are good products, and in a frame house with hollow walls a mesh kit often works well. AI answers tend to recommend one for every building.

A Ridgewood six-family is brick, with plaster walls and floors that separate six households, each with its own router and often its own mesh kit. Units relaying wirelessly through that construction slow down at every hop, and a dozen networks in one small building compete for the same airwaves.

Mesh works best here with a cable to each unit, so the signal is carried by wire to each floor and the wireless part only has to cross one room. That is a cabling job first and a Wi-Fi job second.

"The provider's technician will wire the building for you"

Provider installers are paid to connect one subscriber. They bring service to one outlet, often by the shortest path, which in an older building is frequently up the outside of the facade.

That is how Ridgewood cellar walls fill with boxes and how fronts end up with wire running to windows. The installer is not doing anything wrong; the building simply has no plan for them to follow. Each install is reasonable on its own, and together they become the clutter the next tenant's technician has to work around.

A building-wide plan reverses that. When every flat already has a cable to a labeled panel, the provider's installer connects to the right port in the cellar and is done, and nothing new climbs the brick.

"A wiremap test proves the cabling is good"

Cheaper quotes often promise that every cable will be "tested," and AI answers treat any test as proof of quality. As UniFi Nerds puts it, "A wire map confirms the cable connects end-to-end." It does not show whether the link performs at its rated speed.

Cost guides publish broad averages, and AI answers repeat them as if they described a cabling job in any building. HomeAdvisor's Ridgewood listing for network and wiring installers publishes no prices at all, only installer reviews.

FAQ

Ridgewood Structured Cabling FAQ

Do I need my tenants' agreement before your crew comes into their flats to run the building's cable?
+

You need to arrange access with each of them, in writing and with reasonable notice, and agree a time. The visit inside each flat is short, usually a closet corner and one outlet, so most owners schedule all the flats on the same day and tell tenants exactly which room we need. We keep to the agreed times, protect the floor and leave the flat as we found it, and if a tenant cannot be home, the owner or the super can let us in with the tenant's permission.

The inside walls of our flats are plaster laid straight onto brick with no hollow behind them. Where does a new jack go?
+

Usually on a wall that does have a cavity, such as a closet partition, a later stud wall or the wall beside a radiator riser, with the cable reaching it from the closet. Where the only practical spot is solid masonry, the outlet goes in a slim surface box low on the wall and the cable follows the baseboard or the door trim in a small painted raceway. We avoid cutting a channel into old plaster on brick, because it rarely patches invisibly and gains you nothing a neat raceway does not.

The outlets in our cellar are on the owner's electric meter. Can a tenant's fiber terminal plug into one of them?
+

Physically yes, but it is the owner's decision, because the owner pays for the power and some providers expect their equipment to run on the subscriber's own outlet. Some owners accept the small cost to keep every provider's equipment in one tidy place in the cellar. Others prefer the provider to put its equipment inside the tenant's flat and reach it over the building's cable from the cellar panel. We lay out the cellar to suit either choice, and any new outlet is installed by a licensed electrician.

The old buzzer wire already climbs our stair to every flat. Can the new data cable follow the same path?
+

Usually, yes. Buzzer and intercom wire is low voltage, like data cable, and the path it uses is often the best route through the building. We run the new cable beside it, never spliced to it or pulled tight against it, and label both so nobody confuses them later. What we keep data cable away from is power wiring, which needs separation, and the buzzer's transformer in the cellar. If the entry system is due for replacement, it is worth planning a cable for the new door station on the same visit.

An earlier installer used Cat6A cable in our office but Cat6 jacks at the desks. Does that matter?
+

It does if you paid for Cat6A performance. A link performs at the level of its lowest-rated part, so Cat6A cable on Cat6 jacks behaves like a Cat6 link. For most desks that is still perfectly good, but runs meant for 10-gigabit speeds or high-power access points will not get the full benefit. The fix is to re-terminate those runs with matching Cat6A jacks and panel ports and certify them again, which is far less work than replacing the cable.

Why do you print labels instead of writing on the cable or the faceplate with a marker?
+

Because handwriting fades, and cellars and closets are hard on it. One New York cabling firm notes that handwritten tape fades and peels within 18 months in a warm closet. Printed labels on the cable at both ends, on the patch panel port and on the faceplate stay readable for years, use the same wording every time, and match the port map exactly, so the next provider's technician or the next owner can find a flat's cable without guessing.

Can you work in the cellar while my tenants are at work and come into the flats only at set times?
+

Yes, and that is how most six-family jobs run. The cabinet, the backboard and the cellar lanes are built first, with no need to enter anyone's home. The work inside the flats is then grouped into the windows the owner has agreed with each tenant, and each flat is connected and tested before we move on. Nobody loses their internet for longer than it takes to move their provider's connection to the new panel.

The flats in our building are stacked one above another with the same layout. Does one cabling plan work for all of them?
+

Mostly, and it is one of the advantages of Ridgewood's six-families. Once we find a route that works on the first floor, such as a closet that lines up with the ones above it, the same route usually serves the second and third floors, and the outlets can go in the same place in each flat. Differences come from later renovations: a closet removed, a wall moved or a kitchen rebuilt. The walk-through checks each flat, but the plan is usually drawn once and repeated.

What should my leases say about the jacks and cable I install in the building?
+

That is a question for whoever drafts your leases, and we do not give legal advice. Owners commonly want leases to say that the building's wiring belongs to the building, that tenants and their providers connect at the cellar panel rather than drilling new holes, and that any new cable needs the owner's approval. We give you the port map and a one-page description of the system to attach or refer to, which makes those clauses easier to write and to enforce.

How can I tell what kind of cable is already in my walls?
+

Look at the printing on the jacket where the cable comes out of a wall or into the cellar. It usually states the category, such as Cat5e or Cat6, and a fire rating such as CM, CMR for riser or CMP for plenum. If there is no printing, or it says CCA, which means copper-clad aluminum, treat the cable with suspicion. We read the jackets during the walk-through and test any cable you hope to keep before deciding whether it stays.

Could the cable to the top-floor flats come down from the roof instead of up from the cellar?
+

Rarely, and only for a reason. Providers bring service into the cellar, so a roof route means climbing the building twice and exposing cable on the roof, which needs outdoor-rated cable, sealed penetrations and the owner's approval for roof work. It can make sense when the top floor has a ceiling space that connects to a closet below or the building has an unused roof-level pathway. In most Ridgewood six-families a closet stack or a sealed sleeve through each floor from the cellar is simpler and lasts longer.
