Westchester Co-ops

Westchester's Co-ops Are Mostly in Its Southern Cities, and That Decides the Camera Plan

People outside the county tend to picture Westchester as houses on large lots. The cooperative market says otherwise. The OneKey MLS report for Westchester counted 1,581 co-op sales in the twelve months through October 2026, against 1,250 condominium sales over the same stretch, with a twelve-month median co-op price of $240,000. Co-ops are not a niche here. They are the most common way to own an apartment in the county, and the boards that run them are buying the same things any building owner buys: cameras, entry systems, fobs and the cable that ties them together.

Where those co-ops sit is just as telling. On the day we checked in November 2026, Homes.com showed 466 co-ops for sale across the whole county, and 199 of them were in Yonkers. Mount Vernon had 85, New Rochelle 59, White Plains 48, and the Town of Greenburgh, which takes in Hartsdale, Elmsford and the river villages, had 28, with 19 listings under a Hartsdale address. Listing counts move every week and they are not a census, but the shape is clear: roughly two of every five co-ops on the Westchester market were in a single city, and most of the rest were in the cities and hamlets that border it. A camera plan for a typical Westchester co-op is therefore a plan for an elevator building with a garage or a lot, not a plan for a lawn.

The listings also tell us what a board is protecting. Homes.com's Yonkers and White Plains pages showed buildings advertising garages, parking, elevators, laundry rooms, storage and, in White Plains, doormen. New Rochelle listings mentioned pools and water views on the Sound side. Mount Vernon's included garages, storage and the phrase “near train,” which in that city usually means the Fleetwood or Mount Vernon West stations on the Harlem Line. Greenburgh's mentioned pools and garages alongside elevators. Every one of those features is a place a camera may belong, and every one has its own problem: a garage ramp, a package shelf, a laundry door, a pool gate.

The figures we plan around in Westchester

  • Six cities, 19 towns and 20 villages. That is how the county government describes Westchester's layout, for a population of just over one million on about 500 square miles.
  • Forty-five local police agencies. A clip from a co-op camera may end up with a city department, a village department, the county police or the State Police, depending on the address.
  • 1,581 co-op sales in twelve months. Steady turnover means steady new shareholders, new cars in the garage and new faces at the door, all of which shapes who gets camera access and how the notice is written.
  • Starting price $1,099. Four house-brand cameras with a recorder, installed, from our Westchester price list; every further camera priced after the walk.

What a Westchester board's first call usually sounds like

The call almost never starts with “we want cameras.” It starts with something that went wrong. A shareholder came down to the garage and found her car's exhaust cut open. Packages vanished from the vestibule of a building with no doorman. Somebody has been sleeping in the stairwell between the garage and the lobby. The recorder in the super's office stopped showing three cameras months ago and nobody noticed until the police asked for video. A board member walked the property after a burglary down the block and realized nobody could say what the old cameras actually covered. We build the plan from those problems outward, and a camera that does not answer one of them does not go on the drawing.

Building Types

Six Westchester Co-op Buildings and What Each Needs From Its Cameras

Most of the cooperatives we walk in the county fit one of six patterns. They are not legal categories; they are the shapes that change where cameras go, how cable gets to them and what the recorder has to keep. A board that recognizes its own building in this list already knows half of what the first phase will look like.

The postwar elevator tower over a garage

This is the Yonkers co-op people picture first: ten to twenty stories, a few hundred apartments, a lobby with a mailroom off to one side, and a garage under or behind the building with its own ramp and gate. Many sit along the city's main avenues and parkway corridors, close to bus routes and a short drive from the Metro-North stations. The camera work concentrates at three choke points: the street door and vestibule, the garage gate and ramp, and the door between the garage and the elevator lobby. Everything else, from the trash rooms to the laundry, comes after those three are covered well. Cable in these towers usually runs up an existing riser or along the garage ceiling, which makes the job cleaner than the building's size suggests.

The prewar elevator building near a Harlem Line station

Fleetwood in Mount Vernon, Bronxville, the Garth Road corridor in Eastchester and parts of Scarsdale have five- and six-story masonry co-ops from the 1920s and 1930s within a few minutes' walk of a station. A local history published by Scarsdale10583 in February 2026 described the thirteen residential buildings on Garth Road, just south of the Scarsdale station, as nearly all cooperatively owned. These buildings have plaster walls, ornamental lobbies, small vestibules, a basement laundry and often no garage at all. The cameras have to be small and well placed, the cable has to hide in closets, basements and existing chases, and the board usually cares as much about how the lobby looks afterward as about what the camera sees.

The garden-and-mid-rise campus

Along Central Avenue in Hartsdale and in pockets of Greenburgh, White Plains and New Rochelle, co-ops combine low garden buildings with one or two mid-rises on a shared property, sometimes with a pool, a lot that wraps around the buildings and separate laundry rooms in each basement. The difficulty here is distance. A recorder in one building has to receive video from buildings a few hundred feet away, and the walkways between them are where people actually move. These properties need a site plan, not just a floor plan, before anyone counts cameras.

The downtown doorman co-op

White Plains, and to a lesser degree New Rochelle and Yonkers, have elevator co-ops with a staffed lobby desk, a package room behind it and a garage shared with visitors or a commercial tenant. Here the cameras serve the staff as much as the board. The desk needs a clear live view of the doors it cannot see from its chair, the package room needs coverage that holds up when the desk is unattended for a minute, and the recorder needs separate accounts so a doorman can watch live without being able to export or delete anything.

The Sound Shore building

New Rochelle's waterside co-ops, and a handful in Larchmont, Mamaroneck and Rye, add sun glare off the water, salt air and sometimes a pool or a marina-side lot. Cameras on the water side need wide dynamic range and corrosion-resistant hardware, and anything near the pool or a boat area needs careful masking so the board is recording the gate and the deck entrance, not people swimming.

The river-town hillside building

Tarrytown, Irvington, Dobbs Ferry, Ossining and Peekskill have co-ops built into slopes, with the lobby on one street, a garage or lot on a lower street and long exterior stairs or paths between them. The hill decides the cable route. Video from a lower-level lot may travel up an exterior wall, through a garage ceiling or across a wireless link rather than through the building, and the plan has to show which.

Police & Footage

Forty-Five Police Agencies: Getting a Westchester Co-op's Clip to the Right One

Westchester has no single police force. A Wikipedia summary of law enforcement in the county counts forty-five local police agencies, and notes that some smaller communities have consolidated or contracted their policing: the Town of Cortlandt's department was disbanded, with patrol by the State Police backed up by the county police, and Mount Kisco's department merged into the Westchester County Police, which now patrols the village. The county's Department of Public Safety lists municipal patrol in Mount Kisco and Cortlandt among its own duties. Everywhere else, a co-op's camera footage will usually end up with the city, town or village department that covers its address.

For a board, the practical point is that a camera system is only useful to a detective if the clip arrives quickly, in a format the department can play, with a time stamp that matches reality. That is not a legal issue; it is a setup issue, and it is decided on installation day. We set every recorder to take its time from an internet time server, label each camera with a plain name that matches the plan (“Garage ramp, looking down,” not “Camera 7”), and show the managing agent how to export a clip with its player so a department can open it without special software.

A department asks for video. What happens next

Most requests arrive by phone or email from a detective or a patrol officer to the managing agent or the super. The board's footage policy should already say who handles it, so nobody improvises. In practice the agent or a named director finds the event, exports it, writes down which camera, which date and which minutes were copied, and keeps that note with the board's records. Anything beyond a routine request, such as a subpoena or a request for weeks of footage, goes to the co-op's attorney before anything is handed over. We do not hand footage to anyone ourselves; the video belongs to the corporation.

Why the time stamp matters more than the resolution

An officer comparing a co-op garage clip with a traffic camera or a neighbor's doorbell will line them up by time. A recorder that has drifted twelve minutes makes that comparison hard and invites doubt about the whole clip. A recorder that has lost its date after a power cut is worse. Automatic time sync, a battery backup and a yearly check of the clock cost almost nothing and are worth more in an investigation than an upgrade from 4 to 8 megapixels.

Sharing live video with police is a separate decision

Some departments invite property owners to register cameras or share access. That is a board decision, not a default, and it is entirely separate from installing the system. A board that wants to consider it should read the department's terms with its attorney and decide which cameras, if any, would be included. Nothing we install sends video anywhere outside the building unless the board sets it up to.

Garages & Lots

Tower Garages, Ramps and Surface Lots in Westchester Co-ops

If one space in a Westchester co-op generates more camera calls than any other, it is the garage. In February 2026 The Journal News asked the Yonkers and New Rochelle police departments about car-related crime in the Lower Hudson Valley, and its reporting opened with the observation that police and local repair shops had noticed a rise in thefts of catalytic converters and other vehicle-related thefts. A converter can be cut out from under a parked car in a couple of minutes, and the cars most often targeted are the ones that sit still overnight in the same place: exactly the cars in a co-op garage.

Cameras do not make a two-minute theft impossible. What they do is give the board and the police a vehicle, a direction of travel, a time and sometimes a face, and they change the behavior of people who check for cameras before they commit to a garage. Getting those results takes a plan built around how cars and people actually move through the space.

The gate and the ramp

The most valuable garage camera looks at the vehicle gate from inside, aimed where cars slow down to wait for the door. That is where a camera gets the sharpest view of a front plate and of the driver's side window. A second camera near the bottom of the ramp, looking up, shows anyone who walks in behind a car while the gate is open, which is the most common way strangers get into a garage that otherwise requires a remote or a fob. On a curving ramp we add a camera at the turn so there is no blind section between the street and the parking floor.

The door into the building

A garage that is well covered but connects to the elevator lobby through an unwatched door is only half done. A camera facing that door, inside the garage, shows who entered the building from below. A second camera on the elevator side shows who came down. In towers where the garage stair also serves as a fire stair, we keep the camera on the corridor side so nothing interferes with the stair itself.

Columns, low ceilings and parking rows

Garage columns hide cars. Rather than adding a camera behind every column, we mount cameras at the ends of each drive aisle, looking down the length of the row, so every car is seen from the front or the back as it moves. Ceiling height decides the camera body: a low garage ceiling calls for a compact dome or turret that a tall van will not clip, and for vandal-resistant housings within reach.

Surface lots beside garden buildings

Garden-and-mid-rise campuses usually have open lots instead of garages. Those lots are lit by a few fixtures on the buildings and poles, and the night picture depends on that light. Where lighting is thin we recommend cameras with built-in illuminators on the lot entrance and leave the board to decide, with its electrician, whether to add fixtures. We do not do electrical work, so any new light or outlet is the building's electrician's job, and we say so on the plan.

Garages run by an outside operator

A few downtown co-ops lease their garage to a parking company or share it with a commercial tenant. That raises a question the board should settle before the drawing is final: whose cameras cover the garage, who sees the video and who pays when a camera is hit by a car. We draw the co-op's cameras to cover the corporation's own doors and the routes its residents use, and leave the operator's booth and cash handling to the operator's own system unless the lease says otherwise.

Lobbies & Packages

Lobbies, Doorman Desks and Package Rooms in Westchester Elevator Co-ops

The lobby is where a co-op's cameras earn their keep every day, not only after an incident. Shareholders want to know their deliveries are safe, staff want to see the doors they cannot see from the desk, and the board wants a record of who entered the building. In Westchester's elevator co-ops the lobby is often generous in size but complicated in shape: a vestibule, an inner door, a mailroom alcove, a package room, a seating area and the elevator bank, sometimes on two levels because of the hill.

The vestibule and the inner door

The single most important camera in an elevator co-op looks at people as they come through the inner lobby door, from slightly above head height, so faces are lit by the lobby rather than silhouetted against the street. The outer door gets its own camera only if the vestibule is where packages are left or where problems happen. In prewar buildings near the stations, where the vestibule is small and finished in stone or wood, we place a compact camera on the lobby side and keep the vestibule itself clear of hardware.

Buildings with a doorman

A doorman does not replace a camera; a camera does not replace a doorman. The desk needs a live view of the side doors, the garage entrance and the package room, on a monitor turned toward the staff and away from visitors. The recording is what matters when something happens on a break, at a shift change or after the desk closes. We give staff live-view logins only, and leave search, export and deletion to the managing agent and named directors.

Buildings without a doorman

Most Yonkers and Mount Vernon towers and nearly every garden building run without a doorman. Packages land in the vestibule, on a shelf inside the lobby or in a locked room opened by couriers. In those buildings we put one camera over the place packages actually end up, aimed so it sees the hands and face of anyone picking one up, and a second at the door they leave through. If packages pile up on the floor because the shelf is full, the camera plan should follow the pile, and we say so.

Package rooms with lockers

Smart lockers reduce theft but do not end it, because the overflow that will not fit still sits somewhere. A camera at the locker bank, slightly to one side so the screen is not washed out, and a second covering the overflow shelf, deal with both. Where a courier code or a fob opens the package room, the camera is what shows whose code was used.

Mailboxes

A camera that shows who is standing at the mailbox bank is normal and useful when shareholders report missing mail. One zoomed in tightly enough to read envelopes or the contents of an open box is not, and we never aim a camera that close. The wider view is also the one that shows a face.

Coverage

Room by Room: What a Westchester Co-op Camera Plan Covers and What It Leaves Out

Every camera on the plan answers a question the board can state in a sentence. Here is how we treat each common area in a Westchester cooperative, with the reason for each placement, so directors can strike anything they do not need before the price is fixed.

Side doors, service entrances and loading areas

The service door is where contractors, deliveries, movers and trash go in and out, and where doors get propped. One camera inside, facing the door at an angle that shows the face of the person opening it, is usually enough. In towers with a separate loading dock, the dock gets a camera that covers the truck bay and the door into the building together.

Stairwells and roof doors

Most boards do not need a camera on every landing. A camera on the corridor side of the stair doors on the ground floor and the garage level, plus one at the roof door if people have been getting onto the roof, covers the routes that matter. In co-ops that lease rooftop space to wireless carriers, the roof door camera also shows when carrier crews come and go.

Laundry rooms

A camera at the laundry room door, looking in, shows who came and went. A second over the machines can help when a vendor's coin or card box is broken into. Neither should see into a restroom or any spot where people change.

Storage rooms, bike rooms and cages

Storage corridors are long. One camera at the entrance and one at the far end, looking back toward the door, usually cover a whole aisle of cages better than several cameras in the middle. Bike rooms need a camera at the door and one over the racks, aimed to show the person rather than the bikes.

Trash rooms and compactor areas

Illegal dumping by outsiders, and furniture left behind by departing shareholders, cost Westchester co-ops real money in carting charges. A camera covering the exterior trash enclosure or the compactor room door is often one of the first the board adds after the doors and garage.

Elevator cabs

A small dome in a top corner of each cab, with video carried down the hoistway by a wireless link made for elevators so no cable is added to the elevator's own traveling cable. Anything inside the elevator controller or the cab wiring belongs to the building's elevator contractor, and we coordinate with them rather than working on their equipment.

Pools, gyms and community rooms

The entrance to each amenity gets a camera. The pool gets one over the gate and the deck entry, masked so the water and changing areas are not recorded. A gym gets a door camera and perhaps one over the equipment, never toward a locker room door. Community rooms get a door camera; what happens at a shareholder's party inside is not the board's business.

Spaces that never get a camera

The inside of any apartment, any view through a window into an apartment or a neighbor's property, restrooms, changing rooms, staff locker rooms and the staff break area. Where a camera's view crosses a window or a neighbor's yard, we black out that part of the picture in the recorder, and the mask is drawn on the plan the board approves.

Equipment

The Co-op Security Camera Systems We Install in Westchester County

A co-op's camera system is a small private network: cameras, cable, a switch that powers the cameras, a recorder that keeps the video, a battery that carries it through outages, and accounts that let the right people see it. Each piece is chosen for the building, not pulled from a box.

Camera bodies

  • Compact domes and turrets for lobbies, halls, laundry rooms, garage ceilings and cabs. Vandal-rated domes anywhere a camera can be reached or hit.
  • Bullets for long, narrow views: a garage drive aisle, a lot entrance, a path between garden buildings.
  • Dual-lens and panoramic cameras where one mounting point has to watch a wide courtyard or a lot corner, saving a second cable run.
  • Cameras with built-in illuminators for dim lots and walkways, so night video shows color rather than gray shapes.
  • Pan-tilt-zoom cameras only where someone will actually steer them, such as a staffed desk watching a large campus. Most co-ops do better with fixed cameras.

Recorder and drives

The recorder stores video on drives inside it. How many days it keeps depends on camera count, resolution, frame rate and how much motion the cameras see. A garage camera facing a busy ramp fills a drive faster than a storage-room camera that sees two people a day. We size the drives to the number of days the board picks and write that number on the plan, so nobody discovers later that the recorder only holds a week.

Power and cable

Network cameras take power and video over one cable from a power-over-Ethernet switch beside the recorder, so most camera locations need no outlet. A single run can go about 100 meters before it needs a switch or an extender. In towers we use the existing risers and the garage ceiling; in prewar buildings we use closets, basements and old chases; between garden buildings we use conduit, an outdoor-rated cable on a messenger, or a point-to-point wireless link, whichever the site allows.

Battery backup

A battery unit keeps the recorder, the switch and the cameras running through short outages and shuts the recorder down cleanly in long ones. Westchester's summer storms take power out for hours at a time in some neighborhoods, and a recorder that loses power mid-write can lose its settings or its clock. We size the battery to the actual load and tell the board how long it should last.

Accounts and remote viewing

Each person the board names gets a personal login with only what they need: live view for staff, search and export for the agent, administration for one or two directors. The corporation keeps the master password, sealed and filed with the board's records. Remote viewing by phone works through the manufacturer's app or a secured connection, and we disable the default accounts every recorder ships with.

House system first, name brands when asked

Every price on this page assumes our house-brand cameras and recorder, the line on our published price list. If the board would rather have a name brand, Lorex, Luminys and Reolink are our partner brands, and other major makers can be installed too. Where a board's prior installer left a recorder from another maker that is still sound, we can often add to it rather than replace it.

Analog systems and CCTV upgrades

Plenty of Westchester co-ops still run analog cameras on coaxial cable into an old DVR. Good coax can sometimes carry HD-over-coax cameras on a new recorder, which spares the board new cable in finished halls. Damaged or ancient runs get replaced with network cable. Our general CCTV installation page for Westchester County covers closed-circuit systems for every kind of property; this page covers how we apply that work to cooperatives.

Why we start smaller than the board expects

Six months of living with a modest system teaches a board more than any proposal can: which camera the agent actually searches, which door nobody uses, where the packages really pile up. How we count a camera

One camera body at one mounting point is one camera, even if it has two lenses. A dual-lens panoramic camera covering a lot corner counts as one location on the plan and one line on the price. The board sees the count, the reason and the price for each, and can strike any line before signing.

Co-op Security Camera Installation Services Across Westchester County

The Law

New York Law on Co-op Cameras as It Applies in Westchester

What follows is background from a licensed installer, with sources, so a board can bring better questions to its own attorney. It is not legal advice, and the co-op's counsel has the last word on anything here.

The board's authority over the common areas

The cooperative corporation owns the building, and shareholders hold proprietary leases on their apartments. In Levandusky v. One Fifth Avenue Apartment Corp. (1990), New York's Court of Appeals held that courts generally defer to a co-op board's decisions made in good faith, within the board's authority and for the corporation's purposes. Deciding where cameras go in the corporation's own lobby, garage and halls is the kind of decision that rule protects, provided the board follows its bylaws and proprietary lease and records its vote.

Common areas and privacy

Attorney Ricardo M. Vera told CooperatorNews in 2025 that New York does not recognize a common-law right of privacy in public or common areas. That supports cameras in lobbies, halls and garages. It does not support cameras aimed into apartments or anywhere people undress, which state law covers separately.

No sound, ever

Under the Penal Law, eavesdropping is a class E felony (§250.05), and the definition in §250.00 covers mechanical overhearing of a conversation when not even one party has consented. Two shareholders arguing in the laundry room in front of a camera with a live microphone is that situation. Several cameras on our price list have built-in microphones, so on every co-op job we turn audio off at the recorder and say so on the plan.

Rooms that are off limits

Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people without consent in bathrooms, changing rooms and similar places. Labor Law §203-c separately says no employer may make a video recording of an employee in a restroom, locker room or room set aside for changing clothes unless a court orders it. In a co-op that rules out the staff locker room, any restroom and any changing area, including at the pool.

City laws stop at the city line

A lot of what is written about co-op cameras comes from Manhattan and cites New York City rules. Those do not travel. The Multiple Residence Law, by its own §3, applies to cities under 325,000 people and to all towns and villages, which takes in every Westchester municipality, including Yonkers. New York City's Local Law 63 of 2021 on smart-access data in multiple dwellings is a city law and does not govern a Westchester building. State statutes on eavesdropping, surveillance, labor and licensing apply everywhere.

Who may install

A company that installs or services security systems in New York needs a state license under General Business Law §69-m, and the Department of State's regulations (19 NYCRR Part 195) treat video surveillance used to detect or watch for intrusion as part of that licensed work. Ours is New York State license 12000287431, printed on this page and on every proposal so a board can check it. Westchester County's consumer protection office separately licenses plumbers and electricians, and we do not do electrical work: any new outlet, circuit or light fixture on the plan is marked for the building's own electrician.

Keep the cameras out of the admissions file

Westchester has its own co-op disclosure law, administered by the county's Human Rights Commission, which since August 2021 sets deadlines for acting on purchase applications and requires fair-housing training for board members. Video of a prospective buyer touring the building has no place in an admissions decision, and a footage policy that limits review to incidents, safety and building operations keeps directors clear of that problem.

Fire alarm work stays separate

Camera cable never ties into the fire alarm system, and we keep camera work and fire alarm work on separate scopes. Boards with fire alarm questions should deal with the municipality's fire officials and a fire alarm contractor directly.

Footage Policy

Five Decisions a Westchester Co-op Board Writes Down Before the Cameras Go Live

Mounting cameras is the easy part. Boards get into trouble over who looked at the video, why and what happened to the copy. FirstService Residential's September 2024 guidance for New York co-ops and condos suggests keeping footage for roughly 30 to 90 days, logging every request and every access, and not giving residents copies without board and counsel approval. We hand every board a short draft built around five decisions, for its attorney to adjust before the system goes live.

1. How long the recorder keeps video

Pick a number of days and buy the drives to match. Thirty days catches most incidents that are noticed within a week or two; garage problems are often discovered later, after a car has been sitting, so some boards keep the garage cameras longer than the rest. The recorder erases the oldest video by itself once it reaches the limit, and that automatic overwrite should be stated in the policy so nobody is accused of deleting something.

2. Who may watch live, and who may search

Staff watch live to do their jobs. Searching recorded video is an investigation and belongs to fewer people: the managing agent and one or two named directors. Each has a personal account, so the recorder's own log shows who looked at what and when.

3. What counts as a reason to search

A reported incident, a safety concern, damage to the building, a police request or an insurance claim. Not curiosity about a neighbor, not a dispute between directors, and not the admissions process. Writing the list down protects the board as much as the shareholders.

4. How a copy leaves the building

Only the agent or a named director exports a clip, in the recorder's own format with its player, and records the camera, date and minutes in a simple log. Shareholders who ask for footage of their own incident get a decision from the board, and copies go to them only with counsel's approval. Police requests go through the same log.

5. What shareholders are told

A one-page notice before go-live: what areas are covered, that no audio is recorded, how long video is kept, who can see it and how to report an incident. We supply camera notice signs at the entrances on the plan; the board's notice to shareholders, and through them to any approved subtenants, is the board's document, and we provide a draft.

Self-managed co-ops

Smaller Westchester co-ops, especially garden buildings in Greenburgh and the river towns, are run by volunteer boards with no managing agent. The same policy works with two directors named instead of an agent. We keep alerts to the few that matter, so nobody's phone lights up every time a car passes the lot.

Board Questions

Westchester Co-op Camera Questions, Answered

Electric-vehicle chargers were just installed in our garage. Should a camera cover them?

Usually yes. Chargers are expensive, their cables get cut or stolen for the copper, and disputes over who is hogging a charging space are common in co-op garages. One camera at the end of the charging row, aimed down the row rather than at a single car, shows who plugged in, who unplugged someone else and whether anyone tampered with the equipment. The charger's own power wiring is the electrician's work, and we keep our cable clear of it.

Contractors renovating apartments keep working past the hours in our alteration agreement. Can the cameras help?

They can document it. A camera at the service entrance and one at the service elevator landing show when a crew arrived and left, which gives the agent dated evidence to enforce the alteration agreement with the shareholder who hired them. The board should say in its footage policy that building operations, including alteration rules, are a permitted reason to review video, so nobody can claim the cameras were used for something they were not meant for.

Our garage gate sometimes stays open after a car passes. Can the system tell the super?

The camera alone can only record it. A door contact on the gate wired to the access or alarm system can send an alert when the gate has been open longer than it should be, and the camera then shows why. Some recorders can also raise an alert when a camera sees motion in a defined zone after hours. We price the contact and the camera together, because the alert is what fixes the problem tonight and the video is what explains it tomorrow.

We are converting the old super's apartment into a package room. When should the camera be planned?

Now, before the walls close. Running a cable to the future camera spot while the room is open costs a fraction of fishing it in later, and the camera can be aimed with the shelving and lockers already drawn. Send us the architect's or contractor's layout and we will mark the camera and cable on it, then install the camera itself when the room is finished.

We already have a lobby screen for building announcements. Can it show a camera view too?

It can, if the screen accepts a second input or the announcement system can show a video feed, but think about which view. Shareholders like seeing the package room or the front door on the lobby screen; nobody wants the garage or the laundry room displayed to every visitor. A single live view of the entrance, labeled as such, is the common choice, and it doubles as a reminder that the building is recorded.

One of our shareholders is a police officer and offered to review footage for the board. Is that a good idea?

It is generous, but it is better declined. Reviewing the building's video is a board function under the footage policy, done by the agent or named directors and logged. A shareholder who is an officer looking at a neighbor's comings and goings blurs the line between the co-op and the department, and can put the officer in an awkward position too. If an incident warrants police review, the clip goes to the department through the normal request route.

An outside company runs our garage under a lease. Whose cameras should cover it?

Read the lease first. Often the operator is responsible for its booth, its cash and the cars it parks, and the co-op for its doors, stairs and the route residents walk. The co-op's cameras then cover the garage-to-building door, the pedestrian path and the gate, and the operator keeps its own system for its booth. Where the lease puts security on the co-op, the plan covers the whole garage and the board decides whether the operator gets live view.

Our lobby has a large mirror facing the front door. Will it confuse the cameras?

It can, in two ways. In daylight a camera that sees the mirror records a second, reversed lobby that makes a clip harder to read; at night the camera's own infrared light bounces off the glass and washes out the picture. We aim the lobby camera so the mirror sits outside its view or at a steep angle, mask whatever reflection remains, and where that is impossible we turn off the camera's infrared and rely on the lobby lights. Prewar lobbies with mirrored walls get this treatment as a matter of routine.

A wireless carrier leases space on our roof. Can a camera go on the roof door without affecting its equipment?

Yes. The camera goes inside the building, facing the roof door from the stair or bulkhead side, so it never touches the roof or the carrier's antennas and cabinets. It shows when carrier technicians come and go, which helps the agent check access against the lease. Anything mounted outside on the roof would need the board's lease terms checked first.

Our garage entrance is three floors below the lobby, on a different street. Can one recorder serve both?

Usually. In most hillside buildings a riser, an elevator machine-room chase or the garage ceiling gives a cable path from the lowest level up to the recorder, and network cable can run about 100 meters between switches. When no inside path exists, a small switch on the garage level fed by one cable or a fiber link carries all the garage cameras up together. The plan shows which route we tested.

Can the cameras follow one car from the street gate down a curving ramp to its parking space?

Yes, if the cameras hand off from one to the next with no gap: one inside the gate, one at the ramp turn, and one looking down each drive aisle. On the recorder, the agent plays those cameras side by side on one timeline and watches the car move through the garage. The plan shows each camera's view overlapping the next, so the board can see there is no blind stretch.

Reality Check

Five Things Westchester Co-op Boards Hear About Cameras, Checked

“Westchester co-ops are garden apartments, so cameras are a lawn-and-parking-lot job”

Some are. Most are not. In the November 2026 listings we checked, Yonkers alone accounted for 199 of the county's 466 co-ops for sale, followed by Mount Vernon, New Rochelle and White Plains, and the listings were full of elevators, garages and laundry rooms. The typical Westchester co-op camera job is an elevator building with a lobby, a package problem and a garage or lot, and it should be planned like one.

“We have a doorman, so lobby cameras are a waste”

A doorman sees what is in front of the desk while on duty. Cameras see the side door, the package room and the garage while the doorman signs for a delivery, takes a break or goes off shift, and they keep a record nobody has to remember. In doorman buildings the cameras mostly make the staff's job easier: a live view of the doors the desk cannot see is the first thing they ask for.

“Converter thieves are too fast for cameras to matter”

A converter can be cut out in a few minutes, and no camera stops that by itself. But the vehicle that brought the thieves has to come through the gate or into the lot, and an entrance camera aimed where cars slow down records it. The Journal News reported in February 2026 that police and repair shops in the area had noticed a rise in converter and other vehicle-related thefts. Cameras at the gate, good lighting and a garage that does not let people walk in behind cars work together; none of the three does the job alone.

“Co-op camera footage goes straight to the police”

It does not. The video stays on the corporation's recorder unless the board's agent exports a clip and hands it over, or the board chooses to join a department's camera program. Shareholders sometimes worry about this, and the notice the board sends out should say plainly who can see the video and when it leaves the building.

“A friend of the board can put up a few cameras and save the co-op money”

Installing security systems as a business in New York requires a license under General Business Law §69-m. A co-op that lets an unlicensed friend install cameras gives up insurance, warranty, documentation and someone accountable when a camera falls on a car or the recorder turns out to be open to the internet on its default password. The savings rarely survive the first problem.

What the generic advice gets right

Plenty of online camera advice is sound: video only, no cameras in private spaces, keep the recorder locked, write a retention period down and change the default passwords. Those rules apply in Westchester as everywhere else. Where generic advice goes wrong for a co-op is assuming one person owns the building and makes every decision; in a cooperative, the board decides, the corporation owns the video and the policy matters as much as the hardware.

Field Notes

Field Notes From Co-op Camera Walks in Westchester County

Camera models are the least interesting part of a walk. These are the details that actually change a Westchester co-op's plan, and we look for them in every building.

  • How people really get into the garage. We watch the gate cycle and time how long it stays open. If it stays open long enough for someone to walk in behind a car, the ramp camera moves up the list.
  • Where packages actually end up. Not where the board thinks they go, but where the couriers leave them at four in the afternoon. The camera follows the pile.
  • Which doors are propped. A rubber wedge or a folded flyer in a door frame tells us more than a floor plan does.
  • What the hill does to the cable. In hillside buildings we find the lowest point and the highest point on the plan first, because the route between them sets the price.
  • Where the old system's cable goes. Abandoned coax and old phone conduit can save days of work if they test clean.
  • The light at night. We ask the super which fixtures are on timers and which are burned out, and plan night coverage around what is really lit.
  • Who will use the recorder. If nobody on staff will ever search video, the system should be simple enough for the agent to do it from an office.

A checklist before the board signs

  • Every camera on the plan has a reason written beside it.
  • The recorder sits somewhere locked, ventilated and above any garage level.
  • Retention days and drive size are written down.
  • Audio is off, masked areas are drawn, and signs are included.
  • The corporation holds the master password, and every user has a personal login.
  • Any work for an electrician or the elevator contractor is marked as theirs.
  • Every possible extra is priced before the order.
  • The footage policy and shareholder notice are drafted.
  • The board has checked every bidder's state license with the Department of State.

Do the cameras keep recording while the recorder installs a software update?
+

The recorder restarts during an update and stops writing video for a few minutes. We schedule updates for a quiet hour, check every channel is recording again afterward, and note the update in the system log so a short gap at that time is explained if anyone ever asks about it.
