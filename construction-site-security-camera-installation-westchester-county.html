Element 4 · Why Westchester County Jobsites Get Hit

The County Is Building Faster Than It Can Watch Itself

A Westchester jobsite is an unusually concentrated pile of resale value sitting behind a plywood fence. Power tools are the item most often reported stolen from American construction sites, and recovery on a single stolen tool is poor enough that most contractors never see it again. Copper is worse — the U.S. Department of Energy has put copper theft losses across the country in the range of a billion dollars a year, and the damage on a jobsite is never limited to the metal. Thieves pull out days of finished rough-in to get at wire, so a loss that reads as a few hundred pounds of copper on a police report shows up on your schedule as two weeks of electrical rework.

What makes this county specific is the mix. Within about twenty-five miles you have downtown Yonkers towers going vertical near the Metro-North station, New Rochelle's transit district rebuilding around a planned $125 million station upgrade, the former United Hospital site in Port Chester cleared for a 957-unit mixed-use redevelopment, and — twenty minutes north — a Bedford horse property where the entire finish package for a stone-and-timber addition is stacked in a barn. The first kind of site gets hit by people who came for the copper and the containers. The second kind gets hit by someone who watched the delivery truck turn in and came back at ten at night for the appliances.

Access

Parkway in, parkway out

The Bronx River, Sprain Brook, Saw Mill, Hutchinson and Cross County give a vehicle a fast exit toward three counties and two states. A site a hundred yards off a parkway ramp is a different risk profile than one at the end of a dead-end road in Somers.

Fragmentation

Forty-plus police jurisdictions

Six cities, nineteen towns and twenty-three villages, most with their own department, plus Westchester County Police covering the parks and parkways and New York State Police Troop K in parts of the north. Knowing which desk takes your report matters when you are calling at 6 a.m.

Idle time

The winter gap

Exterior work slows from December through March while finish materials sit inside a dried-in shell. The building is at its most valuable and least visited in exactly the months when it is dark at 4:30 and nobody is driving past.

Element 5 · Systems & Configurations

What Actually Goes On a Westchester Site

There is no single jobsite camera. There are four configurations, and which one you get depends on what the site has, what phase it is in, and how long you need it.

Configuration 1

Battery + cellular unit

Self-contained head with an internal battery and its own LTE radio. Goes up on fencing, a container corner or a temporary pole the day after demolition, with no electrician and no service. This is the default for a Westchester teardown-rebuild or a single-lot custom home, and the one most often moved twice during a build as the work shifts across the lot.

Configuration 2

Solar-assisted unit

Same head, plus a panel sized for the site. Cleared lots with open southern sky — the north-county subdivisions, laydown yards, parking-deck jobs — carry solar well. A tight river-town lot with mature trees and a neighboring building often does not, and gets a larger battery on a swap schedule instead.

Configuration 3

Hardwired PoE

Cameras on Cat6 back to a recorder in a locked temporary office or an electrical room, once temporary power is energized and the building is dried in. This is the multifamily and commercial answer, and it is designed from the start to survive turnover as the permanent building system.

Configuration 4

Mobile surveillance trailer

For cleared acreage, staging yards and phased sites with no structure to mount on. Mast-mounted cameras, on-board recording, battery and solar, moved by truck as the work moves. See solar surveillance trailers for Westchester County.

Element 5.5 · Terminology

The Words on the Quote, Explained

PoE

Power over Ethernet. One Cat6 cable carries both the video and the camera's power, which is why a hardwired jobsite system needs a cable path but not an outlet at every head.

LTE / cellular

The camera has its own SIM and data plan. No site Wi-Fi, no contractor hotspot, no shared password. Data use is why clip length and alert sensitivity get tuned on install day.

AI detection

On-camera classification that separates a person or a vehicle from a branch, a deer or headlights sweeping a fence. It is the difference between forty alerts a night and four.

Talk-down

A speaker in the unit that lets a monitoring operator or the account owner speak to someone on site in real time.

Retention

How long recorded video is kept before it overwrites. Set per project — a nine-month build and a three-week pool dig do not need the same window.

LPR

License plate capture, aimed down a driveway or gate lane at a plate-reading angle rather than at a wide scene. Useful on sites with repeat vehicle traffic.

Element 5.7 · System Selection

Choosing by Site, Not by Brochure

Four questions settle most Westchester specs before anyone quotes a number.

1. Is there power yet?

No temporary service means battery or solar, full stop. Once a service is energized and locked, hardwired becomes available — and cheaper per camera over a long build.

2. How long is the build?

Under six months usually rents. Eighteen months of multifamily usually buys, because the hardware converts into the permanent system instead of going back on a truck.

3. What is the sky like?

Open lot, solar. Tree canopy or a neighbor's wall shading half the day — which describes a lot of Scarsdale, Irvington and Chappaqua lots — larger battery, scheduled swaps.

4. Who needs to see it?

One owner watching a house build is a different account structure than a GC, a developer, a lender's inspector and a project manager all needing their own login.

Element 5.8 · Placement Planning

Where the Heads Actually Go

Placement is decided off the site drawings and a walk, not by mounting cameras at the corners and hoping. The order below is the order that tends to matter on a Westchester site.

  1. The gate. Every vehicle that enters and leaves passes one point. A head aimed at the gate lane at plate height does more evidentiary work than three wide scene cameras.
  2. The container row and gang boxes. Tools do not walk out of the middle of a lot; they walk out of the place they are stored. A dedicated head on conex doors with a tight after-hours motion zone is the single highest-value camera on most sites.
  3. Material staging. Lumber packs, copper, appliances, stone and millwork on delivery week — the pile that is worth the most is the pile that is only there for nine days.
  4. The structure itself. Openings at grade, the point where scaffolding meets the building, and the elevation a neighbor is most likely to complain about later.
  5. The street frontage. On village and river-town sites this doubles as your record of what the sidewalk, the neighbor's wall and the parked cars looked like before your excavator arrived.

Element 22 · Build-Phase Security Map

What to Watch, Phase by Phase

Phase 1

Demo & excavation

Equipment and fuel are the exposure. One battery unit on the gate covers the machine parking area and documents the condition of adjacent property before anything gets blamed on your excavator.

Phase 2

Foundation & framing

Lumber packs and the first tool concentration arrive. Add the container-row head. Motion zones tighten to the storage area so the alerts stay meaningful.

Phase 3

Rough-in

Peak copper exposure — wire on spools, plumbing, HVAC. This is the phase where a loss costs weeks, not dollars. Interior coverage starts here on multifamily and commercial jobs.

Phase 4

Finish & closeout

Appliances, fixtures, stone, millwork and the punch-list traffic of a dozen subs with the door open. Access-driven losses beat fence-line losses at this stage, and the camera record becomes the way disputes get settled.

Element 9 · Questions Westchester Builders Actually Ask

Fourteen Real Questions, Answered Straight

We got hit Saturday night in Yonkers. How fast can something be up?

Emergency deployments are often same-day when a site has just been hit, because a battery-cellular unit needs a mount and a signal, not an electrician. The call that slows things down is not the hardware — it is deciding where the heads go, which is why the walk happens first even on a rush.

There's no power on the lot yet. Does that kill it?

No. Battery and cellular is the normal starting point on a Westchester lot. Power arrives later and changes what is possible, not whether you can be covered now.

My site is in a village historic district. Will the building department care?

Design review cares about permanent attachments to protected structures and what the streetscape looks like. Temporary units on fencing, containers and poles generally sidestep that conversation. When the permanent system goes in at turnover, that is the point to bring the architect and the village into it.

Can one camera cover a whole two-acre Bedford lot?

Realistically, no. One head covers a zone, not a property. On a long-driveway estate lot the productive answer is usually a driveway head at plate angle plus a head on the structure and material staging.

Do I have to sign a year?

Rentals are month-to-month. Builds slip and phases end early; a twelve-month commitment on a seven-month job is how people end up paying for a camera watching a finished house.

Will alerts wake me up every time a deer crosses the lot?

That is what AI classification and zone drawing are for. In north-county sites deer are the single most common cause of nuisance alerts, and tuning them out on install day is part of the job.

Who owns the footage?

The account owner does. Access is granted per user, retention is configurable, and clip export is available to the owner on request.

The neighbors in Larchmont are already complaining. Is a camera going to make it worse?

Aim matters. Heads are set to cover your site and your frontage, not a neighbor's yard or windows. A camera pointed correctly is also the thing that settles the next complaint about when your crew actually started.

Does this help my insurance?

Recorded footage may assist with incident and insurance documentation. Coverage requirements and any possible policy impacts must be confirmed directly with your insurer — we do not make claims about your policy.

Can the same system work on my next job?

Yes. Rental units relocate. Contractors running several Westchester jobs typically move units to the next site as one wraps rather than closing an account and reopening it.

What happens if somebody just steals the camera?

Units are mounted with tamper detection and alert on removal, and footage lives in the cloud as well as on board, so pulling the unit does not erase what it already recorded.

Our district job requires NDAA compliance. Can you document it?

Yes — compliant equipment specified and documentation provided with the submittal package. That is standard on school district, municipal and county work in this county.

Cell service is weak at our Pound Ridge site. Now what?

Carrier selection is part of the spec, and units can be sited for signal rather than convenience. Where coverage is genuinely marginal, on-board recording carries the site through a dropout and uploads when the link returns.

What does removal cost at the end?

Nothing extra on a rental. Closeout removal is included, which matters on a job where the last two weeks are already a scramble.

Element 10 · People Also Ask

What Westchester Searches Return First

How much do construction site cameras cost per month?

Monitored rental units in this market run $349 for one, $649 for a pair and $999 for multi-unit coverage with live operators. Purchased hardwired cameras run $500 to $2,500 per camera installed.

Do construction cameras work without WiFi?

Yes — cellular units carry their own LTE connection and battery, which is the entire reason they exist. No site service, no contractor hotspot, no shared password.

Are security cameras required on construction sites in New York?

There is no blanket statewide camera mandate for jobsites. Requirements come from elsewhere — an owner's contract, a lender, an insurer, a municipal site-safety condition, or a public-work specification. We build to whatever document is actually driving the requirement.

What is the best camera for a construction site?

The one matched to the site's power, connectivity, sky and duration. A battery-cellular head on a raw Westchester lot and a hardwired PoE system in a dried-in multifamily shell are both correct answers to different questions.

Can jobsite cameras be moved as the build progresses?

Yes, and they should be. Coverage that made sense at excavation is aimed at the wrong thing by finish phase. Relocations are included on rental accounts.

How long is jobsite video kept?

Configurable per project. Short retention suits a quick renovation; a long multifamily build usually wants a longer window for dispute and claim purposes.

Element 10.5 · AI Overview Reality Check

What the AI Answers Get Wrong About Westchester

Ask a search engine about construction site cameras and you get a national average built from directory pages, aggregator listings and vendor blogs. Below are the claims that surface most often, and what they actually look like in this county.

1. "Construction site cameras cost $100–$200 per month"

The claimAggregators like Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr generate a national average by blending consumer doorbell cameras and unmonitored trail cameras into the same bucket as commercial jobsite units.
Westchester realityA trail camera is not a jobsite camera. A monitored unit with AI classification, cellular data, cloud retention, talk-down and a service relationship starts at $349 per month here. If a quote is under $200, look for what is missing — usually the monitoring, the data plan, the relocation, or the person who answers the phone.

2. "Solar cameras work anywhere"

The claimVendor pages show a panel and a sunny lot and imply that solar is universal.
Westchester realityHalf this county is tree canopy and steep grade. A Rye Brook lot with open southern sky carries solar comfortably. A Hastings-on-Hudson infill lot between two buildings, in December, does not. Sites like the second one get larger battery banks and a swap schedule, and anyone who promises otherwise has not looked at the lot.

3. "Cameras stop construction theft"

The claimMarketing copy that promises prevention, often attached to a percentage.
Westchester realityVisible surveillance equipment, lighting and monitored audio may help discourage unauthorized activity. Results vary by site conditions, placement, monitoring and response procedures. What a camera reliably does is document — who came in the gate, what left, at what time, on which day — and that documentation is what supports the police report, the insurance file and the conversation with the sub whose story does not match.

4. "Any electrician can install jobsite cameras"

The claimCameras get treated as a trade add-on rather than as licensed low-voltage work.
Westchester realitySecurity and low-voltage installation in New York is licensed work. Abstract Enterprises Security Systems holds NYS license #12000287431. On a public-work project, on an insurance-driven install, or in any dispute where footage becomes evidence, who installed the system and under what license is a question that eventually gets asked.

5. "You need internet on site"

The claimBuying guides assume a network exists.
Westchester realityMost sites here get cameras precisely because nothing exists yet. Cellular units are the default from demo day until a permanent service is live, and on many single-family builds nothing else is ever needed.

6. "One camera is enough for a house build"

The claimSimplified advice aimed at homeowners with a single doorbell camera.
Westchester realityA single head covers a zone. On a custom home the gate and the material staging are usually not the same zone, which is why the two-unit builder pair is the most common configuration in this county rather than the entry-level single.

7. "Police will respond to the camera alert"

The claimVendor pages implying automatic dispatch to a specific department.
Westchester realityMonitoring personnel can follow the customer's approved escalation procedure. Authorities may be contacted when appropriate, but response and outcomes are controlled by the responding agency. In a county with more than forty separate police jurisdictions plus county police on the parkways and State Police in parts of the north, nobody should be promising you a dispatch time.
Element 23 · Delivery-Week Protocol

The Nine Days That Carry the Most Risk

On most Westchester builds the highest-value week is the one where appliances, fixtures, stone, millwork or copper land on site and sit before installation. The protocol is simple and it is mostly scheduling, not hardware.

  1. Tell us the delivery date. Coverage gets re-aimed at the staging area before the truck arrives, not after.
  2. Motion zones tighten around the material for the duration, so alerts mean something during the window that matters.
  3. Overnight monitoring can be added for the delivery week alone on rental accounts and dropped afterward.
  4. Signage goes up at the gate and frontage. Visible equipment is part of the deterrent value.
  5. When the material is installed, zones go back to normal so the site is not generating noise alerts for the rest of the job.

Element 11 · DIY vs Professional

When the Consumer Camera Is Actually Fine

Straight answer: for a small backyard project where somebody is on site most days and there is a garage outlet and house WiFi, a consumer camera on a pole is a reasonable thing to try. It is cheap and it is better than nothing.

It stops being reasonable at the point where any of the following is true — and on a Westchester commercial or custom-home site, at least one always is:

Where DIY breaks

No power and no network for the first three months · a battery that dies in a January cold snap · alerts nobody triages at 2 a.m. · footage stored on a card that walks out with the camera · no signage, no talk-down, no deterrent presence · nobody to call when it goes offline during the one week it mattered.

Where professional pays

Licensed installation on record · equipment specified for the site's power, sky and signal · mounts that survive Hudson wind · cloud retention independent of the device · optional live operators · documented compliance for public work · a service relationship with a three-year warranty on AESS-supplied products.

Element 21 · Field Notes

Notes From Westchester Installs

Practical observations from doing this work in this county, offered as installer guidance rather than as customer accounts:

Tree canopy is the thing that surprises people. A lot that looks wide open in March is a shaded box by late May, and a solar unit specified in early spring can be underperforming by the time the framing is up. On wooded Westchester lots the safer specification is a larger battery bank from the start, with solar treated as a supplement rather than the primary supply.

Gate placement changes more often than anyone plans for. On constrained downtown Yonkers and New Rochelle sites the construction entrance moves two or three times as staging shifts, and every move invalidates the aim of the camera watching it. Building a relocation into the expectation — rather than treating it as a change order — is what keeps the coverage honest through the whole build.

Cellular signal in the river valley is uneven in ways that do not follow a map. Sites down against the Hudson, in the cut below a bluff, or behind a large steel structure can be weak while a site a quarter mile uphill is fine. Carrier selection and a signal check during the walk cost nothing and prevent the install that has to be redone the following week.

Element 14 · Frequently Asked Questions

Westchester Jobsite Camera FAQ

Do Westchester jobsite cameras need power or internet on site?

No. Battery-and-cellular units run on their own power and their own LTE connection, so they can be set the day after demolition and before a temporary service is energized. On cleared lots with open sky, solar keeps the batteries topped off. When a permanent service is live and the building is dried in, the same project can move to hardwired PoE cameras.

Element 26 · Weather & Winter Operations

Nor'easters, River Wind, and December Sun

Three weather realities drive equipment choices in this county. Cold reduces usable battery capacity, so a unit sized for an October install can run short in a January freeze unless the bank was specified with winter in mind. Short days reduce solar harvest at exactly the point in the year when the site is dark for sixteen hours, which is why panel angle gets set for the low winter sun rather than for June. And wind coming off the Hudson and the Sound loads a mast-mounted camera hard, so mounting hardware and tie-downs are specified for the exposure rather than for convenience.

Nor'easters add the other problem: power and network outages across whole municipalities. A cellular unit with on-board recording keeps working through an outage that takes a wired system offline, and uploads what it captured once the link returns.
