The Neighborhood

Why Madison Properties Need Alarm Installation

Madison does not appear on many maps, and the people who live there tend to say Sheepshead Bay, Marine Park or Midwood depending on which side of it they live on. Wikipedia treats it as a subsection of Sheepshead Bay and describes it in one line: "a purely residential subsection," named after James Madison High School, bounded by Kings Highway to the north, Avenue U to the south, Ocean Avenue to the west and Nostrand Avenue to the east, with the ZIP code 11229 and Brooklyn Community Board 15. Other descriptions stretch the eastern line to Gerritsen Avenue, toward Marine Park. We serve the whole of it, and every block on either side, at the same Brooklyn prices.

"Purely residential" is the part that matters for an alarm. Between the shopping streets on its edges, Madison is block after block of houses on a tight grid, with Quentin Road, Avenue R, Avenue S and Avenue T running east and west between Kings Highway and Avenue U, and numbered East streets running north and south. City Neighborhoods describes two-story brick houses and low apartment houses, detached brick and stucco houses with driveways, lawns and gardens, two-family brick houses, limestone row houses and modest single-family homes, most of them built between 1910 and 1930 in Neo-Renaissance, Tudor Revival and Colonial styles, with larger apartment buildings along Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue. Untapped Cities calls it a quiet residential neighborhood of tree-lined streets and historic homes, one of the last parts of Brooklyn to be developed. Its oldest house, the Wyckoff-Bennett Homestead at 1669 East 22nd Street, dates to about 1766.

A neighborhood like that is quiet in the daytime. There are no storefronts on most blocks, no foot traffic heading to a train, and a good many houses where someone is home through the afternoon: retired owners, a parent with small children, a relative who moved in, a person working from a back bedroom. The Department of City Planning's profile of Community District 15, which takes in Sheepshead Bay, Madison, Homecrest, Manhattan Beach and nearby blocks, counted 26,319 residents aged 65 and over in the 2010 census, 16.5 percent of the district, and 8,459 householders aged 65 and over living alone. Those are the households most burglar alarms are set up to ignore during the day, because a system that is switched off whenever somebody is home does nothing at the hour when a stranger is most likely to be standing at the front door.

And strangers at the front door are the pattern that keeps showing up in the reporting from this part of Brooklyn. Police call them deception burglaries. The Brooklyn Paper described two men posing as water department inspectors at the home of an 89-year-old man on 62nd Street in Dyker Heights, checking for "leaks," and noted that the same kind of crew had kept six Bensonhurst seniors busy in 2007 while a partner searched the house. A separate report from the 63rd Precinct described two men claiming to be meter readers at a house on East 36th Street near Quentin Road in Marine Park, a few blocks east of Madison; one forced a window open and let the other in, and they left with a safe holding important papers. Another 63rd Precinct case involved a woman phoning seniors in Marine Park, Mill Basin and Bergen Beach to claim dangerous carbon monoxide readings, then two men arriving in a white van to "repair" the furnace for $1,500 to $2,000. None of those ruses involves breaking a window while the house is empty. They involve an open door, a person who is home and an alarm that is off.

That is why this page is built around daytime. A Madison alarm still has to do the ordinary work of covering the back door, the basement, the reachable windows and the nights. But it should also keep the doors nobody is using watched while someone is home, let the person inside see and talk to a caller without opening up, and tell a son or daughter somewhere else when something unusual happens in the middle of the afternoon. A good camera at the front often belongs in the same plan, which we cover on our Madison security camera installation page, and many families add a code lock on the side door through access control in Madison so that home attendants and relatives stop carrying copied keys.

The Afternoon Problem

The Visitor at the Door: Designing a Madison Alarm for the Hours Someone Is Home

Read the deception cases side by side and the same few moves repeat. The visitor arrives in daylight, often in a vest or something that looks like a uniform, and names a reason that sounds routine: a gas meter, a water leak on the block, a window that needs inspecting, carbon monoxide. In the January 2012 cases near Ocean Parkway reported by NBC New York, one of the pair told an 89-year-old woman that she could not walk onto the property herself, so the owner had to come and show her; the moment the owner stepped out to help, the man went in. At the second house, a 90-year-old let the pair round to the back, and the woman kept her talking while the man walked through the rooms. In April 2012 CBS New York reported three men posing as construction workers at the home of an 86-year-old Brooklyn man; one said he needed to inspect a window, drew the owner into the bathroom, and the other two went into the bedroom.

Three things are true in every one of those stories. The front door was opened because the person inside had no other way to deal with the caller. The house was entered by a second person through a different door or while the first kept the owner in one room. And whatever alarm the house had was off, because somebody was home. An alarm cannot argue with a convincing stranger, but it can change all three of those facts. It can give the person inside a way to talk to the caller through a locked door. It can keep the doors that are not being used watched, so that a second person coming in the back or the basement causes a chime, a spoken warning and a text to the family. And it can keep the bedroom or the room where cash and jewelry are kept armed while the rest of the house is in use.

The advice the utilities and the police give lines up with that design. Con Edison's November 2025 warning says its employees "wear a photo ID with their name and employee number," will give a supervisor's name and a job ticket number, and that a customer with doubts should call 1-800-75-CONED to check. The city's Department of Environmental Protection has said that most water meters are read remotely from outside the home, that authorized staff who enter homes must carry and show photo identification, and that no DEP employee is ever authorized to ask for cash. The 68th Precinct Community Council told Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights residents in December 2012 not to let anyone in without an appointment and not to be fooled by "ID's, uniforms, clipboards or any other disguise." Each of those steps takes a minute or two, and a person can only take them calmly if the door is shut and they are not standing on the stoop with a stranger. A doorbell they can answer from the kitchen makes that minute possible.

The four rules we build into a Madison daytime mode

  • The front door never has to open to find out who is there. A video doorbell, wired where the old bell wire allows and battery-powered where it does not, rings a phone, a tablet or an indoor screen, with two-way talk. The person inside can say "please wait while I call the company" through a locked door.
  • Doors nobody is using stay armed. The back door, the side door off the driveway and the basement door or bulkhead stay on an instant zone while the house is in its at-home mode. Opening one gives a spoken warning inside ("back door open") and sends a text to the people on the family list, without calling a central station unless the family wants that.
  • One room stays closed. The bedroom or office where cash, jewelry and papers are kept keeps its door contact and motion sensor armed through the day, so a visitor who slips upstairs while the owner is downstairs sets off a warning.
  • Two doors at once is a signal. Where the panel and the app support it, we set a rule that if the front door has been opened and another outside door opens within a few minutes, the family hears about it straight away. That is the shape of most two-person ruses.

None of this needs exotic equipment. It is a matter of zoning and of choosing a panel and an app that let a household switch between an away mode, a night mode and a daytime at-home mode with one button, and of deciding in advance who should hear what. Most of the thinking happens at the kitchen table during the survey, not in the equipment catalogue.

A card by the keypad, and a habit to go with it

Every Madison household we set up in this way gets a small printed card fixed beside the keypad or the indoor screen. It lists three things: the Con Edison verification line exactly as the company publishes it, 1-800-75-CONED; a reminder that the city's water meters are mostly read from outside and that the city's own staff never ask for cash; and the words "No appointment, no entry. I will call and check. Please wait outside." It sounds simple, and it is. The point is that the person answering the door does not have to remember anything under pressure, and that the alarm and doorbell give them the time to read it.

We say clearly what an alarm will not do. It will not tell a real utility worker from a fake one, it will not stop a person who has already been invited in and walked to the bedroom with the owner beside them, and it is no substitute for a call to 911 when a visitor turns threatening. What it does is shut the easiest doors, make a second intruder noisy, and bring a family member into the situation within a minute or two. In the October 2018 Manhattan Beach case reported by CBS New York, the woman lived alone and the men had seen the envelope of cash. A text to a relative the moment a second door opened would not have guaranteed a different ending, but it would have meant someone else knew.

If you think a visitor fooled you: the first hour

Families sometimes call us after the fact, when a parent mentions that "the gas man" came by and something now seems missing. The order of steps matters, and none of them depends on having an alarm, though an alarm and a doorbell make several of them easier.

  • Call 911 first if anything was taken or anyone is still nearby, and tell the dispatcher the visitors claimed to be utility or city workers. Police treat these as burglaries even when the door was opened willingly.
  • Save the doorbell clip before it expires. Download it from the app or share it to a family member's phone straight away; short storage plans overwrite recordings within days.
  • Walk the house with someone else and check the rooms the visitors were never supposed to enter, especially the bedroom, closets and anywhere cash or papers were kept, before tidying anything.
  • Call the real utility on its published number, 1-800-75-CONED for Con Edison, to confirm that no worker was sent; for the city's water department, call 311.
  • Change what the visitors may have seen. Alarm codes entered in front of them, a key left on a hook, a checkbook or card on the table: change the codes the same day and call the bank if any card or document was in view.
  • Tell the neighbors on the block. The published cases show the same crews returning to nearby streets within days, and a neighbor who knows will keep the door shut.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Madison's Architecture

Madison was built mostly in a twenty-year burst after 1910, on farmland that became a grid of narrow lots. That gives the neighborhood a small number of house types repeated many times, each with its own weak doors and its own places to hide a cable. These are the ones we meet most often.

Detached brick and stucco houses with driveways

A front door up a short flight of steps, a side door off the driveway that the family actually uses, a back door to the yard, a basement with its own door, and windows on all four sides. The side and back doors are where we put the effort, and where a keypad by the side door often makes more sense than one by the front.

Two-family brick houses

Two front doors or a shared vestibule, an upstairs apartment, a basement used for storage or a third unit, and often a tenant. We split these into two partitions so each household arms its own part, and keep the shared basement and back door on both.

Limestone and brick row houses

Attached on both sides, so only the front and back matter, plus any skylight or roof hatch. The rear windows over a kitchen extension are reachable from the yard, and the cellar door under the front steps is easy to forget.

Low apartment houses inside the grid

Three- and four-story walk-ups with a front door on a buzzer. An apartment alarm here is wireless, stays inside the unit and leans on the apartment door, any fire-escape window and a doorbell camera at the apartment door where the building allows one.

Apartment buildings on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue

Larger elevator buildings with lobbies and supers. Callers who claim to be from management or the gas company reach the apartment door, not the street door, so the peephole camera and a door contact do most of the work.

The few eighteenth-century survivors

The Wyckoff-Bennett Homestead on East 22nd Street is a private Dutch Colonial farmhouse and a National Historic Landmark. We have never worked there. For any very old frame house, we fit wireless devices that leave no mark on original woodwork or glass.

Methods by house type

Detached houses. We run cable through the basement ceiling and up inside interior walls where we can, and use wireless contacts on second-floor windows and anywhere a finished room would have to be opened. The side door off the driveway gets a wired contact and, in most houses, the main keypad, because it is the door the family comes and goes through. The basement door gets a contact on both sides of the stairs if there is an outside entrance and an inside door at the top. The doorbell goes at the front even if the family rarely uses that door, because that is where strangers ring.

Two-family houses. One panel with two partitions, two keypads and two sets of codes is usually cheaper and simpler than two systems. The owner's partition covers the owner's floor and the basement; the tenant's covers the tenant's floor; the shared front vestibule, back door and basement are assigned so that either household arming "away" covers them. If the basement is rented, the rules change, because a rented unit brings its own smoke and carbon monoxide duties; we explain those when we see the house.

Row houses. The back of a row house is the quiet side. We put contacts on the yard door and every rear window up to the height of the extension roof, a glass-break sensor in the kitchen, and a motion sensor at the bottom of the stairs. The cellar door under the front steps gets a contact even if it is nailed shut, because the next owner may not know it is there.

Apartments. Nothing is screwed into the building's walls outside the unit. A wireless panel, a door contact, a motion sensor and a camera or smart viewer on the inside of the apartment door are the core, with a window contact on any window onto a fire escape. Co-op and condo boards sometimes want to see the equipment list first, and we send it.

Rules that apply to Madison alarms

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to be capable of stopping, and to stop automatically, within fifteen minutes of activation. We set every siren we install to cut off well inside that limit.
  • Installer licensing. New York State licenses security and fire alarm installers through the Department of State under Article 6-D of the General Business Law: 81 hours of qualifying education, an exam and fingerprinting. Our license number, #12000287431, is printed on every quote, and anyone who works on your system for us carries identification you can check.
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide in rented units. The city's housing department, HPD, requires smoke detectors in each unit of multiple dwellings and carbon monoxide detection in rented one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of sleeping rooms. A monitored detector on the alarm can satisfy the owner's side of that.
  • Fire alarm systems. Mixed-use buildings and larger apartment buildings can fall under fire-code requirements of their own. Those systems are a separate service; see Madison fire alarm installation.
  • Permits. We have found no city permit that a homeowner must buy for a burglar alarm, and we do not charge for one. If a co-op board, a landlord or an insurer has its own requirements, we meet them.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Madison: Questions Answered

These are the questions Madison households and shop owners raise once they see that an alarm can be useful in the daytime too. Each is answered for this neighborhood; general alarm questions are covered on the Brooklyn alarm installation hub.

When a stranger rings the bell

Two people in safety vests ask to look at the gas meter, and one keeps chatting at the front door while the other waits by the driveway. What should the alarm be doing at that moment?

It should be in its daytime at-home mode, which means the front door chimes but the side door, the back door and the basement door are armed as instant zones. If the second person tries any of them, the panel says so out loud, the family gets a text, and the person at the front door knows at once that this is not a meter reading. Better still, the front door never opened, because the caller was answered on the doorbell. The person inside can say through the speaker that they are calling the company to confirm and that the visitors should wait. A genuine worker will wait; police in the Brooklyn Paper's Dyker Heights report put it plainly: the thief will not.

My mother answers the door to anyone who knocks. How can a doorbell let her deal with a stranger without unlocking anything?

We fit a video doorbell at the front and pair it with something she will actually pick up: an indoor screen on the kitchen wall, a tablet on a stand, or her phone. When the bell rings she sees the caller and can talk to them with the door locked; if she prefers, the same ring goes to a relative's phone at the same moment, so a son or daughter can join the conversation from wherever they are. On brick fronts with no bell wire, a battery doorbell does the job, charged every couple of months. The most useful part, in our experience, is the habit it builds: the door opens only after she knows who is there and why.

Con Edison says its workers wear a photo ID with their name and employee number and can give a supervisor's name and a job ticket. How does the alarm setup help someone actually check all that before opening up?

Mostly by buying time and putting the steps in front of them. With the door shut and the caller on video, the person inside can ask the visitor to hold the ID up to the camera, write down the name and number, and call 1-800-75-CONED, the verification line Con Edison publishes, while the visitor waits outside. The doorbell keeps a recording of the whole exchange. We fix a printed card beside the keypad or indoor screen with that number and a short script, so nothing has to be remembered under pressure. For water, the city says most meters are read remotely from outside the house, so a stranger who insists on coming in to read one is already a reason to call 311 or the precinct.

In one case a visitor kept the owner busy in the basement while a second person went upstairs. Is there a sensor that would notice the second person?

Yes: a motion sensor and a door contact on the bedroom floor or the room where valuables are kept, left armed in the daytime mode. The family uses the kitchen, the living room and the basement freely; anyone climbing the stairs or opening the bedroom door while the house is in that mode sets off a spoken warning and an alert. In a two-story house we usually put the motion sensor at the top of the stairs, aimed along the landing, so it sees a person before they reach any door. Pets that roam upstairs need a pet-tolerant sensor, or the protected zone can be a single room with its door shut.

Daytime with someone at home

We are home most of the day and only ever arm the alarm at bedtime. Is a daytime setting really worth the trouble?

For many Madison households it is the most useful mode they have, because it covers the hours when they are actually at home and a stranger might come to the door. It takes one button: in the morning, instead of leaving the system off, you press "home" and the unused doors and the protected room stay watched while you move around the living areas. If that is too much to remember, the panel can switch into it on a schedule, for example at 9 a.m. on weekdays, and back to off for the evening. We set it up so that the chimes are gentle and nothing calls the police by itself unless you ask for that.

My mother hangs laundry in the yard every morning. Can the alarm tell the difference between her using the back door and someone else using it while she is at the front?

Not by recognizing people, but by sequence and time, which is usually enough. The back door can be set to chime only between, say, 8 and 11 in the morning when she uses it, and to give a full warning and alert at other times. The stronger rule is about two doors: if the front door opens and then the back or side door opens within a few minutes, the family hears about it right away, whatever the time. That combination is rare in ordinary daily life and common in a two-person ruse. Where a household's routine does not fit any schedule, a small keyfob lets her mark that she is the one going out back.

Our home attendant arrives every weekday morning and leaves after lunch. Can the alarm show us that she arrived and left on time, and keep the rest of the house as it should be after she goes?

Yes. She gets her own code or her own app login, so the app shows who disarmed the door and when, and you can see each arrival and departure without asking. When she leaves, she arms the house in the at-home mode if your parent is staying, or in away mode if nobody is. If the agency sends a substitute, a temporary code takes a minute to add from your phone and a minute to delete afterwards, so nobody ever needs a copied key.

Madison houses and apartments

Our detached house has a side door off the driveway that we use far more than the front. Where should the keypad go?

By the side door, in nearly every case, because the keypad belongs at the door you come home through. The front door still gets a contact and the video doorbell, since that is where visitors ring, and a second keypad or an indoor screen can go in the front hall if the house is large. Putting the main keypad at the front of a house whose family lives through the side door is one of the most common reasons older Madison systems were left switched off: people found themselves running down a hall to beat the entry delay.

The basement of our two-family has its own door under the front steps. If the meters are down there, how do we keep that door on the alarm?

Give it its own zone and keep it armed in every mode, including the daytime one. On the rare occasion a real meter reader or a plumber needs access, you let them in through that door yourself and the panel announces it. Inside, the door at the top of the basement stairs gets a contact too, so that someone who enters the basement and comes up into the house is caught at the second door even if the first was opened. Where the basement is a rented apartment, the outside door belongs to the tenant's partition instead, and we wire the inside door to the owner's side.

We live in an old frame house with a deep front porch. Can the porch be watched without drilling into the outside of the house?

Usually, yes. A doorbell on the door frame sees most of a porch, and a small battery camera or outdoor motion sensor can be fixed under the porch ceiling with a bracket, not into the siding. Wireless contacts go on the windows that open onto the porch, inside the frames. If the house is old enough that original glass or woodwork matters, as it does at the eighteenth-century Wyckoff-Bennett Homestead on East 22nd Street, a private house we have never worked in, every device we fit can be removed later without leaving a mark.

Our co-op apartment on Kings Highway gets regular knocks from people who say they are from management or the gas company. Can a small apartment system help without touching the hallway?

Yes. A smart viewer replaces the peephole and shows the caller on a screen inside the door and on your phone, without anything mounted in the corridor. A door contact and a motion sensor inside the apartment, a small wireless panel and cellular reporting complete it. If a visitor claims to be from management, you call the super or the managing agent before opening, with the visitor still outside. Most boards accept this kind of setup; if yours wants an equipment list or our insurance certificate first, we send both.

Shops along the edges of Madison

People sometimes walk into the back of our Kings Highway shop saying they are inspectors. Can the back of the store be armed while we are open?

Yes. The store becomes two areas: the sales floor, which is off during business hours, and the back office, stockroom and rear door, which stay armed. The door from the floor to the back gets a chime and a spoken warning, the office has its own motion sensor, and the safe can have a shock sensor of its own. A real inspector will wait while you check with the agency they name; anyone who tries to slip past the counter sets off the warning at the back door to the office. When you close, one code arms everything together.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Madison

We could not open Google's AI Overview or similar answer boxes from the environment this page was written in, so we quote none of them. What follows are claims that general online answers about home alarms commonly make, set against what we see in a residential neighborhood like Madison. Lead-generation sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to repeat the same points.

"An alarm only matters when nobody is home"

This is the most common framing of home alarms: arm it when you leave, arm it at night, and switch it off the rest of the time. It treats burglary as something that happens to empty houses.

The deception cases in southern Brooklyn happened to people who were home. The 89-year-old near Ocean Parkway, the 90-year-old on 59th Street, the 86-year-old drawn into his own bathroom and the 88-year-old in Manhattan Beach were all at home in daylight. A system that is off whenever someone is in the house was no help to any of them.

In Madison we treat the daytime at-home mode as standard equipment, not an extra. It costs nothing more to program, and for a household where someone is home most afternoons it is the mode that will be in use most often.

"A video doorbell is just for catching package thieves"

Doorbell cameras are usually sold on recordings: who took the box, who rang at 2 a.m. The emphasis is on evidence after the fact.

For the households on this page, the doorbell's main job is live, not recorded. It lets a person talk to a caller through a locked door, ask for an ID to be held up to the lens and say "please wait while I call," which is exactly the step the utilities and the 68th Precinct Community Council told people to take.

So we place the doorbell for the conversation as much as the picture: at a height where a face is in frame, with a speaker the person inside can hear clearly, and paired with a screen or phone that the person most likely to answer actually uses.

"Wireless kits are as good as professional installs for any house"

General advice often says modern wireless kits make professional installation unnecessary. For a small apartment that can be close to true.

Madison's houses have basements with outside doors, side doors off driveways, cellar doors under front steps and two households under one roof. A kit installed out of the box usually covers the front door, a motion sensor in the living room and perhaps a back door, and leaves the basement and the second partition alone.

The equipment may be similar; the difference is the survey. We count every door, including the one behind the boiler, and decide for each one what it should do at night, when the house is empty, and when someone is home.

"Monitoring means the police come quickly"

Monitoring is often described as if a signal automatically brings officers to the door within minutes.

A central station verifies alarms before requesting a dispatch, by calling the people on the list, and response times depend on the precinct's workload. In a daytime ruse, where the visitors may be gone in five minutes, the fastest useful response is usually a call from a relative who saw the text and phoned the house.

That is why we let families decide which alerts go to a central station and which go straight to their own phones. A back door opening at 2 p.m. while mother is home should reach her daughter first; a break-in at 3 a.m. while the house is empty should reach the central station.

"Alarm prices are the same everywhere"

National averages for installed alarms are often presented as if they apply to any house in any city.

Our Brooklyn list, which applies to Madison at the base rate, runs from $600 for a basic system to $2,000–$6,000 for a custom hardwired house, with panel replacements on existing wiring at $800–$1,500. What moves a Madison quote is the number of doors and reachable windows, whether old cable is usable, and whether the house is a two-family.

We publish the list and write the figure down after one visit, so nobody has to rely on a national average for a detached brick house off Avenue S.

"You need a permit before an alarm can be installed in New York City"

Some sites state that a city alarm permit or registration fee applies to every installation.

We have found no primary source for a city permit that a homeowner must buy for a burglar alarm. What does apply is the fifteen-minute siren limit in Administrative Code § 24-238, the state license required of installers, and HPD's detector rules for rented units.

If we ever find a permit rule that applies to your property, we will tell you before the work starts and include it in the written price. We will not add a fee we cannot point to.

"Older people find alarms too complicated, so a simple panic button is enough"

Advice for older homeowners often reduces security to a single pendant or button.

A panic button is useful, and we fit them, but it depends on the person realizing they are in trouble and pressing it. In the published deception cases, the owners did not know anything was wrong until the visitors had gone. A door that reports itself, a protected bedroom and a relative who gets the text do not depend on anybody noticing in time.

The answer is not more buttons but less to remember: one press for "home," one for "away," a doorbell that rings the screen they use, and a family member on the other end of the alerts.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

An apartment on Ocean Avenue and a two-family off Avenue T go through the same seven stages. What makes a Madison job different is that the survey is done with the person who is home in the daytime, and that the plan is written for three situations rather than two: away, asleep, and at home with the door to answer.

1. Talk first, about the daytime

A phone call about who lives in the house, who is home on weekday afternoons, which doors are used, where the meters are and who should get alerts, followed by three photos of the front, the side or back door and the view from inside the front door.

2. Survey with the person who is home

A technician visits on a weekday and walks the house with the person who usually answers the door, starting at the front door and following every route to the side, back and basement doors and the bedroom floor.

3. A one-page plan in plain words

Before equipment is chosen, the technician writes down what each door and room should do when the house is empty, at night, and in the daytime at-home mode, and who hears which alert.

4. Written price the same day

Doors, windows, the doorbell, partitions and any old cable worth keeping are counted and priced from the Brooklyn list in writing, usually before he leaves.

5. Install the doors, rooms and doorbell

Contacts go on every outside door and reachable window, motion sensors on the stairs and the protected room, the doorbell at the front, and the voice sounder where it can be heard from the kitchen and living room.

6. Program three modes and the alert list

Away, night and daytime at-home modes are set up and tested door by door, the two-door rule and any reminders are added, and each alert is sent to the phones that will receive it while the family watches.

7. Hand over, then come back

Each person gets a code or the app, the card by the keypad is fixed in place, and a return visit a few weeks later adjusts chimes, schedules and sensor aim after the household has lived with the system.

Each spring and fall: a fifteen-minute check, done together

Put the system in test mode, then open the side door, the back door and the basement door one at a time while the person who is usually home listens for the spoken name of each. Ring the doorbell and make sure it reaches the screen or phone they use. Check that the family members on the alert list still have the right phone numbers. Then delete the codes of anyone who no longer needs one: an attendant who has moved on, a contractor who has finished, a relative who stayed for the holidays.

FAQ

Madison Alarm Installation FAQ

What would an alarm for a Madison detached house with a side driveway door and a basement cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

Most detached houses here land at or above the complete-home figure, which starts at $2,500, because they have front, side, back and basement doors and windows on all four sides. A smaller house where walls should not be opened can fit the wireless range of $600 to $1,200, and a house with good wiring from an old system can start with a panel replacement at $800 to $1,500. The video doorbell and any second partition appear as their own lines, and the figure is written down after one visit with sales tax added.

Will a video doorbell work on a brick house where the old bell wire is long gone?
+

Yes. Where there is no bell wire and no way to run one without drilling the brick face, we fit a battery doorbell on the door frame and a small chime inside. It needs charging every couple of months, more often in a cold winter. Where a transformer and wire are still in the wall, a wired doorbell is better because it never needs charging and can record continuously.

A caller says the gas company picked up a carbon monoxide or gas reading on our block and needs to check our boiler. Does a monitored CO detector change what we should do?
+

It gives you a fact to check the story against. If our monitored detector in the basement shows no carbon monoxide, the panel has not reported anything, and nobody has called you from the utility's published number, the caller's claim deserves suspicion. Do not let anyone in on the strength of a phone call. If you smell gas or a detector sounds, leave the house and call 911, and call Con Edison yourself on 1-800-75-CONED rather than any number the caller gives you.

Can the doorbell recordings be given to the police if someone does trick their way in?
+

Yes. Doorbell clips are stored in the app and can be downloaded or shared by the account holder. We show the family how to save a clip and how long the plan keeps recordings before they are overwritten, because a deception burglary is often noticed hours or days later, when the clip may already be close to expiry on a short plan.

Can a light come on in the front hall when the doorbell rings after dark, so we can see the caller better?
+

Yes. A smart switch or a plug-in module on the hall or porch light can be tied to the doorbell, so the light comes on for a few minutes whenever the bell is pressed after sunset. The doorbell camera sees a face far better in light than by infrared alone, and the person inside can see through the door glass without opening up. The same light can also come on when the alarm itself is triggered at night.

My father has a pacemaker and worries about radio devices. Are wireless alarm sensors a concern?
+

Alarm sensors transmit short, low-power bursts only when something changes, and we keep the panel and any repeater away from the chair or bed where he spends most of his time. Because people's medical devices and advice differ, we suggest he asks his cardiologist, and if he would rather avoid wireless devices near him, the rooms he uses can be wired instead.

Can we try the daytime mode for a few weeks before deciding whether to pay for monitoring?
+

Yes. Every system we install works without a central station: alerts go to the phones on your list, and the voice sounder and sirens work in the house. Many families use it that way for a month to see which alerts they actually get and who answers them, then decide. Central station monitoring can be added at any point, month to month, from $19.99 a month through our partners.

Can a home attendant or a neighbor let your technician in if we cannot be there all day?
+

Yes, provided the owner or the adult child who booked the job is reachable by phone for decisions and is there, in person or on a video call, for the handover at the end. We confirm the technician's name with you in advance so the person opening the door can check it, and we never ask the attendant or neighbor to choose codes or sign anything.

Can the system send a text if the daytime mode is never switched on by a certain hour?
+

Yes. A reminder can be set so that if the house is still fully off at, say, 10 a.m. on a weekday, a family member gets a text and can switch the at-home mode on from the app. Some households prefer the panel to switch into the daytime mode on its own at a set time, which avoids the reminder altogether.

Can the app tell us that the side door was left unlocked, and not only that it is shut?
+

Not from an alarm contact alone, which only knows whether the door is open or closed. A smart deadbolt reports whether it is locked, and when it joins the alarm app you can see both, lock it from your phone, and have the night mode refuse to arm until it is locked. That is the most common reason Madison families add a code lock on the side door at the same visit.
