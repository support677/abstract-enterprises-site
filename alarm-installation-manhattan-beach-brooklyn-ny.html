Manhattan Beach is the small peninsula at the bottom of Brooklyn where almost every house stands on its own lot. No party walls, no neighbors on either side sharing a roofline, and on many blocks a house three times the size of the cottage it replaced. That changes how a burglar alarm has to be planned. A row house has a front and a back; a detached Manhattan Beach house has four sides, a passage down at least one of them, often a basement door below the level of the yard, and room after room far from wherever the panel sits. This page explains how we plan an alarm for that kind of house, side by side and floor by floor, and how we make sure a call for help reaches the right place on a peninsula with two Ocean Avenues and a street that changed its name.

The Neighborhood

Why Manhattan Beach Properties Need Alarm Installation

Manhattan Beach began as a resort. Wikipedia’s history says the railroad financier Austin Corbin, later president of the Long Island Rail Road, opened the Manhattan Beach Hotel in 1877 and the Oriental Hotel soon after, and that once the hotel trade faded the land was turned into houses and a public park. CityNeighborhoods.nyc adds that the old hotels were demolished after the First World War and the remaining land subdivided for homes. What was left behind is a peninsula bounded by the Atlantic Ocean to the south and east, Sheepshead Bay to the north and Brighton Beach to the west, with Oriental Boulevard and Shore Boulevard running its length and a short grid of cross streets in between. The neighborhood sits in Community District 15, its ZIP code is 11235, and the 2020 census counted 4,684 people, according to Wikipedia. That is a small population for a whole Brooklyn neighborhood, and it tells you something before you see a single house: the lots are big and the houses stand apart.

The cross streets are the first thing visitors notice. Wikipedia lists them running alphabetically from west to east and named after places in England: Amherst, Beaumont, Coleridge, Dover, Exeter, Falmouth, Girard, Hastings, Irwin, Jaffray, Kensington, Langham, Mackenzie, Norfolk, Oxford and Pembroke. A short two-way stretch of Ocean Avenue interrupts the sequence between Exeter and Falmouth. On old maps there were also a Quentin and a Reynolds, which no longer exist. At the western edge, the street that divides Manhattan Beach from Brighton Beach was Corbin Place until it was renamed M. Corbin Place for Margaret Corbin, a Revolutionary War figure; Wikipedia gives the year as 2007 while citing a 2017 Brooklyn Paper article, and we note that conflict rather than pick one. These details sound like trivia. For an alarm they are not, because a monitoring operator reading an address to a dispatcher needs the right Ocean Avenue and the right Corbin Place, and we come back to that further down.

The houses themselves are the main reason an alarm here is planned differently. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes Manhattan Beach as mostly detached brick and stucco houses, often in Mediterranean or Colonial Revival styles, set back behind gardens, with rows of single-family houses and low-rise apartment buildings put up in the 1920s to the 1940s and newer Mediterranean or modern houses replacing some older cottages. Reporting over the past two decades fills in the rest. A 2006 Real Deal article, which we could read only as a search excerpt, said the city had halted construction of nearly 40 so-called McMansions out of scale with the neighborhood, leaving residents angry about large half-finished houses. A 2016 Brooklyn Eagle column, again read as a search excerpt, described houses that had been enlarged and new houses built in place of smaller tear-downs. Forgotten New York’s 2014 piece on the Esplanade mentions a 17,000-square-foot house at the dead end of West End Avenue. Not every house is like that one, but many Manhattan Beach houses are far larger, longer and more complicated than anything on a typical Brooklyn block.

A large detached house is harder to protect than a row house of the same price for three plain reasons. First, it has more sides that someone can approach without being seen: a narrow side passage, a back garden screened by hedges, a basement stairwell below the level of the lawn. Second, it has more openings, and the openings are spread out, so a single motion detector in a central hall sees very little of the house. Third, it is long. Wireless sensors that work perfectly in a twenty-foot-wide row house can fall silent at the far end of a house whose rooms run eighty feet back from the street through masonry walls and concrete floors. None of these problems is solved by buying a bigger kit. They are solved by surveying each side of the house on its own terms and planning how every sensor will talk to the panel.

Crime here is low by city standards, and we say so plainly. Wikipedia reports that the 61st Precinct, which patrols Manhattan Beach from its station house at 2575 Coney Island Avenue, ranked fifth safest of 69 patrol areas for per-capita crime in 2010, and that crime in all categories fell 88.2 percent between 1990 and 2018. Those precinct figures cover Sheepshead Bay, Gravesend and Homecrest as well, so they are context, not a Manhattan Beach statistic. The neighborhood-level reporting we found is older and more specific. In August 2011 the Brooklyn Paper quoted NYPD figures showing burglaries in Manhattan Beach rising from three in 2010 to nine that year, with residents saying the only real patrol was a private one. In December 2011 the same paper’s police blotter described a 52-year-old man arrested after witnesses saw him trying to force open the basement door of a house on Oriental Boulevard near Dover Street at about 12:35 in the morning. And after Hurricane Sandy, in November 2012, a resident told the paper his house had been broken into the night before, while the power was still out a week after the storm. A low-crime neighborhood of big, spread-out houses is precisely the kind of place where a single quiet entry at the side or below grade can go unnoticed for hours. That is the problem an alarm here is designed to solve.

Alarm Installation Services in Manhattan Beach

Each of these is a line of work we carry out on the peninsula. What ties them together is the survey: four sides, every floor, the basement, and a signal check from the panel position to the farthest sensor before anything is fixed to a wall.

🏠

Whole-house alarms for detached houses

Contacts on every reachable door and window, glass-break where the glass is large, motion detectors placed by room shape rather than one per floor, a siren inside and a keypad by the door you actually use. Planned side by side for houses on the lettered streets.

↔️

Side-passage and gate protection

The narrow gap between your house and the next is where nobody sees anyone. We put the openings that face it on their own zone, and where a gate closes it off at the front, a gate contact that chimes in the day and alarms at night.

⬇️

Basement doors and stairwells

Doors at the bottom of outdoor basement steps get a contact and, where the door is solid, a shock sensor that reacts to forcing. The stairwell itself can be watched by an outdoor detector aimed down into it, armed only at night.

📶

Range planning for big footprints

Before we promise wireless, we measure it. Where the far end of the house is out of reliable range, we add a hardwired expander or repeater, or run a short cable, so the panel hears every sensor every time, not most of the time.

📐

Alarms designed into new houses

For a house being built or rebuilt, we mark sensor, keypad and panel positions on the drawings, give the electrician a cable schedule, and come back to trim out and program once the finishes are in.

🛏️

Separate areas inside one house

A guest suite over the garage, a grandparent’s floor or a rented basement apartment can be its own area on the same panel, with its own codes, so one household can arm while the other comes and goes.

🏢

Apartment and condo alarms

For the low-rise apartment buildings and condos on the peninsula: a contact on the apartment door, protection on terrace and ground-floor windows, a motion detector, and nothing that touches the building’s own systems.

🕍

Congregation, school and community buildings

Synagogues, kollels and community centers keep irregular hours. We split such buildings into areas, office, library, hall, so each can be armed when it closes, with an event record that shows exactly which door or window opened.

App control and optional monitoring

Every panel we install runs from a phone. Central station monitoring is an option on top, month to month, with a call list and address notes written for your house, not copied from a form.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Manhattan Beach’s Architecture

The building types below are the ones the sources describe and the ones we plan for. They differ less in style than in how many sides are exposed, what the walls are made of and how far the farthest room is from the panel.

Detached brick houses set behind gardens

The neighborhood’s standard. Brick bearing walls, plaster inside, a front garden and a back garden, a driveway down one side and often a narrow passage down the other. Wired contacts are possible on the ground floor where a basement ceiling lets cable through; upper floors usually go wireless.

Stucco Mediterranean houses

Stucco over masonry, arched openings, tile or flat roofs, sometimes a second-floor balcony over the entrance. Nothing is drilled through the stucco; cable comes up from the basement or down from the attic, and every exterior device is mounted on trim, not on the render.

Colonial Revival houses

Symmetrical fronts, double-hung windows, a center hall and stairs. The center hall is the natural place for the keypad and one motion detector, but it sees little of the side rooms, so the side rooms get their own coverage.

Enlarged houses

An original house with additions, a second story put on, a rear extension pushed back into the garden. The joins matter: an old exterior door that now opens between two rooms, a basement under the old part but not the new, a wireless signal that has to pass through what used to be an outside wall.

New houses on the sites of tear-downs

Large footprints, poured concrete in places, long glass walls to the garden, several floors and sometimes an elevator. The best time to plan the alarm is before the walls close; the second best is a hybrid system with a wired backbone and wireless at the edges.

1920s–1940s rows and low-rise apartment buildings

Smaller, attached or semi-attached, with apartments in some. These are planned more like row houses elsewhere in Brooklyn: front and back first, a common door shared by several households, and an apartment alarm that stays inside the unit.

How the materials change the method

Brick and stucco houses hide cable well once it is inside the wall, but getting it inside is the work. In a house with an unfinished basement we drill up through the basement ceiling into the hollow behind the first-floor trim, which lets us put recessed or concealed wired contacts on most ground-floor doors and windows. Above the first floor, plaster on masonry or on metal lath makes fishing slow and messy, so we usually change to wireless there unless the walls are open for renovation. In new construction with concrete floors, the cable must go in conduit before the pour or along the routes the electrician is already using; drilling a slab afterwards is expensive and risky, which is the main reason we ask to see drawings early.

Stucco gets one rule: nothing goes through it. A hole drilled through stucco for a siren or a contact cable lets water in behind the finish, and on a house exposed to wind off the ocean that water does damage for years before it shows. Exterior devices go on wood or metal trim, inside a soffit, or not at all. Most of the time an outdoor siren is unnecessary anyway; an interior siren in a hall carries well, and the monitoring station or your phone is what brings help.

Four Sides, Not Two

Planning a Manhattan Beach Alarm One Elevation at a Time

On a row-house block an alarm survey runs front to back. On the lettered streets it runs around the house. We draw the house as four elevations, street side, back garden side and the two flanks, and treat each as its own question: who can see this side, how would someone reach it, what openings face it, and what is between it and the neighbor. Only then do we add the floors and the basement. The result is a plan in which every sensor has a reason that can be written next to it on the quote, and in which the sides that nobody overlooks carry more protection than the side that faces the street.

How we read a detached house on the lettered streets. The narrow side and the back carry more sensors than the front because nobody overlooks them.

The street side

The front of a Manhattan Beach house usually sits back behind a garden, as CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes, with a walk or a short flight of steps to the door. The front door gets a contact and, because it is the door most families use less than the side or garage door, a normal entry delay. Front windows get contacts on the ground floor. Large front glass, a picture window or glass beside the door, gets an acoustic glass-break detector inside the room. We rarely put anything outdoors on the street side; the street is the one direction from which someone acting oddly is likely to be seen.

The driveway side

Most houses have a driveway down one side, often ending at a garage or a parking pad. This side is semi-public: the family uses it every day, deliveries come up it, and anyone walking up a driveway with a purpose looks normal. The side door or garage-to-house door here is usually the door the household really uses, so it gets the keypad, the entry delay and, on many plans, a video doorbell. Windows along the driveway get contacts. An outdoor motion detector over a driveway is usually a mistake, because every car and every delivery trips it; where owners want warning of someone in the driveway at night, we prefer a camera alert with a schedule to an alarm zone.

The narrow side

The other side is often a passage a few feet wide between your wall and the neighbor’s, sometimes gated at the front, sometimes open. It is the least visible place on the property: the neighbor’s house blocks the view from next door, the gate or a hedge blocks the view from the street, and once someone is in it they can try every window along that wall unseen. On our plans this side gets every reachable window on a contact, a separate zone so an alert names it, and, where the passage is gated, a gate contact that chimes during the day and becomes an instant alarm at night. A narrow passage is also one of the few places where an outdoor detector works well, because nothing should be moving in it after dark except a cat, and a curtain-pattern detector aimed along the wall can be set to ignore small animals.

The back garden

Back gardens here are larger than most in Brooklyn and often screened. Rear doors and garden-level glass get contacts and glass-break detectors, and rooms that open to the garden get motion coverage inside. We are cautious with outdoor sensors in a large garden, because wind, birds, garden lights and animals cause false alarms; where a garden genuinely needs early warning, we put a beam across a single approach or a camera with person detection, not a ring of motion detectors around the lawn.

Some gardens on the ocean side run down to where the old Esplanade was. Forgotten New York reported in 2014 that the eight-block waterfront walkway between Corbin Place and Ocean Avenue, laid out when the shoreline was straightened in the 1870s, was fenced off by one owner in 1987, that a court sided with him, that other owners have since claimed the strip, and that only a small section near the southwest corner of Manhattan Beach Park remains public, with signs elsewhere warning of guard dogs and security cameras. We take no view on that dispute. What it means for an alarm is that a garden on the water side may have no house behind it at all, so the rear elevation is treated with the same seriousness as the narrow side.

Below Grade

Basement Doors, Stairwells and the Hour Nobody Is Looking

The one Manhattan Beach break-in attempt we found described in detail involved a basement door. The Brooklyn Paper’s 61st Precinct blotter of December 21, 2011, reported that on December 14, at about 12:35 a.m., witnesses saw a man trying to force open the basement door of a home on Oriental Boulevard near Dover Street, and that police arrested a 52-year-old man. No entry was reported, and the item does not mention an alarm. We cite it not as a trend but because it describes exactly the opening we see left out of alarm plans most often: a door at the bottom of a flight of outdoor steps, below the level of the garden, invisible from the street and from the neighbors, used a few times a year.

A basement door like that needs three things on our plans. A contact, so the panel knows when it opens. A shock or vibration sensor on the door or frame, so the panel knows when it is being forced, before it opens; on a solid door that may be the only warning. And a decision about the stairwell: if the steps are inside a gate or along the narrow side, an outdoor detector aimed down into the stairwell, armed only at night, tells you someone is there before they touch the door. We also look at the basement windows, which in many houses are at or just above ground level along the sides and the back, and put each one on a contact or cover the basement with motion and glass-break if there are too many.

The basement is also where most houses keep the panel, the boiler and the electrical service, and that is a reason to move the panel, not to leave it there. A burglar who comes in through a basement door should not land beside the alarm’s brain with thirty seconds to find it. We put the panel and its battery in a closet on a main floor, keep only sensors and perhaps a wired expander in the basement, and give the basement door no entry delay at all unless the household genuinely uses it every day.

Big Footprints

When the House Is Longer Than the Signal: Range, Repeaters and Hybrid Wiring

Kit alarms are designed around an average house, and an average house is not a rebuilt Manhattan Beach house with rooms running far back from the street, a basement under part of it, two or three upper floors and an addition joined on through what used to be an exterior wall. In houses like that the most common failure we see is not a broken sensor; it is a sensor that reaches the panel on a good day and not on a bad one. The panel reports a supervision fault, the owner bypasses the zone to arm, and a month later that window is simply not protected.

We plan range the way an electrician plans circuits: from the panel outward, with a measured margin at every device. The first decision is where the panel goes, and in a long house the answer is near the middle, not by the front door; the keypads go by the doors, connected by cable or wirelessly, but the receiver should sit where its distance to the farthest sensor is shortest. The second decision is whether to add a wired expander or a wireless repeater at the far end. A wired expander is a small board that takes wireless signals from nearby sensors and passes them to the panel over a cable, which removes range from the equation entirely; a repeater does the same over the air. The third decision is which openings should simply be wired, usually the ground-floor doors and windows reachable from the basement ceiling.

Rooms that one detector cannot see

Big houses also defeat the one-motion-detector-per-floor habit. A detector in a central hall sees the hall. It does not see a family room at the back, a wing over the garage or a side sunroom. We place motion detectors by asking which rooms a person entering from each side would have to cross, and we prefer two well-placed detectors to four poorly placed ones. Double-height entrance halls, open stairs and long glass walls each get attention: detectors are mounted so they look across the room, not at the glass, where sun off the water or headlights could reach them.

New and Rebuilt Houses

Building or Rebuilding on the Peninsula: Put the Alarm on the Drawings

Manhattan Beach has seen a great deal of building. The 2006 Real Deal excerpt describes the city halting nearly 40 oversized houses and residents unhappy about half-finished ones; the 2016 Brooklyn Eagle excerpt describes enlarged houses and new houses on the sites of tear-downs; CityNeighborhoods.nyc says newer homes have replaced some older cottages while strict zoning has kept the low-rise scale. Whatever one thinks of that history, it means many owners here are either planning a rebuild or living in a house that was rebuilt without much thought for the alarm.

If you are building, the alarm belongs on the drawings, not in the last week before move-in. We need four things from the plans: where each exterior door and window is, where the panel and keypads can go, which walls and floors are concrete, and where the electrician’s runs already go. From that we produce a cable schedule, a short list of cable runs from the panel location to each wired device and to a wired expander for each wing, and mark recessed contact positions on the window and door schedule so the frames can be bored before they are installed. The electrician pulls the cable during rough-in, or we do; we come back after the finishes to install devices and program.

Getting Help to the Right Door

Two Ocean Avenues, a Renamed Corbin Place and a Peninsula Across the Bay

An alarm is only as good as the response it produces, and response starts with an address. Manhattan Beach has two features that can confuse it. Wikipedia’s article on Ocean Avenue notes that a short stretch of the avenue lies on the Manhattan Beach peninsula, separate from the main route, and that a footbridge over Sheepshead Bay links the two sections; the main avenue begins at Emmons Avenue on the Sheepshead Bay side and runs north to Prospect Park. A house on the peninsula stretch of Ocean Avenue and a house on the mainland stretch can be a short walk apart over the footbridge and a long drive apart by road. And the western edge street, Corbin Place, became M. Corbin Place, so older records and newer maps may not agree.

When we set up central station monitoring, we write the address notes ourselves. For a house on the peninsula stretch of Ocean Avenue the notes say so in words, name the nearest lettered cross streets, Exeter and Falmouth, and say that the house is reached by road from Oriental Boulevard or Shore Boulevard, not across the footbridge. For a house on M. Corbin Place the notes carry both names. For any house the notes say which door is the entrance responders should use, whether there is a gate on the driveway or the side passage, and where a key holder can be reached. It is a small thing that takes ten minutes at handover, and it is the kind of thing nobody thinks about until a dispatcher is reading a confused address over the radio.

When Everyone Is Stretched

What the Week After Sandy Taught About Alarms on This Peninsula

The Brooklyn Paper reported on November 7, 2012, that Manhattan Beach residents had been without power for more than a week after Hurricane Sandy, that a resident, Eugene Fershteyn, said his home had been broken into the night before, and that when residents confronted Mayor Bloomberg at P.S. 195 on Irwin Street he said the NYPD was stretched to the limit. City Limits reported the following month that the surge came from both the bay and the ocean and that the Manhattan Beach Community Group’s president estimated his own damage at no less than $100,000.

Two lessons follow for an alarm, and neither is about flood sensors. The first is that a system which depends on house power and a home internet connection stops working exactly when the neighborhood empties out. Every panel we install has a backup battery and reports over cellular, and for owners who want more, the panel can be connected to a standby generator’s circuit or given a larger battery; what matters is that you know in advance roughly how long it will last and that the panel tells you when power fails. The second lesson is that in a citywide emergency an alarm signal is one call among thousands. An alarm that can show a camera clip with the alert, or a call list with a neighbor who is actually on the block, turns a vague burglary signal into something a busy dispatcher can act on, and lets you decide what to do yourself if help is slow.

The Private Patrol

A Neighborhood That Has Paid for Its Own Patrol Car

Manhattan Beach has a long history of residents paying for their own security. The Brooklyn Paper reported in January 2010 that the Beachside Neighborhood Patrol, founded in 1970, was funded by an annual fee of about $400 paid by some residents, contracted with Bay Ridge Security, and ran one car seven days a week for about eighteen hours a day. In June 2011 the paper said the patrol covered about 200 households, that its unarmed guards could not make arrests but called police and sometimes held a suspect until officers came, and that the Manhattan Beach Community Group was exploring a second patrol amid a dispute with the patrol’s leadership. In August 2011 residents told the paper that the private patrol was the only real patrol in the neighborhood. We found nothing more recent and do not know whether the patrol operates today, how it is funded now, or whether the second patrol was ever formed.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Manhattan Beach: Questions Answered

These are the questions that come from the shape of Manhattan Beach houses and the way the peninsula is laid out. General questions about prices and coverage are in the FAQ near the end of the page.

Big footprints and new construction

Our rebuilt house is roughly three times the footprint of the cottage it replaced, with concrete between some floors. Will sensors at the far end of the house reach a single wireless receiver?

Possibly, but we will not guess. Concrete, brick, stucco on metal lath and foil-faced insulation each absorb part of a wireless signal, and in a long house the losses add up. At the survey we hold a test sensor at each planned position and read the signal strength at the proposed panel location. Where the margin is comfortable, wireless is fine. Where it is thin, we add a wired expander or repeater in that part of the house, connected back to the panel, or wire the farthest openings directly. The panel also goes near the middle of the house rather than by the front door, which shortens the longest path.

We are replacing an old cottage with a new house built from the ground up. Can the alarm be designed into the architect’s drawings before construction starts?

Yes, and it is the cheapest time to do it. Send the floor plans and the window and door schedule. We mark the panel, keypads, motion detectors and recessed contact positions, and produce a cable schedule for the electrician: which runs go from the panel location to which devices, and where a conduit should cross a concrete floor before it is poured. Recessed contacts can be bored into frames before the windows go in. After the finishes we install the devices and program the system, so nothing is surface-mounted on new trim.

Our house has a guest suite over the garage with its own outside stair. Can that suite be a separate area, so visiting family can come and go without our codes?

Yes. Most panels we install support several areas, sometimes called partitions. The suite’s door, windows and a motion detector become one area with its own keypad or fob and its own codes, which can be set to expire when the visit ends. The main house stays a separate area, armed or disarmed independently. The stair door can be set to report even when the suite is disarmed, as a chime, so you know when it is used. If the suite is ever let to a tenant, the same split keeps the two households’ codes and histories apart.

Sides, passages and below grade

One side of our house is the driveway and the other is a passage barely a yard wide between our wall and the neighbor’s. Which side should carry the heavier protection?

Usually the narrow passage. The driveway is used and watched every day; someone in it looks like a visitor and is often seen. The passage is hidden from the street, from your neighbor and often from your own windows. We put every reachable window along the passage on a contact, give that side its own zone so an alert names it, add a gate contact if there is a gate at the front, and consider a curtain-pattern motion detector aimed along the passage at night. The driveway side gets contacts on its openings, the keypad by the door you use and perhaps a camera, but rarely an outdoor alarm zone.

Our basement steps go down from the side yard to a door nobody can see from the street. Should the stairwell itself be detected, before anyone reaches the door?

Often, yes. A contact tells you the door has opened, and a shock sensor tells you it is being forced; an outdoor detector aimed down into the stairwell tells you someone is on the steps, which is earlier still. We arm that detector only at night and with the house set to away or night mode, set it to ignore small animals, and keep it pointed down so it does not see the sidewalk or the neighbor’s yard. A 2011 Brooklyn Paper blotter item described witnesses seeing a man try to force a basement door on Oriental Boulevard near Dover Street just after midnight; in an empty house, that kind of attempt is exactly what a stairwell detector is for.

Addresses, records and response

Our house is on the short stretch of Ocean Avenue on the peninsula, cut off from the rest of Ocean Avenue by the bay. Could an alarm response be sent to the wrong side of the water?

It is a real risk with any address that has a twin across a waterway, so we do not leave it to chance. In the monitoring account we write that the house is on the Manhattan Beach section of Ocean Avenue, between Exeter and Falmouth Streets, reached by road from Oriental Boulevard or Shore Boulevard, not across the footbridge from Emmons Avenue. We do the same for houses on M. Corbin Place, recording the old name Corbin Place as well. We also ask you to give the address the same way when you speak to the operator during a test.

A teenager once got into a Manhattan Beach synagogue at night and police were not sure how. If someone gets in and nobody knows the route, can the alarm’s record show which opening was used?

Yes, if each opening is its own zone. The event history logs every door, window and detector with a time stamp, so the first sensor to report is usually the way in, and the order of the motion detectors after it shows the route through the building. That is why we name zones by place, side door, library window, basement stair, rather than lumping several openings on one loop, as older systems often did. The record can be exported for police or an insurer. In the 2010 case the Brooklyn Paper described, the building was discovered the next morning; a zoned alarm would have reported the entry within seconds.

After Sandy, a resident said his house was broken into while the power was still out a week later, and the mayor said the police were stretched. When response is slow everywhere, what should our alarm plan do differently?

Give the alert something a busy dispatcher, or you, can act on. A camera clip attached to the alarm lets the monitoring station tell police a person is inside, not just that a sensor tripped. A call list with a neighbor who is actually on the peninsula, or a private patrol if your street has one, gives someone local the chance to look. And a cellular panel with a battery sized for a long outage keeps reporting when house power and the internet are gone. We set these up as a plan written for your house, not as optional extras ticked on a form.

Several of us on the same lettered street want our alarms to warn each other, so whoever is home can look out. Can separate houses share alerts?

They can share notifications, though not control. Each house keeps its own panel, codes and monitoring; any owner can add a neighbor’s phone as a notification contact for chosen events, a perimeter alarm or a panic button, without giving them a code. On detached streets, where houses are farther apart than in a row and a siren may not carry, that is often the quickest way for a neighbor to know something is wrong. We help each owner choose which events to share, so neighbors are not woken by every door chime.

Equipment

Types of Alarm Systems We Install in Manhattan Beach

The same families of equipment go into every alarm. What changes in a big detached house is how many of each, where they go and how they reach the panel.

Cellular panels with battery backup

The panel reports over the cellular network, not a landline or your internet alone, and keeps running on its battery when the power fails. For long houses we place it near the middle of the plan.

Wired expanders and wireless repeaters

Small boards that let the far end of a big house talk reliably to the panel, either over a cable back to it or by relaying the radio signal. The difference between a system that works and one that mostly works.

Recessed and surface contacts

Recessed wired contacts bored into door and window frames where a basement ceiling or open walls let cable through; slim wireless contacts on upper floors and on stucco houses where nothing may be drilled through the finish.

Shock and vibration sensors

For solid basement doors, garage doors and anywhere forcing is the likely attack. They report the blows before the door opens.

Indoor motion detectors

Placed by room shape and route, looking across rooms rather than at glass. Pet-tolerant models where there is a dog, set for its weight.

Acoustic glass-break detectors

One detector can cover several large panes in a room, useful for long garden-side glass walls and picture windows.

Outdoor curtain detectors and beams

Used sparingly: down a narrow side passage, into a basement stairwell, across a single approach. Armed only at night, set to ignore small animals.

Keypads, fobs and the app

A keypad at the door you use, a second one near the bedrooms in a large house, fobs on car keys, and the app for everything else, including checking which zone tripped.

Smoke, heat, carbon monoxide and water sensors

Monitored life-safety sensors can share the panel. Water sensors go by the boiler and the lowest point of the basement.

National kits and national brands, measured against a detached house

ADT, Ring and SimpliSafe each sell systems that work well in the house they were designed around, and we install and service all of them. What none of them can do from a box is survey your four sides, measure the signal from your panel position to the far end of a rebuilt house, decide which basement door needs a shock sensor, or write address notes that tell a dispatcher which Ocean Avenue you live on. A self-installed kit tends to put its few contacts on the front door and the most obvious windows, which are the openings the street already watches, and a national dealer’s technician is usually given a fixed time per job. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Manhattan Beach

From the environment this page was built in we could not open Google’s AI Overview, People Also Ask or related searches, so we are not quoting any of them. The statements below are the kind of general advice that circulates in AI answers and home-security articles; we test each against what a Manhattan Beach house actually needs.

“A starter kit with a few sensors covers a typical home”

A starter kit is sized for a small house or an apartment: a handful of contacts, one motion detector, a base station. A detached house on the lettered streets has four exposed sides, often a basement door, and frequently dozens of windows. The kit is not wrong; it is simply short.

The bigger issue is placement. The few contacts in a kit almost always go on the front door and the windows beside it, because that is where people think of protecting first. In a detached house those are the openings the street already watches.

We start with the sides nobody sees, the narrow passage, the basement stairwell, the back garden, and add the front last. A starter kit can be the core of that plan, but rarely all of it.

“Wireless sensors work anywhere in the house”

Wireless range figures are measured in open air. Inside a house the signal goes through brick, stucco on lath, concrete, insulation with foil backing and metal-framed glass, and in a long rebuilt house it goes through several of those in a row.

The result is not a sensor that never works; it is a sensor that works most of the time, reports supervision faults now and then, and eventually gets bypassed by an owner tired of the beeping. That is worse than a sensor that clearly fails.

We measure the signal from each planned position at the survey and add an expander, a repeater or a cable where the margin is thin. Wireless is excellent when it is planned; it is unreliable when it is assumed.

“Burglars mostly come in through the front door”

Plenty of general advice says most burglars use the front door, and in attached houses and apartments that may often be true, because the front is the only way in. A detached house offers other choices that are out of sight.

The detailed Manhattan Beach report we found, from the Brooklyn Paper in 2011, described a man trying to force a basement door on Oriental Boulevard just after midnight. We would not build a statistic on one blotter item, but it illustrates the point: on a house with a below-grade door, the front is not the only door that matters.

Our plans protect the front door, of course. They spend most of their sensors on the doors and windows nobody overlooks.

“Put the alarm panel in the basement, out of the way”

The basement is where the electrical service and the internet line usually come in, so installers like it. It is also the room a burglar coming through a basement door reaches first, and on this peninsula it is the room that flooded when Sandy’s surge came from both the bay and the ocean, according to City Limits.

A panel in the basement can be found and smashed during the entry delay, and a panel under water stops reporting. Neither is hypothetical for a house with an outside basement door near the shore.

We put the panel and battery in a closet on a main floor, keep only sensors and perhaps a wired expander below grade, and give the basement door no entry delay.

“A low-crime area does not need an alarm”

Manhattan Beach is quiet by city standards; Wikipedia reports the 61st Precinct ranked among the safest patrol areas in 2010. But quiet is not the same as never, and the 2011 Brooklyn Paper figures showed Manhattan Beach burglaries rising from three to nine in a year.

The more useful point is that in a neighborhood of large, spread-out houses with little foot traffic, an intrusion at the side or back can go unnoticed for a long time. The less often break-ins happen, the less anyone is watching for one.

An alarm in a low-crime area is less about frequency than about time: it turns a break-in discovered the next day into one reported within seconds.

“Alarm installation costs a few hundred dollars plus a monthly contract”

That describes a dealer model where equipment is subsidized and recovered through a multi-year monitoring contract. It is one way to buy an alarm, not the only one.

On our Brooklyn list a basic residential system starts at $600, complete home protection from $2,500, and a custom hardwired system for a big house runs $2,000 to $6,000. You own the equipment once it is paid for, and monitoring, from $19.99 a month through our partners, is optional and month to month.

A large Manhattan Beach house usually lands in the complete or custom range because of its size, not because of any neighborhood surcharge. Brooklyn is our base rate.

“You need a city permit before installing an alarm in New York”

Some AI answers and older pages mention a city alarm permit. We looked and found no New York City rule requiring a permit for a residential burglar alarm, so we do not tell customers they need one.

What does apply is state licensing of installers, which is why our license number, #12000287431, is on this page, and the city’s fifteen-minute limit on audible alarms under Administrative Code § 24-238. Building work around the alarm, such as electrical work on a new house, follows its own permits through your builder.

If your congregation or school building is a city landmark, that is a separate question for the Landmarks Preservation Commission; National Register listing on its own, according to the State Historic Preservation Office, does not restrict privately funded changes.
