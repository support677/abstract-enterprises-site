The Neighborhood

Why Fiske Terrace Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Fiske Terrace is the small planned enclave on the southern edge of Flatbush, between Foster Avenue to the north and the Bay Ridge freight line to the south, with the Brighton subway cut on its west side and Ocean Avenue on its east. Most of it lies inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, which the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated in March 2008 and which takes in about 250 houses. Nearly all of them went up between 1905 and about 1914, sold by the T.B. Ackerson Company in the Fiske Terrace section and by the John R. Corbin Company in Midwood Park, the part generally north of Glenwood Road. They are freestanding foursquares, temple-front houses, bungalows, Colonial Revival and Dutch Colonial houses and a few stucco and half-timbered ones, set back from the street on lots with driveways, gardens and, from about 1910 on, their own garages at the back.

That shape decides how camera systems here age. A row house gets two cameras, front and back, and both cables reach the cellar in a few feet. A Fiske Terrace house needs more of them to see the whole lot: one or two on the porch, one on each side wall, one or two on the back, and often one on the garage. The side and back cameras are commonly fed through the attic and down from the eaves, and the garage camera may be on a cable buried along the driveway, strung overhead from the house, or running on Wi-Fi across the yard. Each of those paths has its own way of failing. Attic runs cook in summer and get chewed. Overhead spans sag and fray. Buried cable gets cut when someone digs. Wi-Fi to the garage weakens every spring when the trees fill in. The camera itself is often the last thing wrong.

The trees are a second pressure. The streets curve under what one neighborhood guide calls "a dense canopy of century-old oaks and maples." That canopy is the reason many people moved here, and it is also hard on cameras. Branches move across the frame in the wind and set off alerts all night. Leaves and acorns collect on the tops of eave cameras and on porch roofs. Squirrels travel the gutters and the cables. And when a storm brings branches down on the wires, the camera system feels it. On the evening of August 13, 2025, News 12 reported that "over 5,600 customers in the Midwood area" lost power after "last night's weather downed power lines," and the same night FDNY was called to a transformer and wires on fire near Avenue J. Power was back by 10:37 that night for most customers, but a three-and-a-half-hour cut is long enough to drain a small battery backup, and the moment power returns is when recorders and power supplies are most likely to be damaged.

The third pressure is that cameras in this neighborhood tend to matter after something has already happened on the block. In November 2012, after a string of summer break-ins, the Fiske Terrace Association met with the 70th Precinct, and Patch reported the commanding officer's advice in plain words: "Do you want to put bars on your homes? No. But to really, really secure your houses is something we recommend you do." In nearby Midwood in October 2017, CBS New York reported that a burglar had climbed in through back windows reachable from the yards, and that investigators "checked home surveillance systems along East 31st and East 32nd streets." That is how home cameras get used here. Detectives walk the block and ask each house what its cameras saw, and the houses with a working recorder and a correct clock are the ones that can answer.

The edges of the neighborhood bring different problems. Ocean Avenue's frontages were left out of the historic district, and its larger apartment buildings have lobby, hallway and package-area cameras on long cable runs back to a cellar or a super's office. The one apartment house inside the district, the six-story building of 1936 at East 17th Street and Wellington Court, has the same kind of system on a smaller scale. Foster Avenue has stores with apartments above, and Avenue H has shops beside the old station house. In July 2025 News 12 showed Ring footage from tenants of a Flatbush apartment building in which a man walked up to a hallway camera and deliberately covered the lens, two nights running. Brick Underground, quoting a housing attorney, has noted that city apartment buildings are not required to have working security cameras at all, so in a building a repair happens when a board, an owner or a managing agent decides it should, and it helps to know exactly what is broken before that conversation starts.

So a repair in Fiske Terrace means more than replacing a dead camera. It means finding where along a long, branching system the fault is, getting every side of the house and the garage recording again, putting the recorder and the network on power that rides through the next storm, and leaving the owner with logins, labels and alerts that will tell them the next time something stops. Many owners who call about a porch camera also ask about the front door, since the porch is where visitors stand; a working camera there pairs naturally with a video doorbell or a door station, and we can look at both on one visit.

Six Fiske Terrace facts that shape a camera repair

  • Almost every house stands alone. The district's roughly 250 houses are freestanding, so a full camera system covers four walls, a porch and often a garage. Long runs and several entry points into the house mean more places for a fault to hide than on an attached house.
  • Garages sit at the back of the lot. Freestanding garages were built here from about 1910, many from catalog designs, at the end of concrete driveways with a grass strip down the middle. A camera on one of them is the most remote point on the system and usually the first to drop out.
  • The canopy is old and heavy. Oaks and maples shade most streets. They cause false alerts, block views as they grow, carry squirrels onto cables, and bring down wires in storms, as the August 2025 Midwood outage showed.
  • Some service still reaches houses across the back yards. A former Fiske Terrace Association president told the Brooklyn Paper, "We have phone lines in the backyards of our homes," when Verizon put up 20-foot poles in the district to protect access to its underground fiber. Internet that comes in from the back affects where the router, and so the camera network, ends up.
  • The district is landmarked. The Landmarks Preservation Commission says ordinary maintenance and repair of existing safety equipment needs no permit. Moving a camera, adding one or running new visible conduit on a house in the district follows the commission's rules on where small equipment can go.
  • One precinct covers the area. The NYPD's 70th Precinct, at 154 Lawrence Avenue, lists Midwood, Fiske Terrace, Ditmas Park and Prospect Park South as its area. When it canvasses after a burglary, it canvasses all four, house by house.

Building Specialists

Security Camera Repair for Fiske Terrace's Architecture

The houses in this district were sold more than a century ago as variations on a handful of forms, and the forms still decide where camera cables run and where they fail. Six kinds of property account for nearly every call we get from the neighborhood.

Hipped-roof foursquares

Boxy two-and-a-half-story houses with deep eaves on all sides. Side and rear cameras usually hang under those eaves and are wired through the attic, three floors above a recorder in the cellar.

Gable-front houses and bungalows

Houses with the gable to the street or a long, low roof over a deep porch. The porch roof blocks the view from above, so the front camera is often on the porch ceiling or a column.

Stucco, half-timber and Tudor houses

A smaller group with stucco walls, timber framing or brick and stone trim. Mounting into stucco or old masonry is the delicate part, and many earlier installers avoided it by hiding cameras under the eaves.

Houses shared with a tenant

Single-family houses that now have a rented top floor or a separate apartment. One camera system often serves both households, on one owner's internet, with questions about who sees which camera.

Catalog garages and back-lot sheds

Freestanding garages at the end of the driveway, some with pyramidal or gambrel roofs and the original swing-out doors. A camera here is the system's longest and most exposed link.

Apartment buildings on the edges

The six-story apartment house of 1936 on East 17th Street, the larger buildings along Ocean Avenue and the stores-with-apartments on Foster Avenue, with lobby and hallway cameras on cellar recorders.

Foursquares: the distance between the eave and the cellar

A foursquare is the simplest house in the district to picture and one of the more involved to wire. The camera over the back door or under the rear eave is perhaps thirty feet up and the recorder is in the cellar, so the cable climbs the inside of a wall, crosses the attic and drops through the soffit, or it runs outside, down a corner of the house, behind a downspout and into a cellar window. Either path has joints: a splice in the attic where an installer ran short, a connector at the eave, a hole through the foundation. When one eave camera goes dark on a foursquare, we test it at the camera first, then at the attic joint, then at the recorder, so the repair stops at the first bad piece instead of replacing a run that is mostly fine.

Gable-front houses and bungalows: the porch roof gets in the way

On a house with a deep porch, the porch roof hides the front door from any camera under the main eave. The usual answer was to put a camera on the porch ceiling or high on a porch column, which means the camera, its cable and often its power supply live inside or on old wood. Porch ceilings here are commonly tongue-and-groove boards, and the cable runs either above them, through a small hole, or along the edge of a beam. When those porch cameras fail, the fault is often a cable pinched where it passes between boards, a mount that has worked loose as the wood dried and swelled, or a power supply plugged into a porch outlet that someone switches off with the porch light.

The repair respects the porch. A camera that has to come down is photographed in place first, its screw holes are reused where the wood is sound, and a loose mount is moved a short way to solid framing rather than packed with filler. If a bungalow's porch camera has been fighting the light all along, because it looks out from deep shade at a bright street, we tell you whether a camera made for that contrast, or a door station at the door itself, would do better before we replace like with like.

Stucco, half-timber and Tudor houses: delicate walls

Stucco and old masonry do not forgive a careless bracket. A camera screwed into stucco without the right anchor works loose; water gets in around the screws, the stucco cracks around the hole, and the camera tilts until it shows the lawn. On these houses we look at the mount as carefully as the camera. If the old anchors have failed, the replacement goes on a mounting block or junction box fixed into solid backing, with the holes sealed. On the half-timbered houses we keep fixings in the timber or the plain field of the wall, not across decorative work, which also matches what the Landmarks Preservation Commission asks of small equipment in a historic district.

Houses shared with a tenant: whose system is it

Many single-family houses in the district now have a rented floor. The camera system is usually the owner's, but it may run on the tenant's outlet in the attic apartment, or the cameras that watch the side entrance may be the only ones the tenant cares about. On a repair, we confirm who owns the system and who pays before we start, and we look at where each camera gets its power and internet. A camera system that depends on a tenant's router or outlet goes down when the tenant leaves, unplugs something, or changes providers, and moving it onto the owner's own circuit and network is often the real fix.

Catalog garages: the far end of the system

The garages are a defining part of the district. The designation report notes freestanding garages from about 1910, many built to catalog designs, with pyramidal and gambrel roofs and doors that swing out on hinges. Most have no heat and some have no power at all. A camera on one is connected to the house in one of four ways: a cable buried beside the driveway, a cable strung overhead from the back of the house, a Wi-Fi camera or wireless bridge, or a recorder inside the garage itself. Each fails in a known way. Buried cable that was not rated for burial breaks down; overhead spans sag and fray at the clamps; Wi-Fi fades when the leaves come in; and a recorder or power supply in an unheated garage struggles on the coldest mornings. The repair starts by finding which of those you have.

Where the garage link keeps failing, we give you the options in writing: repairing the existing path, replacing it with outdoor-rated cable along the same route, or replacing a weak Wi-Fi link with a point-to-point wireless bridge aimed between the two buildings. In a historic district, work in the back yard can also fall under the Landmarks Preservation Commission, which regulates work in front, side and rear yards as well as on facades, so we keep any change to the path out of sight and tell you before anything would show.

Apartment buildings on the edges: long runs and many hands

The district has one apartment house of its own, the six-story building completed in 1936 at the corner of East 17th Street and Wellington Court. Ocean Avenue, which the commission left out of the district, has larger elevator buildings, and Foster Avenue has walk-ups and stores with apartments above. Their systems follow the pattern of Brooklyn apartment buildings: a recorder in the cellar or the super's office, cameras in the vestibule, lobby, mailroom, elevator and halls, and cables that rise through the building alongside other services. Faults come from building work, leaks, a super who has moved on with the password, and drives that have been recording around the clock for years. Before we touch a building system we confirm who approves the work, whether that is the board, the managing agent or the owner, and whether anyone needs footage preserved.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Freestanding Fiske Terrace House Before Opening the Recorder

These notes describe how our technicians work through a typical freestanding house in this neighborhood when several cameras are out and nobody is sure why. They describe our practice, not a particular job.

Copy first, then look. If anyone needs video from a recent night, it is exported before the recorder is restarted, opened or unplugged, with a note of the recorder's clock against the real time.

Walk the outside clockwise. The technician walks all four sides of the house and out to the garage with the live view on a phone or tester, matching each camera on the wall to its tile on the screen. On freestanding houses the tiles are often mislabeled, and a camera that seems dead on the recorder is sometimes fine and simply plugged into the wrong channel, or a camera that seems fine is a different one recording the same view.

Test the power where the outage hits. The outlet, power strip, adapters, power box, network switch and any battery backup are checked under load. After a storm on a tree-lined block, a cheap adapter or a switch port that took the surge is often the whole fault.

Check the garage link separately. The garage camera is tested at the garage and at the house, so the technician can tell a failed camera from a failed link. Wi-Fi signal is read at the camera, not guessed from the router.

Price it before fixing it. Once the cause is known, the owner gets a written price. Anything that would need a new hole, a new cable path or a change on the facade is shown in a photo and approved before it is done.

Leave it so the next repair is short. Cables are labeled at both ends, the recorder's tiles are renamed to match the walls, alerts for lost video and drive trouble go to the owner's phone, and the owner is shown how to save a clip.

Why Systems Fail Here

Canopy, Storms, Distance and Old Wood: Why Camera Systems on Fiske Terrace Houses Break Down

Most camera faults we find in this neighborhood trace back to five conditions that are particular to a district of freestanding houses under old trees. Knowing which one you are dealing with usually tells you what the repair will cost.

The canopy: shade, motion and animals

A camera under an oak sees a scene that is never still. Branches moving in the wind cross the motion area all night, and the shadows they throw in the morning and evening do the same. The usual result is not a broken camera but a camera nobody trusts: so many alerts that the owner turns them off, and then misses the one that mattered. The fix is in the settings, not the hardware. We redraw the motion area to the ground and the doors, switch the camera to detect people and vehicles where it can, and lower the sensitivity where branches cross the frame. Where a camera's whole view has been swallowed by a tree that has grown over the years, we may move it a short way to see under or past the branches.

The canopy also carries animals. Squirrels run the gutters and the cables between the trees and the houses, and an exposed camera cable along a fascia or across an eave is within easy reach. Chewed cable shows up as a camera that flickers, then fails, often at a spot just out of view from the ground. We replace the chewed section and, where the cable has to stay exposed, run it in a protective sleeve or move it inside the soffit.

Storms and power: the moment the lights come back

The August 2025 storm that cut power to more than 5,600 Midwood-area customers is a fair picture of what happens here. Wind brings down branches and wires, power goes out for a few hours, and when it comes back, it can come back unevenly. A recorder writing to its drive at the moment of the cut can corrupt the drive or lose its settings. A network switch or power box can lose a port or a fuse. Plug-in adapters take the surge. In June 2025, a manhole fire on Beverly Road left at least 1,700 Flatbush customers without power at one point, and underground faults like that one test the same weak points as a storm does.

The repair after an outage is to find which of those parts failed, replace them, and then protect the system from the next one. That usually means a battery backup sized for the recorder, the network switch and the modem or fiber terminal together, so the cameras keep recording and you can still see them on your phone while the block is dark.

Distance: long runs, more joints

A camera cable on a freestanding house can run from a cellar on one side of the house to an eave on the other, then out to a garage. Every extra length adds joints, and every joint is a place for water or corrosion to get in. Network cameras also have a practical limit on how far a single cable can carry both power and data before the picture becomes unreliable. When a far camera keeps dropping out while the near ones are fine, the run itself may be the problem, and the fix can be a small network extender or a switch closer to the cameras rather than a new camera.

Old wood and old walls: mounts that move

The houses here are mostly wood frame with clapboard or shingle siding, stucco on some, and old wood porches on many. Wood shrinks and swells with the seasons, which loosens screws over the years. A camera that slowly drifts until it points at the ground is usually not broken; its mount has worked loose or the trim it is fixed to has softened. We reset or move the mount to solid framing, seal the holes, and aim the camera again, which often costs less than replacing it.

Historic District

Fixing a Camera Inside the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District Without a Landmarks Problem

Because almost every house between Foster Avenue and Avenue H, from the Brighton line to East 19th Street, is inside a designated historic district, owners often ask whether a camera repair needs permission. For an ordinary repair, the answer in the Landmarks Preservation Commission's own permit guidebook is no: its list of work that needs no permit includes "Ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment." Replacing a failed camera with a similar one in the same place, fixing a connector, or replacing a cable along its existing path falls in that category.

Things change when the camera moves, multiplies or gets new visible cable. The commission's guidebook says security cameras "must be well spaced to avoid a cluttered, haphazard appearance," and its rules for small buildings allow no more than two cameras per 25 feet of street frontage. Equipment should be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork, not through decorative surfaces, and staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit." The commission also regulates work in front, side and rear yards, not just on facades, which matters for a camera on a fence or a garage. Its guidebook notes that an application to replace equipment in kind needs the manufacturer's cut sheets but no elevation drawings.

How we keep a repair on the right side of that line

On every repair in the district we photograph each camera and its mount before anything comes down, so the replacement can go back exactly where it was. We reuse existing fixings and cable holes where the material is sound, choose a replacement of similar size and color, and keep cable on its old path. If the camera cannot go back in the same place, for example because the trim it sat on has rotted, we show you the alternatives and which ones would need a permit before we do anything. We do not apply for permits on your behalf without being asked, and we do not move a camera to a new spot on the front of a house without your approval.

Rules that touch a business camera repair

State licensing. The New York Department of State licenses companies that install or maintain security systems, and its rules extend to closed-circuit television used to watch for intrusion, theft or movement. An owner, or an owner's employee, can work on the owner's own system without a license; a company working for someone else needs one. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431.

Cameras that watch employees. New York Labor Law §203-c requires employers who monitor employees electronically, including by video, to give written notice and get acknowledgment. If a repair moves or adds a camera that watches staff, the notice should be updated.

Landmarked frontages. The shops on Avenue H and Foster Avenue sit on the edges of the district rather than inside it, but the Avenue H station house itself is an individual landmark. Work on any landmarked building follows the commission's rules for small equipment and visible conduit.

Buildings and tenants. Housing law requires intercoms in many larger apartment buildings, but there is no general city requirement that apartment buildings keep working cameras. In a building, a camera repair is therefore a decision for the owner or board, and a written condition report of what works and what does not is often the first step.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Fiske Terrace: Questions Answered

These are the situations owners, tenants, boards and shopkeepers in and around the district describe when they call. Each answer is written for the houses and buildings here.

The garage and the back of the lot

Our recorder sits on a shelf in the detached garage, which has no heat. On the coldest mornings it shows no cameras until around midday. Is the cold the problem?

Very likely. Recorders and their hard drives are built for indoor temperatures, and a drive started from cold in an unheated garage in January can fail to spin up properly, or the recorder can restart over and over until it warms. Condensation forms inside when the garage warms up again. We check the drive's health and the recorder's logs to confirm it, and the lasting fix is to move the recorder into the house, usually the cellar, and bring the garage camera's signal to it, rather than heating the garage for a box.

The cable to our garage camera was strung overhead from the back porch roof to the garage eave, and it sags lower every winter. Can it be replaced the same way?

It can, if it is done properly. A camera cable on its own is not built to hang across a yard; over the years its own weight, ice and wind stretch it and the jacket cracks at the clamps. The right overhead replacement is outdoor-rated cable lashed to a steel support wire, anchored solidly at both buildings with a drip loop where it enters each one. If the span crosses a lot of tree cover, a wireless bridge between the house and the garage may be the better choice, and we price both so you can pick.

The garage camera gets its power from an orange extension cord the previous owner ran from an outlet on the back porch. Can the camera stay but the cord go?

Usually, yes. Many garage cameras can take their power through the same network cable that carries their picture, so a single proper cable from the house can replace both the signal path and the cord. If the camera is an older type that needs its own power, a low-voltage supply in the house with an outdoor-rated cable to the garage does the same job. Any change to the garage's own electrical outlets or circuits is a job for a licensed electrician, and we will tell you if you need one.

Trees, squirrels and the canopy

When the wind moves the oak branches over our driveway, the camera sends alerts all night. Can you stop the alerts without missing a real person?

In most cases. Many recorders and cameras can tell people and vehicles from other movement, and the motion area can be drawn to cover only the driveway, the doors and the walk rather than the sky and the branches. Where a camera cannot classify people, lowering sensitivity in the band where branches cross the frame and keeping full sensitivity at ground level cuts most of the noise. We set it up, then watch the alert history for a while with you to make sure a person walking up the drive still gets through.

Squirrels from the oak run along our gutter, and the cable to our eave camera has been chewed twice. Is there cable they leave alone?

No cable is perfectly squirrel-proof, but some routes are far better than others. The best fix is to take the cable off the gutter line entirely and run it inside the soffit or attic to the camera, so nothing is exposed but the camera itself. Where it has to stay outside, it can go in a metal sleeve or rigid conduit painted to match the trim, which also keeps it neat on a landmarked house. We replace the chewed section and re-route or protect the run in the same visit.

Porches, eaves and long runs

The camera on our beadboard porch ceiling shakes whenever someone walks across the porch or the front door slams, and the clips are hard to watch. Is the camera loose?

Often the camera is tight and the ceiling is moving. Old porch ceilings are thin boards on light framing, and footsteps and a heavy door make them flex. The fix is to mount the camera to a framing member or a solid block fixed between the joists above the boards, rather than to a board alone. Where the porch roof has no solid framing near the spot you want, a short move to the beam over the steps usually gives a steadier picture and a better angle on visitors.

The camera under the far gable of our house is wired from the cellar through the attic, and the old installer told us the cable "is too long now." Can a cable be too long?

Yes. A network camera powered through its cable works reliably only up to a certain length of run, and older analog cameras lose picture quality and power over long lengths too. On a big freestanding house, a run from a cellar on one side to a gable on the other, with a loop through the attic, can come close to that limit, especially if it was spliced. We measure the run, and the fix is either a shorter path, a small network switch or extender placed partway along it, or a new run of better cable.

Storms, power and the internet

Our recorder has a battery backup, but during the August storm outage we still could not see any camera from our phones. Why?

Because the phone app depends on more than the recorder. To see the cameras away from home, the recorder needs the router, and the router needs the modem or fiber terminal, and all three need power. If only the recorder is on the battery, it keeps recording through an outage but cannot reach you. On a repair we put the recorder, the network switch, the router and the internet terminal on backup together and size the battery for a realistic outage, so the system both records and stays viewable while the block is dark.

Apartment buildings and shops at the edges

After a man was filmed covering a hallway camera in a Flatbush apartment building, our board asked whether ours would show who did it. Can hallway cameras be placed so one watches the other?

Yes, and in a corridor or vestibule it is good practice. If each camera is placed so another camera sees it and the space in front of it, anyone who reaches up to cover or turn one is recorded doing it by the next. In an older building that often means re-aiming existing cameras or moving one to the opposite end of a hall, not adding many new ones. We review the layout with the board or managing agent and give a written price for the changes.

Our store on Foster Avenue has its cameras on a circuit that belongs to the apartments upstairs, so when the landlord's breaker trips our shop goes dark. Can that be separated?

The camera side can be. We can move the recorder, switch and camera power onto an outlet on the shop's own meter and put them on a battery backup, so the shop's system does not depend on the building's circuits. Changing which meter an outlet is on, or adding a new outlet, is electrical work for a licensed electrician, and it needs the landlord's agreement. We will tell you exactly what the electrician needs to provide, and finish the camera part once the outlet is in.

Footage, households and ownership

Our driveway camera recorded someone trying the car doors, but the date and time stamp sits right over his face in the clip. Can that be fixed for next time?

Yes. The position, size and background of the on-screen time stamp are settings on most recorders and cameras, and they can be moved to a corner of the picture where nothing important happens, usually the top edge over the sky or the house. The time stamp also sits in the recording's own data, so it does not need to cover the picture to be valid. We move it, check the clock against an internet time source, and export a test clip so you can see the result.

My father owned the house and has passed away. The estate is not settled, and the cameras stopped working months ago. Who can authorize the repair and the recorder password reset?

Whoever is legally handling the house, usually the executor or administrator of the estate, can approve the work. For a password reset, the recorder's maker may ask for proof of ownership, and estate papers along with a photo of the label on the recorder usually do. We start by checking whether we can get in without resetting at all, because a reset can erase settings, and we copy any footage the family wants to keep before anything is changed.

Types of Security Cameras and Recorders We Repair

We repair whatever is on the house, the garage or the building, whoever installed it. These are the kinds of equipment we find in Fiske Terrace and the faults each one tends to develop under the trees and on old wood.

Analog and HD-over-coax cameras

Older systems with round coaxial connectors, common on houses wired more than a decade ago. Typical faults: corroded connectors at the eave, worn infrared and failing plug-in power.

Network (IP) cameras

Cameras that take power and data on one cable. Typical faults: long runs near their limit, switch ports lost to surges, address conflicts and lost passwords.

DVRs, NVRs and hybrids

Recorders in cellars, closets, attics and garages. Typical faults: worn drives, settings lost after an outage, overheating in attics and cold starts in garages.

Wi-Fi and battery cameras

Cameras on garages, fences and the backs of houses where nobody wanted to run cable. Typical faults: signal lost to leaves and walls, batteries that fade in winter, app and account trouble.

Pan-tilt and zoom cameras

Moving cameras on corners and driveways. Typical faults: motors that stop turning, presets lost after an outage, and housings that let water in at the seams.

Video doorbells and door stations

Porch doorbells and door stations on their own apps or tied to a recorder. Typical faults: weak Wi-Fi under porch roofs, failing transformers and lost pairing.

Wireless bridges

Point-to-point radios linking the house to a garage. Typical faults: aim knocked off by storms, trees growing into the path, and power supplies that took a surge.

PoE switches and extenders

Small boxes that power network cameras. Typical faults: dead ports after a storm, overheating in attics and closets, and extenders added on long runs that fail quietly.

Power supplies and battery backups

Central power boxes, adapters and battery units. Typical faults: blown fuses, swollen batteries, and backups too small to carry the network through an outage.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Fiske Terrace

These claims show up in AI-generated answers, forum threads and national repair guides. Each is true somewhere. Each sends Fiske Terrace owners toward the wrong repair, the wrong budget or a lost clip.

"A security camera repair costs $100 to $300"

National averages like this assume a small house with one or two cameras next to the router. HomeAdvisor's own Brooklyn page puts the average surveillance camera install or repair at $1,416, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145, though that figure mixes new systems with repairs.

Neither number describes a Fiske Terrace repair well. A single fault here, such as a connector at an eave or a failed power supply, usually falls between $150 and $700 on our Brooklyn list, and the service call is credited toward it. What pushes a repair higher in this district is reach and distance: an attic run, a camera over a porch roof, or a garage link that has to be replaced.

The useful budget is the one written after the diagnosis. That is why the service call comes first, and why the repair price is put in writing before parts are replaced.

"Wi-Fi cameras solve the problem of running cable"

The common advice is that wireless cameras are the easy answer wherever cable is hard. For a camera a few feet from the router, that can be true.

On a freestanding house, the camera that is hardest to wire is usually the one on the garage or the far side of the house, and that is exactly where Wi-Fi is weakest. The signal has to pass through the house's walls, across a yard and, from spring to fall, through leaves. Battery cameras also lose capacity in cold weather. The result is a camera that works on the day it goes up and drops out later.

For the far points of a lot, a proper cable, an overhead span done right or a point-to-point wireless bridge is the repair that lasts. Wi-Fi cameras still have a place, near the house and the router.

"In a landmark district you need a permit to touch a camera"

Owners in historic districts are often told any exterior work needs the Landmarks Preservation Commission's permission, so some leave a broken camera alone rather than risk it.

The commission's guidebook lists "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting of health, safety, and utility equipment" as work that needs no permit. Replacing a failed camera in the same place with a similar one, or fixing its cable on the same path, is repair. What does fall under the commission is moving equipment, adding it, or adding visible conduit, and for small buildings the rules limit cameras to two per 25 feet of street frontage.

So the practical rule is: repair in place freely, and ask before you move or add. We photograph every mount first so the replacement goes back where the old camera was.

"After a power cut, just wait and the cameras come back"

Many quick answers say camera systems restart on their own when the power returns, so there is nothing to do.

Most of the time they do. But the outages that hit this part of Brooklyn, such as the August 2025 storm that brought down lines across Midwood, are exactly the events that damage drives writing at the moment of the cut, blow power-supply fuses, kill network switch ports and reset recorder clocks. The system can look normal on the live screen while one camera is missing from the recording or the time on every clip is years off.

After any long outage, check that every camera is recording, not just showing live, and that the clock is right. If not, that is the moment to call, before the video you need is the one that was never saved.

"Cameras on a quiet residential street rarely matter"

A common assumption is that cameras on a leafy street of single-family houses are a comfort more than a tool.

The record says otherwise. After a summer of break-ins in 2012, the Fiske Terrace Association brought the 70th Precinct in to talk about it. In Midwood in 2017, investigators went house to house checking home surveillance systems after a burglar used back windows reachable from the yards. Quiet streets are where a home camera is often the only witness, and where detectives ask every house on the block what it recorded.

That makes the back and side cameras, which nobody looks at day to day, as important as the porch camera. A repair should cover every side of the house, not just the one that failed first.

"If the live view works, the system is recording"

Many people check their cameras by opening the app and seeing a picture, and assume the recorder is saving the same thing.

Live view and recording are separate. A drive can fail or fill while live view carries on; a recording schedule can be set wrong after an outage; one camera can be shown but not recorded. On systems with a camera on a garage or far wall, we often find one channel that has not saved anything for weeks.

The real test is playback: pick a time yesterday and play back every camera. On a repair we also turn on the recorder's alerts for drive errors and lost video, so the next failure reaches your phone.

"Buy the replacement camera online and swap it yourself"

Online advice often says the cheapest repair is to order a matching camera and plug it in.

Sometimes the camera is not the fault at all. On houses here the failure is often in a connector at the eave, a splice in the attic, a power supply in the cellar or the garage link, and a new camera on a bad run fails the same way. A replacement also has to be compatible with the recorder, and on a landmarked house it should match the old one's size and position.

If you already have a replacement, we can fit it at labor-only rates once we know the model. If you have not bought one yet, let the diagnosis tell you whether you need one.

FAQ

Fiske Terrace Security Camera Repair FAQ

Our eave cameras are wired through the attic, and the only way up is a hatch in a bedroom closet. Will the technician need to go up there?
+

Often, yes. On many houses here the attic is where camera cables cross from one side of the house to the other, and splices and chewed sections are found there. The technician will ask before going up, clear a path through the closet with you, and protect the floor and the hatch opening. If the attic has no safe floor near the cable, we tell you and look for another way to test or replace the run.

Do you photograph the camera mounts before taking anything down, so it all goes back the same way on our landmarked house?
+

Yes. Every camera, bracket and cable entry is photographed before it is removed, and the replacement goes back in the same spot on the same fixings wherever the material is sound. That keeps the repair within ordinary maintenance in the historic district and gives you a record of how the house looked before and after the visit.

If the repair turns out to need new holes in our siding or trim, will you ask first?
+

Always. Any change that would add a hole, move a camera or put new cable on the outside of the house is shown to you in a photo with the reason and the price, and nothing is done until you approve it. If the change could need Landmarks approval, we tell you that too, before the work rather than after.

Can the recorder keep the garage camera's video longer than the video from the other cameras?
+

On many recorders it can. Retention and recording quality can often be set camera by camera, so a camera watching the garage or the driveway can be kept for longer at good quality while a camera on a quiet side wall records at a lower rate. Where the recorder cannot do that, a larger drive is the simpler answer. We set it to the number of days you want and confirm it on the visit.

Our garage is in the back yard. Does replacing the camera on it fall under Landmarks rules too?
+

The commission regulates work in front, side and rear yards, not only on the house, but its guidebook treats ordinary repair of existing safety equipment as needing no permit. Replacing a failed garage camera with a similar one in the same place is repair. Moving it, adding cameras or running new visible conduit to the garage is the kind of change to check first, and we tell you which side of that line a job falls on.

Will your ladders and equipment damage our garden beds and lawn?
+

We plan for them. Ladders go on boards or pads over soft ground and beds, plantings are tied back rather than stepped on, and tools stay on a drop cloth. If the only place to set a ladder for a camera is in the middle of a flower bed, we will tell you before we start and agree where it goes.

The camera we need fixed sits above our porch roof, and the roof is old. How do you reach it without walking on the roofing?
+

Usually from a ladder set beside the porch rather than on it, with a stand-off that keeps the ladder off the gutter and the roof edge. If the camera can only be reached by stepping onto the porch roof, we look at its condition first and use roof pads to spread the load, and we tell you if we think the roof is too fragile to walk on. In that case the better repair may be moving the camera to a spot that can be reached safely.

Could one of our cameras have been turned off in the recorder's settings rather than broken?
+

It happens more often than people expect. A channel can be disabled, its recording schedule cleared, or its picture hidden from the screen layout, by a relative, a previous owner or a reset after an outage, while the camera itself is fine. That is one of the first things we check, because if it is the cause, the fix is a settings change inside the service call rather than a new camera.

Can the camera on the garage turn on the garage's outside light when it sees someone at night?
+

Some cameras and recorders have a small output that can switch a light through a relay, and some cameras have their own built-in light that comes on with motion. Which is possible depends on your equipment and on whether the garage has power. Wiring a relay into the garage's light circuit is electrical work, so if that is the route, we arrange it with a licensed electrician and set up the camera side ourselves.

Can a replacement camera be mounted on our brick chimney to see over the porch roof?
+

Sometimes, but it is rarely our first choice. A chimney gives height, but its brick and mortar are often old, the cable has to be run neatly down the outside or through the roof, and in the historic district a new mount on a visible chimney is the kind of change that may need approval. We usually look first for a spot under the eave or on the porch framing that gives a similar view.
