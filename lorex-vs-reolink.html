The Verdict

Who Should Pick Lorex and Who Should Pick Reolink

There is no single winner for every building, so here is the honest split we give on the phone. It is written for the person paying for the system and living with it, not for someone ranking cameras on a test bench.

Pick Lorex if any of these sound like you

  • You want the camera to react, not just record. The Lorex E910AB we compared flashes a warning light, sounds a siren and plays a pre-recorded message when it detects a person. On a stoop or a driveway that has had package thefts, that reaction is the feature owners talk about most after the install. A recording helps you after something happens; a light and a voice can stop it from happening.
  • You live in an Alexa or Google household. Lorex lists Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant, Chromecast, Echo Show and Google Home Hub on its Fusion recorder page. If the family already asks the kitchen display to show the front door, Lorex slots into that habit with less fuss.
  • You want a few wireless cameras on the same recorder. The Fusion recorder takes eight wired cameras and eight Fusion Wi-Fi devices, such as Lorex doorbells and floodlights. That is useful on a house where one corner, a detached garage or a back fence cannot get a cable without opening a finished wall.
  • You value a polished, single-brand experience. Lorex's own support page says its recorders are designed to work best with Lorex cameras. If you never plan to add another brand, that closed design is a feature: one app, one set of menus, one company to call.
  • Your cameras face real cold. The E910AB is rated down to −22 °F. In the five boroughs that margin rarely matters, but on an exposed Hudson Valley barn or a north-facing Long Island garage it is a point in Lorex's favor.

Pick Reolink if any of these sound like you

  • You want the most cameras for the money. On our current NYC price lists, a 4-camera Reolink system installed starts lower than the Lorex equivalent, and the 12MP Reolink package comes in below the 12MP Lorex package. Over eight or twelve cameras on a walk-up or a yard, that gap adds up.
  • You want a longer manufacturer warranty. Reolink lists a 2-year limited warranty on the recorder and cameras we checked. Lorex's standard warranty is one year from purchase.
  • You might add other brands later. Reolink's PoE and plug-in Wi-Fi cameras support ONVIF and RTSP on their own, and Reolink says its recorders can pull in third-party ONVIF cameras. If you already own a Hikvision dome over the back door or expect to move to Synology, Blue Iris or a professional recorder one day, Reolink keeps those doors open.
  • You want two hard drives in the recorder. The RLN16-410 has two drive bays and up to 16 TB; the Lorex N910A62 has one bay, also up to 16 TB. Two bays let you add storage later without replacing the first drive.
  • You want a very wide view from one mount point. The Duo 3 PoE stitches two lenses into a 180° picture. On a wide backyard, a parking lot or a long storefront, one dual-lens camera can replace two ordinary ones, which also means one cable run instead of two.
  • You want smoother motion. The RLC-810A records 4K at up to 25 frames per second and the Duo 3 PoE runs 16MP at 20 fps, while the Lorex E910AB runs its 12MP picture at 15 fps. For a register, a door that people walk through quickly or a driveway with moving cars, the extra frames make a clip easier to read.

When we recommend neither

Sometimes the honest answer is a third option. A co-op board that wants an enterprise-grade recorder with user accounts for a managing agent, a government-funded site with equipment-origin rules, or a twenty-camera commercial building is usually better served by a professional line such as UniFi Protect, Uniview or Hanwha. A renter who cannot drill and only needs a doorbell may be happier with a battery doorbell and nothing else. And for many homeowners our own non-branded recorder systems, which start from $950 installed in NYC, are the best value of all; that is still our default quote unless you ask for a brand. We will say so on the call if that is where your building points.

Behind the Logo

Who Makes Lorex and Reolink, and Why Owners Ask

We get asked who makes these cameras on almost every Lorex or Reolink call, usually because someone read a Reddit thread or a news story about Chinese camera brands. Here is what the primary sources say, without the scare headlines.

Lorex

Lorex is a long-running camera brand that, for years, was owned by Dahua Technology, the Chinese surveillance maker. Dahua then announced it was selling Lorex to Skywatch, which its press release describes as a privately held cloud services company headquartered in Taiwan, for about $72 million. That is why older forum posts describe Lorex as a Dahua brand and newer ones say it is not. If the question matters to you because of a contract or a grant, the safer move is to ask for documentation on the exact model being installed rather than relying on the brand's history either way.

Reolink

Reolink says on its own About page that it was founded in 2009, and its US contact page lists an office in City of Industry, California. Reddit threads debate where the company's roots are; we do not repeat claims we cannot source from the company or a regulator.

The FCC Covered List

The list owners actually need to check is the Federal Communications Commission's Covered List, the official record of equipment the FCC considers a national security risk. On the version dated October 23, 2026, the camera makers named are Dahua Technology and Hangzhou Hikvision; neither Lorex nor Reolink is named. The listing for Hikvision and Dahua is scoped to equipment used for public safety, government facilities, critical infrastructure and other national security purposes, so for a private house the list is background reading rather than a rule. For a government-linked building or a federally funded project, the contract's own wording is what decides, and we check the exact models against it before anything is ordered. Our brand comparison hub explains the Covered List and NDAA in more detail.

What we tell owners who are worried about privacy

Whatever the brand, the practical privacy steps are the same and they matter more than the logo. Keep the recorder's firmware current, change the default password the day it is installed, give each person who needs the app their own login instead of sharing one, and decide deliberately whether you want remote viewing through the manufacturer's servers at all. Both brands record to a hard drive inside your building, so footage stays on your property unless you choose a cloud plan. On the systems we install, we walk through each of those settings with you before we leave.

Cameras

Lorex vs Reolink Cameras: Picture, Night Video and Deterrence

Both brands sell bullets, turrets, domes, dual-lens panoramas, pan-tilt-zoom cameras, floodlight cameras and doorbells. To keep the comparison honest we picked models from each manufacturer's current wired PoE line that we are quoted and asked to install most often, and we quote their specifications exactly as the product pages state them.

Resolution: bigger numbers, different trade-offs

The Lorex E910AB uses a 12MP sensor at 4640×2624 and records at 15 frames per second at every resolution, from full 12MP down to 720p. Reolink's Duo 3 PoE records 7680×2160, which Reolink calls 16MP, at 20 frames per second, but that figure is two lenses side by side making one very wide image; per degree of view, the detail is closer to two 8MP cameras than to one 16MP camera. Reolink's RLC-810A is a conventional 8MP (3840×2160) bullet that records at up to 25 frames per second. In practice, the question is not which number is bigger but what you need to read on the footage. A face at the door, a license plate in a driveway and a hand at a register each need a certain number of pixels across the target, and that depends as much on how far away and how wide the camera is as on its megapixels.

Frame rate: why it matters more than the box admits

Frame rate is the number of still pictures the camera captures each second. At 15 frames per second, a person walking briskly past a door moves a noticeable distance between frames; at 25, the motion is smoother and you are more likely to catch a clean frame of a face turning toward the lens. For a stoop where people stop and ring the bell, 15 is fine. For a store entrance, a hallway or a gate where people pass quickly, we lean toward the higher frame rates Reolink offers on these models, or toward a Lorex model rated for 30 frames per second; Lorex sells several, and they appear on our Lorex price list.

Field of view: one wide camera or two narrow ones

The Lorex E910AB covers 139° through a fixed 2.8 mm lens. The Reolink Duo 3 PoE covers 180° horizontally. The RLC-810A comes in three lens versions: 101° with the 2.8 mm lens, 87° with the 4 mm lens and 53° with the 6 mm lens. The narrower lenses see less of the yard but more detail at a distance, which is why we often mix lens sizes on one property: a wide camera to show the whole backyard, a narrower one aimed down the side alley where someone would actually walk. Neither brand has an advantage across the board here; it is about choosing the right lens for each spot, and the cable for a camera costs the same whichever lens is at the end of it.

Night video: infrared, color and spotlights

Both brands rate their infrared range at 30 meters on the models we compared, which Lorex lists as 98 feet. Infrared gives you a black-and-white picture in the dark without lighting up the neighbors' bedrooms. For color at night, Lorex advertises Color Night Vision and Smart Security Lighting on the E910AB, and Reolink's Duo 3 PoE uses a 560-lumen spotlight. Reviewers on the ranking sites tend to give Lorex the edge on night video, and a Reddit homeowner thread we read had a relative recommending Lorex for exactly that reason. In our experience the bigger factor is the scene: a streetlight shining into the lens, a white wall bouncing infrared back, or a spider web across the dome will ruin night footage from any brand. Placement and a test at night before we leave fix more night-video complaints than switching brands does.

Deterrence: Lorex's clearest advantage

The Lorex E910AB lists active deterrence with a warning light, a siren and a pre-recorded message. The Reolink pages we used for this comparison confirm a spotlight on the Duo 3 PoE and two-way audio, but they did not confirm a siren or a voice warning on those two models, so we do not claim one. If scaring someone off the property is the main goal, Lorex currently makes that easier to buy as a standard feature. If recording clearly is the main goal and you plan to rely on a separate alarm system for noise, the difference matters less.

Audio: listen, talk, or neither

The Lorex E910AB has a microphone and a speaker, so you can listen and talk through it. The Reolink Duo 3 PoE also offers two-way audio, while the RLC-810A has a built-in microphone for audio recording but no speaker. Before you turn audio recording on, think about who is being recorded. A camera that records strangers talking on the sidewalk or tenants talking in a hallway raises legal questions that a lawyer, not an installer, should answer for your situation. On many apartment and commercial jobs we leave microphones switched off unless the owner asks otherwise.

Weather and cold

Every camera in this comparison is rated IP67, which means dust-tight and able to survive temporary immersion; rain and snow are not a problem for either. The temperature ranges differ: the Lorex E910AB is rated from −22 °F to 140 °F, the Reolink RLC-810A from 14 °F to 131 °F. New York City winters rarely sit below 14 °F for long, but upstate in Ulster, Orange or Dutchess, or on an exposed waterfront, a cold snap can go lower. On those sites we check each model's rating against where it will hang, and we keep cameras out of the direct path of roof runoff that freezes into icicles across the lens.

Recorders

Lorex vs Reolink Recorders: the Box That Decides Everything

Owners compare cameras; installers compare recorders. The network video recorder, or NVR, is the box that powers the cameras, stores every hour of video, runs the person and vehicle detection, and connects to the app. It decides how many cameras you can add, how long footage is kept, and whether you can mix brands. Here are the two 16-channel recorders we compared.

Camera count: 16 and 16, counted differently

The Lorex N910A62 Fusion recorder is sold as 16-camera capable: eight wired cameras plugged into its built-in PoE ports, plus eight Lorex Fusion Wi-Fi devices such as doorbells, floodlights and Wi-Fi cameras. The Reolink RLN16-410 has sixteen PoE ports for wired cameras and supports up to 24 IP cameras in total, with a maximum of sixteen wired. In plain terms, if you want more than eight wired cameras on one Lorex Fusion recorder, you need a different Lorex recorder; the Reolink box takes sixteen wired cameras on its own ports. For a house with five or six cameras, both are plenty. For a walk-up with cameras on every floor landing, the wired port count is the number to watch.

Storage: one drive or two

The Lorex recorder ships with a 2 TB drive and supports up to 16 TB in one bay. The Reolink recorder ships with a 3 TB drive and has two bays, up to 8 TB each, for 16 TB total. The maximums are the same; the difference is how you get there. With one bay, adding storage means replacing the drive and moving or losing the footage on it. With two bays, you can add a second drive and keep the first. For owners who are told by an insurer, a landlord association or a lawyer to keep footage longer after an incident, that flexibility is worth having.

Power over Ethernet: how many watts reach each camera

Power over Ethernet lets one network cable carry both video and power, so each camera needs only one cable and no outlet. Lorex lists 25.5 watts per port and 72 watts total on the N910A62. Reolink lists 25 watts per port on the RLN16-410 and supports both 802.3af and 802.3at, but the product page we checked did not state a total budget, so we do not invent one. The total matters because cameras with spotlights, sirens and heaters draw more power, especially on a cold night when everything lights up at once. For reference, Lorex rates the E910AB at 9.5 watts maximum and Reolink rates the RLC-810A at under 12 watts. When a design gets close to a recorder's total, we add a separate PoE switch rather than run the recorder at its limit.

Resolution support

The Lorex recorder supports 12MP and 4K+ cameras; the Reolink recorder supports cameras up to 16MP and lists playback resolutions up to 7680×2160 for the dual-lens models. If you plan to add Reolink's 16MP dual-lens cameras, buy the recorder that supports them. This sounds obvious, but we regularly meet owners who bought a newer camera only to find their older recorder shows it at a lower resolution or not at all.

Detection on the recorder

The Lorex recorder page lists person, vehicle, face, mask, package (missing and left behind) and animal detection. The Reolink recorder page lists person, vehicle and animal detection, noting that the exact set depends on the hardware version. Detection is what turns hundreds of motion alerts from swaying branches and passing headlights into a handful of useful ones, and it is also what makes searching a week of footage fast. Package detection is a Lorex extra that delivery-heavy households appreciate. Whichever brand you choose, detection only works as well as the zones it is given, which is why we draw motion zones and test them with someone walking the property before we hand the system over.

Where the recorder lives

Neither brand's recorder likes heat, dust or damp. In New York buildings we put them in a ventilated closet, a dry basement shelf away from the boiler, an office cabinet or a small wall rack, ideally on a battery backup so a short power blip does not reboot the whole system. We also keep the recorder out of plain sight; a burglar who can see the box can take it, and the footage with it. Remote viewing and, if you want it, a cloud copy of important clips are the backup for that.

Storage

How Many Days of Video You Actually Get

"How many days does it keep?" is the second question owners ask, right after price, and the honest answer for both brands is "it depends on settings you control." A recorder's hard drive fills up and then overwrites the oldest footage. How fast it fills depends on four things: how many cameras, their resolution, their frame rate and bitrate, and whether they record around the clock or only on motion. A 12MP camera recording continuously at a high bitrate uses far more space than a 4MP camera recording only when someone walks by.

That is why we do not print a single "days of recording" number for either brand: anyone who does is guessing about your settings. What we do is set the recorder to meet a target. The packages on our Lorex and Reolink price lists are sized for at least two weeks of recording, and if you need more, because a lease, an insurer or plain peace of mind calls for it, we size the drive to reach it and show you the retention on the recorder screen before we leave. Reolink's second drive bay makes adding capacity later simpler; with Lorex's single bay, we size the drive generously up front.

Two settings change retention more than the brand does. The first is the substream: both brands record a smaller second stream alongside the full-resolution one, and the app uses it for quick viewing on a phone. The second is motion recording with a short pre-record buffer, which keeps a few seconds before each event so you see the person arrive, not just leave. A sensible mix, continuous on the front door and motion-based on a quiet side yard, can double the days you keep without losing anything you would ever look for.

Apps

Lorex Home App vs Reolink App: the Part You Touch Every Day

Once the ladders are gone, the app is the system. It is where alerts arrive, where you scrub back through last night, where you export a clip for the police or the landlord, and where your teenager, your tenant or your store manager gets access. The app is also where most complaints about both brands live, so it deserves a section of its own.

Lorex: one household app, deep smart-home hooks

Lorex's current app for the Fusion recorder is Lorex Home, and the product page also refers to the older Lorex Classic app and the Lorex Cloud client. That naming history is worth knowing: if you are buying a used or older Lorex recorder, check which app it uses before you assume it works with the newest one. The upside of Lorex's approach is integration: the recorder page lists Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant, Chromecast, Amazon Echo Show and Google Home Hub. In a household that already lives on smart displays, "show me the front door" on the kitchen screen is the feature that gets used every day. In the r/homedefense thread we read while researching this page, the poster had heard more complaints about the Lorex app than the Reolink app; app reviews swing with every update, so we treat that as one data point, not a verdict.

Reolink: one app on the phone, a real client on the computer

Reolink pairs the Reolink App on phones with the Reolink Client on Windows and Mac computers. For a store owner or a building super who reviews footage at a desk, a proper computer client is a bigger deal than it sounds: scrubbing a day of video on a 27-inch monitor is faster and less error-prone than on a phone. Reolink's smart-home support is narrower in what we could confirm: the Duo 3 PoE page lists Google Assistant. If Alexa on an Echo Show is a must, check the specific Reolink model before buying, or lean Lorex.

Sharing access without sharing your password

Both apps let the owner add other users. Do it. Shared logins are how a former tenant, an ex-employee or a contractor ends up with access to your cameras months later. On the systems we install, the owner gets the administrator account, and everyone else gets their own login with only the cameras and permissions they need: the super sees the lobby and the boiler room, the tenants' association sees nothing, and the owner can remove anyone in two taps.

Exporting a clip that someone else can actually play

The moment that tests every camera system is the morning after something happens, when you need to hand a clip to a police officer, an insurer or a landlord. Both brands can export video from the app and from the recorder to a USB stick. Alerts that you do not learn to ignore

With both, the recorder in your building stores the video, the detection runs locally, and the app shows live and recorded footage without a monthly bill.

Openness

ONVIF, RTSP and Mixing Brands: Reolink's Clearest Advantage

ONVIF is an industry standard that lets a camera from one maker talk to a recorder or software from another. RTSP is a simpler video-streaming standard that many programs can read. Together they decide whether your system can grow beyond one brand, and here the two companies really do take different paths.

Reolink: open by default

Reolink says its PoE and plug-in Wi-Fi cameras, including the RLC series, the E1 Outdoor series and the Duo series, support ONVIF on their own, and the RLC-810A page advertises "Universal RTSP & ONVIF Support." Battery models such as the Argus and Altas lines need a Reolink Home Hub for ONVIF, and cameras sold inside an NVR kit need the Reolink NVR. Reolink also says its recorders can pull in third-party ONVIF cameras, and it publishes setup guides for Blue Iris and Synology Surveillance Station, with QNAP, Milestone, iSpy and Digital WatchDog listed on its compatibility page. In practice that means a Reolink camera you buy today could end up on a professional recorder or a network storage box in five years, and a Reolink recorder can adopt a camera from another brand you already own.

Lorex: ONVIF cameras, recorders built for Lorex

Lorex says its cameras are ONVIF Profile G, S and T compliant, and the E910AB page lists "ONVIF Conformant Products (Profiles G, S, T)" among its compatible recorders. On the recorder side, Lorex's own PRO site answers the question directly: Lorex NVRs are designed to work best with Lorex cameras. Lorex does publish an article on connecting ONVIF-conformant cameras to its X Series recorders, and the E910AB lists the Lorex Connect XC recorder as having limited compatibility via ONVIF. Our reading: a Lorex camera can usually go onto another brand's recorder, but a Lorex recorder is not the box we would choose if you plan to fill it with other brands' cameras.

What "works with ONVIF" really means on a job

ONVIF gets you live video and recording across brands. It does not always get you the fancy parts: smart person and vehicle detection, the siren, the spotlight trigger, two-way talk and some app features often work only when the camera and recorder are the same brand. So when an owner asks us to mix brands, we check the exact camera and recorder models against each other first, and we tell you in writing which features will and will not carry over. That conversation is the difference between a mixed system that works and a mixed system that technically records but annoys you every day.

Before You Buy

Seven Questions to Answer Before You Buy Either Kit

These are the questions we ask on the first call. Answer them honestly and the Lorex vs Reolink decision usually makes itself.

  1. What exactly do you need to see? "The backyard" is a start; "a face at the back gate at night" is a specification. Write down each spot and what you need to recognize there: a person, a face, a car, a plate, a package.

Year Two and Beyond

Living With Either System After Year One

The differences between Lorex and Reolink shrink once a system is installed well; the habits of the owner start to matter more. These are the small jobs that keep either brand working.

  • Update firmware a few times a year. Both brands push updates for the recorder, the cameras and the app. Updates fix security holes and app bugs. Do the recorder first, then the cameras, and check that every camera comes back online afterward.
  • Look at the footage, not just the live view. Once a month, scrub back a week on one camera. If footage stops short of what you expect, the drive may be full, failing or set to the wrong recording mode, and it is far better to find out on a quiet Tuesday than the morning after a break-in.
  • Clean the domes and lenses. Spider webs, pollen and city grime blur the picture and set off false motion alerts at night when infrared lights them up. A soft cloth twice a year fixes most of it.
  • Replace the hard drive before it fails. Surveillance drives work around the clock. If the recorder shows drive errors or the retention drops, replace the drive; with Reolink's second bay you can add one, with Lorex's single bay you swap it.
  • Review who has access. When a tenant moves out, an employee leaves or a contractor finishes, remove their login the same day.

Customers on our installs can call us for any of this. Many prefer a yearly visit where we update firmware, clean cameras, check the drive and re-aim anything that has been knocked by a ladder, a storm or a delivery truck.

The Installer's Call

Do It Yourself or Have It Wired? Our Honest Call

Both brands are sold to do-it-yourselfers, and plenty of people install them successfully. Here is where we think DIY is fine and where a wired, professionally run system holds up better, regardless of which of the two brands you choose.

Fine for DIY

  • A one-family house with an unfinished basement or attic, where cables can run in open joists to two or three cameras.
  • A battery or Wi-Fi camera on a porch or a back fence, close enough to the router for a strong signal, that you are happy to recharge or check now and then. Reolink's battery Argus line and Lorex's Wi-Fi cameras are built for exactly this.
  • A floodlight camera that replaces an existing outdoor light, where the wiring is already there and the person doing the swap is comfortable with a light fixture.
  • A kit that sits on a shelf with the cameras indoors, watching a front hall or a stockroom, where no holes go through an exterior wall.

Holds up better wired by an installer

  • Brick, stone and concrete. New York walls are thick, and Wi-Fi signals fade fast through masonry. A camera that worked on the setup bench in the living room often drops out once it is outside the back wall of a brick row house. A PoE cable does not care about the wall.
  • More than a few cameras. Each Wi-Fi camera shares your home network with every phone, laptop and television. Six or eight wired cameras on the recorder's own ports take that load off your Wi-Fi entirely.
  • Anything above a first-floor ladder. Second-story eaves, cornices and fire-escape landings are where most DIY installs stall. A pro install is also insured if something or someone falls.
  • Exterior penetrations. Every cable that passes through an outside wall needs to be sealed and dripped so water runs away from the hole. Leaks into a party wall or a ceiling below are the costliest DIY mistake we fix.
  • Buildings with other people in them. Landlords, condo boards and commercial tenants often need a licensed, insured contractor on paper, and someone accountable for where cameras point.

That is the whole reason we install both brands: the logo matters less than the cable, the mounting and the settings. A Lorex kit run cleanly with sealed penetrations and tuned detection will outperform a Reolink kit thrown up in an afternoon, and the other way round.

Switching

Moving From Lorex to Reolink, or Reolink to Lorex

A common Reddit question is whether a Reolink recorder is a good upgrade for an existing Lorex system, and we get the reverse too. The short answer: the cable in your walls is the valuable part, and it almost always stays. Both brands use standard network cable for their PoE cameras, so swapping brands is mostly a matter of the recorder and the cameras.

Going from Lorex to Reolink, the question is whether your Lorex cameras will work on the Reolink recorder over ONVIF. Going from Reolink to Lorex, the question runs the other way: Lorex says its recorders are designed to work best with Lorex cameras, so we usually plan to replace the cameras along with the recorder, while keeping every cable run. Older analog systems with coaxial cable are a different job altogether; both brands' current PoE lines need network cable, and our NVR installation team can tell you whether the old coax can be reused with adapters or should be replaced.

Reality Check

What Reviews and AI Answers Get Wrong About Lorex vs Reolink

"Lorex is a Dahua brand, so avoid it"

Dahua owned Lorex for years and then announced its sale to Skywatch, a Taiwan-based cloud company. Lorex is not named on the FCC Covered List as of the October 23, 2026 version. Whether that history matters to you is a personal or contractual judgment, but the blanket statement is out of date.

"Reolink has 16MP, so it has the sharpest picture"

The 16MP figure on the Duo 3 PoE is two lenses combined into one 180° image. It is a superb way to cover a wide area with one camera, but per degree of view it is not sharper than a good single-lens 8MP or 12MP camera. Compare detail on the spot you care about, not the headline number.

"No subscription means no remote viewing"

With both brands, live and recorded video are available in the app without a plan. The optional cloud plans add an off-site copy of clips, not basic access.

"Wi-Fi cameras are easier, so they are better"

Easier to put up, yes. In New York's brick and stone, wired PoE cameras are more reliable, need no charging, and keep your home Wi-Fi free for everything else. Both brands sell both kinds; the building should pick, not the box.

"Any camera works on any recorder"

ONVIF gets you basic video across brands, and Reolink is more open than Lorex here. But detection, deterrence and some app features usually only work brand-to-brand, and an older recorder may not support a newer camera's resolution. Check the models together before you buy.

"The review site picked Lorex, so Lorex is better for me"

Security.org and SafeHome.org both rate Lorex above Reolink, and Lorex quotes those ratings on its own comparison page. Those rankings weigh things like build quality and app polish that are real. They do not weigh your building's walls, your camera count, your budget or whether you will ever add another brand, which is where Reolink often wins on our jobs.
