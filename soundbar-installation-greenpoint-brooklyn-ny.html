The Neighborhood

Why Greenpoint Homes Need Soundbar Installation

A soundbar is a small, light thing. What makes it awkward in Greenpoint is the wall it has to hang on, and the people on the other side of the floor. Brooklyn's northernmost neighbourhood was built fast in the second half of the nineteenth century for shipbuilders, refinery hands, potters and pencil makers, and much of what they built is still standing. Since then almost every house has been resurfaced, subdivided, insulated, rewired or opened up at least once, and often several times. The result is a neighbourhood where two houses that look alike from the pavement can have completely different walls behind the television, and where a living room is usually one floor of a building that holds two, three or more households.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's report on the Greenpoint Historic District, designated in September 1982, describes exactly this mix. It lists substantial rowhouses built for the owners and managers of the local shipyards and factories, more modest rowhouses, and numerous flathouses — walk-up apartment houses — for the workers, along with commercial buildings on the shopping streets. It also says plainly that most of the district's early frame houses have been resurfaced with modern materials, while their original mass, window arrangement and underlying framing survive. Street by street the report records houses now covered in aluminum, in asphalt shingles, in composition siding and in imitation brick. If you live in one of those houses, the siding tells you that you live in a wooden building; it does not tell you what the wall behind the TV is made of.

Sometimes that wall holds a surprise. When Brownstoner followed the restoration of an 1870s three-family frame row house in Greenpoint in 2018, the builders stripped away the asphalt and aluminum siding and found the original wood frame filled in with brick. Brick between studs changes how a bracket is fixed and whether a cable can ever travel inside the wall, and no stud finder will warn you about it in advance. Other walls in the same houses have been strapped, panelled, re-plastered or covered in new drywall over the old lath, so the first job on any Greenpoint visit is simply to find out what is there.

The second job is about neighbours. The NYU Furman Center puts the homeownership rate for Greenpoint and Williamsburg at 16.6 percent in 2024, which means most people here rent, and most households share a building with others. In the frame houses that is often an owner on one floor and tenants on the others; in the flathouses on Manhattan Avenue and Greenpoint Avenue it can be a shop at street level with families stacked above. Wood-framed floors carry low-frequency sound well, so the subwoofer that feels pleasant in your living room can be the loudest thing in the flat underneath. Residents here are sensitive to noise for good reason: in 2019 the Brooklyn Eagle reported historic-district neighbours on Milton Street asking Community Board 1 to slow the spread of bars, one of them describing summer nights as sound ricocheting in a canyon. On r/Greenpoint, a resident asking about a loud weekly party near Franklin Street was told to file 311 complaints, contact the community board and go to the 94th Precinct council meetings. A soundbar should never add to that list.

Then there is the waterfront. The 2005 rezoning of 175 blocks across Greenpoint and Williamsburg opened the old industrial shoreline to housing, and Greenpoint Landing is the result most people can see from Manhattan Avenue: a 22-acre master plan that, according to New York YIMBY in August 2025, had already delivered nearly 3,000 apartments, with roughly 5,500 planned in all. Those buildings bring a completely different job — steel studs, concrete, glass walls facing the river, and a management office with rules about contractors — just a few blocks from houses that were standing before the Civil War. A single soundbar installer in Greenpoint has to be comfortable in both.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Greenpoint's Architecture

Greenpoint packs six quite different kinds of building into about a square mile and a quarter. These are the ones we work in most, from the historic district around Milton and Noble Streets to the towers north of Commercial Street, and what each means for the bracket and the wire.

Sided Frame Houses

Two- and three-storey wooden houses from the 1850s onward, many re-sided in aluminum, asphalt or vinyl. Inside: wood studs, original plaster on lath or later drywall, often several generations of renovation layered on one wall. Usually two or three households to a house.

Brick-Nogged Frames

Some of the same frame houses, opened up, prove to have brick laid between the studs. Outside they look identical to their neighbours. Inside they behave like a masonry wall with timber running through it, which changes both the fixing and the cable route.

Brick and Brownstone Rowhouses

The Italianate, Second Empire and neo-Grec houses built for shipyard owners and managers on Kent, Noble and Milton Streets. Solid masonry party walls, plaster on the inside of them, high parlour ceilings and owners who want original detail left untouched.

Flathouses Over Shops

Three- and four-storey walk-ups with a storefront at street level, such as the long 1870s and 1880s rows on Greenpoint Avenue the LPC describes, built with stores below and families on each floor above. Brick outer walls, plaster partitions, narrow rooms and a business underneath.

Worker Apartment Houses and Tenements

Larger walk-up buildings put up for factory workers, from ordinary tenements to the six-storey Astral Apartments on Franklin Street, built in 1885–86 for Charles Pratt's oil workers. Plaster on masonry or block, older wiring and lots of stairs.

Waterfront Towers and New Mid-Rises

Greenpoint Landing's towers, Eagle + West's pair at 30 and 40 storeys, and the mid-rise buildings that have filled in lots since 2005. Steel-stud partitions, concrete columns and slabs, big windows and a management office that sets the rules.

How each Greenpoint wall takes a bracket and a cable

Frame walls with plaster or drywall. Studs in a nineteenth-century frame are not always at modern spacing, and they may have been doubled, notched or moved during a renovation. We scan, then confirm each stud with a small pilot hole, because a stud finder reads plaster, lath and old repairs unreliably. The bracket goes into solid wood with wood screws sized for the load; between studs we use toggles that clamp behind the plaster rather than plastic plugs that rely on it. A soundbar's weight is small, so the aim is a fixing that will still be tight in ten years on a wall that moves a little with the seasons.

Frame walls packed with brick. If the pilot hole meets brick dust instead of wood, we stop and map the wall. Studs still run through it, so where a stud lands at a bracket hole we use it; elsewhere a masonry anchor goes into whole brick, not the soft mortar around it. These walls cannot take an in-wall power kit, so the cable route moves to a paintable channel, to the console below, or to a neighbouring partition that is hollow.

Masonry party walls in rowhouses and flathouses. Old brick, often soft and sandy at the surface, behind a coat of plaster. Masonry anchors set into the brick, depth-limited so nothing comes through into next door, with the first hole tested before the rest are drilled. Plaster is cut cleanly around each hole so it does not spall.

Tower partitions and concrete. Steel studs take fasteners and toggles designed for light-gauge metal, never wood screws. Concrete columns and walls in towers are drilled only where the building permits it, because new construction can contain conduit or post-tensioned tendons; where it does not, the bar goes onto the partition or onto the television's own mount. Many new buildings also install wood blocking behind the drywall at the TV position, which, when we can find it, is the best fixing of all.

When the wall behind the TV is already open for other work, a network cable to the screen costs very little and frees the bar from a crowded wireless signal. That run belongs to our Greenpoint structured cabling crew and can be planned into the same visit.

Compliance notes for business soundbars in Greenpoint

  • The noise code's three tests. DEP's plain-language summary of the city Noise Code limits music from a business to 42 dB measured inside a nearby home; between 10 PM and 7 AM, to no more than 7 dB over the street's ambient level at 15 feet or more; and, on streets already above 62 dB(C), to 6 dB(C) over ambient for bass. In a shop with flats above, the bass test is the one to respect, so commercial soundbars start with the sub off.
  • Neighbours who organise. Historic-district residents have taken late-night noise from bars and backyards to Community Board 1 and the State Liquor Authority, as the Brooklyn Eagle reported in 2019. A soundbar on a side screen should be set so it never becomes part of that conversation.
  • Projection into a walkway. The 2010 ADA Standards limit objects mounted between 27 and 80 inches above the floor along a circulation path to a 4-inch projection; a bracketed bar usually exceeds that, so in an aisle or corridor it goes higher, into a recess or out of the route.
  • Who does which wiring. Signal cabling — HDMI, speaker leads, network — falls under our low-voltage licence and does not trigger a DOB permit. Adding a receptacle behind a screen is different: that goes to a licensed electrician, and in either case the landlord may have contractor rules of its own.
  • Insurance paperwork. Commercial landlords and managing agents usually want a contractor's certificate of insurance naming them; ours is free and normally issued the day it is requested.
  • Facades in the historic district. A soundbar inside a shop on Franklin Street or Manhattan Avenue is interior work and, without a building permit, is generally of no concern to the Landmarks Preservation Commission. Put a speaker or bracket on the outside of a designated building, though, and the owner should clear it with LPC before anyone drills.

Behind the Siding

Reading a Greenpoint Wall Before Anything Is Drilled

The most useful ten minutes of a Greenpoint soundbar visit happen before the drill is plugged in. In a neighbourhood where so many buildings have been resurfaced and rebuilt from the inside, the wall behind the television can be any of half a dozen things, and each one wants different hardware, a different cable plan and sometimes a different place for the bar altogether. Here is how we work it out, and why we would rather spend that time than guess.

What the outside does and does not tell us

From the street we learn the building type: a sided frame house, a brick rowhouse, a flathouse over a shop, a walk-up, a tower. That narrows the options but settles very little. The Landmarks Commission's report on the historic district notes house after house whose original clapboard or shingles were covered over in the twentieth century with aluminum, asphalt or composition siding, and plenty of houses outside the district went the same way. Siding is a skin; it says nothing about whether the interior walls are original plaster, 1970s panelling on furring strips, drywall over lath, or a frame filled with brick.

What the wall tells us from inside

Inside, we start with the cheap tests. A knock along the wall tells hollow from solid and finds where panelling or drywall has been laid over something older. A scanner shows stud lines and, in newer walls, electrical cable, though it reads poorly through thick plaster. Outlets and switch plates are useful windows: with the power and the cover off, the edge of the box shows the layers of the wall in cross-section. Then, at the spot where the bracket's first screw will go anyway, a small pilot hole settles it, and what comes out on the bit — pale wood shavings, grey plaster, red brick dust, gypsum or nothing at all — decides the hardware.

Why a renovated wall can be harder than an original one

An untouched nineteenth-century frame wall is fairly predictable once you know its spacing. A wall renovated three times is not. We regularly find drywall laid over old plaster with a gap between them, furring strips that do not line up with the studs behind, studs that were cut to move a doorway, and patches where a chimney or a pipe once ran. Every one of those changes where a screw can safely go, so on a layered wall we confirm each fixing point individually instead of assuming the pattern repeats.

When we suggest not drilling at all

Sometimes the right answer is to leave the wall alone. If the plaster under the screen is cracked and loose, if the wall is a finished feature the owner wants untouched, or if a renter's lease rules out new holes, the bar can sit on the console or hang from a bracket attached to the television itself. We would rather tell you that on the phone than discover it with a hole already in the wall.

What we write down for you afterwards

Once the wall has been read, we note what it turned out to be and which fixings went into it: studs or blocking, toggles in plaster, anchors in brick, fasteners in steel. That short record is useful later to whoever paints, renovates or hangs the next television on the same wall, and to a landlord who wants to know what is behind the bracket. In a building that has been altered as often as most Greenpoint houses, knowing what one wall is made of saves the next person from guessing.

Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Greenpoint: Questions Answered

These are questions that Greenpoint rooms and buildings raise and a generic install guide skips: televisions forced over doorways in narrow flats, equipment closets at the far end of a floor-through, the blocking hidden in a new tower wall, bass that booms in a small parlour, and two kinds of commercial wall common near the creek. Booking and practical questions are in the FAQ further down.

Narrow flats and long floor-throughs

In our narrow flathouse living room, the only spot for the TV is above a doorway — where does the soundbar go?

Usually directly under the screen, on the wall above the door frame, provided there is enough height. A typical interior door with its trim reaches about seven feet, and a TV hung above it is already high, so the bar sits in the strip between the top of the trim and the bottom of the screen, tilted slightly downward if the bar's bracket allows it. We measure that strip first. If the gap is too tight for the bar to clear the trim, the alternatives are to hang the bar from the television's own mount, which keeps it tight to the screen, or to move it to a low shelf or console nearby and accept a little less precision in where voices seem to come from.

Two practical points matter above a doorway. The wall over a door is a header, so the framing is different from the rest of the wall: there is usually solid timber across the opening, which is excellent for fixing, but no stud directly above the centre of the door below the header. And the door itself must still open fully, including any door that swings toward the TV wall from the other side. We check the swing, the trim and the header before marking anything, and we keep the cable route away from the frame so the wiring does not end up running across the doorway's casing.

Our cable box and console have to live in a closet at the far end of a long floor-through, well over 25 feet from the TV — will HDMI survive the distance?

It can, but not with an ordinary cable from the drawer. A passive HDMI cable works reliably over modest lengths, and as the distance grows, especially with 4K and high-frame-rate signals, the picture and sound start to drop out. For longer runs the options are an active or fibre-optic HDMI cable, which carries the signal over much greater distances, or an HDMI-over-network extender that sends it along a category cable. Our published cable-run line covers up to 25 feet; anything longer is quoted in writing before the visit, with the cable type named.

There is often a simpler arrangement. If the sources go in the closet and only the TV and bar stay at the front, the remote also needs a path: infrared will not reach through a closed closet door, so either the box supports a radio or app remote or an IR repeater is added. Another approach is to keep a small streaming device at the TV and leave only the cable box in the closet. We sketch both on the visit and price whichever suits the flat, so you are not paying for a long run you do not need.

New buildings on the waterfront

Our tower's leasing office says there is wood blocking behind the drywall where the TV goes — how do you find it, and does it help the soundbar?

Blocking is a horizontal piece of timber or plywood fixed between the studs before the drywall goes up, so that a television bracket can be screwed straight into it. When it is there, it is the best fixing in the building, and a soundbar bracket a few inches lower can often use the same strip if the blocking is tall enough. The difficulty is that it is invisible. We ask management for the drawing or the height it was set at, scan for the change in density, and confirm with a small pilot hole inside the area the TV will cover, so a miss stays hidden.

If the blocking turns out to be narrower than promised, or set only at the height of the TV bracket, the soundbar can still hang from toggles rated for steel studs or from the television's own mount. We never assume blocking is present because a listing or a neighbour says so, and if management's paperwork disagrees with what the wall shows, we follow the wall and tell you.

Sound inside small old rooms

The subwoofer booms in one corner of our small frame-house parlour and sounds thin on the sofa — is the room the problem?

Very often, yes. Every room has dimensions that make certain bass notes pile up in some spots and cancel in others. In a small, boxy parlour those peaks and dips are large, so the same subwoofer can rumble in a corner and almost vanish at the sofa a few feet away. Turning the sub up to fix the sofa makes the corner, and the flat downstairs, much worse. Moving it is the real cure: we try the sub in two or three positions while someone sits in the main seat, and a few feet along a wall can make a striking difference.

After placement come the settings. Many bars and subs have a room-calibration routine that measures from the seating position and evens out the worst peaks; we run it where the bar supports it. We also lower the sub's level relative to the bar, which in a small wooden room usually sounds fuller rather than weaker, and check that the bar's own bass setting is not doubling up with the sub. If the corner position still wins on sound but not on neighbours, an isolation pad and a lower night setting are a fair compromise.

Commercial walls near the creek

Our industrial unit near Newtown Creek has corrugated metal walls — can a TV and soundbar be fixed to sheet metal?

Not to the sheet alone. Thin corrugated or ribbed panels are made to keep weather out, not to carry a load that sticks out from the wall, and screws in the sheet itself will pull through or rattle. What usually sits behind the panel is a steel frame of girts and columns, and that is what a display and soundbar should hang from. We locate it, then either fix to it with self-drilling fasteners suited to the steel, or mount a plywood or unistrut backer across two members and put the bracket on that.

Metal walls also bring two side issues. Sound reflects strongly off bare metal, so in a hard room the bar's dialogue setting and position matter more than its size. And if the wall is an exterior skin, condensation and temperature swings are part of its life, so we keep the cable entry on the inside face and avoid creating holes that let weather in. In a unit with no suitable framing nearby, a floor-standing display stand with a bar shelf is often the better answer.

Can the cables run above the drop ceiling in our Manhattan Avenue storefront, and does the cable have to be a special type?

Often they can, and it is a tidy way to get from the screen to a back-room rack. The question is what kind of space is above the tiles. If that void is used as a return-air plenum for the heating and cooling, the electrical code requires cable rated for plenum use, which is made to limit smoke and flame spread, and ordinary cable is not allowed there. We look above a tile or two, ask the landlord or building engineer when it is unclear, and use plenum-rated HDMI or network cable where the space requires it.

Above the ceiling, cable should be supported independently on its own hangers or hooks, not laid on the ceiling tiles or tied to sprinkler pipe, and it must not block access panels or fire-protection equipment. Where the ceiling is old plaster rather than tiles, or there is no void at all, the route moves to a surface channel along the wall or into the counter below the screen.

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Greenpoint

AI search summaries about soundbar installation are fluent and confident, and they are built from national cost guides and product pages that picture a suburban living room. Seven of their most repeated points look different from inside a Greenpoint frame house, flathouse or waterfront tower.

Claim one: a soundbar mount is a cheap, standard job

Summaries usually quote one national range. HomeWyse estimated $58.43 to $231 per soundbar in September 2026, and Thumbtack listed national TV mounting at $121 to $234. For a bar on drywall over wood studs, those are reasonable numbers.

The trouble is the word standard. A Greenpoint TV wall might be original plaster, drywall over plaster, brick between studs, a masonry party wall, steel studs or concrete, and the hardware and time differ for each. A national average cannot know which of those is in your living room.

So our Brooklyn list separates the wall from the mount: $129 for a bar on drywall over wood, with published lines for metal studs, plaster, brick, concrete and tile. You see the wall line on the quote before we arrive.

Claim two: a stud finder tells you where to drill

AI how-to answers treat the stud finder as the answer to every wall. On modern drywall it is a good tool. On a wall with an inch of old plaster, metal lath, panelling over furring or brick between the studs, its readings range from vague to wrong.

That is why we treat a scanner as a first guess in older Greenpoint houses and confirm with a small pilot hole at a point the bracket will cover. What comes out on the drill bit is more reliable than any display, and it costs nothing in looks because the bracket hides it.

In the towers the opposite problem applies: the scanner works well, but what it finds — blocking, steel or concrete — still has to match what management allows to be drilled.

Claim three: you need eARC for Dolby Atmos

There is truth in it, but only for discs and some downloads. Uncompressed formats like Dolby TrueHD and DTS-HD Master Audio require the extra bandwidth of eARC. What Netflix, Disney+ or Max send is Atmos packed inside Dolby Digital Plus, and a standard ARC socket handles that.

So a five-year-old set with plain ARC is no barrier to streamed Atmos. The requirements are practical: the bar plugged into the ARC-labelled socket, CEC enabled in both menus, and the TV’s digital audio output on pass-through or auto rather than PCM.

Those three settings are the first things we check on a repair call, because a firmware update can quietly reset any of them.

Claim four: height channels work in any room

The marketing reads as if sound will come from overhead in every room. Up-firing drivers actually work by bouncing off the ceiling, and Dolby’s own advice is a flat, hard ceiling somewhere between 7.5 and 14 feet high.

Greenpoint ceilings vary. Parlour floors in older rowhouses can be generous; top floors under a mansard roof slope; flats with a dropped ceiling or a soffit for ductwork break the reflection; tower apartments often have a bulkhead along the window line.

We measure the ceiling, keep the bar's upward drivers clear of beams and soffits, run calibration from your seat, and say plainly when the height layer is likely to be subtle.

Claim five: the screws in the box will do

A typical answer tells you to open the box and follow the leaflet. Usually the metal bracket inside is perfectly good; the little bag of screws and plastic plugs is the weak point, since it assumes timber or solid backing. Angi’s guide prices a replacement soundbar bracket at $30 to $100 for the cases where the supplied one is unusable.

Put those plugs into a Greenpoint wall and you can predict the result: old plaster crumbles round them, soft brick lets them turn, tower steel studs strip them, and concrete refuses them entirely. What holds is hardware chosen for what the pilot hole found.

Our routine is to use the maker’s bracket with our own fixings. If the carton had none, we fit the Sanus bracket from the price list.

Claim six: nobody needs to approve a soundbar

On the city side the summaries are right: per Angi, only electrical or structural work brings permits, and a bar under a TV involves neither. What they leave out is private permission, and that is where Greenpoint visits actually stall.

Waterfront towers usually want a certificate of insurance and a booked time slot before a contractor goes up. Commercial landlords want their names on the certificate. Renters in frame houses need the owner's agreement before holes go in the plaster.

Each step is simple; skipping one is what costs a day. The certificate is free from us, it goes out ahead of time, and we check access with the desk or the owner the evening before.

Claim seven: more bass makes it feel like a cinema

Reviewers love a big low end and AI summaries echo them. That advice was written for houses with no one underneath. Here a living-room floor is usually joists and boards with another household’s bedroom ceiling nailed to the bottom of them.

Wood-framed buildings pass low frequencies through joists and studs far more readily than speech, so the neighbour hears the rumble long before they hear the words. A sub turned up for drama mostly turns up the flat below.

What works is modest and unglamorous: the sub moved away from party walls and corners, lifted onto a pad, its level dialled back from the showroom setting. Voices get clearer through the speech mode, while the bass stays in your own room.

Stacked Households

Sound in a Three-Family House or a Flat Over a Shop

Greenpoint's older buildings stack households on top of each other, and they do it on wooden floors. The LPC's report records frame houses built as three-family homes in the 1880s and flathouse rows planned with stores below and two families on each floor above. Those layouts survive, and they shape how a soundbar should be set up as much as the wall does.

How sound moves through a frame building

Speech and most music travel through air, and plaster walls and floors block a fair amount of it. Bass is different. Low frequencies make the structure itself vibrate, and in a building whose floors are timber joists under boards, that vibration crosses to the next flat easily. That is why a neighbour downstairs can feel a film's soundtrack as a rumble without hearing a word of the dialogue. The parts of a soundbar system that cause complaints are therefore the subwoofer and the bar's own bass, not the voices.

Where the subwoofer should and should not go

Keep it off a shared wall and out of the corner nearest the neighbour's bedroom, because corners and walls make bass louder. Set it on an isolating pad rather than straight on the boards, so less vibration passes into the joists. And set its level by ear from the sofa, with someone listening in the flat below if they are willing; most subs ship turned up for the showroom. In a top-floor flat the same advice helps the people underneath; on the ground floor of a flathouse, the shop below you is usually closed at night, but the family across the landing is not.

A quieter setting saved for late evenings

Most current bars have a night mode, which narrows the gap between quiet and loud passages, and a speech or dialogue setting, which lifts voices. Together they let a late film be followed at a lower overall level. We save that combination where the bar allows it and show you how to switch it on from the remote or the app, so the evening setting is one button away instead of a menu dive.

When the owner lives downstairs

In many Greenpoint frame houses the owner occupies one floor and rents the others, and either side may book the visit. Whoever books, the person whose wall is being drilled must agree to it, and a tenant should have the owner's permission in writing for new holes. We are glad to talk to both, and to set the system up so its sound suits the house rather than one floor of it.

FAQ

Greenpoint Soundbar Installation FAQ

Our TV is curved — will a straight soundbar look odd, and does it need a special bracket?
+

A straight bar under a gently curved screen looks fine to most people, because the curve is shallow and the bar sits below the edge rather than along it. Fix it to the wall centred on the screen, close enough that its ends tuck under the curve. Curved TVs often have an unusual back, so a TV-attached soundbar bracket may not fit; check the maker’s compatibility list first, and if it does not fit, mount the bar on the wall.

Does the soundbar have to sit directly under the screen, or can it go on a shelf off to one side?
+

It works best centred under or just above the screen, because the bar carries the centre channel and voices should seem to come from the picture. A few inches off centre is barely noticeable; a bar on a shelf to one side makes dialogue sound as if it comes from beside the TV. If the space under the screen is impossible, mounting the bar on the TV itself usually beats a side shelf.

Our TV has only three HDMI sockets and they are all in use — does the soundbar need one of its own?
+

Yes, the bar should take the one labelled ARC or eARC, which is how the TV sends its sound out. If a cable box or console is already in that socket, move it to another, and if you run out, plug a source into the bar’s own HDMI input when it has one, or add an HDMI switch. We sort the sockets on the visit and label each lead so it stays right.

Does a bigger, heavier soundbar cost more to mount?
+

No. The published Brooklyn line for hanging a bar under the TV is the same whatever its size, and the wall and access lines are what change the total. Size matters in other ways: a long bar needs enough clear wall under the screen, and a heavier one needs a bracket rated for it, which we check before drilling. A full system with a subwoofer and rear speakers can add Sonos or home-theater setup lines.
