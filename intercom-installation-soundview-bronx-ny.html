The Neighborhood

Why Soundview Properties Need Intercom Installation

Soundview was built late and built quickly. Apartments.com's neighborhood guide says the area stayed largely undeveloped until the 1950s, when the Bronx River Parkway and the Bruckner Expressway were constructed, and Point2's census summary puts the median construction year at 1956. That one number matters more for intercoms than almost anything else about the neighborhood. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law, as Parker Custom Security summarizes it, requires an intercom at the self-locking main door of every apartment building of eight or more homes erected or converted after January 1, 1968. A building finished in 1956 sits on the other side of that line. In those older buildings, the sources we read describe the intercom as something tenants can require by asking as a majority, not something the law hands them automatically. So in a Soundview walk-up on Watson Avenue or Soundview Avenue, the question "does this building need an intercom" often has a different answer from the same question asked about a newer building three blocks away, and an owner, a tenant association and an installer all need to know which kind of building they are standing in before anyone talks about equipment.

The second thing that shapes intercom work here is who owns the door. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes Bronx River Houses, Soundview Houses and Clason Point Gardens as modernist brick towers surrounded by green courtyards, and Wikipedia records that Clason Point Gardens was the first public housing development the city's housing authority built in the Bronx. In those buildings the authority owns the entrances and decides who works on them; the city Comptroller's audit found broken locks on 40.1 percent of NYCHA entrance doors citywide, and NYCHA's own testimony describes new entrance intercoms, key fobs and doors under its layered access program. That work runs through NYCHA. Around those campuses, though, are thousands of homes that belong to private owners, co-op boards, condo associations and, in Harding Park, a homeowners association that bought the land from the city in 1981. Point2 counts 6 percent of Soundview's homes as detached houses, 8.5 percent as attached houses and 12.7 percent as two-unit buildings. Those are the doors this page is mostly about: the two-family where the owner and a tenant share one bell, the walk-up with a buzzer that rings but cannot talk, the co-op whose lobby panel calls over decades-old riser pairs, and the new building that opens with a networked panel nobody in the management office has used before.

Soundview facts that shape an intercom plan before any equipment is chosen

  • Much of the private stock predates 1968. Point2 gives a median construction year of 1956 and says about 36.6 percent of homes were built before the 1940s, with another 9 percent by 1949. Buildings that old fall under the older-building rules, not the post-1968 requirement.
  • Houses are a real share of the work. Detached, attached and two-unit buildings together are more than a quarter of the 15,405 homes Point2 counts, which means many two- and three-button jobs, not only lobby consoles.
  • Walk-ups line two avenues. CityNeighborhoods.nyc places modest apartment buildings along Watson Avenue and Soundview Avenue, the kind of building where a buzzer system rings each apartment and a riser climbs one stair hall.
  • Doors face courtyards. The postwar campuses CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes were built around green courtyards, so the entrance a visitor needs is often off a path, not a curb.
  • The street grid is incomplete in places. Streets that end midblock in Clason Point and the unnamed lanes of Harding Park make clear labeling and well-placed stations worth more than in a neat grid.
  • No landmark district turned up. We found no confirmed historic district or individual landmark designation in Soundview; Wikipedia cites a Landmarks Preservation Commission source about Park Versailles to the north, but we could not confirm a designation, so we check each address.

Same-Day Repair in Soundview

Need Repair in Soundview? A Walk-Up Buzzer That Rings but Won't Talk, Upper Floors That Went Silent, or a Lobby Panel That Freezes

Repair calls from Soundview buildings tend to sort into five patterns. The first is the walk-up where the buzzer still rings each apartment but nobody can speak to the visitor, or the speech is so faint and crackly that tenants simply buzz anyone in. In buildings of this age that usually means the amplifier or the door speaker has worn out, or the old talk pair in the riser has broken somewhere between floors. The second is a building where everything below a certain floor works and everything above it is dead. That points at the riser: a splice in a junction box on one landing that has corroded, a pair nicked during a renovation, or cable damaged by heat or water in the stair hall. The third is the release that hums or clicks upstairs while the street door stays locked. That is almost never the intercom. It is the strike, the latch or the door: a door that has been slammed for years until the strike's keeper has bent, a latch that now lands an eighth of an inch from where the strike was cut, or a closer that leaves the door pressing on the strike so it cannot let go. The fourth shows up in two-families north of Lacombe Avenue: a visitor presses the tenant's button and the owner's chime answers, because a second button was tapped into the first one's wire decades ago. The fifth comes from co-op and condo lobbies and newer buildings: a panel that freezes, reboots or drops calls because its power supply is tired, its internet or cellular link has dropped, or nobody remembers who holds the administrator login.

Until the repair, keep the street door locking with its key, and resist the urge to leave it propped so visitors can get in; that only trades a broken buzzer for an open building. If the transformer or amplifier in the cellar is hot to the touch, buzzing loudly or smells burnt, switch off its breaker and leave it off. Then call (646) 490-0629. Many single faults, such as a failed amplifier, a dead transformer, a corroded splice on one landing, a strike that needs to move to where the latch really lands, or a panel that lost its programming, are found and fixed on one visit. On the phone we tell you honestly what the photos let us promise and what they don't. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we estimate the tracing time before we start. Yelp reviews of Bronx intercom companies include customers who waited past a four-hour window or never heard that a technician was running late, so we give a time, and if that time changes, we call. If a repair would cost a large share of a new system, or the old parts are no longer made, we write down both choices so you don't pay to patch something that will need replacing next year.

The walk-up on Watson or Soundview Avenue: a buzzer that rings, a riser that climbs one stair, and a rule that depends on the year

The modest apartment buildings CityNeighborhoods.nyc places along Watson Avenue and Soundview Avenue are where most of our Soundview intercom work happens. A typical one has a street door, sometimes a vestibule door, a row of buttons or a panel beside the entrance, and a cellar where every apartment's pair of wires starts. In the oldest systems, a button at the door rings a buzzer in the apartment and a button in the apartment buzzes the strike, and that is all. There is no voice, so the tenant either walks down or buzzes in whoever is there. Later systems added a speaker at the door and a handset in each apartment, run from an amplifier in the cellar, and many of those amplifiers and handsets are now well past the age where parts are easy to find.

Before we quote one of these buildings we establish three things. The first is the count: how many apartments the building actually has today, because a building that was split or combined over the years may not match its original button plate. The second is the date the building was erected or converted, which decides whether the post-1968 requirement applies or whether the older-building rules do; the rules section below lays out what the sources say about each. The third is the riser. We tone and test every pair from the cellar to each apartment. In many buildings of this age, most of the old cable is continuous and clean enough to carry a modern two-wire audio or video system, and reusing it saves opening plaster on every landing. Where pairs fail, or where a junction box on one floor holds a nest of splices, we replace that section or pull a new riser through the stair hall in surface raceway, painted to match, and we show the owner the route before any drilling.

The door gets as much attention as the electronics. In a building of three or more households, HPD's ABCs of Housing says the building entrance door must be self-closing, and the strike has to let it relatch after every release. Street doors in these buildings are often steel, heavily used and slammed, so we check the hinges, the closer and the frame before the strike goes in, and we fit a strike rated for the traffic, not the lightest one that fits the hole. A new panel beside a door that hangs open defeats the purpose of the whole job.

The attached or two-family house: two households and a bell from another era

Point2 counts 8.5 percent of Soundview's homes as attached houses and 12.7 percent as two-unit buildings, and CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes one- and two-family houses filling the streets north of Lacombe Avenue. In many of them, the house was built with one bell and one chime, and a second button was added when an upper floor or a lower floor was rented. Often the second button shares the first one's transformer and part of its wire, so a visitor for the tenant rings the owner's hall, or both floors ring at once, and only one floor can release the door, if anyone can.

We give each household its own line from the start. The existing bell wire is tested, and where one run is long and clean, it carries a two-wire audio or video station to one floor. The other floor usually gets a new run along the cellar ceiling and up through a closet, a pipe chase or a corner of a shared hall, whichever keeps the cable out of the other household's rooms. The door station gets two clearly labeled buttons, each ringing only its own station, and each household can release the street door for its own visitors. On video systems, each station sees the door only when its own button is pressed or when its own household chooses to look, and we set that up with both households present so nobody wonders what the other floor can see. The power supply goes in the cellar on its own labeled breaker, where the owner can reach it and neither household can switch off the other's intercom by accident. In attached houses we also keep every hole and anchor on our side of the party line, and choose a station that looks clearly different from the neighbor's if they have one.

The house with a household below: a station at the door that household actually uses

Some Soundview houses have someone living in the lower level who comes and goes by a side door or a door at the bottom of outside steps. The basement break-in Patch reported on Elder Avenue is a reminder that these doors deserve the same care as the front. We are not the people to decide whether a particular lower level is a legal apartment, and nothing we install changes a house's legal status. What we can do is give the person living there a reliable way to know someone is at their door and to see and speak to the caller before deciding whether to open it.

Where the lower household uses its own door, that door gets its own small video station and an indoor station inside, and the front station stays with the household upstairs. Where it shares the main door, the main station gets a second labeled button and the lower level gets its own indoor station, with the release limited to the street door. In a stairwell below grade, the station goes on the wall beside the door at a height where a standing visitor's face fills the picture, with a sealed cable entry so rain that collects in the well does not follow the wire inside, and a light that comes on when someone approaches if the well is dark. Cable for the lower station comes from above, along the ceiling, not across the floor, and the power supply stays upstairs or high on a wall. Every door we fit with an electric lock has to let the person inside get out without a key, a code or a button.

The Harding Park bungalow: small houses, narrow lanes and an association that owns the ground

Forgotten New York describes Harding Park as a maze of little unnamed streets and bungalows along Bronx River, Leland, Gildersleeve and Cornell Avenues, and The Neighborhoods newsletter records that the city sold Harding Park to the Homeowners Association of Harding Park in 1981, making it the first cooperatively owned low- and moderate-income community in New York. Wikipedia adds that Robert Moses tried to clear it and that it survived. For an intercom, three things follow. Visitors often walk in from the avenue along a lane, so the door station needs to be easy to see from the lane and labeled with the house number in large type. The houses are close together, so the camera is aimed at the step in front of the door and not into the neighbor's window across the way. And anything that leaves the footprint of the house, such as a cable to a gate, crosses ground whose ownership and rules belong to the association, so we ask the owner to check with the association before we plan a run outside the walls.

The co-op or condo tower: a lobby panel, a riser and a board

News 12 has described 825 Morrison Avenue as a 19-story building, and Brick Underground has written about co-op apartments there; Redfin lists condo buildings with recent sales at 875 Morrison Avenue, 880 Boynton Avenue and 1869 Lafayette Avenue. Buildings of this kind have a lobby panel that must reach every apartment, a directory that changes every time someone moves, and a board or association that decides and pays. Co-op and condo work therefore starts with the decision makers. We survey the lobby, the panel, the riser and a sample of apartments, including top-floor lines and lines that have given trouble, and write a scope the board can vote on: which parts stay, which are replaced, what happens to apartments whose owners bought their own monitors, and how the work is scheduled floor by floor so nobody goes without a working door.

The big choice in these buildings is between keeping the riser and moving to a panel that calls phones or networked stations. A tested riser can carry a new two-wire system to every apartment. A panel that calls residents' phones needs only power and an internet or cellular connection at the lobby, but leaves residents who prefer a wall station needing one. Most current systems can mix both on one panel, household by household. During the changeover the lobby door keeps locking with its key. Afterward, the super gets a plain-language note on where the power supply is, which breaker feeds it, who can change the directory and what to check first when a resident says the intercom is dead.

The new building near Soundview Park: required equipment on the drawings, not after them

Lemle & Wolff describes a Soundview project with 120 one-, two- and three-bedroom apartments in an eight-story building adjacent to Soundview Park, and 86 one-bedroom apartments for low-income seniors in another eight-story building. New buildings of this size, with eight or more apartments, fall under the post-1968 requirement for a two-way voice intercom between each apartment and the front door, with tenants able to release it. For these buildings the intercom is planned with the general contractor: the lobby panel's location and height, the cable path from the panel to an equipment closet and up to every apartment, the strike or release on each entrance, and a dedicated circuit for the power supply, which a licensed electrician installs.

A senior building adds its own needs. Residents may take longer to reach a station, may hear less well or see less well, and may not use smartphones. We choose indoor stations with large buttons, adjustable ring volume and a visual signal, set the call to ring long enough for someone to cross a room, and keep wall stations as the default with app answering available to residents who want it. Where the panel is networked, the closet gets a switch on battery backup and a documented connection, and the owner, not the installer, holds the administrator account. Before move-in, every apartment is called from the lobby, every release is tested while someone watches the door relatch, and the directory is loaded.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Survey a Soundview Walk-Up Before Writing a Quote

Start on the sidewalk, not in the cellar. The technician walks up to the building the way a visitor would, from the bus stop or the 6 train, and notes which door a stranger would try first. In buildings with courtyard entrances, that is often not the door the owner thinks it is.

Ring every button with someone upstairs. We press each button while a tenant or the super stands in a sample of apartments, including the top floor. One test usually shows whether whole floors are silent, whether some buttons ring the wrong home, and whether voice works anywhere at all.

Count the homes, not the buttons. Old panels often have buttons for apartments that were combined or split years ago. The survey sheet records who actually lives where today.

Go to the cellar next. The amplifier or transformer, its breaker, and the way the riser leaves it tell the age of the system. A unit wired straight into a junction box, a second supply added beside it, or pairs spliced with tape tell us what can stay.

Walk the stair hall. On each landing we look for junction boxes, raceway, damage from past renovations or fires, and the place a new riser could climb without crossing anyone's apartment.

Close the door and let it go. We open the street door fully and release it. If it doesn't close and latch on its own, the door work goes into the quote before any strike does.

Ask who answers and how. Before quoting, we ask the owner or board which households want a wall station, which want phone answering, which language instructions should be left in, and who keeps the administrator login.

Brick, Risers and Power

Brick Walls, Stair-Hall Risers and a Power Supply That Stays On: Wiring a Soundview Building

Testing old pairs before trusting them. Many current two-wire intercoms carry voice, video and the door release over a single good pair, so a clean old riser can save opening walls. We check each pair for continuity from the cellar to the apartment, look for breaks at junction boxes and staples, and measure leakage between conductors. A pair that passes is reused. A pair that fails, or that shares a splice with another apartment, is replaced, because a new station on a bad pair inherits every fault the old system had.

Running a new riser through masonry. Postwar brick and block buildings rarely have hollow walls to fish through. Where a new riser is needed, the cleanest route is usually up the stair hall in a surface raceway, fastened to masonry and painted to match, with a short branch into each apartment beside its door. Where the building has a pipe chase or an unused shaft that can legally take low-voltage cable, we use it. We avoid routes through apartments altogether when we can, and when we can't, we say so and agree it with the owner and the household first.

Crossing courtyards and connecting wings. Some buildings have entrances on two sides of a courtyard, or a side door far from the cellar. Cable between them runs in conduit along the building, or underground in conduit where an existing path allows, never loose across a lawn or stapled to a fence. Where trenching would mean breaking up paving or plantings, a wireless or network link between buildings sometimes does the job, and we explain the trade-offs in the quote.

Fitting the release to the door that is actually there. Soundview street doors range from original wooden doors on two-families to steel doors with push bars on walk-ups and aluminum-and-glass doors in tower lobbies. A strike lives in the frame, so we look at the jamb, the latch's position with the door pushed shut, and whether the door has sagged. Steel doors with a push bar on the inside often need an exit device with an electric latch retraction or an electrified strike made for that hardware, not an ordinary strike. Wherever an electric lock goes, the door has to open freely from inside without a key, a code or a button.

Making the door close and latch. In a building of three or more households, the entrance door must be self-closing, and the strike must let it latch after every release. We adjust or replace the closer so the door shuts firmly past the strike without slamming, then test by releasing from several apartments and letting the door close on its own each time.

Putting power where it belongs. The power supply goes on its own labeled circuit, mounted where the super or the owner can find it, and connected so nobody can unplug it while plugging in something else. It should not sit inside any one apartment, where one household could switch off everyone else's intercom. Any new circuit is installed by a licensed electrician; low-voltage intercom work is done under our New York State license #12000287431.

Checking the connection a networked panel depends on. A lobby panel that calls phones needs internet or a cellular connection. Cellars and equipment closets in masonry buildings can be poor places for a cellular signal, so we measure it where the equipment will sit before choosing that option, and we plan an antenna or a wired connection if the reading is weak.

Leaving a record. Every job ends with a written note: which pair serves which apartment, where the power supply is and which breaker feeds it, the strike and closer models, and, for networked systems, the administrator details. Buildings here change hands and change supers, and that page is what keeps the next repair short.

The Rules at the Door

Before 1968, After 1968 and Under Eight Homes: What New York's Rules Mean for a Soundview Intercom

Soundview's mix means the same block can hold a two-family no intercom rule touches, a 1950s walk-up covered by the older-building rules and a new building that must have a station in every apartment. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, and where they disagree, so owners, boards, developers and tenants can ask the right questions of counsel, HPD or the Department of Buildings.

Buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. Parker Custom Security summarizes Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a as requiring every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after that date with eight or more apartments to have an intercommunication system at its self-locking main entrance door. The new eight-story buildings beside Soundview Park fall into this group.

Older buildings of eight or more apartments. With a median construction year of 1956, much of Soundview's private apartment stock is older than the 1968 line. The sources we read describe these buildings as needing an intercom when a majority of the apartments request one, and the Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01, written for buildings erected or converted before 1968, describes the minimum system as a bell or buzzer system or a speaking and listening device. The statute text quoted on JustAnswer adds that in housing-authority buildings, the installation need not be completed until one year after the owner receives requests or consents from a majority of tenants. If you own or live in an older Soundview building of eight or more homes, the date the building was erected or converted is the first fact to confirm.

Buildings of fewer than eight apartments, where the sources disagree. Parker Custom Security says buildings with fewer than eight apartments are not covered by the intercom requirement, though the self-closing and self-locking door rule still applies. Seneca Security says Housing Maintenance Code section 27-2045 applies to most residential buildings with three or more units, while Impera Security cites section 27-2044 for buildings with more than eight. Those cannot all describe the same rule. For a Soundview building of three to seven homes, ask counsel or HPD and read the code text rather than relying on any website, including this one.

One- and two-family houses. A one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling, and none of the sources we read require it to have an intercom. Whatever goes on the door of a house north of Lacombe Avenue or a Harding Park bungalow is the owner's choice.

Video is not required. Impera Security says a basic audio intercom meets the requirement. We recommend video where households want it and the wire can carry it, not because any rule asks for it.

Broken intercoms, complaints and violations. Parker Custom Security describes a broken intercom as a Class B hazardous violation that HPD enforces on tenant complaint. HPD's code-enforcement guide tells tenants to contact the landlord first and, if the landlord is unresponsive, to complain to 311 by phone, online or through the app, and says the inspection should be within 30 days. HPD's ABCs of Housing says the manager, superintendent or owner should immediately repair broken locks or intercoms.

Self-closing doors. In all multiple dwellings, HPD says building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors must be self-closing, and its self-closing door rule says HPD will select 300 buildings each year for self-closing door inspections. A trade summary of the city's self-closing door law describes a failure to keep these doors in good repair as a class C immediately hazardous violation. A strike has to work with that rule, never against it.

Mounting heights. 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. On a stoop or a sunken stairwell we measure from where a visitor actually stands. In places open to the public, accessibility reach rules usually put buttons lower.

Permits and licensing, where sources also disagree. The Department of Buildings' electrical page says no installation or modification to any electrical system can be performed without a permit and a licensed master electrician. A trade guide says low-voltage work under 50 volts typically does not need a traditional electrical permit but must be done by a business with a certified low-voltage installer. We hold a New York State license for low-voltage work, and we bring in a licensed electrician for any new line-voltage circuit; where a filing question comes up, we raise it before work starts.

Landmarks. We found no confirmed historic district or individual landmark in Soundview. That suggests Landmarks Preservation Commission facade rules don't reach these doors, but a search that found nothing is not a confirmation, so we check the address.

Getting out. Every door fitted with an electric lock must still open freely from inside, without a key, a code or a button. Every system we install controls the way in only.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Soundview: Questions Answered

These are situations Soundview owners, boards, households and managers run into: walk-ups after a fire, buildings that share equipment, basement households, family businesses at home, mixed buildings and complaint inspections. Each is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ below.

Walk-ups after damage, and buildings that share equipment

After a fire in our walk-up's stair hall, every apartment above the third floor lost its intercom. Does heat and smoke damage mean the whole riser has to be replaced?

Not always the whole riser, but almost always the part that was in the fire. News 12 and Hoodline have reported apartment fires in Soundview buildings in 2026, and after any fire like that, low-voltage cable in the affected hall deserves suspicion even where it looks fine. Heat softens and cracks insulation, smoke and soot leave residue that can carry current between conductors in damp weather, and water from firefighting fills junction boxes. A pattern like yours, where every floor above one point is dead, usually means the damage is at or just above that point. We start by confirming the building's electrician or restoration contractor has cleared the area and that nothing is energized that shouldn't be. Then we test each pair from the cellar, find where continuity breaks, and open the junction boxes on the damaged landings. Cable that ran through the burned or soaked section is replaced, not reused, even if it tests well that day, because it tends to fail later. Cable below the damage that tests clean can stay. If the stair hall is being rebuilt anyway, that is the cheapest moment to put the new riser in a raceway or chase that the next owner can reach. Keep the photos and our written findings for the insurance claim, because the adjuster will want to see what was replaced and why.

Our family owns two neighboring walk-ups that have run off one intercom amplifier in the first building's cellar since before we bought them. We may sell one. How do we separate the systems?

Separate them before the sale, not after. A shared amplifier in one building's cellar means the other building's tenants depend on a room the new owner of the first building controls, on that building's electrical service and on whatever cable crosses between them, through a party wall, a shared cellar or an outside run. Once the buildings have different owners, nobody is responsible for the shared parts, and a fault in one can silence both. The work is usually straightforward. We trace which pairs serve which building, label them, and give the second building its own door station, amplifier or power supply and riser connection on its own electrical service. Any cable that crosses between the buildings is cut and capped at the line, and the record for each building shows only its own system. You can keep the same make and model in both, which simplifies parts and service, and it often makes sense to do both buildings in one schedule. Tell your attorney and the buyer what was separated and when, and hand the buyer the written record and any administrator details for the building they are buying.

Our street door and intercom get repaired, and within weeks something is broken again. What makes a repair last at an entrance that takes this much abuse?

Usually the door, not the electronics. The Comptroller's audit of NYCHA buildings found 40.1 percent of entrance door locks broken citywide and residents saying repaired doors and intercoms were soon broken again, and NYCHA told a Bronx paper that funding and repeated vandalism are its main obstacles. Private buildings see a smaller version of the same pattern. A strike fails early when the door slams into it hundreds of times a day, when the closer is set too hard or not at all, or when people pull the door while the latch is still engaged. A panel fails early when it is cheaply mounted, when buttons are pried, or when the cable is exposed where it can be yanked. For a lasting fix, we set the closer so the door shuts firmly but doesn't slam, fit a heavy-duty strike sized for the traffic, protect the hinge side, choose a vandal-resistant panel with recessed buttons and a metal face anchored into the masonry, bring the cable in from behind the panel instead of along the surface, and put the power supply where only the owner or super can reach it. We also ask why the door is being forced. If tenants prop it because their release doesn't work, or because visitors can't reach them, fixing that removes the reason. A door-ajar alert to the super helps catch a propped door before it becomes the new normal.

Houses, basement households and family businesses

After the break-in Patch reported at a basement apartment on Elder Avenue, the person who rents our lower level wants to see and talk to whoever is at her door without opening it. Will a video station really help her decide?

It helps with the decision, which is the part she can control. A video station beside her door, with an indoor monitor inside, lets her see the caller's face, hear and answer them, and refuse to open without the caller knowing whether she is home. For that to work in practice, three details matter. The camera has to be at the height of a standing visitor's face at the bottom of the steps, not on the sidewalk above, and the stairwell needs light, either from the station itself or from a fixture that comes on when someone approaches. Her monitor should be where she can reach it quickly from the room she uses most, not only beside the door. And she should be able to talk without opening, so a caller who claims to be a delivery or a utility worker can be told to leave a package or show identification to the camera. Just as important is the door itself: a solid door, a frame that holds, and a lock she uses every time. The station doesn't replace any of that, and we tell her so. If she shares the main door with your household instead, her button on the main station rings only her monitor, and her release opens only the door she uses. Her station's cable comes from above and her power supply stays where your household can reach it.

My wife runs a family daycare on the ground floor of our two-family. How should the door work at drop-off and pickup without parents wandering up to the tenant's floor?

Start by asking the agency that licenses the daycare what it requires at the entrance, because we don't know those rules and won't guess at them. From the intercom side, the usual layout gives the daycare its own labeled button on the street station, ringing a station in the daycare room rather than the family's kitchen, with a video picture so whoever is with the children can see who is at the door before releasing it. The tenant upstairs keeps a separate button and station that the daycare's station can't answer. If the house has an inner door between the shared entry and the stair to the upper floor, that door stays locked and is not released by the daycare's station, so a parent who comes in the street door can only reach the daycare. Where the daycare has its own side entrance, giving that door its own station is cleaner still. Some systems can show a short clip of who rang, which some providers find useful for confirming who collected a child, but recording at a home business raises its own privacy questions, so decide that with the licensing agency and the families. Whatever is installed, the daycare's exit door must open from inside without a key or a button.

We just closed on a Soundview two-family, and the seller's video doorbell was tied to the seller's own phone account. Nothing came with the house. What should we do in the first week?

Assume someone else may still be able to see your front door until you prove otherwise. Many consumer video doorbells are registered to one account in the maker's app, and unless the seller removed the device from that account, it may still be linked to their phone. Ask the seller, through your attorney if needed, to remove it from their account. If that doesn't happen, the maker's support process for a new owner, or a factory reset, usually breaks the link; check the maker's instructions for your model. Then decide whether a single-household doorbell is what this house needs. In a two-family, one doorbell on one account means one household sees every visitor to both, and nothing releases the street door from either floor. If both floors will be occupied, a two-button station with a separate indoor station for each household, and a release each one controls, is usually the better fit, and the old doorbell can come down. While you're at it, have the existing bell wire tested, find the transformer and its breaker in the cellar, and write down what you find. Sellers rarely leave that information, and it saves time on every repair after.

Towers, condos and mixed buildings

The lobby panel we're considering would call residents over a cellular connection, but the only spot for its equipment is our cellar, where nobody's phone gets a signal. Is that a dealbreaker?

No, but it has to be solved before the panel is chosen, not after. Masonry cellars in postwar Soundview buildings can be close to dead zones, and a cellular module that drops calls defeats the point of the system. On the survey we measure signal strength where the equipment will actually sit, not where the super's phone happens to have bars. If the reading is weak, there are several answers. The cellular module can sit in the lobby or on an upper floor while the power supply stays in the cellar, with a cable between them. An external antenna can be mounted higher or on the outside of the building with its cable run to the module. Or the panel can use a wired internet connection instead, if the building has one it can dedicate to the intercom rather than sharing a tenant's Wi-Fi. We also think about what happens when that connection fails. A panel that calls phones should have a fallback, such as a few wall stations on a local wire or a way for the super to be reached, so a lost signal doesn't lock out every visitor for a day. The quote states which connection we're using, where its parts sit and what the fallback is.

Our building on Westchester Avenue is a condo with a store unit and six apartments sharing one front wall. How should the cost of a new intercom be split between the store and the residents?

The condo's declaration and bylaws decide that, not the installer, so read them or ask the board's attorney before anyone agrees on a split. What we can do is write the quote so any split is easy. We list separately the parts that serve only the apartments, such as the residents' door station, the apartment stations and the riser, the parts that serve only the store, such as a station at its back door or a release on its entrance, and the parts that genuinely serve everyone, such as a shared power supply, an equipment closet or work on a shared wall. Many mixed buildings find that the residents' intercom is a common element of the residential units and the store's entry system is the store's own, but that's a reading of documents we haven't seen, so treat it as a question, not an answer. Whatever the split, keep the systems separate in operation: no store station that releases the residents' door, and no apartment station that opens the store. Separate systems also make the next repair simpler to bill, because each fault is clearly one side's or the other's.

Our walk-up's street door has a push bar on the inside instead of a knob. Can the apartments still release it with a buzzer?

Yes, but usually not with the ordinary strike you'd fit beside a knob latch. A push bar is an exit device, and its latch is built differently: it may latch at the top and bottom of the door, at the side, or both. Releasing it from an apartment generally means one of two things. One is an electrified strike designed to work with that particular exit device's latch, set in the frame where the latch lands. The other is an electric latch retraction kit fitted inside the exit device, which pulls the latch back when an apartment presses the release. Both keep the push bar working as it should, so anyone inside can still leave by pushing it, which is the point of the device. The choice depends on the make and model of the bar, the frame, how heavy the traffic is and how much power the release needs, which affects the power supply. We read the label on the device during the survey, check the latch positions, and quote the option that fits. Whatever we install, the bar keeps opening from inside with one push, and the door still relatches on its own after a release.

Complaints and inspections

A tenant in our Soundview walk-up filed a 311 complaint about the intercom. Before HPD's inspector comes, what should the owner have working?

We can't speak for HPD's checklist, but we can tell you what a working system looks like from the door, which is what a tenant complaint is about. HPD's code-enforcement guide says the inspection should come within 30 days of the complaint, and its ABCs of Housing says broken intercoms should be repaired immediately, so the time to act is now, not the day before. In a building the rules cover, every apartment should be reachable from the door, every apartment should be able to talk to the visitor and release the door, and the door should close and lock on its own after each release, because HPD treats self-closing entrance doors as a separate requirement. In practice that means calling each apartment from the street panel with someone listening upstairs, releasing from each one while someone watches the door relatch, and fixing any that fail. Keep a written note of what was repaired, when and by whom, plus our test results apartment by apartment. If a single station or a single riser pair is the problem, that can often be fixed on its own. If the system is beyond repair, an interim fix and a written plan for replacement are better than leaving it as it is. Tell the tenant who complained what has been done; a building where tenants know repairs are underway is easier for everyone.
