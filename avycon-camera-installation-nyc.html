The Neighborhood

Why NYC Properties Need AVYCON Camera Installation

New York is a city of old buildings asked to do new things, and a wired camera system is one of the most demanding of those things. The Citizens Housing and Planning Council reports that more than 80 percent of the city's housing was built more than 50 years ago, and that over half of all units, about 2 million homes, went up before 1947. The Rent Guidelines Board's 2026 housing supply report puts the pre-1947 share at 54 percent of units, out of a total stock of 3,705,000. CHPC describes the range as running from mansions in Riverdale to small homes in Midland Beach, from garden apartments in Sunnyside to high-rises near Central Park, from brownstones to tenements. Every one of those building types hides a different path between a camera and the recorder that powers it. An AVYCON system, like most modern camera systems, runs on Power over Ethernet: one network cable from the recorder to each camera carries the picture one way and the power the other. That is a clean, reliable design. It also means a physical cable has to travel from the cellar or a back office to the stoop, the lobby, the hallways, the rear yard and the roof, through brick, stone, plaster, terra cotta and concrete laid down a century ago.

The city's history explains why those paths are so varied. Manhattan, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island became one city on January 1, 1898, after a consolidation vote that New York City's archaeology office records as 64,744 in favor and 64,467 against. The subway opened in 1904 and tied the new metropolis together, and building followed the lines outward. The Architectural League's history of New York housing describes most tenements going up quickly and in huge numbers between 1860 and 1910 in Manhattan, Brooklyn and later the Bronx, on lots that were usually 25 feet wide, while rowhouses spread into Brooklyn, the Bronx and Queens as they were linked to Manhattan. A walk-up tenement from that period has a cellar, a narrow stair hall, a roof bulkhead and a rear yard, and every one of those is a place an owner wants to see. A 1920s elevator building adds a lobby, a mail area, a laundry room, a service entrance and sometimes a courtyard. A postwar tower adds parking, a package room and a dozen floors of identical corridors. The camera count changes, but the problem is always the same: getting a certified cable to each spot without damaging the building or annoying the people who live in it.

The second reason New York properties need careful installation is that a camera here sits inside a web of people and rules. Most residents rent. The Independent Budget Office counts about 3.7 million housing units in the city, two-thirds of them rentals, and about one million rent-stabilized apartments. HPD's housing and vacancy survey findings put 829,200 units, 22 percent of the stock, in buildings with 100 or more apartments. In prewar buildings, HPD found that only 12 percent of units were condos or co-ops, and about 70 percent of those were co-ops. So in most buildings someone other than the person asking for cameras has to agree: a landlord, a managing agent, a co-op board, a condo board, or all of them. There is no general city law that requires cameras in apartment buildings, as compliance writers at Seneca Security point out, but many commercial property insurers set camera coverage as a condition of theft and vandalism coverage, and owners respond to break-ins, package thefts and lobby incidents. Tenants, meanwhile, have pushed back when cameras turn into something more. At Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville, two brick high-rises that already had cameras on the grounds, doorways, elevators and halls, tenants fought the landlord's plan to add facial-recognition entry. The city's Tenant Data Privacy Law, adopted in 2021, now limits what owners can do with data collected by smart entry systems.

The third reason is specific to AVYCON. The AVYCON ONE app offers AI filtering for motion, people and vehicle detection, license plate reading and facial recognition. Those features are useful, but in New York a few of them carry legal weight. Lawyers at Kennedys explain that ordinary CCTV appears to be exempt from the city's biometric identifier signage requirement, as long as a commercial establishment does not use software to analyze the footage. Turn on facial recognition in a store, a restaurant or a theater lobby and the rule may apply: a sign at every customer entrance, at least 8.5 by 11 inches, and individuals can sue for five hundred dollars per violation for a missing sign, according to Hinshaw & Culbertson. The same app also draws real complaints. On the App Store, AVYCON ONE carries a 2.9 out of 5 rating, and one reviewer wrote that the app seemed to hang about half the time when viewing cameras and that their installer also had trouble getting the app to save the device. In other words, an AVYCON system in New York is only finished when the building is wired, the AI settings are lawful for the property, and the app works on the phones of the people who need it. That is the job we do.

There is also the city's own camera network to think about. The NYPD's Domain Awareness System, as The Intercept reported in October 2025, draws on footage from tens of thousands of privately owned cameras outside shops, businesses and banks. Through a program described on NYC 311, any business with a security camera can choose to share live video with the NYPD during emergencies and register camera locations at NewYorkCityConnect.org. amNY reported in May 2024 that the "Connect New York" pilot, built on technology called Fusus developed by Axon, had expanded to nine precincts across the five boroughs after a demonstration that began in Flushing and College Point, Queens, and that businesses can choose how and when their footage is accessible. Whether to join is your decision, not ours, but if you plan to, the cameras should be aimed and recorded with that in mind, and the network should be set up so outside access is limited to what you approve.

Finally, the city keeps changing the outside of buildings. The Department of Buildings' facade inspection program covers buildings taller than six stories, with inspections on a multi-year cycle, and an "Unsafe" facade means the owner must immediately install protection such as a sidewalk shed. In April 2025 the Mayor's Office announced laws to shorten shed permits and raise under-shed lighting from 45 to 90 lumens, and a March 2026 release reported sheds down 17 percent citywide. NYCHA told the City Council in 2023 that cameras blocked by sidewalk sheds or other obstructions are relocated. Private owners face the same problem every time a shed goes up over an entrance: the camera that watched the door now watches plywood. A wired system that was installed with labeled cable and spare length can be moved in a visit. A system that was stapled up in a hurry often has to be rebuilt.

New York facts that shape an AVYCON install

  • Old housing stock: 54 percent of the city's 3,705,000 units are in buildings built before 1947, per the Rent Guidelines Board, so most cable runs cross masonry and plaster.
  • Small prewar buildings: in one listings sample reported by RentReboot, 47.7 percent of prewar buildings had fewer than 10 apartments and only 41.5 percent listed an elevator, which means walk-ups with cable running floor by floor.
  • Mostly renters: about two-thirds of units are rentals and about one million are rent-stabilized, per the Independent Budget Office, so owner or board approval is part of most jobs.
  • A large protected stock: the Landmarks Preservation Commission protects 1,460 individual landmarks, 121 interior landmarks and 12 scenic landmarks, and its rules cover security cameras on regulated buildings.
  • Public housing cameras at scale: NYCHA testified in April 2025 to over 20,000 cameras across 214 developments and 1,519 buildings, which shows what citywide coverage looks like when it is planned.
  • Licensing: New York State requires a license from the Department of State to install or maintain security systems, and the rule covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement.

What owners usually add after the cameras. Once the cameras are in, the next request in most New York buildings is the front door: a video intercom so the person in the lobby can be seen and answered from a phone, or a change to the buzzer panel. A loose termination, a crushed run or a cable that is too long for the standard can produce a camera that drops out every few hours or goes dark when the heat comes on. The walk-up tenement

The four-to-six-story walk-up is the most common call in large parts of Manhattan, Brooklyn and the Bronx, and it is the one where planning saves the most money. Old Law and New Law tenements were built for gas light and coal heat, not for data. There is rarely a shaft we can use. What there usually is: a cellar with a ceiling of timber joists or steel and arches, a stair hall that runs from vestibule to roof, a roof bulkhead, and a rear yard reached through the cellar or a ground-floor door. The owner's list is predictable: the vestibule and front door, the mailboxes, the cellar door, the rear yard, sometimes the roof door and one camera per landing. The recorder goes where it can be locked and where it has a power outlet and the internet modem nearby, which is usually the cellar. From there, cameras at the front and rear are reached along the cellar ceiling and up through the floor or wall at each end. Hallway cameras are reached by a surface raceway that climbs the stair hall beside the existing wiring, painted to match if the owner wants. The roof door camera is the longest run in the building, and on a six-story walk-up it can approach the distance limit for a single network cable, which we measure, not guess.

The 1910s and 1920s elevator building

An eight-to-twelve-story elevator building from the decades after the subway arrived brings more cameras and more rules. The lobby, the elevator landings, the laundry room, the service entrance and the courtyard all come into play, and the building often already has an old camera system, usually analog cameras on coaxial cable feeding a DVR in the super's office. In buildings like these, we look first at what can be reused. AVYCON's own support guidance shows its recorders talking to third-party cameras over ONVIF, but old analog cameras need either replacement or a different kind of recorder, so the honest conversation is about which runs are worth keeping as pathways and which cameras are worth replacing. Many of these buildings are co-ops. A board may require the alteration agreement, proof of insurance and limited working hours, and any new opening through a fire-rated wall or floor has to be sealed properly. We plan the risers before we quote so the board sees exactly where every new hole goes.

The brownstone, rowhouse and two-family

The one- to four-family houses of Brooklyn, Harlem, the Bronx, Queens and Staten Island are simpler buildings but fussier surfaces. Brownstone is soft, and a front facade on a stoop house is the one everyone sees, so we avoid drilling the stone face where a mortar joint, a cornice return or a window reveal will do. A camera over the parlor-floor door often works best mounted under the door hood or on the side of the stoop, with its cable dropping straight into the basement. The rear of these houses is usually brick and much more forgiving, and the rear yard is often where the real security problem lies. In a two-family, the owner may want each unit to see the front door and only the owner to see the yard and the basement, which an AVYCON NVR can do by giving each user access to particular cameras. If any part of the street front is in a historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules apply, and we cover that further down.

The postwar and newer tower

Newer towers are the easiest to wire and the hardest to coordinate. Most have a telecom room or riser closet, ceiling grid in the corridors, and garages and loading areas where cables can run on J-hooks or in conduit. The work is fast, but it is usually done for a managing agent under a written scope, with a certificate of insurance, elevator reservations and set hours. These buildings also tend to have other systems already in the risers, including the intercom, the fire alarm and the internet provider's equipment, and we keep camera cable separate from all of them and labeled so the next contractor knows what is ours.

The storefront under apartments

The ground-floor store is New York's most common business setting: a bodega, a laundromat, a pharmacy, a restaurant or a phone repair shop with apartments above. The owner usually controls only the store, not the building, which limits what can be drilled outside. Inside, the recorder goes in the back office or under the counter, cameras watch the register, the door, the aisles and the rear exit, and an exterior camera, if the landlord allows it, watches the sidewalk in front of the door. The cable runs above the drop ceiling where there is one and through the stockroom where there is not. The legal questions in a store are different from those in an apartment building: facial recognition and the biometric sign rule, audio recording, and whether to share with the NYPD. Those are covered in the business section below.

Decide what you need to prove

The most useful question we ask in New York is not "how many cameras" but "what happened last time, and what would the footage have needed to show." A package thief in a lobby is a face and a time. A car hitting a gate is a plate and a direction. A dispute about who left the cellar door open is a wide view of a door and a clock that is correct. Write down two or three incidents, real or likely, and we design the system backwards from them. That usually means fewer cameras placed better, with a high-detail camera at the one spot where faces matter, rather than a ring of wide cameras that show a lot and identify no one.

Know who can and cannot see the footage

Before the first camera goes up, decide who gets access. In a rental building that usually means the owner and the managing agent, sometimes the super, and in a co-op the board's designee. Tenants do not get access to common-area recordings by default, and the person who manages access should be named, not assumed. If your building also uses a key fob or app-based entry system, the Tenant Data Privacy Law limits how data from that system can be used and requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days, so keep the camera system's logins separate and documented. We set up one AVYCON ONE login per person, with only the cameras they need, and give you the list.

Check the power and the internet

An AVYCON NVR powers its cameras through its PoE ports, so the recorder itself needs a reliable outlet, ideally on a circuit that is not shared with a boiler, a freezer or a washing machine. If power in the building is unreliable, a small battery backup keeps the recorder and cameras running through short outages. The recorder also needs a network cable to the internet router for remote viewing. In walk-ups where the only internet is in one tenant's apartment, the owner usually needs a separate building line; ask your provider about it before the install date, because we cannot connect the recorder to a tenant's private network.

On masonry fronts. Most New York facades are brick or stone, and both take a camera well if the fixings go in the right place. In brick, anchors go into the face of a sound brick, not the mortar joint, wherever the camera's base allows. In brownstone, which is a sandstone and much softer, we drill slowly with a sharp masonry bit, avoid carved features entirely and prefer a mounting point on an adjacent wood door hood or a joint in a plain wall. Every exterior penetration gets sealed so water does not follow the cable into the building. Where the camera is on a landmarked building, the LPC Permit Guidebook asks for a camera on a primary facade to be as small as possible and painted to match, so we choose the smallest housing that will do the job and finish it accordingly.

On plaster ceilings and stair halls. Inside prewar buildings, hallway cameras often go on plaster ceilings or high on plaster walls. Plaster over wood lath or metal lath is brittle, and a camera bracket screwed into lath alone will loosen over time. We find the framing or the masonry behind it, use fixings rated for the material, and run cable in a slim raceway along the line where the wall meets the ceiling so it disappears into the trim. In a building with pressed-tin ceilings, we avoid the tin and mount on the wall instead.

On fire separations. In many buildings the camera cable has to pass through a floor, a stair enclosure or a wall between the cellar and the boiler room. Where it does, the opening is sealed with a firestop product rated for that kind of assembly, not left open or stuffed with insulation. Altering fire-rated door assemblies, changing a means of egress or tying into the building's fire alarm is a different kind of work that can require filing, and we stay out of all three on a camera job.

What a business camera has to do in New York

A store camera in New York works harder than a home camera. It watches a door that opens hundreds of times a day, a register where cash and cards change hands, and aisles where shoplifting happens in seconds, and it records around the clock because a dispute may surface a week later. It also has to work in poor conditions: a bright window behind a dark interior, a sidewalk in full sun at noon and in shadow by three, a steel gate that comes down at night and blocks the exterior view. We place a camera inside the door looking in, so faces are lit by the window rather than silhouetted against it, and a second over the register at an angle that catches hands and the counter without catching a customer's PIN. Recording retention is set to how quickly the owner realistically finds out about a problem, and the recorder is locked away from the sales floor so a thief cannot walk off with the evidence. Where an exterior camera is allowed by the landlord, it goes under the sign band or inside the gate box, not where a ladder or a pole can reach it.

For restaurants and bars, the kitchen door, the cash area and the rear delivery entrance usually matter more than the dining room. For laundromats and pharmacies, the counter and the door. For garages and warehouses, the gate, the loading area and the plate of every vehicle that enters. That last one is where AVYCON's license plate reading in the app can help, and it is also where camera height and angle matter most: a plate camera has to see the plate straight on at a low enough angle, with lighting that works at night, or the software has nothing to read.

Rules New York businesses should know before switching cameras on

  • Biometric signage. The city's rules require a covered commercial establishment that collects biometric identifier information to post a clear sign at every customer entrance, at least 8.5 by 11 inches. Kennedys Law notes ordinary CCTV appears exempt as long as no software analyzes the footage, so turning on AVYCON facial recognition changes the picture. We leave it off unless you decide otherwise after reading the rule.
  • Penalties for a missing sign. Hinshaw & Culbertson reports that individuals can recover five hundred dollars per violation for a failure to post the required sign. That is a reason to make the decision before the install, not after.
  • Audio. Audio recording is governed by New York's eavesdropping law, which turns on consent of a party to the conversation. For stores and offices we normally leave camera microphones off; if you want audio, talk to your lawyer first.
  • Sharing with the NYPD. Sharing live video during emergencies through the city's program is voluntary, and the registration site named by NYC 311 is NewYorkCityConnect.org. amNY reported that businesses can choose how and when footage is accessible. A proposed state "safe streets security camera registry act" would create an NYPD inventory of private cameras on public spaces, but that is a bill, not law.
  • Licensing. NYC Business states that a license from the New York State Department of State is required to install or maintain security systems, and the underlying rule covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. Ask any installer for the license number.
  • Low-voltage wiring and permits.   • Insurance conditions. Many commercial property insurers set camera coverage as a condition of theft and vandalism coverage, according to Seneca Security. Bring the insurer's requirements to the walk-through and we design to them.

AVYCON Camera Installation in NYC: Questions Answered

AVYCON hardware and compatibility

Our building has a few good cameras from another brand. Will an AVYCON NVR record them alongside new AVYCON cameras?

Often yes, as long as those cameras speak ONVIF, the common standard that lets cameras and recorders from different makers talk. AVYCON's own support FAQ says a third-party camera that supports ONVIF can be added to its recorder using that protocol. In practice, basic video almost always works over ONVIF, while some extras, such as the other brand's own smart detection or two-way audio, may not carry over. On the walk-through we check each existing camera's model, test it against the recorder where we can, and tell you which ones are worth keeping. The ones that are not, usually older analog cameras on coaxial cable, are replaced, and their cable path is reused for new network cable where it is sound.

A managing agent wants the lobby cameras to show up in their own video software as well. How do other programs pull an AVYCON stream?

Most third-party video software connects to cameras through RTSP, a streaming protocol, and connection guides such as iSpy's list AVYCON cameras as typically using port 554 for it. That is the technical answer; the practical one in a New York building is about who should be allowed in. Opening a camera stream to outside software means opening a path into the building's network, so we set it up only for the specific program and user the owner approves, with a separate login, and never by forwarding ports wholesale on the building's router. If the agent only needs to watch, a viewer login in the AVYCON ONE app is often simpler and safer than connecting another system.

Our insurer and a grant program asked whether the cameras are NDAA compliant. What can we say about AVYCON?

We can tell you what AVYCON says and help you check the specific models. AVYCON's NDAA statement says its compliant products use approved chipsets and were manufactured and substantially transformed in the United States, South Korea and/or Vietnam. That is a statement about its compliant product line, not a blanket statement about every model ever sold, so the right step is to list the exact recorder and camera models on the quote and confirm each one against AVYCON's current documentation before ordering. If your program needs a written confirmation, we include the model list and the manufacturer's statement with the proposal so the program or insurer can review it directly.

Can a single AVYCON camera work without a recorder, say for one rear door?

It can. The AVYCON ONE listing notes that standalone cameras require a microSD card for storage and playback. That suits a single door in a small house or a store's back exit where a full recorder would be overkill. The trade-off is that the card sits in the camera, so if someone pulls the camera down, the footage goes with it, and cards wear out over time. For anything more than one or two cameras, or for any spot where a thief can reach the camera, we recommend a recorder in a locked location instead, and we will say so on the quote.

The AVYCON ONE app

We have read reviews that the AVYCON ONE app hangs. Is getting it working on our phones part of the installation?

Yes, and we do not leave until it is. The app carries a 2.9 out of 5 rating on the App Store, and a reviewer there described it hanging about half the time and an installer struggling to save the device in the app. Many app problems trace back to how the recorder was connected: a weak network link, a recorder on a tenant's Wi-Fi, a stream setting too heavy for a phone on cellular data, or one login shared across several people. We give the recorder a wired connection and a fixed address on the network, set a lighter sub-stream for phone viewing, create a separate login for each user, and test live view and playback on cellular data with Wi-Fi turned off on your phone. Then we show each person how to find a clip and export it.

After the old recorder was swapped out, none of our cameras show in the app anymore. How do they get back in?

This is the question one App Store reviewer asked AVYCON directly: how to get cameras back into the app after installing a new base unit. In most cases the cameras did not disappear; the app was tied to the old recorder's identity, and the new recorder has to be added to the app as a new device, then each camera assigned to it. On a PoE recorder, AVYCON's FAQ notes a camera plugged into a PoE port appears on the screen after about a minute, so the cameras themselves usually come up on the recorder quickly. The work is in removing the old device from each user's app, adding the new one, rebuilding user permissions and checking the recording schedule and retention, which reset to defaults on a new unit. We do all of that and give you the updated login list.

AI features and New York privacy rules

Our restaurant wants AVYCON's facial recognition to flag repeat shoplifters. What does New York City require before we turn it on?

Read the city's biometric identifier rule first, because facial recognition is exactly what it covers. As Kennedys Law explains, ordinary CCTV appears to be exempt from the signage requirement as long as the business does not use software to analyze the footage; facial recognition is that software. Covered commercial establishments, which include food and drink businesses, must post a clear sign at every customer entrance, and the city's rule sets a minimum sign size of 8.5 by 11 inches. Hinshaw & Culbertson notes individuals can recover five hundred dollars per violation for a missing sign, and the law also restricts selling or profiting from the data. We install with facial recognition off by default. If you decide to use it after talking to your lawyer, we turn it on, and the signs have to be up at every entrance first.

Wiring New York buildings

Our walk-up has no shafts and the owner hates surface wiring. How do PoE cables reach the hallway cameras on each floor?

In a building without shafts there are three honest options. The first is a slim, paintable raceway that climbs the stair hall in the corner where the wall meets the stair stringer or the ceiling, which nearly disappears once painted. The second is to use paths that already exist: an old dumbwaiter shaft, a chase beside the plumbing stack, or the sleeve the old intercom or cable TV used, if they connect the floors. The third is to drill through each floor inside a closet or behind a door that lines up from floor to floor. We look at all three on the walk-through and price the one that fits. Whichever is chosen, openings between floors are firestopped, and each cable is labeled at the camera and at the recorder.

We are a co-op replacing the whole lobby and hallway camera system. What does the board usually need from the installer?

Usually four things, though every board writes its own rules. A scope of work that lists each camera position and every new opening in walls or floors. A certificate of insurance naming the parties the managing agent specifies. Working hours that fit the house rules, which in many buildings means weekdays during business hours with the service elevator booked in advance. And a plan for the old equipment, which we remove. Seneca Security notes that building-wide camera replacements may need board approval and compliance with building-specific standards, so we prepare the scope as a document the board can approve rather than a verbal estimate. If the building uses fob or app entry, the board should also have its Tenant Data Privacy Law policy in place, and we keep the camera logins separate from that system.

How do you place hallway cameras in a rental building so they show the corridor but not inside people's apartments?

We place each one by standing in the hallway with the camera's actual field of view on a phone or the recorder screen, then having someone open each nearby apartment door. If the view reaches past the threshold, we move or re-aim the camera, or use the recorder's privacy masking to black out that part of the image. Connextivity's guide to NYC apartment camera rules says hallway cameras are permitted when positioned to cover corridor traffic without seeing into apartments as doors open, and notes that a covert camera inside a unit can support a felony charge under Penal Law 250.45. Common-area cameras should also be plainly visible; we do not install hidden cameras in residential buildings.

Our building is in a historic district. Do the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules stop us from putting cameras on the front?

No, but they shape where the cameras go and what they look like. Section 2-20 of the LPC rules covers health, safety and utility equipment, including security cameras, and the LPC can approve cameras that meet its criteria at staff level. One published LPC approval reviewed nine security cameras at a building's ground floor, five on the west facade and three on the east, under Section 2-20(c)(4). The rules prefer secondary facades and inconspicuous spots; on a primary facade, the guidebook asks for the smallest possible camera, painted to match. We plan the camera positions with that in mind, avoid significant architectural features, and prepare the drawings and photos you or your architect need to file. Cameras inside the vestibule, looking out, sometimes avoid the question altogether.

What AI Gets Wrong About AVYCON Camera Installation in NYC

We could not capture Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask boxes or related searches for this topic from our build environment, so we are not going to pretend to quote them. What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers and installer blogs about camera systems in New York, set against manufacturer, government and legal sources and our own field practice. National marketplaces such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish cost guides that many of those answers draw on; they give a rough sense of the market, but they are not a quote for your building.

"PoE cameras are plug and play, so installation is the easy part."

The plug-and-play part is true. AVYCON's own FAQ says a camera plugged directly into the recorder's PoE port appears on the screen after about a minute. In a showroom, that is the whole job.

In a New York building, that one minute comes after the cable has been pulled from a cellar through a brick foundation wall, up a stair hall and out to a vestibule ceiling, terminated, tested and labeled. On a six-story walk-up, the roof-door run alone can take longer than mounting every camera in the building.

So when an answer says installation is simple, it means the connection is simple. The labor, the approvals and nearly all the risk are in the path, which is why we price by building and borough rather than by camera alone.

"NYC apartment buildings are required to have security cameras."

Several quick answers say or imply this. Compliance writers at Seneca Security state plainly that there is no general NYC law mandating cameras in apartment buildings. What the law requires are things like adequate common-area lighting under the Multiple Dwelling Law and, in many buildings, intercoms and self-locking doors.

What actually drives most building camera systems is insurance and experience. Seneca notes that many commercial property insurers set camera coverage as a condition of theft and vandalism coverage, and owners add cameras after lobby thefts and package losses.

The difference matters, because an owner who believes cameras are mandatory may install them in a hurry and in the wrong places. Cameras chosen to match an insurer's written requirement and the building's real incidents work better and cost less.

"Facial recognition is just a feature you switch on in the app."

The AVYCON ONE app does list facial recognition among its AI filters, alongside motion, people and vehicle detection and plate reading. Technically, it is a setting.

Legally, in New York City, it is a decision. The city's biometric identifier law applies to covered commercial establishments that use software to analyze customers' faces, requiring a clear sign at every customer entrance of at least 8.5 by 11 inches, and allowing individuals to sue over a missing sign. In residential buildings, tenants at Atlantic Plaza Towers in Brownsville fought a landlord's facial-recognition entry plan, and a Council Member's site recounts another rent-stabilized tenant's fight in 2018.

We install with facial recognition off and explain the rule to the owner before anyone decides otherwise. It is the one AVYCON feature where reading the law first is part of the installation.

"Only a licensed electrician can install wired security cameras in NYC."

This claim appears on at least one installer blog, and it conflicts with the official route. NYC Business states that a license from the New York State Department of State is required to install or maintain security systems, and the rule behind it covers CCTV used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement.

On the electrical side, Local Law 128 of 2024 updated the city's electrical code so that low-voltage work under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data generally needs no electrical permit when done by a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person. Sources still disagree on the details, which is a good reason to ask any installer exactly which license they hold.

Our answer: the camera system work is done under NYS license #12000287431. If a job needs a new outlet or circuit for the recorder, that line-voltage work is for a licensed electrician, and the quote says so.

"NYC Local Law 50 requires notice to tenants about hallway cameras."

Some vendor pages state that a "Local Law 50" requires residential buildings with common-area cameras to notify residents. We could not confirm that in any official source, so we do not repeat it as law.

What we can point to is Penal Law 250.65, which, as a legal analysis by Ambient.ai summarizes, protects systems where a written notice is conspicuously posted that video surveillance is installed, or where cameras are installed so their presence is clearly and immediately obvious. Visible cameras and a posted notice are sensible either way.

So we install residential common-area cameras in plain view and recommend a posted notice at the entrance. If a lawyer confirms a specific notice requirement for your building, we will build the system to match it.

"A renter can install any camera they like inside their own apartment."

FAQ

NYC AVYCON Camera Installation FAQ

Does whoever installs our AVYCON cameras need a New York State license?
+

Yes. NYC Business states that a license from the New York State Department of State is required to install or maintain security or fire alarm systems, and the underlying rule covers closed-circuit television used full-time or part-time to detect or monitor intrusion, theft, movement, sound or fire. That describes almost every AVYCON system. Ask any installer for their license number before work starts; ours is NYS license #12000287431.

If the cameras are tied into a video entry or access system at the front door, does the same license cover that?
+

A Kirschenbaum law firm analysis of this exact question points to the state rule that brings CCTV used for detecting or monitoring intrusion or theft into the security system license, which is why video entry and access systems with cameras are generally treated as security work. In buildings that also use fobs or app entry, the city's Tenant Data Privacy Law adds limits on how entry data is used and requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days, so we keep camera and entry logins separate and documented.

Is a landlord legally required to put cameras in a New York City apartment building?
+

Generally no. Compliance guides note there is no general city law that requires cameras in apartment buildings. The legal requirements that touch building security are things like adequate common-area lighting and, in many buildings, self-locking doors and intercoms. In practice, owners add cameras because insurers ask for them, after thefts or lobby incidents, or because tenants want them.

Who in a building is allowed to view and download the recorded footage?
+

Whoever the owner or board authorizes, which usually means the owner, the managing agent and sometimes the super, not every tenant. We set up a separate AVYCON ONE login for each authorized person with only the cameras their role needs, and leave the owner a written list so access can be removed when someone leaves. A clear access policy also helps when police or an insurer later asks for footage.

As a renter, can I have a wired AVYCON system put into my own apartment?
+

Only with the landlord's written agreement, and in a co-op or condo often the board's too, because a wired system means drilling and running cable. Some guides note that a Wi-Fi camera that needs no drilling is usually allowed inside an apartment. If the owner will not agree to wiring, a no-drill camera may be the better fit, and we will tell you so rather than sell you a system you cannot install.

Should tenants be told before new cameras go into the lobby and hallways?
+

We recommend it, even though we could not confirm a specific city notice law that some vendor sites cite. New York Penal Law 250.65 favors systems with a conspicuous posted notice or cameras whose presence is clearly obvious, and one installer guide advises owners of rent-stabilized buildings to document the security reason in writing and notify tenants in advance. We install common-area cameras in plain view and suggest a posted notice at the entrance.

Can a resident point their own camera down the shared hallway outside their door?
+

Sources disagree. One legal Q&A says a tenant needs the landlord's permission for a camera in a common area and the landlord can remove it, while another source says no state or city law clearly forbids a camera on a resident's own door. Audio in a shared hallway raises eavesdropping questions, which is why audio is usually left off. Ask the owner or board first, and speak to a lawyer if there is a dispute.

Can I mount AVYCON cameras on the outside of my own house in the city?
+

Yes, on your own property, aimed at your own entrances, driveway and yard. Avoid aiming into neighbors' windows or private spaces, and leave audio off on cameras that face the street. If your house is a landmark or in a historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules apply to cameras on the facade, which usually means small, finished cameras in discreet spots, sometimes with an LPC staff approval.

How is the price of a four-camera AVYCON system worked out for a New York home?
+

Start with your borough's AVYCON price page, which lists the recorder, cameras and installation for that area. Then the building adjusts it: how far each camera is from the recorder, whether cable runs through masonry or an easy basement, how high the cameras go, and whether a board or landmark review is involved. We give a written quote with each of those named, after seeing photos or walking the house.

Does a commercial AVYCON install for a store or office cost more than a home system?
+

Usually per camera it is similar, but business systems tend to need more: more cameras, longer recording retention, conduit where cable could be cut, a locked recorder location, after-hours work so the business stays open, and sometimes a PoE switch for extra cameras. Insurers may also set specific coverage requirements. We price each of those as separate lines on the borough list so the owner can see what is driving the total.

Each area has its own AVYCON price page, and we book a visit within 2 hours of your request. Owner-operated since 2002, the same licensed team handles the walk-through, the install and the app handover.

My building put a camera that seems to record audio right outside my apartment door. Is there anything I can do?
+

Start by asking management in writing what the camera records and why it faces your door. New York's eavesdropping law is about recording conversations without the consent of a party, and installers commonly leave hallway audio off for that reason. A camera that sees into your apartment when the door opens is a further concern. If writing to the owner does not resolve it, a tenant lawyer or your local tenant organization is the next step.

Do we need a Department of Buildings permit for the camera cabling?
+

Generally not for the low-voltage cabling itself. Local Law 128 of 2024 updated the city's electrical code so that work under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data circuits generally does not need an electrical permit when done by a licensed master or special electrician or a qualified person. A new outlet or circuit for the recorder is line-voltage work for a licensed electrician, and work that alters fire-rated doors, exits or the fire alarm can require filing.
