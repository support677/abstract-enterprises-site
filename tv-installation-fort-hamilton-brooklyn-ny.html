The Neighborhood

Why Fort Hamilton Properties Need TV Installation

Fort Hamilton is a name with more than one meaning, and for a TV job only one of them matters. The Army post, at the Brooklyn end of the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, is a controlled installation; Military OneSource notes that only one taxi service is even authorized to drive onto it. The neighborhood we work in is the residential and commercial ground around the post, the part of Bay Ridge south of 86th Street that Wikipedia calls a sub-neighborhood and that the post office, the library on Fourth Avenue and the subway terminal at 95th Street all name Fort Hamilton. That ground holds a mix of buildings very different from one another: six-story apartment houses along Shore Road from the 1930s to the 1960s, mid-century elevator buildings inland, attached row houses, small condo buildings that replaced two-families, and apartments over stores on Fourth and Fifth Avenues. Every one of them puts a different wall behind a TV.

The people are as varied as the walls. Military OneSource counts 573 active-duty service members, 942 family members, 700 civilian employees and 880 Guard and Reserve members attached to the garrison, from every branch. On-post housing is small: six historic homes on Colonel's Row, 70 townhouses, 60 older high-rise apartments and 150 newer three- and four-bedroom apartments, all run by Balfour Beatty Communities. MyBaseGuide puts it bluntly: on-post family housing is "extremely limited," and "most personnel live off-post." For many of those households, off post means an apartment on the blocks around the gate, rented for an assignment that lasts two or three years. They arrive with a household goods shipment and a date the moving truck may or may not keep, and they leave on orders with a pack-out day fixed by someone else. A TV on the wall has to fit around both ends of that stay, and we plan it that way from the first call.

The other half of Fort Hamilton has stayed put for decades. When Brownstoner profiled the Fort Hamilton section in 2013 it passed on the New York Times' description of it as a "naturally occurring retirement community," a place where people age in the apartments they have owned for years. The NYU Furman Center's figures for Community District 10, which takes in Bay Ridge and Dyker Heights, show the same trend: 20.2 percent of residents were 65 or older in 2024, up from 13.1 percent in 2010, and 41 percent of homes are owner-occupied. In those homes the TV call is a different one. It is a long-time shareholder replacing a heavy set that has stood on a wall unit for twenty years, a widower who wants the screen at the height of his armchair, or a daughter in another state arranging the visit for her mother. Those jobs need patience, a simple remote and a technician who explains things once, slowly, and then writes them down.

Then there is the building stock itself, which arrived in layers. Along Shore Road, the co-ops run from 8701 Shore Road, a 1932 building of six stories and 174 apartments with four elevators, a live-in super and two daily porters, to 9255 Shore Road, a 1951 co-op with 235 apartments, to 8901 Shore Road, a ten-story building finished in 1962. Brownstoner's August 2026 piece on 9437 Shore Road described a six-story, 55-unit Art Moderne building that opened as a rental in 1940, with parquet floors and a faceted wall in the apartment it featured; a broker's page for 9701 Shore Road, from 1935, describes curved corners and oversized windows facing the harbor and the bridge. Inland, 9040 and 9330 Fort Hamilton Parkway both went up in 1955, and 9205 Ridge Boulevard in 1961. The newest layer is small and recent: Arcadia Bay Ridge at 401 95th Street, a seven-story, 36-unit condo from 2018, an 18-unit building permitted at 432 97th Street in April 2026, and the six-to-twelve-unit buildings that City Planning's 2007 rezoning study described replacing one- and two-family houses. Plaster on lath, plaster on block, brick, poured concrete and drywall on metal studs all turn up within a few blocks of each other.

Getting to the door is its own question here. The Verrazzano's Brooklyn ramps are being rebuilt, and Brooklyn Reporter's account of the MTA's plan describes Belt Parkway ramp reconstruction starting in spring 2026, temporary noise barriers along Fort Hamilton Parkway, and John J. Carty Park closed from summer 2026 to summer 2029, with the work due to finish late in 2029. Farther north, the Fort Hamilton Parkway bridge over the Gowanus Expressway at 81st Street is under a $36.9 million rehabilitation with its parking lane closed until summer 2027. None of that stops a TV installation, but it changes where a van can stand while a 75-inch box is carried in, and it is why we ask about parking and the nearest corner when you book. We would rather plan the carry than discover it on the day.

Same-Day Repair in Fort Hamilton

Need Repair in Fort Hamilton? A Mount Left Behind by the Last Tenant, a Screen Drooping on Old Plaster, or a TV That Has to Come Down Before the Movers

Three urgent calls come up again and again south of 86th Street. The first is the rental that changes hands every few years. A new tenant moves into an apartment near the gate and finds a bracket already on the wall, sometimes with a cable poking out of a hole beneath it, put up by a household that left on orders. Before anyone hangs a new screen on it, we check what it is fixed into, what it is rated for and whether the wire below is a TV power cord pushed through the wall, which the electrical code does not allow. The second is a screen in an older apartment that has started to lean forward or sag on one side, usually because the anchors were chosen for drywall and the wall is plaster over masonry block. The third is the reverse of an installation: the movers are booked for Thursday and the TV is still on the wall.

If a screen is tipping, do not push it back. If two people can lift it safely, take it off the bracket, unplug it and lay it flat on a blanket, screen up. Then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV is going straight back up on a properly anchored bracket on the same wall, the job is priced as a new mount for that wall type with the take-down added, and we tell you the total before we start. For a move, book the take-down a day or two ahead of the pack-out rather than the morning of, so the bracket screws, the TV's own bolts and the remote can be bagged, labeled and taped to the bracket for the next house. If the problem turns out to be the wall itself, a crack running through old plaster or a loose section around an old opening, we say so plainly so the landlord or the building can deal with it.

TV Installation for Fort Hamilton's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Fort Hamilton TV call. They differ in three ways that decide the plan: what the wall behind the screen is made of, how the TV gets from the curb to the room, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled.

The prewar Shore Road co-op

Six-story apartment houses from the 1930s and 1940s, such as 8701, 9701 and 9437 Shore Road, with curved corners, big harbor windows, plaster walls and a board.

The postwar Shore Road co-op

Larger buildings from 1951 to 1962, such as 9255, 9801, 9201 and 8901 Shore Road, with doormen or porters, more apartments and walls that are often block behind the plaster.

The mid-century elevator building inland

Six-story buildings from 1955 to 1961 on Fort Hamilton Parkway and Ridge Boulevard, co-ops and rentals both, with compact living rooms and masonry partitions.

The row house and the two-family

Attached brick houses on the side streets between the avenues, with plaster inside, a party wall on each side and, in many, a fireplace or chimney breast.

The new condo or small infill building

Buildings from 2000 on, such as 401 95th Street, and the six-to-twelve-unit buildings that replaced two-families, usually drywall on metal studs with rated walls between units.

Two- and three-story mixed-use buildings on Fourth and Fifth Avenues and Fort Hamilton Parkway, with a narrow stair beside the shop and a front wall over the street.

The prewar Shore Road co-op: harbor windows, curved corners and a wall that has to be found

The apartment houses that went up along Shore Road in the 1930s and 1940s were built to face the water. A broker's page for 9701 Shore Road, from 1935, describes its curved corners and oversized windows looking at the harbor and the bridge, and Brownstoner's piece on 9437 Shore Road describes an Art Moderne building from 1940 with a faceted wall in the living room. That architecture is lovely to live in and awkward for a TV. The long wall opposite the windows is often broken by doorways into the foyer and the bedroom hall, the corner where two walls meet may be rounded, and the wall with the windows is mostly glass. The screen usually ends up on the one flat stretch between two doorways, or on a side wall where it can be seen from the sofa without a window behind it.

The walls in buildings of this age are usually plaster, and what is behind the plaster varies from wall to wall. Partitions between rooms may be plaster on metal lath over light framing or plaster on hollow clay tile or gypsum block; the walls along the public hallway and around the shafts are commonly masonry. A scanner gives confusing readings on all of them. We confirm with a single pilot hole drilled where the bracket plate will later cover it, and price by what we find: the plaster add-on of $45 where there is framing behind it, the brick or stone add-on of $65 where the plaster sits on masonry. Many of these buildings also have parquet floors, which we cover with blankets and drop cloths before the ladder comes out, because a dropped drill is the commonest way an old floor gets marked.

Then there is the building. A co-op of this size has a super, often porters, a managing agent and a board, and Brick Underground notes that alteration agreements commonly set insurance minimums and the days and hours when work is allowed. Hanging a TV is usually treated as minor work, but opening a wall for an in-wall power kit may not be, so we ask you to check before we suggest one. Where the building would rather nothing is cut, the cables go into a paintable cord cover for $59 or drop directly into a console under the screen.

The postwar Shore Road co-op: more apartments, a front desk and walls of block

The second wave of Shore Road buildings is bigger. 9255 Shore Road, from 1951, has 235 apartments on six floors. Shore Lane Arms at 9801 Shore Road went up as a co-op in 1957, and its listing notes a doorman most hours of the day, a resident super and board approval for buyers. 9201 Shore Road, from 1960, lists a full-time doorman, a live-in super and porters, and 8901 Shore Road, finished in 1962, rises ten stories with 52 apartments. In buildings like these the visit starts at the front desk: the doorman needs your name and the technician's arrival window, the super may want to see the insurance certificate, and the service elevator may need to be booked for a large box.

Behind the plaster or skim coat, postwar walls are more likely to be concrete block, sometimes with poured concrete around the columns and the shafts. Block is a good wall for a TV if the anchors go into the solid parts and not the hollow cores, and if the bracket uses every fixing point on its plate. It is a bad wall for hiding wires, because there is no cavity to fish a cable through. In most of these apartments the clean answer is a screen placed directly above a console or a low cabinet, so the cables drop straight down behind the furniture, or a paintable cord cover running down to the outlet. We price block walls on your quote once the photos show what we are dealing with, and we say if the plan changes after the pilot hole.

Living rooms in these buildings are often wider than in the prewar ones, with a dining area at one end. That makes a full-motion arm tempting, so the screen can turn toward the table. On block it works, but an arm pulled out from the wall puts its whole leverage on the top fixings, so we place them carefully and check them under load. If the room has a radiator cover or a through-wall air conditioner under the window, we keep the screen well away from the warm air.

The mid-century elevator building inland: compact rooms and masonry partitions

Away from the water, the six-story elevator buildings on Fort Hamilton Parkway and Ridge Boulevard date mostly from the 1950s and early 1960s. 9040 Fort Hamilton Parkway, a co-op its listing calls The Roosevelt, has 84 residences and was built in 1955; 9330 Fort Hamilton Parkway, from the same year, has 48 units; 9205 Ridge Boulevard, a rental from 1961, has 63. Buildings of this kind house a wide mix of people: shareholders who have owned for decades, renters who sign two-year leases, and some who are both, subletting to a newcomer while they spend winters elsewhere.

The living rooms here are compact and the TV wall is usually obvious: the one facing the sofa, often an interior partition with the kitchen or the bedroom behind it. In buildings of this era those partitions are frequently masonry block or gypsum block with plaster over it, not framing. A stud finder beeps everywhere or nowhere. The pilot hole settles it, and the anchor follows: masonry anchors set into the solid web of the block, never into a hollow core or a mortar joint. Because a partition may carry the kitchen plumbing or a gas line on the other side, we look at what is behind the wall before drilling, which in practice means going into the kitchen and checking where the pipes run.

Renters in these buildings should look at the lease before booking. Some leases simply ask tenants to restore the walls at move-out; others restrict fixtures altogether. If yours says anything about alterations, send the landlord a short note naming the wall, the size of the holes and where the cables will go, and keep the reply. The no-drill alternative, the TV set up on its own stand on a good console, is $119.99 on our list.

The row house and the two-family: party walls, a parlor and often a fireplace

Between the avenues, the side streets are lined with attached brick houses, many of them two-families with an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other. In these houses the longest wall in the living room is usually a party wall shared with the house next door. The Brownstoner forum's long thread on mounting a TV over a fireplace sums up what we find in old Brooklyn houses: party walls often have no studs at all, just plaster laid on brick, while the interior walls are usually plaster on wood lath. A party wall of solid brick is a strong place for a heavy TV, priced with the $65 brick add-on, as long as the anchors go into the brick and not the softer mortar.

Many row houses also have a fireplace or a chimney breast in the front room, and owners naturally look at the space above it. Mounting above a fireplace is a $130 add-on on our list, because it means drilling the chimney breast and usually setting the screen high. The forum thread makes the two points we make on site: a working fireplace's heat "won't be good for the TV," and over the mantel is often "awfully high" to watch comfortably. If the fireplace is used, we look for another wall. If it is decorative or sealed, we check the height from the sofa with painter's tape before anything is drilled, and a tilting bracket brings the picture down toward your eyes.

The two-family brings the human side too. If you rent the upper floor, the owner downstairs will hear the drill and may be the person who decides whether the TV can go on the wall. A short message before the visit, saying which wall, roughly how big the holes are and whether the cables will be in a cover, avoids most awkward conversations.

The new condo or small infill building: metal studs and rated walls

City Planning's 2007 study for the Dyker Heights and Fort Hamilton rezoning described one- and two-family houses being torn down and replaced by attached buildings of six to twelve units, and building continues: Arcadia Bay Ridge at 401 95th Street opened in 2018 with 36 condos on seven floors, a permit was filed in April 2026 for an 18-unit building at 432 97th Street, and BKReader reported in April 2026 on a proposed eleven-story, 292-unit building at 9305 Fifth Avenue, on the site of a Staples and its parking lot. Buildings of this generation are where we most often find drywall on light-gauge metal studs.

Metal studs need a different fixing. A lag bolt that bites into a wood stud strips a thin steel one, so the bracket goes up on toggles or steel-stud anchors rated for the load and spread across more than one stud, and we price that on your quote from the photos. The other thing that changes in a new building is the wall between apartments. Walls separating one home from the next are typically built as fire-separation assemblies, and we do not cut openings for an in-wall kit into a wall we believe is one. On an interior partition between your own rooms, an in-wall power and cable kit at $169 is usually straightforward.

Condo boards and managing agents in newer buildings tend to be organized. Expect a request for an insurance certificate, set work hours and a question about whether the wall is being opened. We answer all three before the visit.

The apartment over an avenue store: a narrow stair and a wall over the street

Fourth Avenue, Fifth Avenue and Fort Hamilton Parkway carry two- and three-story buildings with a shop at street level and apartments above; a permit filed in August 2026 for 9055 Fort Hamilton Parkway describes a new three-story, three-unit building of exactly that kind. For a TV, the stair is the first problem. It is usually a straight, narrow flight beside the store, and a large box sometimes has to go up on its edge. Keep the TV in its original carton until we arrive; a screen that has been unpacked and wrapped in a blanket is far more likely to be damaged on a tight stair.

The second is the front wall. In these buildings the front room faces the avenue, and the wall behind it is brick. It is a good wall to mount on, but it is also the wall with the most street noise and light, and a screen on it faces the window across the room. More often we put the TV on the side wall, which may be a party wall with the next building, and set the soundbar so the dialogue is clear over the traffic. If the store downstairs is yours, we can do the shop screen and the apartment TV in the same visit.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Hanging a TV for a Household That Will Move Again

These notes describe how our technicians approach a TV in a Fort Hamilton rental taken for an assignment. They are our working practice, not a story about any one address.

Ask how long first. Before the scanner comes out, the technician asks how long you expect to be in the apartment and whether the lease says anything about walls. A household that expects orders within eighteen months gets a plan with fewer holes and a cable route that can come off cleanly.

Fewer holes, in the right place. A fixed or tilting bracket placed on one solid run of wall, with its fixings where a patch will be easy to blend, is better than a large articulating arm whose plate covers a broad area. If the screen has to swing, we choose the smallest arm that does the job.

Surface before inside. In a rental, a paintable cord cover is fixed with a few small screws and comes off with little to patch. An in-wall kit means two openings in the drywall. Both are tidy, but the cover is the one most landlords accept without a second thought.

Bag it and tape it. When the TV goes up, the spare bolts, spacers and the bracket's instruction sheet go into a labeled bag. When it comes down, the bag goes back on with the bracket's screws and the TV's own bolts inside, taped to the back of the bracket, so the next installer, at the next duty station, has everything.

Photograph the wall. Before we leave, we suggest you photograph the wall with the TV on it and keep the picture with your lease. When the day comes to restore it, you will know exactly where every fixing was.

Arriving and Leaving on Orders

Report Date, Delivery Day, Pack-Out: Timing a TV Around a Fort Hamilton Assignment

For a household attached to the garrison, the TV is rarely the first thing on the list, and it should not be. The order of events matters more than the bracket. This is how we suggest sequencing it, based on the way these moves usually run and the things that go wrong when they are rushed.

Before the shipment arrives. Sign the lease, find out from the landlord or the super what the building allows on the walls, and send us photos of the room you have in mind. We can tell you from those photos which wall is likely to be plaster, block or drywall and what the job will cost, so the money is planned. What we do not suggest is booking the installation for the day the household goods are due. Delivery dates move, and a technician who arrives to an empty apartment, or to an apartment full of unopened cartons, cannot do much. Wall by Wall

Plaster on Lath, Plaster on Block, Brick or Metal Stud: What Holds a TV in a Fort Hamilton Wall

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs, labor only. Very few Fort Hamilton living rooms are drywall on wood studs, which is why the wall comes first in every quote. Each add-on exists because that wall needs different bits, different anchors and more time, and the photos you send usually tell us which one applies before the visit.

Plaster on wood lath (+$45). The interior walls of the older row houses and many prewar apartments are thin wooden strips nailed to studs and covered in two or three coats of plaster. The surface is hard and the layer behind it is crumbly, so a plastic plug in plaster alone can feel solid on the day and loosen over months. The fixing that lasts goes through the plaster and the lath into the stud, drilled slowly so the plaster does not crack outward, or uses a toggle rated for the load where the bracket cannot reach a stud. Studs in old houses are not always on modern spacing, so we find each one rather than measuring from a corner.

Plaster on block or tile (priced by what is behind it). In many of Fort Hamilton's apartment buildings from the 1930s to the 1960s, the plaster is laid on hollow clay tile, gypsum block or concrete block rather than on lath. This is the wall that catches out handymen. It sounds solid, but a standard sleeve anchor can break through the thin face of a tile into the void behind, and a plastic plug simply spins. The answer is an anchor made for hollow masonry, placed in the solid parts with a slow drill. Once the pilot hole shows what is there, the wall is priced on your quote.

Solid brick or stone (+$65). Party walls in the row houses, the front walls of buildings on the avenues and many chimney breasts are solid brick under the plaster. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor sits in the brick itself and not in the mortar joint, which is softer and can crumble. We use a hammer drill and sleeve or wedge anchors sized for the bracket, and keep holes clear of the edges of each brick.

Poured concrete. Columns and some walls in the postwar towers are poured concrete. It is the hardest drilling of all, and a steel bar inside it stops a hole dead; when that happens we move the bracket slightly rather than fight it. Concrete is quoted from your photos.

Drywall on metal studs. Common in buildings from 2000 on and in renovated apartments. Thin steel strips out lag bolts, so the bracket hangs on steel-stud toggles spread across more than one stud. Priced on your quote.

Above a fireplace (+$130). Added on top of the wall material, because it means drilling the chimney breast, working higher on a ladder and checking the heat and the viewing angle.

Hiding the wires the way the electrical code allows

After "put it on the wall," the most common request is "make the wires disappear," and there is a right and a wrong way. The wrong way is to feed the TV's own power cord into a hole behind the screen and out of a hole near the floor. The National Electrical Code does not allow it. As EC&M's reading of section 400.12 puts it, flexible cords "are not permitted to be run through holes in walls" and "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." A cord inside a wall can be crushed by a fixing, can overheat where it is bundled and cannot be inspected.

The right way, on a framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list including the kit. It puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall that plugs into your existing outlet, carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV with a separate pass-through for the HDMI and other low-voltage cables. It needs a hollow framed cavity, so it suits plaster on lath and drywall and does not suit brick, block or concrete. On those walls the honest choices are a paintable cord cover, $59, fixed with a few small screws and painted to match, or a screen placed straight above a console so the cables drop behind it.

In a co-op, an in-wall kit may count as opening a wall under the alteration agreement, and in a rental it means two openings to patch at move-out. A cord cover means a few small screw holes. Both are easy to restore, but it is worth asking the building or the landlord which they prefer before the day.

We kept the tilting bracket from our last duty station. It held the TV on drywall there, but the living room wall in our new apartment is plaster over block. Can the same bracket go up?

FAQ

Fort Hamilton TV Installation FAQ

What is the total for a 55-inch TV on a plaster wall with its wires hidden in an in-wall kit?
+

On our Brooklyn list it is $179.99 for the mount, $45 for plaster and $169 for the in-wall power and cable kit, so $393.99 in labor and kit. The bracket is extra at list price unless you already own one rated for the TV. The kit needs a framed wall, so we confirm the plaster is on lath and not on block first.

Can you install a TV in family housing on the Army post?
+

Only once the housing office has said yes. On-post homes are run by Balfour Beatty Communities and access to the installation is controlled, so ask the housing office what is allowed on the walls and how an outside contractor is admitted. Once you have that answer in writing, send it with your photos and we will plan the visit.

My lease says Bay Ridge but my mail says Fort Hamilton. Does that change the price?
+

No. Both names cover the same part of Brooklyn as far as our list is concerned, and the whole borough is billed at one Brooklyn rate. What changes a price is the wall, the fireplace and the cable plan, not what the listing or the post office calls the block.

Do you offer a military discount on TV installation?
+

Our TV list does not include a military discount. Every household pays the same published Brooklyn prices, and the total is agreed in writing before the visit, so there are no surprises for anyone. What we can do for a household on orders is plan the mount so the take-down and the next installation cost as little as possible.

Will you leave the bracket's spare parts and instructions for our next move?
+

Yes. After mounting, spare bolts, spacers and the bracket's instruction sheet go into a labeled bag for you. When we take the TV down later, the bag goes back on with the screws and the TV's own bolts and is taped to the bracket, so the next installer has everything.

Do you need to see my lease or my co-op's approval before you book?
+

We do not need to see it, but you need to have checked it. If the lease or the building's rules say anything about fixtures, alterations or contractor insurance, get the answer first and tell us what the building asked for. If the building wants an insurance certificate, give us the exact names to put on it.

Can the drilling be done at the start of the visit so an older relative can rest afterward?
+

Usually, yes. Once the wall is read and the screen position is agreed with tape, the drilling is a few minutes of noise and can come early in the visit. The hanging, cable work and setup that follow are quiet. Tell us when you book and the technician will warn the household before the loud part starts.

Is the paintable cord cover stuck on with adhesive or fixed with screws?
+

We fix it with a few small screws so it stays straight and does not peel paint when it is removed. The holes are much smaller than the bracket's and fill easily at move-out. The cover can be painted to match the wall after it is up.

Where do I find the details about my TV that you need before the visit?
+

On the sticker on the back of the TV, which shows the model number. A clear photo of that sticker tells us the screen size, the weight and the bolt pattern the bracket has to match. If the TV is still sealed, a photo of the carton's label does the same job.

Do you put up screens in church halls and community rooms south of 86th Street?
+

Yes. Halls and meeting rooms are priced from the same list as any mount, by wall and cable plan. We set the screen high enough to be seen over a standing crowd, keep it clear of exits and alarm devices, and confirm who has approved the wall before the visit.

Can I send a video of the room instead of photos?
+

Yes, and it often helps. A short video on WhatsApp that pans across the TV wall, the outlet, the spot for the box and the doorways tells us more than a single photo. Add a photo of the TV's model sticker and your cross streets, and we can usually give you the total from that.

What if the TV we ordered has not arrived by the booked day?
+

Tell us as soon as you know and we will move the visit to a day after the delivery. Rescheduling terms are set out on our TV installation policies page. It is better to move the booking than to have a technician arrive with nothing to hang.
