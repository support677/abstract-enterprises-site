Intercom installation in Gerritsen Beach is mostly a house job, not a lobby job, and that changes what a good system looks like. City land-use records for the peninsula south of Avenue X list about 1,429 residential lots, and nearly all of them hold one- and two-family houses; we found no building of four or more apartments there at all. The Neighborhood

Why Gerritsen Beach Properties Need Intercom Installation

Gerritsen Beach sits on a peninsula between Sheepshead Bay and Marine Park, with Plumb Beach Channel on one side, Shell Bank Creek and Knapp Street on the other, and Gerritsen Avenue as the only road in and out. It was laid out around 1920 by the Realty Associates as a summer colony. Untapped New York describes William Greve's crews putting up more than six hundred houses in two years and about fifteen hundred eventually, small one-story bungalows with peaked roofs on lots so tight that, as Wikipedia notes, many had no backyard at all. Over the decades families winterized them, added rooms where they could, and turned a summer place into a year-round neighborhood of about 4,800 people at the 2020 census. For an intercom, that history explains the wiring we expect to find. The original houses were built for a doorbell and not much more: a button by the door, a thin pair of wire to a chime in the front room, and a small transformer somewhere near the floor. Nobody planned for a voice at the door, a picture of the visitor, or a second floor that had to hear the ring.

Hurricane Sandy changed the houses again. Wikipedia records that almost every home in Gerritsen Beach was affected in October 2012, and the Department of City Planning's 2017 report says floodwater reached as high as seven feet above grade. In the years that followed, owners lifted houses onto new foundations, rebuilt them from the ground up, or added floors, because on a 40-by-45-foot lot the only direction left was up. Brick Underground describes storm-damaged houses here being expanded with added floors for exactly that reason, and the city counted more than two hundred households enrolled in its Build It Back program by spring 2017. The design flood elevation in most of the neighborhood is about three to five feet above grade, and five to ten feet along the waterfront, according to the same DCP report. So a house that used to have its front door two steps up from the court may now have it a full story up, with living space starting above the flood line and a lower level kept for storage and parking. One resident quoted by the Brooklyn College journalism blog in December 2025, Barbara Curran, put the payoff simply: "Since we elevated our house we haven't had any flooding." The same changes that kept the water out moved the bedrooms two and three floors away from the door.

That is the most common intercom problem we expect on the peninsula, and it is not a security emergency. It is a house where nobody upstairs hears the door, where a delivery waits at the foot of an outside stair because the person who would answer is on the top floor with the television on, and where an older parent has to take the stairs to find out who is ringing. The second problem is power. CBS New York reported on November 14, 2012, that nearly half of Gerritsen Beach was still without electricity more than two weeks after the storm, and a November 7, 2012 press release from State Senator Martin Golden asked licensed electricians and plumbers to volunteer, because almost every home needed an inspection before electricity or gas could be turned back on. A door system whose transformer sat on the lowest floor went under with everything else. The third problem is the two-family house. City land-use data lists 156 two-family houses on the peninsula, and in each of them two households share a front door or sit side by side with separate ones. A single chime that rings in both apartments, or a button that only one family hears, causes daily friction that a two-household intercom solves.

What Gerritsen Beach does not have, at least on the record, is the kind of building that drives most intercom work elsewhere in Brooklyn. A search of HPD's open violation data found no intercom, buzzer or doorbell violations on any street of the peninsula, against 294 across ZIP code 11229 as a whole, most of them on large apartment streets such as Ocean Avenue, Nostrand Avenue and Kings Highway. The city's 311 data shows a single bell, buzzer or intercom complaint on the peninsula since 2018. Those numbers say something honest: almost nobody here is required to have an intercom, and almost nobody is calling the city about one. When a Gerritsen Beach household installs one, it is because the house has changed shape, because the family wants to see and talk to a visitor before opening a door a few feet from a narrow court, or because a tenant or an aging parent needs to answer without the stairs. Our job is to design for those reasons, not for a building code that mostly does not apply.

Six Gerritsen Beach facts that decide an intercom before a brand is chosen

  • Almost every lot is a one- or two-family house. The city's PLUTO file for lots south of Avenue X lists 1,264 one-family houses, 156 two-family houses, 2 three-family houses and no buildings of four or more units among about 1,589 homes. That points to door stations with one or two buttons, not lobby panels.
  • The houses were built small and grew upward. DCP describes the neighborhood as mostly one- and two-family detached houses, typically two stories, many built as summer bungalows, with only about five percent built after 1983. Added floors mean added places that need to hear the door.
  • Living space now starts higher. DCP puts the design flood elevation at roughly three to five feet above grade inland and five to ten feet at the waterfront, and nearly all of the neighborhood south of Avenue W lies in the one-percent annual-chance floodplain. Power supplies and network gear belong above that line.
  • The lots are tiny and the houses sit close. DCP lists typical lots of about 35 by 55 feet in the northern section, 40 by 45 feet in the southern section and 24 by 66 feet along the canal, and Urban Omnibus describes homes built right to the property line. Door stations often face a neighbor's wall or window a few feet away.
  • Some streets are barely wider than a driveway. DCP measured streets designed for pedestrians, 30 feet wide narrowing to about 15 feet including sidewalks in places. Visitors often reach a door on foot from the end of a court, which shapes where a station and its light should face.
  • Intercoms here are a choice, not a citation. HPD data shows no intercom violations on the peninsula and 311 shows one complaint since 2018. The one multi-unit rental we found, 2402 Gerritsen Avenue with eight apartments built in 1964, sits in the Avenue U to Avenue X strip north of the peninsula study area.

Repair Calls in Gerritsen Beach

Need Repair in Gerritsen Beach? A Chime That Went Quiet After the Water, a Kit That Lost Its Signal, or a Station That Rings Only Downstairs

Repair calls on the peninsula tend to fall into three patterns, and each points to a different part of the house. The first is a doorbell or intercom that stopped working the day the water came in and never started again. In a house where walls, floors and appliances were replaced after 2012 and the doorbell was left for later, the button at the door is often fine and the wire through the wall may still be sound, but the transformer and the chime or master unit sat low in the house and corroded. Whether that system is worth reviving depends on what the rebuild did to the wire between them. If the walls were opened and closed again with the old pair left in place, it may still test clean. If a contractor cut every low-voltage wire at the old floor line during a lift, there is nothing left to save, and the honest answer is a new run.

The second pattern is a wireless kit that worked when it was new and now drops calls or loses its picture. Wood-frame houses are easier on a radio signal than brick, but houses here are a few feet apart, and every one of them has its own router, cameras and smart devices. A wireless door station sharing that crowded air with a dozen neighbors' networks can work at noon and fail at eight in the evening when everyone is streaming. The third pattern is a system that rings on one floor and not another, usually because a floor was added and somebody extended the chime wire or added a plug-in wireless chime upstairs that has since died. A repair visit is where we find out which of these it is, and whether a repair still makes sense. Service calls are billed at $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and the technician explains what was found before recommending anything larger.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Gerritsen Beach's Architecture

Six kinds of house account for almost every intercom request we expect from Gerritsen Beach. They differ less in what the walls are made of, since most are wood frame, than in how many floors the house has gained, where the old wire was cut, and where the power and the router now sit. The cards name them; the notes underneath explain how the plan changes from one to the next.

Original one-story bungalows

Winterized summer houses with a peaked roof, a door close to the court and an attic or crawl space overhead, still on their first foundation.

Bungalows that went up a floor

Original houses with a second story or a raised roof added later, where the old ground floor and the new floor were framed decades apart.

Lifted houses on new foundations

Houses jacked up after Sandy, with the living floors now a story or more above the court and a lower level of storage, parking or flood vents.

Ground-up replacement houses

New houses built on the old lot, often two or three floors over a raised base, with modern framing and a chance to plan every cable before the walls close.

Attached and semi-attached houses north of the canal

Houses in the newer section, some brick, some sharing a wall with a neighbor, on the wider streets where DCP found attached and semi-detached stock.

Narrow lots on the canal

Long houses on 24-by-66-foot waterfront lots, with the street door at one end, the dock at the other and the router usually somewhere in between.

Original one-story bungalows: the attic is the easy road

A bungalow that never went up a floor is the simplest intercom job on the peninsula, and the attic is the reason. Under a peaked roof there is usually a crawl space or a small attic that runs the length of the house, and from there a cable can drop into the wall beside the front door and into the wall where the indoor station will hang, with a small opening at each end and nothing on the ceilings in between. If the house has a crawl space below instead, the same thing can be done from underneath, though after a flood that space is often damp, and we avoid leaving connections or equipment down there. The old doorbell pair in these houses runs from the button to a chime, usually in the front room or a hallway, and from the chime to a transformer. If it tests clean, a two-wire video door station can often use it, which means the walls stay closed. If it fails, the attic route makes a new run cheap.

The bigger questions in a one-story house are about people, not wire. Who answers the door, from which room, and does anyone in the house need to answer without walking to the front? In a small bungalow the kitchen is rarely more than a few steps from the door, so a screen there and a phone app for whoever is out is usually enough. Where an older resident spends most of the day in a back bedroom, a second indoor station there saves a walk on a bad day. And because these houses often stand close to their neighbors on the court, the station's camera angle, its night light and the volume of its speaker get set during handover with the household standing at the door, not left at the factory settings.

Bungalows that went up a floor: two generations of framing

When a bungalow gains a second story, the new floor sits on top of framing that may be close to a hundred years old, and the two rarely line up neatly. The old ceiling joists may have been doubled or replaced, a new stair may have been cut through the middle of the house, and the original attic, which would have been the easy cable route, has disappeared into the new floor. The route to an indoor station upstairs usually follows the new stair or a stack of closets, because those are the places where the builder had to open both floors at once. The technician looks for a wall that runs straight from the old ground floor into the new upper floor without a beam across the top of it, and checks whether the new floor's walls are insulated with batts, which a cable can be pushed through, or foam, which it often cannot.

The chime is the other problem. Owners who added a floor often extended the chime wire upstairs, or added a plug-in wireless chime there that has since stopped pairing. A new intercom replaces both with indoor stations that ring, show the visitor and talk back, wherever the household needs them. In a house like this we usually propose a station on the ground floor where the family cooks and eats, another on the new floor near the bedrooms, and phone answering for anyone who prefers it, with the old chime either removed or wired to ring alongside the new system if the household likes its sound.

Lifted houses on new foundations: the door moved, so did the water line

Lifting a house moves its front door up, often by a full story, and puts an outside stair and a landing between the court and the entrance. It also moves the line below which nothing important should live. DCP's design flood elevation of roughly three to five feet above grade inland, and five to ten feet along the water, is the number that matters, and in a lifted house the lower level under the living floors is usually built to let water in and out through flood vents. For an intercom, that sets two rules. First, the transformer or power supply, any battery backup and any network switch go upstairs, inside the living space, never on a wall of the lower level. Second, any cable that has to pass through the lower level, for example to a gate or a station at the foot of the stair, travels in sealed metal conduit with no splices or connections below the flood line.

The stair itself decides where the door station goes. Mounted at the top, beside the door, the station sees a visitor's face clearly once they have climbed, but a courier who will not climb never presses it. Mounted at the foot, it catches everyone, but it sits lower, closer to the water and further from the house. Many lifted houses end up with a station at the door and a second call point at the gate or the foot of the stair, ringing the same indoor screens. That is a conversation for the survey, with the household's habits in mind; we do not assume one answer for every lifted house.

Ground-up replacement houses: decide while the studs are bare

A house rebuilt from the foundation up is the one place on the peninsula where the intercom can be planned completely before anyone lives in it. With the studs bare, a cable to every indoor station and to every door that will have a station can be pulled in one visit, labeled at both ends and left coiled in the wall until the stations go in after the drywall and paint. A second cable to the front door costs almost nothing at that stage and leaves room for a camera or a future upgrade. The power supply, battery and any network switch get a planned spot inside the living space, on a circuit the electrician has marked, instead of ending up on a shelf in the lower level. Owners rebuilding here should ask their contractor when the walls will close and call us before that date; after it, every one of those runs means cutting into a finished wall.

Replacement houses also tend to arrive with a smart doorbell already chosen by somebody, or with a network jack by the front door that the builder installed without a plan. Neither is a problem if it is known early. A smart doorbell can stay as a camera and a second door can get a proper station; a jack by the door can carry a wired door station if the cable behind it tests well and runs back to somewhere sensible. We check both during the survey rather than assuming.

Attached and semi-attached houses north of the canal: one more wall to respect

North of the Gotham Avenue canal, Wikipedia describes a newer section with brick houses, wider sidewalks and the neighborhood's stores, and DCP found attached and semi-detached houses there and along parts of Gerritsen Avenue. These houses are closer to an ordinary Brooklyn intercom job. Some walls are masonry, so the route to the door may follow an existing chase, the old bell pair, or a short run of painted raceway rather than an attic drop. The shared wall needs care: we do not drill into a party wall without knowing what is on the other side, and in a semi-detached pair whose front walks or stoops sit side by side, the station goes where it serves this house's visitors and does not look straight into the neighbor's door.

Two-family houses are common in this part of the neighborhood. Where the two households share one front door, the station gets two buttons, each ringing its own household's screens, and each household's release opens only the shared door. Where each household has its own entrance, the cleaner design is usually two separate door stations on one system or two independent systems, so neither household's settings, codes or app accounts can be changed by the other. We ask the owner which arrangement the house actually uses before quoting either.

Narrow lots on the canal: two ends and a long middle

Waterfront lots of about 24 by 66 feet produce long, narrow houses with the street door at one end and the water at the other. Urban Omnibus noted that most waterfront houses have docks and boats, and Untapped New York points out that the canal is visible between the homes on Bartlett Place and Gotham Avenue. For an intercom, the length of the house matters more than the water. A doorbell chime by the street door is hard to hear from a kitchen or deck at the far end, and a Wi-Fi door station at the street end may be the device furthest from the router. That makes these houses good candidates for a wired door station on a single network cable, with indoor stations near the back of the house where the family actually sits, and phone answering for anyone on the deck or the dock.

Whether the water side needs a station of its own is the owner's choice. Some households want guests who arrive by boat to be able to ring the house; others want the water side left alone. If a station goes there, it gets the same rules as any station below the flood line: rated hardware, cable in conduit, and no connections in the part of the yard that floods. We describe the options during the survey and leave the decision to the people who live there.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Look at Between the Court and the Top Floor of a Gerritsen Beach House

Count the floors that answer, not the floors that exist. A house may have four levels if the lower level and an attic room are counted, but the household may only need to hear the door on two of them. The technician asks where people actually are in the evening, where an older resident spends the day, and where someone works from home, and places stations there.

Find the transformer and note its height. The old doorbell transformer is traced to its breaker and photographed, along with how far above the floor it sits. If it is on a wall of a lower level that floods, it moves upstairs with the new system, whatever else happens.

Follow the wire through the rebuild. In a house that was lifted, extended or rebuilt, the old pair may have been cut at the sill, spliced inside a new wall, or left intact. The technician tests it from the button to the chime before anyone decides whether it can carry video.

Stand where the visitor stands. At the foot of the stair, at the gate and at the door, the technician checks what a camera would see at each height, how much light there is after dark and how close the neighbor's window is, so the station's angle and light are chosen for this court.

Look at the door and its frame. If the household wants to release the door from upstairs, the latch, the frame and the closer matter more than the station. A door that does not close and latch by itself should not be released electrically until it does.

Find the router and test the air. Where the household is considering a wireless or app-only door station, the technician checks signal strength at the door and how many neighboring networks share the channel. On a crowded court that test often settles the wired-versus-wireless question by itself.

Switch off the power and try the door. With the intercom and any lock power off, the door is opened by hand from inside and with a key from outside, and the battery backup, if there is one, is watched to confirm it takes over. That test is repeated at handover.

Houses That Grew Upward

Hearing the Door Three Floors Up: How a Gerritsen Beach Intercom Reaches Every Level of a Taller House

The single most common reason a Gerritsen Beach household needs more than a doorbell is height. A house that began as a one-story bungalow and now has living space on two or three levels above a raised base has outgrown the idea of one chime in the front room. The family does not need a louder bell; it needs the door to reach wherever they are and to show them who is there, so nobody climbs or descends a full stair only to find a flyer on the step. Planning that well is mostly a matter of deciding three things in order.

Where people answer from. We start with the rooms, not the wire. In most taller houses the main indoor station goes in the kitchen or the room where the family eats, because that is where someone usually is. A second station goes near the bedrooms on the top floor, at a height where it can be answered without bending, and a third may go in a home office or a room used by an older parent. Phone answering covers everyone else, including anyone in the yard, on the dock or away from the house. A household that already lives on its phones may want fewer wall stations and more app answering; one with grandparents or young children usually wants more screens on the wall that work without a phone.

How the signal climbs. Once the rooms are known, the technician looks for a route that reaches each of them. In a house with an added floor, that is usually the new stair wall, a stack of closets, or the space beside a plumbing chase the builder already opened through both floors. In a replacement house with open framing, it is wherever the drawings say. A wired system needs one cable to each indoor station or a cable to each floor and a short branch from there, depending on the brand. A two-wire system can share a single pair along a chain of stations. A networked system needs a network cable to each station and a small switch, ideally beside the router, on battery backup. The quote names which approach we expect and why.

Who can open the door, and from where. If the front door will have an electric strike, every station that can release it is a door that can open from that room. Most families want the release on the main station and the bedroom station, and many want it off on a station used by children or in a room a tenant can reach. Phone apps add another question: who has the app, and whether a release from a phone in another borough should be allowed at all. These choices are set station by station at handover and can be changed later from the administrator account, which stays in the owner's name.

One more detail matters in taller houses. A screen on the top floor that shows the visitor at the door is far more useful than a speaker, because it lets someone decide whether the trip downstairs is worth it. For an older resident or anyone recovering from surgery, a picture and a voice at the bedroom station, and a release that lets a known visitor in without the stairs, is the difference between answering the door and ignoring it. We plan for that when the household tells us someone in the house has trouble with stairs, and we make sure the release on that station opens only the door it should.

The Count That Decides

One Family, Two Families or Eight Rentals: What New York Rules Ask of a Gerritsen Beach Front Door

Because the peninsula is almost entirely one- and two-family houses, most of New York's intercom rules never reach it. That is worth saying plainly, since installers sometimes sell intercoms as if every door in the city needed one. Which rules apply depends on the building, not the neighborhood. Nothing here is legal advice; it is a guide to public sources so owners and tenants know what to ask an attorney or the city, and we note where those sources leave questions open.

One- and two-family houses. The city's intercom and entrance-door rules are written for multiple dwellings, which begin at three apartments. In a one-family bungalow or a two-family house, an intercom is the owner's choice, and nothing in the housing code requires one. Once a tenant in a two-family depends on a door system the owner installed, keeping it working becomes part of keeping the apartment in good repair, and a tenant who loses the ability to let visitors in has a reasonable complaint. That is an argument for a system the owner can maintain, with parts that are still made and an account the owner controls.

Three to seven apartments. City data lists only two three-family houses on the peninsula. Parker Custom Security summarizes the state intercom requirement as reaching buildings of eight or more apartments, which leaves smaller multiple dwellings outside it, while the city's rule that a building's entrance door close and lock by itself still applies. A three-family house here needs a street door that shuts and latches on its own whether or not it has an intercom.

Eight or more apartments. Parker Custom Security describes Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a as requiring an intercommunication system at the self-locking main entrance of every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 that contains eight or more apartments. For buildings of that size erected before 1968, the same summary says an intercom becomes required once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing, and the city rule 1 RCNY 42-01 also lists pre-1968 buildings of eight or more apartments among those that need self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercommunication system. The only building of that size our research found anywhere near Gerritsen Beach is 2402 Gerritsen Avenue, which CityRealty lists as eight rental units on three floors, built in 1964, in the strip between Avenue U and Avenue X. A building like that falls on the older side of the 1968 date, so the owner should have counsel read the rule and the building's records before assuming either way.

What counts as a minimum system. The city rule accepts a bell or buzzer system, or a speaking and listening device, as the minimum. That is a low bar. It explains why so many older buildings in Brooklyn still have bell-and-release systems, and it is also why we never describe a code-minimum system as a good one: it lets a visitor ring but gives the household no way to see or speak to them.

Mounting heights. The same rule says no push button may be more than six feet above the floor and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet above it. On a lifted house, the floor is the landing where the visitor stands, not the court below. A station placed for the person at the foot of the stair has to be checked against that rule if the building is one the rule covers.

When it breaks. Parker Custom Security describes a non-working intercom in a covered building as a Class B hazardous violation, enforceable on tenant complaint, and HPD's own guidance tells owners and superintendents to repair broken locks and intercoms immediately. HPD's data shows none of those violations on the peninsula, which is consistent with how few covered buildings there are.

The door that has to close. In multiple dwellings, HPD requires building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors to be self-closing, and its rule says it selects 300 buildings a year for self-closing door inspections. An electric strike fitted for an intercom must never stop a door from closing and latching by itself, in any building of any size.

Getting out. Every door we fit with an electric strike or lock opens from inside without a key, a code or a button. The intercom controls who comes in, never who can leave, which matters in a house where the only stair to the court is the one at the front door.

When the Power Goes

Power Above the Water Line: Battery Backup and Fail-Safe Doors for Gerritsen Beach Intercoms

Most intercom pages mention backup power as an optional extra. On the peninsula it belongs near the top of the list, for two reasons the neighborhood learned the hard way. The first is outages. CBS New York reported that more than two weeks after Sandy, nearly half of Gerritsen Beach was still without power, and the state's call for volunteer electricians that month said almost every home needed an inspection before electricity could return. A long outage is the time when a household most needs to know who is at the door, and it is exactly when a door system plugged into a dead outlet stops working. The second reason is water. The doorbell transformers and intercom power supplies we find in older houses here were often mounted low, beside the electrical panel or the boiler, because that is where the wiring started. After a flood, those were among the first things to go.

Where the power supply goes. In every Gerritsen Beach house we plan the intercom's power supply, battery and any network switch for a spot inside the living space, above the design flood elevation, on a wall or in a closet the owner can reach without moving furniture. In a lifted house that usually means the first living floor, near the router or the electrical panel if the panel was moved up. In an unlifted bungalow it means a closet or a cabinet off the floor. AESS does not do electrical work, so if the best spot has no outlet or needs a dedicated circuit, the owner's electrician adds it and we connect to it; the quote says so in advance.

How long the battery should last. A small battery backup can keep a door station, an indoor screen or two and the network switch running for hours rather than days, depending on the equipment and how often the door is used. That covers the common case of an evening outage and the first night of a bigger one. For a longer event, some households plug the intercom's battery into a generator circuit or a home battery system once it is safe to do so; that connection is the electrician's work, not ours. We size the battery on the quote for the specific stations chosen and tell the household roughly how long it should run.

What the door does when the power dies. If the front door has an electric strike, the type of strike decides what happens in an outage. A fail-secure strike stays locked when it loses power, so the house is never left open, and the door still opens by key from outside and by hand from inside. A fail-safe strike unlocks when it loses power, which suits certain fire-rated or commercial exits but is rarely what a homeowner wants on a front door. For houses we specify fail-secure strikes, a closer that pulls the door shut every time, and a key path that works with no power at all. At handover we switch the power off and prove it.

What the internet does. A door station that only rings a phone app depends on the home's internet staying up, and in a widespread outage the router goes down with everything else. Indoor wall stations that talk to the door station directly, over their own cable, keep working as long as the battery does. For households that lived through 2012, that is often the deciding argument for at least one wired indoor station, even when everyone also wants the app.

Wired or Wireless

Crowded Air on a Narrow Court: When a Gerritsen Beach Door Station Should Be Wired

Wood-frame houses are friendlier to radio signals than brick, which is why many Gerritsen Beach households start with a wireless doorbell or a battery video kit. Some of them work for years. Others start dropping calls, freezing the picture or taking several seconds to ring, and owners assume the device is faulty. Often it is the neighborhood. On streets that DCP measured as narrow as fifteen feet including sidewalks, with houses built to the lot line, a door station may be within range of a dozen other households' routers, cameras and devices, all sharing a handful of channels. In the evening, when every house is streaming, a wireless door station at the far end of a narrow house can lose the fight for air.

We do not treat wireless as wrong. For a renter who cannot drill, a one-story bungalow where the router sits a few feet from the door, or a household that only wants a camera and a chime, a well-placed wireless doorbell is a reasonable choice. But for a household that wants to answer reliably from three floors up, release the door, and keep working when the internet is down, a wired door station is the better tool. The two common wired options are a two-wire system, which carries picture, voice and release over one pair and can often reuse an old doorbell wire that tests clean, and a networked system, which uses a network cable to the door that also carries power from a small switch.

The choice between those two usually comes down to what is already in the walls. A bungalow whose doorbell pair still runs from the button to a chime near the kitchen is a natural fit for two-wire video on that pair. A replacement house with a network cable already run to the front door, or a lifted house where a new cable can be fished up the stair wall, is a natural fit for a networked station. Either way, the indoor screens that matter most get a wired connection so they do not depend on the household's Wi-Fi. During the survey the technician measures signal at the door and tests any existing cable, and the quote explains which route we recommend and what the alternative would cost.

After the Water

What to Keep and What to Replace When a Gerritsen Beach Doorbell or Intercom Took Water

Many systems we expect to see on the peninsula were damaged in 2012 or in a smaller flood since, and owners reasonably ask whether any of the old parts can be saved. The answer depends on where each part sat relative to the water and what the rebuild did to the wire between them.

The door station or button. If it sat above the water line on the front wall, it may have survived, though years of salt air this close to the water can also wear out an older speaker or button. For a household upgrading to video, the old button is usually replaced anyway. If it sat low, at the foot of a stair or on a gate post, it was very likely submerged, and corrosion inside is common even when the face looks clean.

The wire. Low-voltage wire that was under water and then dried can still test clean, especially if its insulation was intact. Wire whose insulation was nicked, or whose splices were in a wall that stayed wet for weeks, often fails. And wire that a lifting contractor cut at the sill is gone, whatever its condition. The only way to know is to test it from end to end, which the technician does before anything else is decided.

The transformer, chime or master unit. These were usually the lowest parts of the old system and the most likely to have been under water. We replace them, and we move the new power supply upstairs. Where an old room-to-room master unit survived above the water line, its speakers are generally not worth reusing for a modern door system, because nobody makes parts for most of them anymore.

What we tell you before you pay. If the survey shows the old pair is usable, the quote says so and prices a system that uses it. If it shows the pair is gone, the quote prices a new run along the route the technician found. If a wall has to be opened to find out, we say that too, and we do not open it without the owner's agreement. Because AESS does not patch or paint walls, any opening we make is kept as small as the work allows, and we say in advance where it will be so the owner can arrange a painter if one is needed.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Gerritsen Beach: Questions Answered

These are the situations Gerritsen Beach owners, tenants and business people are most likely to face: a house that grew taller, cable and networks in close-built wood houses, life after the water, and the few shops, halls and rentals that need more than a doorbell. Each is answered here once and is not repeated in the FAQ below.

Houses that grew a floor or two

Our bungalow was rebuilt with two floors above the old one, and the bedrooms are now three levels from the front door. How many indoor stations does a house like that actually need?

Usually two wall stations and phone answering, not one on every level. The first station goes where the household spends its waking hours, typically the kitchen or the main living floor, and the second goes near the bedrooms on the top floor so someone can see and answer the door without the stairs. A lower level used for storage or parking does not need one, and a home office or a room for an older parent gets a third only if someone is there most of the day. Phone answering then covers the yard, the dock and anyone away from the house. If the budget allows only one wall station, put it on the floor where people are most, and use the app for the rest.

The new top floor was insulated with spray foam. Does that make it harder to get intercom cable up there?

It can. Closed-cell foam fills the wall cavity solid, so a cable cannot be pushed through it the way it slides past fiberglass batts. The usual answer is to avoid foamed exterior walls entirely and follow an interior route: the stair wall, a closet stacked over another closet, or a chase beside the plumbing that the builder opened through both floors. If none of those exists, a short run of painted raceway in a closet or along a stair stringer is often neater than cutting into foam. The technician checks which walls were foamed during the survey and the quote names the route.

When the second story went on, the builder kept the bungalow's original front wall and door. Is the old doorbell wire inside that wall still any use?

Possibly, and it is worth testing before anything else. If the original front wall stayed in place, the pair from the button to the chime may still be intact, and a two-wire video door station can often carry picture, voice and door release over it. What usually breaks is the far end: the chime and transformer may have been removed, moved or buried behind new drywall during the addition. We test the pair from the door to wherever it now ends. If it is continuous and clean, it becomes the backbone for the door station, and only the runs to the upstairs stations are new.

Cable, Wi-Fi and the house network

Our house is a few feet from the neighbors on both sides and our phone shows dozens of Wi-Fi networks. Is that why our wireless video doorbell keeps dropping calls?

It may well be part of it. Wireless door stations share a small number of channels with every router, camera and smart device in range, and on close-built streets that can be a lot of devices. Signal strength at the door, how far the router is from it, and how much of the household's own traffic is on the same network all add up. During the survey the technician measures signal at the door and looks at channel crowding. Sometimes moving the router or adding an access point fixes it; often, for a household that relies on the door, a wired station on a single cable is the lasting fix.

Our house was rebuilt under Build It Back and the contractor left a network jack beside the front door. Can a video intercom run on it?

Often, yes, if the cable behind the jack is good and ends somewhere sensible. A networked door station can take power and data over one network cable from a small switch, so a jack by the door is a head start. We test that cable for continuity and performance, find where its other end lands, and check whether that spot is above the lower level and close to the router. If the cable passes and the far end is usable, the door station goes on it and the switch goes on battery backup. If it fails or ends in a lower level, we either repair the route or run a new one, and the quote says which.

We want to keep the camera on our porch and add an intercom. Will they end up as two separate apps on our phones?

Not necessarily. Many door stations can share a brand and an app with cameras, and some recorders can show the door station's picture alongside the cameras. Whether that works for your porch camera depends on its make and model. If it already works well and you are happy with its app, we can leave it as it is and add a door station on its own system. If you would rather have one app, we will tell you which door stations work with the camera you have, or whether replacing the camera at the same time would be simpler. We confirm compatibility from model numbers before quoting, not after installing.

After the water

Our old doorbell transformer is still screwed to the wall of the lower level, where the water came in. Can the new intercom use it?

We do not reuse a transformer that has been under water, and we do not leave a power supply on a wall that floods. Even a transformer that still hums may have corroded terminals, and a door system is only as reliable as its power. The new system gets its own power supply, sized for the door station and indoor stations chosen, mounted upstairs in the living space above the design flood elevation, ideally with a battery backup. If the best spot has no outlet, the owner's electrician adds one first. The old transformer is disconnected and, with the owner's agreement, removed.

Our house sat empty for months after the storm and the old intercom speakers were left behind in the walls. Should those be pulled out before a new system goes in?

Only if they are in the way. An abandoned speaker in a wall that is otherwise sound does no harm, and removing it means patching a hole. If a new station is going in the same spot, the old one comes out and the new one covers the opening. If the old unit is somewhere nobody needs a station anymore, it can be disconnected and left, or covered with a blank plate. We note every abandoned unit and its wire on the wiring record so the next person to open the wall knows it is dead.

Shops, halls and the one building with eight apartments

Our eight-unit rental building was finished in 1964, a few years before the date in the state law. Is it held to the same intercom rule as buildings put up after 1968?

Not the same way, according to public summaries. Parker Custom Security describes the state requirement as applying directly to Class A buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and to older buildings of that size once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask for it in writing. The city rule 1 RCNY 42-01 also lists pre-1968 buildings of eight or more apartments among those that need self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercommunication system. Because those sources frame the older case differently, and because a past conversion could change the building's status, an owner of a 1964 eight-unit building should have counsel read the rule against the building's records. Either way, the entrance door must close and lock by itself.

Our boat business closes for the winter but people still show up at the gate. Can the station there reach the owner's phone in the off-season?

Yes. A door station at a gate or an office door can ring a phone app instead of, or as well as, a station inside, and the call routing can be changed by season. In summer it might ring the office and the dock; in winter it can ring only the owner's phone, with the release turned off so nobody can be let in remotely unless the owner chooses to. The station's camera still records who called if the system includes recording. Because a gate near the water sits low, its station, cable and power follow the same flood rules as any station below the flood line.

Our parish hall is rented out on some evenings. Can the door station ring whoever has booked the hall that night instead of the rectory?

It can, with a system that supports scheduled call routing or a list of answering phones that an administrator can change. On a normal day the door rings the office or the rectory; on a booked evening, the administrator adds the organizer's phone for those hours and removes it afterward. The release can be limited the same way. What matters is that one or two people at the hall hold the administrator account and know how to make those changes, and that the account stays in the hall's name. We set up the routing, show the administrators how to change it and leave written instructions.

FAQ

Gerritsen Beach Intercom Installation FAQ

If we send photos of our Gerritsen Beach house tonight, when will someone get back to us?
+

Requests get a booked callback within two hours on working days. Many estimates can be given the same day from photos of the front door, whatever is beside it now, the old chime and the transformer, together with the number of floors and households.

Is an address at the end of the peninsula billed differently from the rest of Brooklyn?
+

No. Gerritsen Beach, from the southern courts to the blocks near Avenue U, is billed at our Brooklyn rate, and every charge appears on the written quote before anything is booked.

Our house is one of the original 1920s bungalows. Does its age alone make the job cost more?
+

Not by itself. What moves the price is whether the old doorbell pair still tests clean, whether the attic or crawl space gives an easy route, how many rooms need a station and where the power supply can go. An untouched one-story bungalow is often one of the simpler jobs on the peninsula.

Will you patch and repaint the shingles or siding where the old bell button comes off?
+

We do not do patching or painting. The new door station is usually sized and placed to cover the old button's hole, and any new opening is kept as small as the work allows. If a patch is needed, we say where in advance so you can arrange a painter or siding contractor.

We bought a video intercom kit online before calling anyone. Will you put it in?
+

Yes, as labor-only work, provided the kit suits the house, the door and the wire. We check the model numbers before the visit and tell you about anything missing, such as enough indoor stations for your floors or a suitable power supply. Customer-supplied equipment is covered by its manufacturer's terms, not ours.

Our house is partway through being lifted. Should we wait until the new stair is built before asking for an intercom quote?
+

Ask now and install later. The best time to plan the cable is before the new walls close, so a survey during framing lets us pull runs to every floor while they are open. The door station itself goes in once the stair, the landing and the door are finished.

Visitors struggle to find our house on the court after dark. Can the door station help them?
+

It can help. Many door stations have a lit name or number plate and a light that comes on when someone approaches, which makes the door easier to pick out at night. We set the light's brightness at handover so it guides visitors without shining into a neighbor's window a few feet away.

Can a neighbor who watches our house while we are away be one of the people the door rings?
+

Yes, on systems with phone answering. The owner can add the neighbor's phone as an extra user, decide whether that user can release the door, and remove the user again on return. The administrator account stays in the owner's name the whole time.

How much of the installation happens outside, and how much inside the house?
+

Most of the visible work is at the front door and on the floors that get indoor stations. Outside, the door station is mounted and its wall entry is sealed. Inside, cable is routed to each station and the power supply and any battery are mounted upstairs. In most houses the openings are kept to the door and the spot behind each station.

Who keeps the app login and the administrator password after the technician leaves?
+

You do. The administrator account is set up in the owner's name and email, and the password is handed over with the wiring record. We do not keep control of your system. For a two-family house, each household gets its own user account under the owner's administrator account.

Does someone have to be at a Gerritsen Beach house the whole time the work is done?
+

Yes. An adult who can approve where stations go and open every floor should be home for the full visit, and for the handover at the end, when every station, phone and release is tested with the household watching.
