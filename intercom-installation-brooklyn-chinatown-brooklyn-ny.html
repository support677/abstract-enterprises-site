The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Chinatown Properties Need Intercom Installation

In a building with a shop at street level, the residents' door is usually a slim entrance beside the store window, sometimes squeezed between the shop's roll-down gate and the next building. It is the only lock between a busy sidewalk and the stair that leads to the families' homes. When the intercom at that door fails, the households upstairs have two bad choices. They can walk down every time someone rings, which is hard for an older relative on the third floor, or they can prop the door or leave the lock unlatched, which lets anyone in. The systems we find on these doors are often decades old. Our own repair records for prewar walk-ups across Brooklyn, including Sunset Park, list Lee Dan, M&S, Nutone and older Aiphone audio units, many running on wiring that has been spliced, extended and painted over through several owners. Some still work well and can be kept. Others have handsets that hum, buttons that ring the wrong floor, or a single tired transformer feeding everything.

Then there are the people answering the door. Many households here include several generations, and the person home during the day is often the one least comfortable with English. A panel that lists names only in English letters, a monitor whose menus are in English, or a phone app set up in a language nobody in the apartment reads can make a new system harder to use than the old buzzer it replaced. We plan the directory, the indoor stations and any app accounts around who will actually use them, which is one of the things that makes an intercom here different from the same hardware installed somewhere else. Many owners also pair the work with access control for the street door, so tenants can use a fob or code while visitors still ring through the intercom.

  • The avenue is shops with homes above. PLUTO lists 265 store-and-residence lots among 398 lots with an Eighth Avenue address in ZIPs 11220 and 11232. The residents' door shares a facade, and often a cellar and an electrical service, with a business.

Same-Day Repair in Brooklyn Chinatown

Need Repair in Brooklyn Chinatown? A Street Door That Won't Release, Handsets That Hum, or a Button That Rings the Shop

Most urgent intercom calls from buildings on and around Eighth Avenue are one of four faults. The first is a door that will not release: tenants press the button, hear the buzz in the handset, but the lock at the street door does nothing. That usually points to the strike in the door frame, a burned-out coil, a keeper knocked out of line by years of slamming, or a power supply too weak to throw the latch from the top floor. The second is a hum or a dead line on the handsets, which in older audio systems is often a single splice that has corroded, or a cable nicked when the shop below was renovated. The third is a crossed button: a panel where the third floor's button rings the second floor, or where one button rings the store's back room because someone reused a pair of wires during a fit-out. The fourth is a whole system gone quiet, which almost always leads to the cellar and the transformer or power box that feeds it.

If the street door will not lock, deal with that first, because an unlocked door is the real hazard. Many residents' doors here can be held shut with the key lock alone while the intercom is down. Then call (347) 934-8335. Single faults on systems we can work on are often traced and fixed in the same visit, and we tell you on the phone what we can and cannot promise from the photos. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our service rate of $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum, and we say at the start how long we expect the trace to take. If the fault is in a cable that passes through a leased store, we need the shopkeeper's permission and sometimes their hours. If the repair would cost a good share of what a new system costs, or the parts for a decades-old brand cannot be found, we say so plainly and give you both numbers, so the owner can decide with the full picture rather than paying for the same fault twice.

Building Specialists

Intercom Installation for Brooklyn Chinatown's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up nearly every intercom call we get from these blocks. They differ in how many households share the door, where the door sits, how cable can travel from the cellar to each floor, and who besides the owner has a say. Those four things decide the plan long before anyone compares brands.

The most common lot on Eighth Avenue by PLUTO's count: a store at the sidewalk, two apartments upstairs, one narrow residents' door, and a cellar often used by the shop.

The wider avenue building

Three to six apartments over one or two stores, with a row of mailboxes in a small vestibule and an older multi-button panel by the door.

The brick two-family on a numbered street

Two households stacked in a 1920s brick house, often an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, sharing a stoop, a front door and a cellar.

Three- and four-family houses where a landlord controls the hall and the panel, and where the legal count must match the buttons.

Mixed-use buildings of 8 to 28 stories planned or rising on Eighth Avenue, with lobbies, elevators, retail below and, at 6414, a cellar garage.

Shop below, two homes above: one narrow door and a cellar that belongs to someone else

In the typical Eighth Avenue store-and-residence building, the shop takes the full width of the ground floor except for a slot at one side, where a door opens onto a straight stair up to two apartments. The intercom panel goes on that door or the wall beside it, and the space is tight. There may be a shop sign above, a gate housing beside, and a steady stream of shoppers, delivery carts and hand trucks passing within inches. We mount the panel flush or in a low-profile surface box where the wall allows, so it does not catch a cart or a bag, and we keep it clear of the gate track and housing, which we do not drill into. The DOB's entrance-door rule, 1 RCNY 42-01, sets the heights for buttons and the speaking device, and the panel goes where those heights work on your particular door and step.

The harder part is often below the sidewalk. In many of these buildings the cellar is used by the store, for stock, storage or a kitchen's back-of-house, and the old intercom transformer, the building's meters and the cable to the apartments all pass through it. That means three things for the job. We schedule around the shop's hours and ask the shopkeeper before working in their space. We do not take power from a store circuit, and we will not turn off a circuit that feeds the shop without the shopkeeper's agreement. And where it is possible, we move the intercom's power supply out of the leased cellar to the residents' stair or a locked box the owner controls, so the households upstairs are not dependent on getting into a store to restore their front door. If the store has been renovated, we also look for intercom cable that was cut, spliced or buried behind new finishes, because a fit-out is the most common reason a button in these buildings stops working.

The wider avenue building: several households in a tiny vestibule

Some Eighth Avenue lots are wider or taller, with three to six apartments over the shops. PLUTO lists dozens of these among the avenue's store-and-residence classes, most built in the same 1920s era. Their entrances usually open into a small vestibule with mailboxes on one wall and a second, inner door before the stair. The original panel is often a multi-button plate with name cards, and the handsets upstairs may be from three different decades because tenants replaced their own when they broke. On these buildings we look first at whether the riser, the bundle of wire that climbs from the cellar to each floor, is intact and has enough pairs for the system you want. A building with a sound riser can often take a modern audio or video system that runs on the existing wire. A riser that has been patched floor by floor, or that lost pairs to a renovation, usually means pulling new cable up the stair hall in a protected pathway.

The vestibule also raises a question about which door the intercom controls. In many buildings the outer door stays unlocked during shop hours and the inner door is the real lock. In others, the outer door is the lock and the inner one is just a draft door. The release has to fire the door that actually protects the stair, and the panel has to sit where a visitor will find it, which is usually outside the outer door even when the lock is inside. We sort that out with the owner on the survey, because getting it wrong leaves either a panel nobody can reach or a release that opens a door that was never locked.

The brick two-family on a numbered street: two households and a stoop

The side streets between Seventh and Ninth Avenues are lined with brick two-families from the 1920s. Many have a short stoop or a few steps up to the main door, a separate cellar entrance under or beside it, and two apartments stacked above. Some have one door for both households; some have a separate door for each. For these houses, a two-button audio or video system is usually the right size, and the question is less about the product than about the path. Where the old bell wire runs up inside the wall to the second floor and tests sound, a two-wire video system can often reuse it, which avoids opening plaster. Where it does not, the cleanest path is often up through the cellar ceiling and inside a closet or along the stair, and we show the owner that route before anything is drilled.

In many of these houses the owner lives on one floor and rents the other. The owner usually wants a system they can manage, and the tenant wants a handset or monitor that works without having to ask. We set up each apartment's station on its own, so the tenant's calls go only to the tenant, and if the system calls phones, each household gets its own account rather than sharing the owner's. Where a parent or grandparent spends the day home alone, we often suggest a video station in the room they use most, with large, simple controls, so they can see a delivery person at the door without going down the stoop. Two-families sit below the eight-apartment line, so the law does not require an intercom at all; families install one because the stair is steep and the door is busy.

Three or four households, one door: the landlord's hall and the honest count

Three- and four-family houses make up a large share of the side-street stock, and they are where the household count matters most. These houses often have one front door, one hall and one stair serving every apartment, with the landlord responsible for all of it. When we plan an intercom here, we ask for the number of legal apartments, which comes from the Certificate of Occupancy or the building's HPD registration, and we size the panel to that number. If more people live in the house than the paperwork allows, that is a conversation for the owner, the city and possibly a lawyer, not something we can wire around. What we can do is make sure every legal household has a working station, a clear listing on the panel and a door release that works from their floor.

A house with three or four apartments is also where the oldest systems tend to survive, because nobody thought it was worth replacing a buzzer that still mostly worked. Those systems often run on low-voltage AC from a doorbell-style transformer in the cellar, and the door release gets weaker the farther the apartment is from it. Before recommending a replacement, we measure the voltage at the strike when each apartment presses its release. If the top floor gets too little, a properly sized supply can fix it without replacing the whole system. If the handsets themselves are failing and parts are gone, a new system usually makes more sense, and we can often run it on the same wire if that wire tests sound.

The building that is getting taller: plan the intercom with the new floors

The permits YIMBY reported near the avenue, adding two or three floors to two-story buildings, change the front door as much as they change the skyline. A building that had a shop and two apartments may end up with six, eight or sixteen. The best time to plan the intercom is before the new floors are framed, when cable can be run up a shaft or stair wall that is still open and stubbed into each new apartment's entry. Pulling cable into finished apartments later costs more and leaves more patches. We work from the architect's floor plans where they exist, mark where each apartment's station will hang, and coordinate with the general contractor and the electrician so the cable path and the power supply are on their drawings, not improvised at the end.

The household count after the expansion also decides what the law expects. A building that ends up with eight or more apartments, under the Attorney General's summary of Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, needs a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door, and because the new floors are a conversion after 1968, the owner should plan for that from the start. The old two-button buzzer from the original building cannot simply be extended; it was never built for that many households, and its single transformer will not drive that many stations. We size the new system for the final count, leave a few spare pairs in the riser for future changes, and make sure the new apartments and the old ones are listed on the panel the same way.

The new tower and its garage: lobbies, elevators and the parking gate

The towers going up on Eighth Avenue are a different kind of job. A building like 6208 Eighth Avenue, with 497 units over retail, will have a lobby entrance for residents, separate entries for the stores, service doors, and likely elevator and amenity controls, and the developer will usually choose a networked entry system as part of the building's design. The eight-story building planned at 6414 Eighth Avenue, with 68 enclosed parking spaces, adds a garage entrance where drivers need to call in or use a credential without leaving the car. For buildings of this size, the intercom is tied to the building's network, its access control and often its cameras, and it is designed with the architect and the electrical engineer, not picked off a shelf.

We work on those projects as the low-voltage installer when the developer or general contractor brings us in, and we also work for the people who buy or rent in these buildings once they are open: owners who want a monitor in their apartment, boards or managers who need an extra panel at a side entrance, or a store tenant who wants its own door station separate from the residents'. The concrete and block in a new tower behave differently from the brick of the 1920s buildings around it, and cable often travels in conduit rather than through open walls. Where a new run has to pass from a garage or cellar through a block wall into the telecom room, we core the wall cleanly, sleeve the opening and seal it as the building's design requires.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Street Door Beside a Shop Window, From the Sidewalk to the Top Floor

These notes describe how our technicians approach buildings like the ones on Eighth Avenue and its side streets. They are our practice, not a story about any one address.

Stand on the sidewalk first. Before touching anything, the technician watches the door for a few minutes. How often does it open? Does it close and latch on its own, or bounce back? Do people hold it for each other? A door that does not latch is the first problem to fix, because the best intercom in Brooklyn cannot protect a stair if the door behind it stays open. The closer, the hinges and the strike are checked before the panel.

Count the buttons and the boxes. The technician counts the buttons on the panel, the mailboxes in the vestibule and the doorbells upstairs. If the three numbers do not agree with the number of apartments the owner gave us, we ask why, politely and before going further. The answer is usually simple, like an old unit combined with another years ago, but sometimes it is not.

Test the release from the top. We go to the farthest apartment and ask the resident to press the door release while a second person watches the strike. A release that works from the second floor but stutters from the third tells us about power and wire length before any meter comes out.

Go to the cellar with the owner, and with the shopkeeper if it is their space. The technician finds where the old system is powered, what else shares that circuit and whether anything around it looks unsafe: loose fuses, extension cords, stored batteries. If something in the cellar is a hazard beyond our work, we say so to the owner in plain words.

Ask who answers the door. The last question on the survey is about people. Who is home during the day? Which languages do they read? Does anyone have trouble with stairs, small buttons or hearing a handset? The answers decide the station, the directory and the setup more than anything in the cellar.

The Rules at the Front Door

The Eight-Apartment Line: What New York Law Says About Intercoms in Brooklyn Chinatown Buildings

Many owners on these blocks are not sure whether their building has to have an intercom, and the honest answer depends on two numbers: how many apartments the building has, and when it was built or converted. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, so you can ask the right questions of your own attorney or managing agent before choosing a system.

Eight or more apartments. The New York Attorney General's Residential Tenants' Rights Guide summarizes Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a this way: buildings with eight or more apartments must have a two-way voice intercom system from each apartment to the front door, along with self-locking and self-closing entrance doors. That applies to buildings built or converted after January 1, 1968. For buildings from before 1968, the guide says an intercom is required if a majority of all the apartments request one, and it adds that landlords may recover the cost of providing the equipment from tenants. On these blocks that difference matters. Our PLUTO check counted 127 lots with eight or more apartments across the study area, and 90 of them were built before 1968. A building that adds floors and new apartments, though, is a conversion, and the owner should plan for the post-1968 rule.

Fewer than eight. Two-, three- and four-family houses, and most of the store-and-residence buildings on the avenue, sit below the line. The law does not require them to have an intercom at all. But if they have one, the city expects it to work. An installer's guide to New York's intercom rules describes a broken intercom or buzzer as a Class B hazardous violation under the Housing Maintenance Code, enforced on tenant complaint, and HPD's own records for ZIP 11220 show 51 bell or buzzer violations issued since the start of 2024, mostly Class B, with wording that tells the owner to properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell buzzer. Habitat magazine reported in 2024 that courts have treated a working intercom as an essential service rather than an amenity. Put simply: once tenants rely on a system, keeping it working is part of the job of running the building.

Heights and placement. The DOB rule on entrance doors, 1 RCNY 42-01, sets limits on where the parts go. No push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device has to sit between four and five feet from the floor. On an Eighth Avenue residents' door that opens a step or two above the sidewalk, or on a side-street house where the panel sits at the top of a stoop, those heights are measured from where the visitor actually stands. We measure on the survey rather than assume. For businesses and public spaces, the ADA's reach-range rules for operable parts also apply, which usually means keeping buttons within easier reach than the residential maximum.

Doors and locks. An intercom only works if the door behind it closes and locks by itself. The same tenants' guide pairs the intercom rule with self-closing, self-locking doors, and HPD treats broken entrance-door hardware seriously. When we fit an electric strike, we make sure the door can still be opened freely from the inside without a key or a button, because nobody leaving a building in an emergency should be stopped by the intercom's lock.

Landmark districts. The four Sunset Park historic districts, North, South, Central and 50th Street, were designated on June 18, 2019, and Brooklyn Paper reported that inside them homeowners cannot change a house's exterior without Landmarks Preservation Commission approval. The districts cover roughly twenty blocks between 44th and 59th Streets and Fourth and Seventh Avenues, which puts the Eighth Avenue fronts outside them. Some side-street houses near Seventh Avenue may be inside. If your address is, a new panel on the facade or a new opening for cable is exterior work that needs LPC review, and we plan the panel so it can be mounted in the existing door frame or a reversible location where possible. Check the address on the LPC's map before we start.

Paperwork and household counts. Every one of those rules depends on knowing how many apartments the building legally has. That is why we ask for the Certificate of Occupancy or the HPD registration on buildings near the line, and why we wire the system to that number. If your building's paperwork is missing or out of date, sorting it out comes first, and we would rather wait than install a system that matches neither the building on paper nor the building in real life.

Language at the Door

Who Answers the Door: Setting Up an Intercom for Families Who Read Chinese, English or Both

An intercom is a conversation between a person on the sidewalk and a person upstairs. In much of Brooklyn Chinatown, the person upstairs may be a grandmother who reads Chinese characters but not English letters, a father who speaks Fuzhounese and some Mandarin, a teenager who does everything on a phone in English, or a tenant who speaks Cantonese. The hardware does not care, but the setup does. We plan four things around language and age on every job here, and none of them costs more than the time to ask.

The panel directory. Older panels use paper name cards. Many newer video panels show a scrolling or searchable directory on a screen, and some list only apartment numbers. We ask each household how they want to be listed: apartment number only, an English name, a name in Chinese characters, or two of those together. On many panels the directory font and character set are limited by the manufacturer, so before choosing a model we check whether it can display the names your households want. Where a screen directory cannot show characters, listing by apartment number with a printed card beside the panel is a practical answer. Some families prefer not to show any name at all, for privacy, and that is their choice.

The indoor station. A handset with one talk button and one door button is easy for anyone. A video monitor with menus is not, especially if the menus are in a language the user cannot read. Where the manufacturer offers a Chinese menu option, we set it; where it does not, we set the monitor to do as little as possible by default, so pressing the screen answers and pressing one marked button opens the door. Then we mark the two buttons that matter with simple labels in the language the household chooses.

The phone app. Systems that call phones depend on an app, and app language usually follows the phone's language setting. We set up each account with the person who will use it, on their own phone, and test a call while they hold it. If a grandparent will not use an app at all, the household can keep a handset or monitor on the wall as well, so the door does not depend on one person's phone.

The handover. At the end of the job we show each household how to answer, talk and open the door, and we leave short written instructions. We cannot promise a technician who speaks Mandarin, Cantonese or Fuzhounese on every visit, and our quotes and written notes are in English, so many families ask a relative to join the walk-through. We slow down and demonstrate rather than explain, because pressing a button twice while someone watches teaches more than a page of instructions.

This is also where an honest count of households pays off again. Each household gets its own listing, its own station and its own app accounts. A system that lumps two families onto one button, or lists one household under a name that left the building years ago, creates the kind of confusion at the door that makes people start propping it open again.

  • Free exit comes first. Doors with electric strikes or locks must still open from the inside without a key or special knowledge. We fit hardware so the intercom controls entry only, never the way out.

Your Questions Answered

Intercom Installation in Brooklyn Chinatown: Questions Answered

These are the situations owners, tenants and businesses on and around Eighth Avenue describe when they call: buildings growing taller, households that do not all read the same language, stores downstairs, old cellars, and new towers. Each one is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Buildings that are adding apartments

Our two-story building off Eighth Avenue is being raised to four floors and will end up with eight apartments. Does reaching eight change what the front door legally needs?

It can, and it is worth planning for now. The Attorney General's tenants' guide summarizes Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a as requiring a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door in buildings with eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968, along with self-closing, self-locking entrance doors. Adding floors and apartments to an existing building is generally treated as a conversion, so an owner taking a building to eight should assume the post-1968 rule applies and confirm it with the architect or expediter handling the filing. In practice that means every one of the eight apartments needs its own station that can talk to the street door and release it, and the old buzzer from the two-apartment building will not do the job. Plan the cable path while the new floors are still open.

The contractor raising our building wants to keep the old two-button buzzer and just add buttons for the new floors. Why might that fail?

Because the old system was built for two households and almost everything about it is sized for that. The transformer that powers it may barely drive two handsets and one strike; adding six more stations, plus longer cable runs to higher floors, often leaves the top apartments with a release that will not throw the latch. The panel may have no room for more buttons, and the original wire up the building usually has only enough pairs for the apartments it served. Even when an add-on works on the day, it tends to fail the first winter. A new system sized for the final number of apartments, on cable pulled during construction, usually costs less over the life of the building than repeated repair calls on an extended buzzer. We are happy to walk the contractor through what we found.

Households, extended families and the people inside

My parents live in a room at the back of our apartment and want to answer the door themselves. Can they get their own station without a separate button on the street panel?

Yes. Most audio and video systems allow an extension station inside the same apartment, so one button on the street panel rings two stations at once, one in the front room and one at the back. Either can answer and either can open the door. That is very different from adding a button for a separate household, which we only do for legal apartments. An extension keeps the panel honest, one apartment, one listing, while giving a grandparent a monitor in the room where they spend the day, so they can see a delivery or a relative at the door without walking through the apartment. If the system calls phones, a second family member's phone can be added to the same apartment account too. We set the extension's ring volume and menu language for the person who will use it.

Our pre-1968 building has nine apartments, and a majority of the tenants have asked for an intercom. Can the cost be passed on to them?

The Attorney General's tenants' guide says that in buildings from before 1968 with eight or more apartments, an intercom is required if a majority of all the apartments request one, and that landlords may recover the cost of providing the equipment from tenants. How that recovery works in a rent-stabilized or rent-controlled building, and how it is calculated and shown on rent, is a question for your attorney or managing agent, not for an installer. What we can do is give you a written quote that separates the equipment, the cable and the labor clearly, so whoever handles the rent side has the numbers they need. We also suggest keeping a copy of the tenants' request with the building's records, along with our finished wiring record.

Names, screens and languages

Some households in our building go by Chinese names and others by English ones. How should they be listed on a new video panel?

Ask each household, and list them the way they choose. Some want an apartment number only, some want a family name in English letters, some want characters, and some want both. The limit is usually the panel itself: many screen directories handle only certain character sets or fonts, so before choosing a model we check whether it can show what your households want. If it cannot, listing every apartment by number on the screen, with a printed directory card beside the panel in both scripts, works well and is easy to update. Whatever the format, the listing should match the apartment numbers on the mailboxes and doors, so a courier who looks up 3R finds 3R. When someone moves out, the listing should change the same week, which is easier on a system the owner can update from an app.

My parents speak Fuzhounese, read Chinese and do not read English. Can the monitor and the phone app be set up so they can use them?

Usually, with some care. Phone apps generally follow the phone's language setting, and many major intercom apps offer a Chinese interface, so we set up your parents' accounts on their own phones and test a real call while they hold them. Wall monitors vary: some offer Chinese menus, others only English. Where a monitor has no Chinese option, we set it so it needs no menus at all for daily use: the screen lights when someone rings, one touch answers, and one marked button opens the door. We label those buttons in the language you choose. During the handover we demonstrate rather than explain, and a family member who can translate is welcome to join. Where a monitor is too much, a simple handset with two buttons is a perfectly good answer.

The shop downstairs and the busy sidewalk

Strangers press every button on our panel until somebody lets them in. What can a new intercom do about that?

Several things. A video panel lets each household see who is at the door before releasing it, which ends most of the casual buzzing in. Systems that call phones show the caller on the screen too, and a tenant away from home has no reason to open the door blind. Some panels can be set so that a visitor has to choose one apartment from a directory rather than pressing a row of buttons, which slows down someone trying every number. A door that closes and locks every time, with a strike that releases for a few seconds only, matters as much as the panel. And the building can agree a simple rule with tenants: no releasing the door for someone you have not seen. On an avenue as busy as Eighth, that combination does more than any single gadget.

Our residents' door opens one step up from a crowded sidewalk. Where exactly does the panel go so it can be used and not knocked?

The DOB's entrance-door rule sets the heights: push buttons no more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device between four and five feet. We measure those from where a visitor actually stands, which on a door one step up may be the sidewalk rather than the threshold, and choose a spot that works for both. On a narrow residents' door beside a shop, we mount the panel flush in the wall or the door frame where possible, or in a slim surface box, on the side away from the store's gate housing so it is not hit when the gate goes up or down. We keep it out of the path of delivery carts, and on a video panel we check the camera's view at different times of day, since a low winter sun or a bright shop sign can wash out the picture.

Power, cellars and outages

The old intercom is powered from a cellar full of old fuses, and after a battery fire on our block everyone is nervous. How do you make the new system's power safe?

An intercom is a low-voltage system, but it still starts at a building power source, and that is where we look hardest. We do not plug an intercom supply into an extension cord or a store's outlet. Where the building needs a new or dedicated circuit for the power supply, that is work for a licensed electrician, and we coordinate with one. The intercom's supply then sits in a protected, labeled enclosure, ideally in a space the owner controls rather than a leased store cellar, with nothing stored around it. Where the system calls for it, a battery backup keeps the panel running through short power cuts. If we see a cellar hazard outside our work, such as overloaded fuses, stored e-bike batteries or blocked access to the meters, we point it out to the owner plainly, because a front door is no use in a building that is unsafe behind it.

What does a video intercom do when the power or the internet goes out?

It depends on the system, and it is worth asking before you choose. A wired audio or video system that runs only inside the building does not need the internet at all; it keeps working as long as it has power, and with a battery backup it can ride through a short outage. A system that calls phones needs the building's internet to reach the app, so if the internet drops, the app calls stop, though many panels can still ring indoor stations if the building has them. In a power cut, an electric strike that is fail-secure keeps the door locked, so residents use their keys; one that is fail-safe unlocks, which is rarely what a residents' door on Eighth Avenue should do. We explain which way your system behaves before we install it, and set it up so the door stays secure.

New buildings and garages

We are buying into one of the new buildings rising on Eighth Avenue. What should we ask about the entry system before closing?

Ask who makes the entry system and whether it is wired inside the building, cloud-based or both. Ask whether residents get an indoor station, a phone app or both, and whether the app and the lobby directory can show Chinese. Ask what happens in a power cut, whether there is battery backup on the lobby panel and the door hardware, and who the building calls when the system fails. Ask whether there are ongoing service or software charges, who pays them, and whether they are in the common charges. If there is a garage, ask how guests and deliveries get in. And ask whether you are allowed to add your own monitor or extension inside your apartment, because some building systems allow it and some do not. Those answers tell you how the front door will work on an ordinary Tuesday, not just on the tour.

Our building has a garage in the cellar. Can the garage door's call box run on the same intercom as the lobby?

Often, yes. Many entry systems support more than one door station, so a call box at the garage entrance can ring the same apartments as the lobby panel and open the garage door instead of the lobby door. The garage station has to be mounted where a driver can reach it from the car window, at a height that suits ordinary cars, and protected from bumpers with a post or a recessed mount. The garage door's own operator needs a contact the intercom can trigger, which the garage door installer may need to provide. Cable from the garage to the system usually passes through block or concrete, so it travels in conduit with sealed penetrations. For a building like 6414 Eighth Avenue, planned with 68 enclosed spaces, those details belong on the design drawings early, not after the garage door is hung.
