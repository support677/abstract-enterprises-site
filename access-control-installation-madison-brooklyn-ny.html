The Neighborhood

Why Madison Properties Need Access Control

Madison is a neighborhood of doors that nobody stands behind. Wikipedia describes it as "a purely residential subsection of Sheepshead Bay, located just south of Midwood," named for James Madison High School and bounded by Kings Highway to the north, Avenue U to the south, Ocean Avenue to the west and Nostrand Avenue to the east, all in ZIP code 11229 and Brooklyn Community District 15. City Neighborhoods, a neighborhood guide, draws the eastern edge a little further out, at Gerritsen Avenue; we mention both because callers use both. Inside those lines there are no office towers and very few lobbies with a doorman. The listings we checked for the big apartment buildings on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue either give no doorman at all or list the doorman as "None." What there is, block after block, is a front door that dozens or hundreds of people open with keys of their own, and that is the reason building owners and boards here call us.

The houses explain part of it. City Neighborhoods describes Madison’s side streets as a mix of two-story brick homes, detached brick and stucco houses with driveways, lawns and gardens, two-family brick houses, limestone row houses and modest single-family homes, most of them built between 1910 and 1930 in Neo-Renaissance, Tudor Revival and Colonial styles. Untapped Cities places the start of development earlier, calling Madison one of Brooklyn’s last neighborhoods to be built up, beginning in 1877; both dates appear in the sources, so we give both. Either way, a large share of these houses have been in the same families for a long time. The city’s planners counted 43.1 percent of Community District 15’s households as owner-occupied in 2010, and 26,319 residents, 16.5 percent of the district, were 65 or older. A house that has had a tenant upstairs, an aide on weekdays and grown children visiting on weekends has handed out a lot of keys.

The apartment buildings explain the rest. City Neighborhoods notes "larger apartment buildings on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue," and the building records bear that out: six-story elevator buildings put up, most of them, between the late 1920s and the 1950s. CityRealty lists the co-op at 2425 Kings Highway as built in 1932 with six floors and 137 apartments, and gives its doorman as "None"; it lists 2301 Kings Highway, between East 23rd and East 24th Streets, as a 1936 pre-war co-op of 96 apartments with an elevator; 2705 Kings Highway, near East 28th Street, as a 1950 co-op of 108 apartments; and 2282 Ocean Avenue, between Avenue R and Avenue S, as a 1938 co-op of 36 apartments with an elevator. Openigloo, which files these blocks under Madison, lists 2232 Ocean Avenue as a 72-unit, six-floor building from 1927 with rent-stabilized apartments, and 2306 Ocean Avenue as a 72-unit building from 1935. Wikipedia’s article on Kings Highway sums up the stretch along Madison’s northern edge in one line: "East of Ocean Avenue, the street is largely residential."

Those buildings share a pattern that matters more for access control than any crime figure. They were built in an era when a building had staff, and many of them were laid out with more than one way in: a main lobby on the avenue, a door on the side street, a stair down to the cellar and the laundry, a service passage for garbage, and, in some of the post-war buildings, a garage. CityRealty records several of these co-ops under more than one street number. It gives 2301 Kings Highway alternate addresses at 1552 to 1558 East 24th Street and 1561 and 1563 East 23rd Street, and 2425 Kings Highway an alternate address at 1511 East 24th Street. Over the decades the staff thinned out and the keys multiplied. A board that wants to know who can get into its building tonight has to start by finding every door, not just the one with the name on the awning.

Residents notice when that work has not been done. On Openigloo, a former tenant of 2306 Ocean Avenue wrote, more than three years ago, that "packages get lost all the time" and that there were "No working cameras in the building just a decoration." Those are one person’s comments about one building, and the same page shows only three open, non-hazardous HPD violations there, so we do not treat them as a verdict. They do describe what an unmanaged entrance feels like from the inside, and what most of the Madison boards and owners who call us are trying to fix.

Not every call is a new system. If the fob reader at an Ocean Avenue lobby has stopped answering, a Kings Highway co-op’s side-street door releases for a fob but never locks again, the keypad on a house’s driveway door has stopped taking codes, or the entry panel lost its connection after a building changed internet providers, ring (347) 934-8335 and tell us what the door is doing, or send a short video of it opening and closing. In the prewar buildings the cause is very often mechanical: a heavy lobby door whose closer has worn out and no longer pulls it into the latch, a hinge that has dropped so the latch misses the strike, or a frame that has been painted so many times the strike lip no longer clears. In newer installations it is more often a power supply sharing a circuit with the laundry, a backup battery at the end of its life, or an administrator login that belonged to a board member who has since sold and moved. We work on systems other companies installed as well as our own, and we say plainly when a part is not worth repairing.

What the materials mean for the install

The prewar co-ops are solid buildings, and that cuts both ways. Their walls are brick and terra cotta under plaster, their lobbies are often finished in marble, stone or terrazzo, and their lobby doors are heavy, sometimes original, sometimes replaced in the 1960s or 1970s with a metal-and-glass door in the old opening. There is rarely a hollow wall to drop a cable through. The usual route is up from the cellar: through the floor under the door frame, inside the frame itself where it is hollow metal, or through a ceiling space in the vestibule, rather than across a finished lobby wall. We look for the old intercom riser and the conduit that fed the original lobby lights, because those paths often still have room in them. Where nothing hidden exists, a slim metal raceway painted to match the frame is better than a channel cut into marble, and we say which we intend before work starts.

The houses are easier to wire and easier to get wrong. A detached brick or stucco house usually has a wood-framed interior wall beside the side door and an open basement below, so the cable can drop straight down. The risk is the door. A side door that has been used thousands of times a year by a whole family has a worn latch, a loose strike plate and often a frame that has shifted, and an electric strike only holds if the latch meets it squarely every time. We probe the jamb, check the latch, and fix the door before any electronics go on. On two-family houses with a basement apartment, the door under the porch or at the side often has a step down and a drain in front of it, so the reader goes on the frame at a height that keeps it above splash and snow. In the mixed buildings on Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue, the shop doors are aluminum storefront doors with narrow stiles, and their locks usually go into the frame header or the exit device rather than the door edge.

One Co-op, Several Street Numbers: Counting the Doors Before Counting the Fobs

The first thing we ask a Madison board is a question most boards have never been asked: how many doors lead into your building? The answer is almost never one. A six-story co-op on Kings Highway or Ocean Avenue usually has a lobby on the avenue and an inner vestibule door behind it, and then a list of other openings that grew up over ninety years: a side-street door that residents of the back wing use, a door from the courtyard or the rear yard, a stair down to the cellar from outside, a service passage where the garbage goes out, a laundry room with its own exterior door, a fire escape that ends at a gate, and, in some post-war buildings, a garage with a door into the cellar. When CityRealty lists 2301 Kings Highway under four East 24th Street numbers and two East 23rd Street numbers as well as its avenue address, that is a clue that the building was planned with more than one way in. Whether those doors still open today is something only a walk around the building will tell.

We do that walk on the first visit, before anyone talks about readers. For each opening we write down what it is, who uses it, how it is locked now, who holds its keys, and whether it is an exit that must always open from inside. Some doors are obvious candidates for a reader: the lobby, of course, and the side-street door if residents of one wing use it every day. Some are better turned into exit-only doors, with no outside handle and a strike that never releases, because nobody needs to enter there and every extra entrance is one more door to watch. Some, like a cellar areaway used only by the oil delivery or the boiler contractor, are better on a keypad code that works only for those vendors, on the days they come. And a few, like a fire escape gate, are not ours to lock at all.

The door count changes the budget more than anything else. A 36-apartment co-op with a lobby and one rear door may need only one or two controlled doors and fit inside the single-door or video-panel lines of our Brooklyn list. A 137-apartment building with a lobby, two side-street doors, a cellar stair, a laundry door and a package room is a multi-door system in the $4,000 to $15,000 range, or a full building system above that if the board wants every common door covered. Boards that know the count before they ask for a price get prices they can compare, and they do not discover in the middle of installation that the side-street door the porters prop open every morning was never on the plan.

What we put on the door list for a Madison co-op

  • Every opening, named the way the residents name it, so "the 24th Street door" in the minutes is the same door in the system.
  • Who uses it and when: all shareholders, one wing, porters, the super, vendors, deliveries, the garage list.
  • What it must do in an emergency: which doors are exits, which must open from inside without a key, and which, like a roof door, must close by itself without locking.
  • How it closes today: whether the closer, hinges and latch actually pull it shut and lock it without a push.
  • What happens to its old keys: which cylinders stay as a backup, who keeps those keys, and which doors are rekeyed.

The list becomes part of the written plan and stays with the board after installation. When a future board asks why the courtyard door has no outside reader, the answer is on paper.

No Doorman, One Elevator and a Residential Avenue Outside

A Madison lobby has to do on its own what a doorman would do in Manhattan. It has to let residents in quickly, keep strangers out without being rude to them, send visitors to the right apartment, take in deliveries without letting couriers wander, and record enough to answer a board’s questions after something goes wrong, without recording more than the city allows. In the prewar buildings it has to do all that through a door that was hung when the building was new and has been pulled open millions of times since.

Kings Highway shapes that job in a particular way. East of Ocean Avenue the road is "largely residential," as Wikipedia puts it, but it is still a wide, busy avenue with buses on it, and the lobbies of the big co-ops open straight onto its sidewalk. A building there sees a constant flow of people passing its door and waiting near it, most of whom have no interest in it. The door’s job is simply to close and lock behind every resident, every time, so that nobody can follow a shareholder in by accident or design. That is a mechanical problem before it is an electronic one. A closer that brings the door shut in three seconds, a latch that catches every time, and a strike that holds are worth more than any reader, and they are the first things we check.

Inside, the single elevator sets the pace. In a six-story building with one car, a visitor buzzed in from the lobby may wait several minutes to go up, and a resident on the sixth floor who comes down to let an aide in loses ten minutes doing it. A video station that rings the resident’s own phone or an indoor screen, with a door release they can press from the apartment, saves that trip. We leave the elevator itself alone in almost every Madison job: the lobby door, not the elevator cab, is where control starts, and the elevator keeps running the way its own contractor set it up.

Deliveries without a desk

The packages complaint on Openigloo is a common one in buildings with no staff, and access control can help with part of it. A courier who rings the panel can be released into the vestibule only, or into a package room or closet off the lobby, with a code that works for that one door during delivery hours. The rest of the building stays locked to him. What access control cannot do is stop a resident from holding the door for someone who looks like they belong, or stop someone who already has a key from taking a parcel that is not theirs. For those, a camera over the package area and a log of who opened its door are the tools, and we usually recommend both together.

Porters, supers and the management office

Many Madison co-ops are run by a managing agent with an office elsewhere and a super or porter who covers the building, and sometimes several buildings, on a schedule. Each of them needs a different kind of access. The super needs a credential that opens every common door, including the boiler room and the roof, and nothing inside any apartment. A porter on the morning shift may need the lobby, the cellar and the garbage passage, but only during the shift. The managing agent needs to add and remove shareholders from a computer in the office, without being able to open a door from there. We set those up as separate roles, so that when a porter leaves, one credential is switched off and the building is exactly as secure as it was the day before.

Ocean Avenue’s Rental Buildings and the Practices at Street Level

Not every big building on Madison’s western edge is a co-op. Openigloo lists 2232 Ocean Avenue as a 72-unit rental from 1927 with rent-stabilized apartments, managed by a property management company, and shows 127 open HPD violations over the past ten years there, 1.76 per unit against a city average of 0.81. The categories it names include smoke detectors, lighting in the stairways and missing peepholes, not intercoms or locks, so we draw no conclusion about that building’s doors. The point is a general one. In an older rental with a long list of open violations, the money for a new entry system competes with repairs the city has already ordered, and the owner should know whether the street door itself is on that list before ordering fobs.

Rent-stabilized buildings also have their own rules for any change to the way tenants get in. Brick Underground reported in 2018 that state housing regulators had ruled that fobs must be provided free to all lawful occupants, with a capped fee for lost replacements, and that an owner could not use a fob rollout to collect new identification from tenants. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, Local Law 63 of 2021, applies on top of that to any multiple dwelling that uses smart access. In practice that means the rollout plan for an Ocean Avenue rental is written before the hardware is ordered: who gets fobs, how many, what the tenants are told in writing, what is recorded and for how long, and how the mechanical key stays available.

At street level, the same avenue holds medical and professional offices. CommercialCafe lists a 3,000-square-foot first-floor medical office at 2269 Ocean Avenue, filed under Madison, as fully built out and ready to lease. Practices like that need a different kind of door from the lobby beside them. The front desk wants to see and release patients from behind the counter; doctors and staff who arrive before the desk opens want to let themselves in; cleaners and the after-hours answering service need limited access; and a supply room, a medication cabinet or a file room behind the treatment area may need its own shorter list. Where the suite has a door into the building’s lobby or hallway, that door belongs to the building as much as to the practice, and the landlord and the practice should agree in writing who controls it before anything is installed.

When a suite changes hands

Ground-floor suites on Ocean Avenue and Kings Highway turn over, and the departing practice often leaves its door hardware behind. A new tenant moving in should ask three questions before the lease is signed: whether the reader and controller belong to the landlord, to the old practice or to an installer’s service contract; whether anyone from the previous practice still holds a working credential; and whether the system’s administrator login can be handed over or must be reset. We survey left-behind systems as takeovers, list what can be reused, wipe the old user list, and give the new practice its own login. Often the strike, the cable and the controller are fine, and only the credentials and the reader need to change.

Houses on the Numbered Streets: The Side Door Off the Driveway

On the side streets between Ocean and Nostrand, the door that matters is rarely the front door. City Neighborhoods describes detached brick and stucco houses with driveways, lawns and gardens, and in houses like that the family parks in the drive and goes in through the side door, which opens into the kitchen or a back hall a few steps from the car. The front door, with its porch and its bell, is for visitors and the mail. When a Madison family asks for "a keypad on the house," the side door is almost always where it should go first.

That door has its own needs. It is used many times a day, often with full hands, so a code on a lit keypad or a fob tapped from a key ring is quicker than a key. It is usually the door children come home through, so each child can have their own code and the parents can see from the log that they got in. It may also be the door the aide, the cleaner or a relative uses, each with a code that works only on their days. And because it faces the driveway rather than the street, it is the door a stranger can stand at without being seen from the sidewalk, which is why we fit a door position contact that reports it when it has been left unlatched, and why many owners add a camera that covers it.

Where there is a gate across the driveway, it can join the same controller. A pedestrian gate with an electric lock and a keypad is a modest addition; a motorized vehicle gate needs its operator’s own safety devices, photo eyes and edges, in charge of closing, and we connect a reader to it only as an open command. Garages at the back of the lot, the detached kind many of these houses have, are usually left on their own openers unless the owner wants the garage’s side door on a code as well.

Two-family houses and the basement apartment

In the two-family brick houses, the front door is often shared and the basement apartment, where there is one, has its own door under the porch or at the side. The owner holds the administrator account; the tenant household gets its own credentials for the shared door and, if it wants, can add and remove its own guests inside limits the owner sets. For Residents Who Will Not Tap a Phone: Shabbat, Older Shareholders and the Mechanical Key

Two facts about Madison shape how we set up credentials here. The first is age: the city’s planners counted 26,319 residents of Community District 15 aged 65 or older in 2010, and 8,459 householders of that age living alone. The second is observance: Untapped Cities notes several synagogues and Jewish community centers in the neighborhood, and amNY’s 2019 report on keyless entry recorded tenants’ concerns, in other buildings, about Sabbath observance for residents who do not use electricity on Saturdays. Neither fact is a reason to avoid access control. Both are reasons to make sure the system never depends on a phone.

For older residents, the simplest credential is often a fob on the key ring they already carry, tapped against the reader the same way they used to turn a key. A keypad with large, lit buttons and a code they chose themselves works for others. A shareholder who never wants to touch either can keep a mechanical key, because every system we install keeps a keyed cylinder at each controlled door, and amNY reported that the Department of Buildings requires a key-based way in. On the answering side, the door station can ring an indoor screen or a landline rather than a smartphone, and the family member who manages the account can do it from their own phone across town.

For residents who keep Shabbat, the question is how they get into the building from Friday evening to Saturday night without operating anything electric. We do not decide what is permitted; that belongs to the resident and their rabbi. What we can do is give the building options to choose from: a mechanical key path that always works, a door set to a scheduled mode by the system itself rather than by a person, or another arrangement the community’s own authorities approve. Whatever the board chooses, the rest of the building’s security stays as it was, and the choice is written into the plan so the porter and the next board know how the door is meant to behave.

The Rules Behind a Madison Door

An entry system in a New York City building touches several sets of rules at once, and which ones apply depends mostly on how many households live behind the door and whether the door is an exit. None of them make access control hard to install in Madison. All of them shape how it is set up.

Self-closing, self-locking street doors

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires automatic self-closing and self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and an intercom with voice and a remote door release in buildings of eight or more apartments. Buildings older than that, which includes most of the prewar co-ops and rentals on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue, must install them when a majority of tenants ask in writing. Roof doors must be self-closing but must not be self-locking. In practice, any lobby we work on in Madison is set up to meet the newer standard whatever its age, because a door that does not lock itself is the problem the board is trying to solve.

Three or more households: the Tenant Data Privacy Law

HPD’s summary of Local Law 63 of 2021 says owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access, which covers fobs, key cards, apps and biometrics, must get tenants’ consent, give them a written privacy and data-retention policy, collect only the minimum data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and may not require smart access for anyone to enter their own apartment. Every co-op and rental building in Madison is inside that law; a two-family house is not, and a three-family house is. We set retention to the shortest period the board or owner is comfortable with and provide policy text they can hand out.

Rent-stabilized apartments

In buildings with stabilized apartments, state housing regulators have ruled, as Brick Underground reported in 2018, that fobs must be free to all lawful occupants, lost-fob fees capped, and no new identification collected as a condition of getting one. Changes to how stabilized tenants enter their building can also come before the state agency. We plan those rollouts around the rules before ordering hardware.

Exits and electric locks

The NYC Building Code allows electrically locked egress doors only on conditions: a sensor on the inside, unlocking when power fails and when a fire alarm or sprinkler system activates, and a manual PUSH TO EXIT button near the door that unlocks it for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock needs release hardware in the door that works with one hand. A lobby door whose inside lever or panic bar always opens it mechanically, fitted with a fail-secure electric strike, needs none of that, which is why it is the usual choice on Madison lobbies and side doors. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294, and we keep the listings in the job file.

Who may do the work

The city’s electrical code treats signaling, alarm, communication and data wiring under 50 volts as low-voltage work outside its permit requirements; the 120-volt outlet a power supply plugs into is ordinary electrical work for a licensed electrician. New York State licenses the installation of security systems through the Department of State. AESS does the low-voltage side under New York State license #12000287431, and where an outlet is needed we mark it on the plan so your electrician can add it before installation day.

Who opens up, and what the log is for

The difference between a shop on Avenue U and a practice on Ocean Avenue is who arrives first. In a family store the owner or a relative usually opens and closes, and the job is mostly about the back door and the cellar: who has a key, whether it gets locked at night, and whether a delivery driver can drop goods inside without walking onto the sales floor. In a practice, the first person in might be any of several doctors, nurses or front-desk staff, and the log answers the question every office manager eventually asks: who was in the suite, and when. A personal code or fob per person, valid during that person’s hours and nowhere else, replaces the spare key in the desk drawer.

Inner doors follow the same logic. A supply room, a medication cabinet room, a record room or a cash office can each have a reader with a shorter list. In our experience, a back room that opens only for the two or three people who need it, with a record of every opening, does more for shrinkage and disputes than a camera nobody reviews.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Madison: Questions Answered

Prewar co-ops on Kings Highway and Ocean Avenue

Our Kings Highway co-op is listed under several street numbers on the avenue and two side streets. Before anyone prices readers, how do we find out how many doors actually lead into the building?

Walk the outside of the building with the super and a copy of the key ring, and write down every opening you pass: the lobby, any door on each side street, gates into a courtyard or rear yard, stairs or areaways down to the cellar, the laundry’s outside door, a garbage passage, a garage, and where each fire escape ends. Then walk the inside and match each opening to a key on the ring. Doors without a key on the ring are the ones to worry about, because someone else has one or the door is never locked.

We do the same walk on our first visit and turn it into a door list: what each door is, who uses it, what it must do in an emergency and how it closes today. Multiple street numbers do not always mean multiple working entrances; some side doors were sealed decades ago. The list tells the board which doors need readers, which should become exit-only and which can stay as they are, and that is what the price is based on.

Our 1930s building has a single elevator and no doorman. If the lobby goes on fobs, does the elevator company need to be involved at all?

Usually not. In a six-story building the control point is the lobby door: once someone is through it, the elevator carries them the way it always has. A reader at the street door and another at the inner vestibule door, a station that calls each apartment, and a release residents can press from upstairs cover what most Madison co-ops want, and none of it touches the elevator machine, its controller or its cab.

The elevator contractor needs to be part of the conversation only if the board wants the elevator itself to respond to credentials, for example limiting a visitor to one floor, or if a reader would go inside the cab. That is unusual in a building of this size and adds cost and coordination. We would rather spend that money on the side-street and cellar doors, where an uncontrolled entrance undoes everything the lobby reader achieves.

On garbage nights our porter wheels the cans out through a side-street passage and props the gate open while he works. Can that gate stay unlocked for him on those evenings only, and relock on its own?

Yes. The gate gets an electric lock and a door position contact, and the porter gets a credential that does two things: it opens the gate, and, on the evenings and hours the board sets, it can hold the gate unlocked for a fixed period, say forty-five minutes, after which the gate relocks by itself whether or not he remembers. A held-open alert goes to the super if the gate is still standing open after that window, so a wedge left in the gate does not go unnoticed until morning.

The same approach suits the cellar door the porter uses to bring the cans up, and the recycling area if it has a door. Every hold-open is logged, so the board can see that the gate was open on Tuesday from 8:10 to 8:55 p.m. and closed afterward, without anyone checking by hand. If the gate is also an exit from the courtyard, it keeps its inside handle or push bar so people can always get out.

Our co-op on Ocean Avenue has only 36 apartments. Is a full building system overkill, or can the lobby and one back door be done by themselves?

They can be done by themselves, and for many smaller buildings that is the right scope. A video station with a fob reader at the lobby, which sits on the $1,500 to $3,000 video-panel line of our Brooklyn list, and a keypad or reader on the rear or cellar door, on the $1,500 to $2,000 keypad line or the single-door line, cover the doors that matter in a building of that size. The controller we fit has room for more doors, so a laundry room or a package closet can be added later without starting again.

A full building system, $15,000 to $40,000 on the list, makes sense when a building has many common doors, a garage, separate wings or a board that wants every door reporting to one place. A 36-apartment prewar co-op rarely needs all of that on day one. The door list from the first visit tells you which side of that line you are on.

Rental buildings on Ocean Avenue

Tenants in our Ocean Avenue rental say packages go missing and the lobby cameras are only for show. If the owner can fix one thing first, should it be the door or the cameras?

The door, in most buildings, as long as it closes and locks by itself. Cameras record what happens after someone is already inside; a street door that latches every time and opens only for residents, with couriers released to the vestibule or a package area rather than the hallways, cuts down the number of people who are inside without a reason. If the lobby door is often found unlatched or propped, a reader alone will not solve that, and the first spending should go on the closer, the hinges and the latch.

Once the door is sound, cameras earn their keep, especially one over the package area and one at the lobby door that the system can link to each fob event. In an older rental, also check whether the entrance door is already the subject of an open violation, and remember that in a stabilized building tenants’ fobs must be free and the rollout should follow the state’s rules before any hardware is ordered.

Practices and offices at street level

Our practice is moving into a ground-floor suite on Ocean Avenue that another practice just left. What should we ask the landlord about the door hardware and the old practice’s credentials?

Ask who owns the reader, the controller and the lock, whether they are on any service or cloud contract, and who holds the administrator login. Ask for a list of every credential that was enrolled, or, more realistically, a commitment that the old user list will be wiped before your first day. Ask whether the door between the suite and the building’s lobby or hallway, if there is one, is controlled by the landlord’s system or the suite’s, because that door decides whether your patients can wander into the building.

We can survey what was left behind before you sign. Often the strike, the cable and the controller are sound and only the credentials, the reader and the login need to change, which is far cheaper than a new installation. If the system is tied to a cloud account that the old practice still pays for, it may need replacing; we say so in writing so it can be part of the lease negotiation.

Our office keeps paper patient charts in one back room. Should that room have its own reader, and who should be on its list?

A record room is one of the best uses of an inner reader. Put on the list only the people whose work requires the charts: usually the physicians, the office manager and the staff who file. Cleaners, temporary staff and visiting vendors stay off it, and if a cleaner must enter, a code limited to the cleaning hours does the job. The reader keeps a record of each opening, so if a chart goes missing the question of who was in the room has an answer.

The room’s door should be on a fail-secure lock, so it stays locked if the power fails, with an inside lever that always lets people out. Keep the room’s mechanical key with one person. The entry records belong to the practice, not to the building, and how long they are kept is the practice’s decision; we set the retention period it chooses.

Houses on the numbered streets

We rent out the apartment in our basement, which has its own door under the front porch. Does putting a keypad on that door change which city rules apply to our house?

The keypad itself does not change the house’s legal status; the number of households does. A one-family house with a lawful basement apartment is a two-family house, and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which HPD describes as applying to multiple dwellings, does not reach it. A house with a third household is a multiple dwelling for that purpose, and then consent, a written privacy policy and data limits apply. Either way, the principles are good practice: tell your tenant what the keypad records, keep only what you need, and never make an app the only way in.

What does matter on that door is its role as the apartment’s way out. A door under a porch is often the only exit from a basement unit, so its lock must always open from inside without a key or code. We use a fail-secure strike or a keypad lock with a mechanical inside lever, and the tenant controls their own guests’ codes inside limits you set.

Our detached house has a driveway gate and a side door, and we always use both, one after the other. Can a single code open the gate and then the door?

Yes, with a little care in how it is set up. Both openings go on one controller. The simplest arrangement is that a family member’s code or fob opens the gate, and the same credential opens the side door when it is presented there a few seconds later. A second option is that a code at the gate releases the gate and, after a delay, unlocks the side door for a short time, but we prefer not to unlock a door nobody has reached yet, so the first arrangement is our usual choice.

Each person still has their own credential, so the log shows who came in, and the aide or cleaner can have a code that opens the gate and the side door on their days only. If the gate is motorized for cars, its operator’s own photo eyes and edges stay in charge of closing; our reader only sends an open command, the same as the button in the house.

Congregations and community rooms

Our rabbi lives next door to the shul and is usually the one who opens it. When he travels, how does whoever covers for him get in without a copy of his key?

Give the person covering a credential of their own, valid only for the dates the rabbi is away. A gabbai, a board member or the caretaker can get a code or fob that opens the doors they need for those dates, and it stops working on its own when the rabbi returns. Nobody copies a key, and nobody has to remember to take one back. If the shul’s doors follow a schedule for services, the schedule keeps running in his absence, and the person covering only needs to deal with the exceptions.

The rabbi, or whoever administers the system, can issue that temporary credential from a phone before leaving. For members who do not use electronics on Shabbat, the arrangements the congregation has chosen stay in place; we set the system up around the community’s own decisions and do not make them for it.

Shops on Avenue U, Nostrand Avenue and Kings Highway

The stair to the apartment over our Avenue U shop starts behind our counter, and our family lives upstairs. How should that inside door be handled once the shop is on a reader?

Treat it as the family’s front door, not as a shop door. The stair door gets its own lock and list, holding only the family and anyone they choose, and it is never on the shop’s opening schedule. Staff who need to reach a stock room upstairs get a credential for that room only. The shop’s street door, back door and cellar door are on the shop’s list and hours. One controller can hold both, split so that a staff member’s code never opens the stair.

Because the stair is also the family’s way out, its lock must open from the apartment side at all times without a key, and in a fire it must not trap anyone upstairs. A fail-secure strike with a mechanical lever on the stair side does that. If the apartment also has its own door to the sidewalk, that door goes on the family’s list as well, so the family can come and go without passing through the shop after closing.

What moves a Madison price up or down

  • The number of entrances. The door list from the first visit decides more of the price than any device.
  • The lobby finish. Marble, stone or terrazzo with no hidden route means raceway or a longer path from the cellar.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Door Nobody Put on the List

This describes how we approach a Madison building, not a particular customer. When a board asks for a lobby reader, we ask for twenty minutes to walk the outside of the building before we talk about anything else, and we bring the super’s key ring. In a prewar building that walk almost always turns up a door the request did not mention: a side-street entrance the back-wing residents use, a cellar areaway the oil company opens, a laundry door to the yard, a gate at the end of the garbage passage. Then we try each one. Does it close by itself? Does it latch? Is there a key for it on the ring?

That walk decides more of the plan than any product sheet. A reader on the lobby while the side door swings free only records who used the front. A door that does not latch gets fixed first. A door nobody needs to enter becomes exit-only. The best access system in a Madison co-op is the one that leaves no door off the list.

FAQ

Madison Access Control FAQ

What would a prewar co-op’s lobby door plus one rear service door come to on your Brooklyn list?
+

Usually two lines of the list added together. A video panel with fob access at the lobby, replacing the old bell column, is $1,500 to $3,000, and a keypad or reader on the rear or cellar door is $1,500 to $2,000 on the keypad line, or $1,500 to $2,500 if it gets a full reader and strike. Where both doors share one controller, the board pays for that controller once. The exact figure is written after the visit, once we have seen the frames and the cable route.

Our six-story co-op has no building-wide fire alarm, only smoke detectors in the apartments. What lets people out through a locked lobby door in a fire?
+

The door’s own inside hardware. We fit a fail-secure electric strike in the frame and keep a lever or panic bar on the inside that always opens the door mechanically, with or without power, so nobody depends on a signal to get out. Because nothing has to release electrically, the absence of a building fire alarm does not matter for that door. If a magnetic lock were used instead, the Building Code would require a sensor, a push-to-exit button and release on fire alarm, which is one reason we avoid them on these lobbies.

If our building changes internet providers or the router is replaced, do the doors stop working?
+

No. The controller in the cellar holds the list of credentials and makes every decision on site, so fobs, codes and the mechanical key keep working with no internet at all. What pauses is the remote part: changes from the managing agent’s office, phone keys that check in over the network, and alerts. Once the new router is in, the controller is reconnected, and we can do that remotely or on a short visit.

The only dry room in our cellar is at the far end of the building from the lobby. Can the controller go there?
+

Usually yes. Low-voltage cable for a reader, a strike and a door contact can run a long way along a cellar ceiling, and a dry, locked room is a better home for the controller and its battery than a damp spot close to the door. We check the length and the route on the visit, and in a very long building we may put a small door module near the lobby that reports back to the main controller.

Our lobby is due to be repainted and refloored next spring. Should the readers go in before or after?
+

Plan them together. The cable route is easiest while the floor is up or the ceiling is open, so we prefer to run cable and fit back boxes during the renovation and mount the readers and station once the painters are done. If the board wants fobs sooner, the hardware can go in now on a surface route and be tidied into the new finishes later, but that costs more overall.

Can the board see which apartments hold fobs without seeing when anyone came or went?
+

Yes. The system can give a board member a role that shows the roster, which apartment has how many fobs and codes, and lets them add or cancel credentials, without access to the entry history. The history can be limited to one or two people, or switched to the shortest retention the board chooses, which fits the city’s rule that buildings collect only what they need.

Can a held-open alert go to the super and the board president but not to every shareholder?
+

Yes. Alerts are set per door and per person. A side-street door standing open for more than a few minutes can text the super and the board president, a forced door can go to the managing agent as well, and shareholders hear nothing unless they ask to. The alert names the door the way the building names it, so nobody has to guess which entrance it means.

Will you work alongside the intercom or elevator company our building already uses?
+

Yes. If an existing intercom company services the apartments’ handsets, we coordinate where our station connects and who owns which part, and we put that split in writing. Elevator work is rarely needed for a lobby job in a building of this size, but if the board wants the elevator to respond to credentials, the elevator contractor does that part and we supply the reader signal.

Our lobby door sits beside a bus stop on Kings Highway and people lean on it all day. Will a reader there get damaged?
+

Not if the right one is chosen and placed well. We use metal-housed, vandal-resistant readers rated for outdoor use, mounted on the frame rather than the glass and at a height that is easy to reach but not in the line of someone leaning on the door. The strike and the cable are inside the frame, out of reach, so even a reader that is struck hard does not unlock the door.

Our prewar lobby has marble walls and a terrazzo floor. How does the cable reach the door without damaging them?
+

Usually from below. We look for a route up from the cellar through the floor under the door frame, inside a hollow metal frame, through the old intercom riser or along a vestibule ceiling, so the marble and terrazzo are not cut. Where no hidden path exists, a slim metal raceway painted to match the frame is the fallback, and we show you where it would run before any work starts.
