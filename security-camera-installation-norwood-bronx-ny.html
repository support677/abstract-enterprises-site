The Neighborhood

Why Norwood Properties Need Security Cameras That Work Through the Night

Norwood is a neighborhood of apartment buildings. NeighborhoodScout classifies 94.8% of its real estate as apartment complexes or high-rise apartments, and puts 60.2% of its residential buildings at 1939 or earlier. Walk along Bainbridge Avenue, Decatur Avenue, Hull Avenue or the blocks off East 204th Street and that is what you see: five- and six-story brick apartment houses from the 1910s through the 1930s, many in Art Deco, Tudor Revival or neo-Renaissance dress, with a handful of rowhouses and small homes between them. Webster Avenue adds a newer layer of taller elevator buildings and recent affordable developments. For a camera installer, that means old masonry, long stairs, cellars that hold the boiler and the internet line, and a very large number of households sharing a single street door.

It also means cars. Norwood sits beside Mosholu Parkway, the Bronx River Parkway and the Major Deegan, and plenty of residents drive to work at Montefiore's Moses campus, to jobs across the borough or out of the city. The cars stay on the street, in lots beside buildings and in garages tucked under them, and that is where a lot of the neighborhood's camera questions start. In May 2025 Norwood News collected a run of car crimes from the neighborhood: surveillance video of thieves taking all four tires off a car at Webster Avenue and East 205th Street, footage of two people trying to steal a license plate on Decatur Avenue between East 207th and East 209th streets, and videos of hooded people breaking into cars at night around Reservoir Oval. In January 2025 News 12 reported ten cars broken into overnight inside a gated underground private garage at 91 Van Cortlandt Avenue West, after someone broke a window to get in.

Those reports carry a lesson that shapes how we plan cameras here. The tire-theft video existed, but the thieves' hooded sweatshirts made them hard to identify. Many victims never called the police at all; Francisco Marte, a Norwood resident who heads the Bodega and Small Business Group, told Norwood News, "Almost every day, someone comes to tell me about that." A camera in Norwood earns its place when it is close enough, low enough and lit well enough to give a clear face or a clear plate at 4 a.m., and when the recording is still there a week later when someone finally asks for it. A camera on a roofline that shows the whole block at once rarely does either.

Then there are the public spaces that anchor the neighborhood. Williamsbridge Oval, the 1937 park built in the bowl of a former reservoir, is Norwood's backyard, and after violent incidents in and around it in 2024 and 2025, the city and State Sen. Gustavo Rivera's office put cameras into the park and its recreation center. Before that, a 52nd Precinct deputy inspector told a community meeting plainly, "We don't have any cameras in that park." The way those cameras were planned, counted and powered turns out to be a useful guide for private owners on the streets around the Oval, and we come back to it below.

What the record shows about Norwood and cameras

  • Cameras came to the Oval only recently. In September 2025 the NYPD and Sen. Rivera's office announced cameras for Williamsbridge Oval park, funded with $138,000 Rivera provided after a request from Bronx Community Board 7.
  • Twenty at the recreation center. In December 2025 Rivera confirmed 20 cameras installed at the Williamsbridge Oval Recreation Center: 14 inside and 6 outside.
  • Park cameras on a timed pole. The NYPD said the two Argus cameras near the recreation center were connected to a light pole whose lights ran on a timer, and that Parks was working on a way to keep them running around the clock.
  • Car crime caught on private video. Norwood News's May 2025 report relied on residents' surveillance video from Webster Avenue, Decatur Avenue and Reservoir Oval.
  • Store video released by police in 2026. The NYPD released video after a February 2026 armed robbery inside a laundromat at 3601 Bainbridge Avenue and after a June 2026 assault inside a store at 286 East 204th Street.
  • A precinct that leans on cameras. In 2018 the 52nd Precinct's commanding officer said cameras "come in great use" for the precinct, and that most incidents around the Oval at that time happened between 3 a.m. and 8 a.m.

Lessons From the Oval

What the Williamsbridge Oval Camera Rollout Teaches Norwood Building Owners

Public camera projects are rarely written up in detail, so when one happens in the middle of a neighborhood, it is worth reading closely. The Williamsbridge Oval project was covered step by step by Norwood News in the fall of 2025, from the community meeting where it was announced to the senator's confirmation that the recreation center cameras were up. We are not connected to that project and had no part in it. We point to it because the questions the city had to answer at the Oval are the same questions every Norwood owner faces on a smaller scale: how many cameras, inside or outside, how they are powered, and whether they actually run at the hour something happens.

"We don't have any cameras in that park"

At the 52nd Precinct Community Council meeting on September 25, 2025, Deputy Inspector Chase Maneri said, "We don't have any cameras in that park," and added, "The Williamsbridge Oval park is difficult to close." Residents had been asking for cameras for years; a 2018 report had already described neighbors from the Friends of Williamsbridge Oval pushing for them. A park that cannot be closed at night is a lot like a Norwood apartment building with a street door that never quite latches or a lot that anyone can drive into: the space stays open, so a record of who came and went is the main protection left. For a building, that record starts at the door people actually use, not the one on the floor plan.

Fourteen inside, six outside

When Sen. Rivera confirmed the recreation center installation in December 2025, his office gave the full count: 20 cameras, 14 inside and 6 outside. Inside, there were three in the lobby, three in the fitness room, two in the game room, two in a second-floor hallway and vestibule, two in the computer room and two in the multi-purpose room. Outside, there were two at the front entrance, two at the rear entrance and one at each staircase near the outdoor restrooms. Two things in that list stand out for a private owner. Entrances got pairs of cameras, front and back, rather than one. And most cameras went inside, where light is steady and faces come close to the lens, rather than outside, where distance and darkness work against the picture.

A Norwood walk-up follows the same logic at a smaller size. A pair at the street door (one inside the vestibule at face height, one wider view of the entrance), a camera at the mailboxes, one or two on the stair, one at the cellar door, and a single outside view of the curb is a common shape. The inside cameras do most of the identifying. The outside camera explains what happened on the sidewalk and which way someone went.

The timer on the light pole

The detail from the Oval that matters most for private buildings came almost as an aside. Deputy Inspector Maneri said the two Argus cameras near the recreation center were connected to a light pole whose lights run on a timer, and the Parks Department was working on a way to run the cameras 24 hours a day. Rivera's district director said she was not sure whether the park cameras would run around the clock. In other words, a camera can be installed, visible and paid for, and still lose power at exactly the hours it is needed.

We see the private version of this problem in Norwood buildings regularly. An old analog camera over a garage ramp is plugged into the same outlet as the ramp light, and that outlet is switched by a photocell or a timer. A porch camera runs off an adapter in a hallway outlet that a super shuts off to save power. A doorbell camera's transformer shares a circuit with the vestibule light. Every one of these works fine during the day and goes dark on a schedule. On every job we trace where each camera gets its power. Our standard is that cameras run on one cable each from a switch beside the recorder, so power comes from the head end, which sits on a battery unit, and nothing in the hallway or the garage can switch a camera off.

Who pays, who runs it, who watches

The Oval cameras were paid for with state capital money and installed by city agencies; the NYPD and Parks decide how they are used. Private systems have the same three questions, and they are worth answering in writing before installation. Who pays: the owner, the co-op, the nonprofit landlord, the store. Who runs it: usually a super or a managing agent, sometimes the owner directly. Who watches: a short list of named people, each with a personal login and only the cameras they need. When those answers are settled first, the system tends to be maintained. When they are not, cameras are often still on the wall years later with nobody sure whether they record.

Park cameras do not cover your door

The Oval's exterior cameras were described as mostly facing the recreation center, not the park and certainly not the streets around it. Owners on Reservoir Oval East and West, Bainbridge Avenue, Wayne Avenue and the blocks near the Oval should plan as though no public camera watches their entrance or their curb. If the park's lighting spills across the street and helps your night picture, that is a bonus we check for on the night visit, not something to build a plan around.

Cars, Curbs and Garages

Cameras for Norwood's Parked Cars, Lots and Underground Garages

If one kind of call sets Norwood apart from other Bronx neighborhoods we work in, it is the car call. A resident on Webster Avenue wants to know why the building's camera shows a dark shape at the curb instead of a face. A co-op on Van Cortlandt Avenue West wants its garage covered after a break-in. A store owner whose tenant lost all four tires wants a camera that would have caught it. These jobs need different cameras and different placement from a lobby, and they are where the most money gets wasted on the wrong equipment.

Why the Webster Avenue video wasn't enough

Norwood News reported that surveillance video showed the thieves who took a car's tires at Webster Avenue and East 205th Street, but that their hooded sweatshirts made them hard to identify. That is the most common outcome for curb video in the city, and it is mostly a problem of geometry. A camera high on a building, looking down at the street, sees the top of a hood. A camera at the height of a first-floor window, angled along the sidewalk toward where people approach the car, has a chance at the face under the hood. Distance matters too: a person 15 feet from the lens fills far more of the frame than one 60 feet away, whatever the camera's resolution.

So for curb coverage we usually recommend two cameras with different jobs. One narrow view, aimed at the stretch of curb where the building's residents actually park, set to show a car and a person clearly. One wider view of the sidewalk approach, mounted lower, to catch faces as people walk toward and away from the cars. Neither one tries to show the whole block.

License plates on a side street

The Decatur Avenue footage of two people trying to steal a license plate shows a different need: reading a plate, not a face. Plate cameras are a specialty. They need a narrow view, a fast shutter and their own infrared or white light, and they are aimed at a spot where cars slow down or stop, such as a garage entrance or a lot gate. A general-purpose camera aimed at a moving car on a dark street will usually record the headlights and nothing else. If plates matter, we put a dedicated camera on the one point every car has to pass, and set it up only for that.

Underground garages and gated lots

The Van Cortlandt Avenue West garage was gated and underground, and the intruder still got in by breaking a window. That pattern is common in buildings with garages under them: the gate and the ramp get attention, and the side openings, the pedestrian door from the building and the stair from the street do not. When we plan a garage, we start by walking every opening, including windows and vents large enough for a person, then cover each way in with a camera at the inside of the opening, so the person coming through faces the lens. Rows of parked cars get a lower priority than the ways in, because a camera that shows who entered is more useful than one that shows a window breaking from forty feet away.

Garages bring practical problems too. They are concrete, often damp, and usually have no internet line. Cable reaches them from the building's cellar through a sleeved hole in the foundation wall or through an existing conduit, never loose along the ramp. Lighting in a garage is often dim and uneven, so we pick cameras that hold color in low light and check every view after dark with the garage's own lights on, because headlights sweeping across a lens can wash out a picture that looked fine during the day.

Open lots beside buildings

Some Norwood buildings, especially along Webster Avenue, have small surface lots behind or beside them. These are hard to cover with one camera because a car can be approached from any side. The practical layout is a camera at the lot's entrance, aimed where cars turn in, and one or two mounted on the building wall looking along the rows at a shallow angle, with light on the lot. A lot with no light is the single most common reason a lot camera disappoints. Sometimes the best first purchase is a properly aimed fixture, not another camera.

When nobody files a report

Many car crimes here go unreported. The Webster Avenue tire victim did not call the police, according to Norwood News, because of an earlier experience. A building's recording still matters in that situation. A clip saved the morning after can support an insurance claim, can show a pattern that convinces a board or a landlord to add lighting, and can be handed to police later if the same people come back. We set recorders to keep video long enough that a resident who discovers a broken window three days later still has something to save, and we show the super how to export a clip in a few minutes.

The same problem one neighborhood south

Car-part theft is not unique to Norwood. In June 2026 News 12 reported Bedford Park residents fed up with a spike in tire and wheel thefts, with cars left on milk crates, and resident Devin Whitehead said, "You come out and you're missing wheels." We mention it because owners whose buildings sit on the boundary streets between the two neighborhoods sometimes assume the problem belongs to the other side of the line. It doesn't; the curb is the curb.

Who Decides

Renters, Co-op Boards and Nonprofit Owners: Who Approves a Norwood Camera

In a neighborhood that is nearly all apartment buildings, the person who wants a camera is often not the person who controls the wall. Norwood adds a few owner types that are less common elsewhere in the Bronx, including prewar co-ops that converted decades ago and housing owned by a nonprofit that a hospital founded. Sorting out who decides is the first step on every job, and it costs nothing.

Renters and the camera on the apartment door

A tenant who buys a video doorbell and sticks it on the outside of an apartment door is putting equipment in a common area. In May 2024 News 12 reported that management at a Mott Haven building gave tenants a deadline to remove cameras from their apartment doors. One tenant, Maria Bellechea, took her doorbell camera down for "fear of a violation or a fine." A representative of the Bronx Legal Services Tenant Rights Association advised: "Review the lease, see if they're anything that explicitly says you can't have a camera on your door." That is good advice in Norwood too. The safest place for a renter's camera is inside the apartment, aimed at the inside of the front door. For anything on the hallway side, ask the landlord in writing and keep the answer.

Co-op shareholders and the board

Norwood has prewar co-ops, including The Lenru at 3388–3400 Wayne Avenue facing Williamsbridge Oval, a 1928 Jacobethan Revival building that completed its co-op conversion in 1991. In a co-op, the hallways, lobby, courtyard and facade are common areas the board controls. Habitat Magazine, writing about video doorbells in New York co-ops and condos, notes there is no city law banning them in hallways but that building rules still govern them, and quotes lawyer Debra Guzov: "If you're living in a co-op, you can't just unilaterally do what you'd like to do in the common hallways." The same article quotes Heidi Boghosian, a lawyer who sits on her own co-op's board: "We as New Yorkers, we don't want our neighbors recording our comings and goings 24-7." Boards that want cameras tend to do best when they write a short policy first: where cameras go, who may watch, how long video is kept and how a shareholder asks for a clip.

Hospital-linked and nonprofit housing

Montefiore has anchored Norwood since 1913, and in 1981 the hospital founded the Mosholu Preservation Corporation, which owns and maintains affordable housing in the neighborhood around the Moses campus. HUD's case study notes the corporation started by renovating five apartment buildings the health system had obtained and owned 112 housing units in the Bronx as of summer 2019. Residents of those buildings, and of the supportive and senior housing that has gone up along Webster Avenue, have a landlord with its own procedures. Camera decisions there go through the owner's management, not through individual tenants or outside contractors, and requests from residents carry the most weight when they are specific: which door, which stair, which hours, and what happened.

Small owners of walk-ups and two-families

Plenty of Norwood buildings are still owned by one person or a family, often an owner who lives in the building or nearby. These are the most straightforward jobs to approve, because the person paying is the person deciding. They also have the most at stake when a camera is wrong, because a small owner usually handles the complaints personally. We spend time with small owners on two things: making sure no camera looks into any tenant's apartment, and giving tenants a short written notice of where the common-area cameras are and what they record.

Shops under apartments

A store on Bainbridge Avenue or East 204th Street usually leases its space from the owner of the apartment building above it. The store controls the inside within its lease; the building owner controls the facade, the sign band and anything above the storefront. Outside cameras, including one aimed at the gate after closing, need the landlord's written approval. Inside, we plan around the lease and write down which equipment the store owns, so there is no argument when the lease ends.

Writing the permission down

Whatever the owner type, the written approval should name each camera location, describe how it mounts, say whether drilling is allowed, confirm that no camera films into an apartment or a neighbor's window, say who removes it and patches the wall if it comes down, and say who has access to the video. We are glad to describe the mounts and views in plain language so the request is easy to approve. We don't mount anything on a common-area wall until that note exists.

Prewar walk-ups: long stairs and one way in

The classic Norwood building is a five- or six-story brick walk-up with forty to sixty apartments, a single street door into a small vestibule, a lobby with mailboxes, one main stair, a cellar with the boiler and the utilities, and a roof door at the top. Apartments.com lists 3225 Bainbridge Avenue as built in 1924 with 54 units on five stories; 3265 Bainbridge Avenue has 51 units on five floors. In buildings this size, the street door carries an enormous amount of traffic, and that is where the camera budget should go first. A vestibule camera at face height and a wider lobby view do most of the work. Stair cameras come next, placed at the landings where the stair turns so each camera sees a full flight. The cellar door, which usually opens to a rear yard or an areaway, gets its own camera because nobody sees it from the street.

Cable is the hard part. Many of these buildings have no clean chase from cellar to roof. When they do, often beside an old dumbwaiter shaft or an existing riser, cable goes up quickly and invisibly. When they don't, we run painted conduit in a stair corner and fire-stop every floor penetration, because a stair in a prewar walk-up is the building's way out in a fire and cannot become a path for smoke. In August 2026 Councilman Eric Dinowitz said of a Norwood building hit by an explosion and fire, "A lot of the buildings in the Bronx are older," and that the building was not fireproof. We treat every hole we make in an old Norwood building with that in mind.

Angled and rounded corner buildings

Forgotten NY points out that the Mosholu Parkway grid forces some Norwood streets to meet at irregular angles, which pushed architects toward triangle-shaped buildings with rounded corners. These buildings often have an entrance at the curve or on the short side, and two sidewalks meeting at a sharp angle out front. One camera at the corner can sometimes see both sidewalks, but usually sees neither well, because a wide lens pushes everything far away. We usually plan one camera per street front, mounted where each can look along its own sidewalk toward the entrance, and a third inside the entrance itself. Mounts go into solid brick, never into the curved trim or the cornice.

Art Deco entrances

Norwood's Art Deco and Art Moderne buildings near Mosholu Parkway have some of the best entrances in the borough: stone or terracotta surrounds, metal-framed doors, patterned brick. None of the ordinary apartment buildings in Norwood are in a Landmarks historic district according to our research, so cameras on them aren't reviewed by the Landmarks Preservation Commission, but that is no reason to drill into a decorative surround. We mount cameras inside the vestibule wherever possible, use the plain brick beside the entrance for any outside camera, choose small housings in a color that matches the wall, and seal every hole so water can't follow the cable into the masonry.

Prewar co-ops: the board's building

A co-op building's camera plan is a board decision, and boards in older buildings often want the system to be as quiet as possible. That suits good camera practice anyway. When Video Matters

From a Bainbridge Avenue Laundromat to an East 204th Street Store: What Makes a Clip Useful

Twice in the first half of 2026, the NYPD released video to the public after crimes inside Norwood businesses: the February laundromat robbery on Bainbridge Avenue and the June assault on East 204th Street. Norwood News's reports don't say where the footage came from or how it was obtained, so we don't make claims about either case. What both show is how often investigators in this neighborhood ask for video, and how much depends on the recording being usable when they do. These are the practical things that decide that.

The camera has to be where the face is

The clearest picture of a person in any building or store is from a camera close to the entrance, at about head height, looking at people as they come in. That is why the inside-entrance camera is the first one on almost every Norwood quote. Overview cameras on the ceiling explain what happened; entrance cameras identify who did it. A system can have twenty cameras and still not have one clear face if none of them is placed this way.

The 3 a.m. to 8 a.m. problem

When the 52nd Precinct's commander described incidents around the Oval in 2018, he said most happened between 3 a.m. and 8 a.m. Those hours are the hardest for cameras: streets are dark, lighting is uneven, and people are often bundled up. We set up outdoor cameras with a night profile that favors a faster shutter and steady exposure over brightness, so a moving person isn't a smear, and we check every outdoor view after dark before the job is finished. We also confirm that every camera stays powered through the night, which, as the Oval showed, is not something to assume.

Continuous recording at the doors

Motion-triggered recording saves storage, but it can miss the seconds before something starts and anything just outside the motion zone. For street doors, garage entrances, store entrances and laundromat floors, we generally record continuously with motion markers, so nothing is lost and you can still jump to the moments when something moved. Drives are sized so the recorder keeps enough days for someone to notice a problem and save the clip, including the resident who finds a broken car window days later.

Correct time and the original file

Every recorder we install syncs its clock automatically, because a clip with the wrong time is hard to use. The main recording stream stays at full quality; a lighter stream is used for phone viewing. When police or an insurer ask for video, they want the original file exported from the recorder, not a phone recording of a screen. Export just the requested camera and time window to a fresh drive, keep a copy, and note the officer's name and the case number. You don't have to hand over the recorder or your password.

Before anything goes online

After something happens on a block, there is pressure to post video to neighborhood groups. A clip can show neighbors, children, apartment numbers and people who had nothing to do with anything. Share with police first. If something must be shared publicly, make a separate copy with bystanders blurred and keep the original untouched.

Field Notes: How a Norwood Walk-Through Actually Runs

A Norwood visit usually starts at the curb, not the door. We stand where the building's residents park, look at the streetlights, the trees that block them in summer, and the angle from the building to the cars. Inside, we go floor by floor up the stair, noting where it turns and how bright each landing is, then down to the cellar, where the internet line comes in and where a recorder can sit off the floor away from the boiler.

The step people don't expect is the power check. We find every existing camera, light and outlet near the places we plan to cover and ask which ones are on a timer, a photocell or a switch. In buildings with a garage, we walk every opening, including windows and the pedestrian door, before looking at the cars. Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Norwood: Questions Answered

Owners, boards and residents

Our building is one of the Mosholu Preservation Corporation buildings near Montefiore. Can tenants ask for cameras, and who do we ask?

Yes, tenants can ask, but the decision belongs to the owner. Mosholu Preservation Corporation owns and maintains its buildings, so a camera request goes to the building's management in writing, not to an outside installer. Requests work best when they name the exact door, stair or area, the hours that worry residents and what has happened there. If management decides to add cameras, the work is arranged through them.

Our new Webster Avenue building came with cameras the developer's contractor installed. Who should hold the master login now that it is open?

The owner, or the owner's managing agent acting for the owner, should hold the master account, written down and stored safely, with the installer's access removed or reduced to a separate service login. Ask for the recorder's admin credentials, a camera list, the cable map and the warranty paperwork at turnover. If nobody can produce the admin password, a takeover visit can usually reset the recorder and bring the cameras back under the owner's control.

We live on a high floor of a Webster Avenue elevator building. Can residents get a view-only lobby camera on their phones?

If the owner agrees, yes. Most recorders let the owner create view-only accounts limited to one or two cameras, such as the entrance and the lobby, with no access to playback or other cameras. For residents who have trouble getting down to the door, seeing who is in the lobby before going down can help. The owner decides whether to offer it and who gets an account, and accounts are removed when residents move out.

Cars, curbs and the night hours

If thieves pull their hoods up, like the ones in the Webster Avenue tire theft, is a curb camera still worth having?

Yes, though it should be placed for faces rather than for the whole block. Even when a face is hidden, a good recording shows clothing, build, the exact time, the direction of travel and any vehicle used, which helps police connect incidents and helps insurers. A second camera mounted lower, near where people approach the cars, gives the best chance at a face under a hood. A camera high on the roofline mostly records the tops of hoods.

Most of the break-ins on our block are never reported. Is building video still worth keeping?

Yes. A saved clip can support an insurance claim, show a pattern that persuades an owner or board to add lighting or another camera, and be given to police later if the same people come back. Norwood News found many car-crime victims here don't call police. We size recorders so a resident who notices damage days later still has video to save, and we show the super how to export it.

The precinct has said most late-night incidents happen between 3 a.m. and 8 a.m. Can our outdoor cameras record at a higher quality during those hours?

Many recorders allow scheduled recording profiles, so outdoor cameras can use a higher bit rate or a different night setting during chosen hours and a lighter setting the rest of the day. That uses more storage overnight but keeps the most detail when it matters most. We set the schedule during the night visit, after checking how each camera handles the light on your block.

Power and the Oval

The Oval's park cameras were tied to a light pole on a timer. Could our building's outdoor cameras be wired to a circuit that switches off the same way?

They could be, and in older systems they often are. Cameras plugged into an outlet controlled by a photocell, a timer or a hallway switch lose power whenever that circuit turns off, usually at night or in daylight depending on the control. We trace each camera's power on every takeover and new install. Our standard is to power cameras over their own cable from a switch beside the recorder, on a battery unit, so no light control can shut them off.

The Oval's recreation center put 14 of its 20 cameras inside and 6 outside. Is that a good guide for a Norwood walk-up?

The idea behind it is a good guide: inside cameras, close to people and in steady light, identify faces far better than outside cameras looking across a dark street. Most Norwood walk-ups end up with more cameras inside the street door, at the mailboxes, on the stairs and at the cellar door than outside. One or two outside views explain what happened at the curb. The exact count depends on the building's doors, floors and parking.

Old buildings and odd fronts

The front of our prewar walk-up is covered by fire escapes. Can an outside camera still see the entrance?

Usually, if it goes below the first fire-escape landing. A camera mounted on the brick beside the entrance, under the lowest platform, sees the door and the sidewalk without the ironwork in the way. Mounting on the fire escape itself is a bad idea: it can't obstruct an exit route, the ironwork moves and rusts, and it puts the camera within reach of anyone on the stairs. Inside the vestibule is often the better place anyway.

Our building curves around a rounded corner where the parkway grid meets the side street. Will one corner camera see both sidewalks?

It can see both, but rarely well. A lens wide enough to cover two sidewalks at a sharp angle makes everyone small and far away. We usually place one camera on each street front, looking along its own sidewalk toward the entrance, plus a camera inside the entrance. Mounts go into plain brick on the flat sections, never into the curved trim, cornice or corner stone.

Stores

Our Bainbridge Avenue laundromat is open late. Which views matter most?

The entrance from the inside at face height comes first, set back from the glass and tuned for backlight. Then the change and card machines, the attendant's counter and a wide view down the rows of machines and folding tables. Record continuously while open, because the room is never still and motion-only recording misses context. Outside, a camera at the door covers the sidewalk after closing.

The NYPD released store video after the East 204th Street assault. If police ask our store for video, will our customers end up on the news?

Giving footage to police does not by itself make it public. Investigators decide whether to release images to identify a suspect, and they normally choose only the frames they need. Export just the camera and time window requested, keep a copy, and note the officer and case number. You keep control of your recorder and password. If you have concerns, ask the detective how the footage will be used.

The Law in Plain Words

New York Rules Behind a Norwood Camera Job

Walk-ups, co-ops, garages and stores in Norwood all run into the same handful of New York rules. This is a plain summary of how we work within them. It is not legal advice; for a dispute, talk to a lawyer.

No general camera mandate

Security sources covering New York building rules say there is no general city law requiring cameras in apartment buildings. City rules do require working entrance equipment, such as self-closing, self-locking doors and an intercom or buzzer system, in many multiple dwellings, which is a separate subject. Cameras are the owner's decision, made for the building's own reasons.

Private places stay off camera

Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people without consent where they reasonably expect privacy, including inside homes, bathrooms and changing areas. A hallway camera may watch a corridor; it may not look into an apartment when a door opens. A curb camera may watch the sidewalk; it may not look into a window across the street. Privacy masks black out those parts of the frame in both live view and recordings.

Sound is treated differently

Recording a conversation without the consent of at least one person in it can be eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05. Talking through a door camera to a visitor is a conversation you are part of; a hallway or laundromat camera quietly recording other people's conversations is not. We leave continuous audio off on common-area, store and outdoor cameras.

Neighbors' back yards

Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a civil claim against an adjoining neighbor who installs video surveillance of their back yard without consent. Yards behind Norwood's walk-ups and houses are small and touch several others, so yard cameras are aimed at your own yard and rear door and masked over the rest.

Who decides

In a rental building the landlord controls common areas, and tenants need written permission for anything there. In a co-op, the board controls common areas under the building's documents. In nonprofit and supportive housing, the owner's management decides. Store tenants control the inside of their store within the lease; the building owner controls the facade and the sidewalk side.

Who may install

Under 19 NYCRR 195.2, a New York State license is required to install CCTV or video surveillance systems used to detect or monitor intrusion, theft or movement. The Department of State exempts an owner who installs a system personally, or through their own employee, on their own property. We work under NYS license #12000287431 and carry insurance; ask any installer for their license number before work starts.

Landmarks

Norwood has two individual city landmarks, neither of them an apartment building: the Valentine-Varian House at 3266 Bainbridge Avenue, built in 1758 and designated in 1966, now the Museum of Bronx History, and the Williamsbridge Reservoir Keeper's House at 3400 Reservoir Oval, built in 1889–90 and designated in 2000. Our research found no Landmarks historic district in Norwood, so ordinary apartment buildings and houses here are not subject to Landmarks review for cameras. Exterior work on the two landmarks themselves is a matter for their owners and the Landmarks Preservation Commission.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Norwood

More Norwood owners, boards and shopkeepers now arrive with an answer from a search engine's AI summary or a chatbot, usually built from cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr and from installer blogs. Some of it is fine. These are the claims we hear most often that steer people in this neighborhood the wrong way.

"Higher resolution solves the identification problem."

AI answers often recommend 4K or the highest megapixel count you can afford, as if detail were simply a matter of pixels. The advice is easy to give because resolution is the one number every camera box prints in large type.

The Webster Avenue tire thieves were on video and still hard to identify because of their hoods. More pixels would not have changed the angle: a camera high on a building sees the top of a hood at any resolution. In the 3 a.m. to 8 a.m. hours the 52nd Precinct has pointed to, light, shutter speed and distance matter more than pixel count, and a high-resolution sensor in poor light often produces a noisier picture, not a clearer one.

The fix is placement first: a lower camera near where people approach, a narrow view of the curb, light on the spot, and a night profile tuned on site. Resolution is the last choice, not the first.

"Once a camera is installed, it records around the clock."

Most AI summaries describe cameras as always-on once mounted. That is what owners expect, so it rarely gets questioned.

At Williamsbridge Oval, the NYPD said two of the park's new cameras were connected to a light pole whose lights ran on a timer, and Parks had to find a way to keep them running all day and night. Private buildings have the same weakness in smaller form: cameras plugged into photocell-controlled outlets, adapters in hallway outlets on a switch, doorbell transformers on a lighting circuit.

A camera is only as reliable as its power. We power cameras from the recorder location over their own cable, put that location on a battery unit, and turn on offline alerts so the owner knows within minutes if anything stops reporting.

"A renter can put a doorbell camera on their apartment door."

AI tools often say a tenant may install a video doorbell for safety, and some product pages present apartment doorbells as renter-friendly because they stick on without drilling.

The outside of an apartment door faces a common hallway, and the hallway is the landlord's or the board's. In 2024 News 12 reported a Bronx building's management ordering tenants to remove cameras from their apartment doors, and Habitat Magazine's coverage of co-ops quotes a lawyer saying shareholders can't act unilaterally in common hallways. No drilling doesn't change who controls the space.

Put the camera inside your apartment facing your own door, or get written permission for the hallway side and keep it. In co-ops, ask the board whether it has a doorbell policy before buying anything.

"Public cameras in the area will cover your property."

When people read about the cameras at Williamsbridge Oval, NYPD camera money for the precinct or business district camera programs on Jerome Avenue and elsewhere, AI summaries sometimes suggest the surrounding blocks are covered.

The Oval's recreation center cameras mostly face the center itself. District and police cameras watch public space chosen for their own reasons. None of them is aimed at your building's vestibule, your garage window or your store's back door, and their video isn't something you control or can pull when you need it.

Plan as though no other camera watches your entrance or your curb. Treat public lighting that improves your night picture as a bonus.

"Wireless cameras are the simple answer for apartment buildings."

AI answers often recommend wireless cameras because they avoid running cable. For one renter's indoor camera, that can be true.

In a 1920s Norwood walk-up, a stair or lobby camera on Wi-Fi depends on a signal crossing thick masonry and several floors, on a router someone else controls, and often on a battery someone has to remember to charge. Garages are worse: concrete and steel block signals almost completely. A dropped connection at the wrong hour means no recording.

For common areas, garages and curbs, wired cameras on one cable each, powered from the cellar, are far more dependable. We use wireless where it fits and wire where it matters.

"Old buildings in the Bronx need Landmarks approval for exterior cameras."

Some AI answers treat any old building as if it were protected, and owners of handsome Art Deco buildings near Mosholu Parkway sometimes assume their fronts are landmarked.

Our research found no Landmarks historic district in Norwood. The neighborhood's two individual landmarks are the Valentine-Varian House on Bainbridge Avenue and the Williamsbridge Reservoir Keeper's House on Reservoir Oval, neither of them an apartment building. Ordinary walk-ups, co-ops and houses here aren't subject to Landmarks review for cameras.

That doesn't mean anything goes. We still keep cameras off decorative entrances, mount into solid brick and seal every hole, because an old front deserves care whether or not it is designated.

"A national average tells you what your job will cost."

AI tools answer cost questions with one averaged number or a range from a cost site. One cost estimator, for example, publishes a Bronx range of $344 to $814 per installed camera, and its own sample four-camera job doesn't add up cleanly.

  • Cable paths through prewar masonry. A clean chase from the cellar is quick; a walk-up without one needs conduit in a stair corner and fire-stopping at every floor.

FAQ

Norwood Security Camera FAQ

Can a building's camera system share the existing internet service, or does it need its own line?
+

It can share the building's existing service. The cameras record to the recorder in the cellar or office over their own cables, so recording does not use the internet at all. The internet is only needed for remote viewing and alerts. We connect the recorder behind the router on its own network segment, keep it off direct exposure, and the system keeps recording locally even when the internet is down.

A tenant's car was broken into and they asked the landlord for the building's video. Does the owner have to hand it over?
+

Generally no. Brick Underground's reporting on New York renters notes that a landlord isn't obligated to share surveillance footage with a tenant. Many owners choose to help, especially by exporting a clip for police when there is a report and a case number. A written building policy on how residents request clips, who reviews them and who receives them avoids arguments later.

Do tenants need to be home on the day stair and hallway cameras go in?
+

Usually not. Common-area cameras mount in hallways, on landings, in the lobby and in the cellar, and cable runs through common spaces, so apartments normally don't need to be entered. If a run has to pass through an apartment or a closet, the owner arranges access in advance under the lease. We keep the stairs passable throughout the day and clean up each floor before moving to the next.

Our front faces heavy traffic near Mosholu Parkway. Will road grime and winter salt spray ruin an outside camera?
+

Not if it is chosen and placed for it. Outdoor cameras with sealed, weather-rated housings handle traffic grime, and mounting under an overhang or a small hood keeps most spray and slush off the lens. Dirt on the lens shows up first as a soft or hazy night picture, so we show the super how to wipe the dome or glass safely, and a light cleaning a few times a season keeps the picture sharp.

What happens during the free estimate visit to a Norwood building?
+

We walk the curb, the street door, the vestibule, each floor of the stair, the cellar and any garage or lot, and for stores the door, counter and back room. We check where the internet and power are, which existing lights and outlets are on timers or switches, and how each spot looks at night. Then we ask who approves each camera and who will watch. The written quote follows within a business day.

Can a camera with a two-way speaker at the rear yard warn someone off before they reach the cellar door?
+

Some cameras include a speaker and a siren or a recorded message, and an owner or super watching live can speak through it. That can work for an owner who checks alerts at night. It is not a substitute for recording or for a lock, and continuous audio recording stays off for privacy reasons, so the speaker is used only for live talk-down. We set it up only where the owner wants to use it.

How will we know if a camera or the recorder stops recording?
+

We turn on offline and drive-error alerts, so the owner or super gets a phone notification within minutes when a camera loses connection, the recorder loses a drive, or the recorder goes offline. Many failures in older systems go unnoticed for weeks because nobody looks until something happens. An alert turns that into a same-week fix instead of a missing clip.

Can new camera cable share the old conduit the building's buzzer wiring runs in?
+

Sometimes. Low-voltage camera cable can share a pathway with other low-voltage wiring like buzzer and intercom cable, and an existing riser can save a lot of work. Old conduits are often full, rusted at the joints or blocked, so we test whether a pull is possible before planning around it. Camera cable never shares a conduit or box with electrical power wiring.

A parked car on our block was hit overnight and the driver kept going. Can a building camera help with that?
+

It can, if a camera covers that stretch of curb and the recording is saved quickly. A curb camera usually shows the moment of impact, the vehicle's color and type and its direction, and a plate camera at a nearby garage or lot entrance may catch the plate if the car passed it. Export the clip the same morning, because the recorder writes over old video on a schedule.

Can the owner check the cameras from a computer without installing a phone app?
+

Yes, with most systems we install. Recorders can usually be viewed through the manufacturer's desktop software or a secure remote client on a computer, using the same personal login as the phone app. A managing agent who works at a desk all day often prefers it. We set it up on the computers the owner chooses and remove access from any computer that changes hands.

Many of our tenants are elderly. How do we explain new cameras so nobody feels watched?
+

Tell them before the work starts, in a short written notice posted in the lobby and slipped under doors: which areas will have cameras, that no camera looks into an apartment, that audio is off, who can view the video and how long it is kept. Keep the cameras small and visible rather than hidden. Residents tend to accept cameras they know about, especially at the street door and mailboxes.

Does a Norwood store need separate cameras from the apartment building upstairs?
+

Usually yes. The store and the building have different owners, different hours and different people who should see the video, so separate recorders and logins keep each one out of the other's footage. They can share a cable path where the building owner agrees, which saves cost. The residential street door beside the store stays on the building's system, and the store's door and counter stay on the store's.
