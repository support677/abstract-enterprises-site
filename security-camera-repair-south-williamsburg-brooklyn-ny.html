Why South Williamsburg Properties Need Security Camera Repair

South Williamsburg is usually drawn as the part of Williamsburg south of Division Avenue, with Broadway on its northeast side, Flushing Avenue on the south and the East River on the west, though local writers and older maps put the lines in slightly different places. Most of it is home to one of the largest Hasidic communities in the country, centered on Lee and Bedford Avenues, alongside the long-established Puerto Rican community of Los Sures closer to the Williamsburg Bridge. That is the background. For a camera repair, what matters is how the buildings are used, when people are home, and who is, and is not, looking at the cameras.

Start with the weekly rhythm. Commercial life on Lee Avenue slows almost to a stop from Friday evening until Saturday night, and many residents do not use phones, screens or electrical switches during that time. A rabbinic source, Peninei Halakha, goes further about video: it says an observant Jew should not stand where his image will be captured on a monitor or press an intercom button to be let in. Need Repair in South Williamsburg? A Break-In Before Dawn, a Lobby Theft After Shabbos, or a Recorder Nobody Knew Had Stopped

Urgent calls from South Williamsburg tend to arrive in three shapes. The first follows a break-in or an attempt: a neighbor or Shomrim asks what your camera saw on the porch, the side alley or the back door at four or five in the morning, and the clip is a grey smear, a white glow where the face should be, or nothing at all because the camera that faces that way has been dark for months. The second follows a theft from the lobby or mailroom, often noticed on Saturday night once the building is moving again, when a board member goes to look and finds that the camera over the parcel shelf shows only the tops of heads, or that the recorder overwrote the hours in question while nobody was watching. The third is quieter: someone checks the system for an ordinary reason and discovers that the recorder stopped saving video weeks ago, or that half the cameras have been showing "no signal" since a power blip, and nobody was told.

If you need footage from a particular day, leave the recorder running and do not unplug it, restart it or press anything that says format or initialize. Recorders overwrite their oldest video once the drive is full, so the clock is already running, and a restart can also wipe the log that shows when a camera dropped or the drive stopped writing. If the hours you need fall over Shabbos or Yom Tov and you will not touch the system until afterward, that is fine: tell us on Saturday night or the morning after, with the date and approximate time you need, the camera it should be on, and a photo of the recorder's screen or front panel. We plan the visit so that clip is copied first, before any cable, drive or setting is changed.

For everything else the path is short. The $150 Brooklyn service call books a two-hour arrival window, and requests are booked within 2 hours. On Fridays we book visits early in the day, so no repair is left half-finished going into Shabbos. The technician finds the fault, tells you what failed and why, and writes down the price from our Brooklyn list before any part is replaced. Most single-fault repairs are finished on that visit, with the camera checked in daylight and in darkness before we leave, and the recorder's own alerts tested so you know they reach the person who will act on them.

Brick walk-ups and row houses: one recorder, many hands

A typical older building toward Lee Avenue has a recorder on a cellar shelf or in a hallway closet and four to eight cameras: one over the street door, one over the stoop or porch, one in the hall, one or two at the back and perhaps one on a side alley. They were rarely installed at the same time. A first set went up years ago on coax cable to a DVR; later someone added a Wi-Fi camera at the back because pulling cable through the brick was hard; later still a family member put a doorbell camera on the front door with its own app. When something fails, the owner often does not know which camera is on which box. We begin these repairs by mapping every camera to its recorder or app, labeling cables and channels, and only then fixing what is broken, so the next call does not start from nothing.

Brick also shapes the repair itself. Cable on these buildings runs along mortar joints, through a hole beside a window or down from the cornice, and every hole that was not sealed is a path for water into the connector. Many "dead camera" calls on old brick turn out to be a corroded connector a foot from the camera, which is a much cheaper fix than a new camera. And because the September 2026 reports describe someone testing rear entrances and ground-floor windows, we look hard at the back and side cameras on these buildings, which are the ones most likely to have been neglected.

Family-size condos: the developer's system, the board's problem

The condo buildings that have gone up on Wallabout Street, Williamsburg Street East, Lee Avenue and the side streets in recent years are often designed for large households, with front balconies stacked so each one is open to the sky. Their camera systems are usually network cameras on a PoE switch in a meter or telecom room, a recorder beside it, and a video intercom at the street door. The hardware is generally fine. What goes wrong is ownership: the developer's electrician set it up, the recorder's admin password went with him, the cloud account is in a sponsor's email, and the board that took over the building has no way in. A repair in these buildings starts with getting the board into its own system, documented, with separate logins for each person who needs one.

Small condos have a second issue. In a four-unit building like the walk-up condo CityRealty lists at 217 Lee Avenue, there may be no shared recorder at all, only each owner's doorbell camera on its own app. That works until something happens in the shared hall or at the street door and nobody's camera covered it. We can fix what each owner has and, if the owners agree, put the shared spaces on one small recorder they all can reach.

Kent Avenue and waterfront towers: scale and contractor rules

A tower system is large, and large systems fail quietly. With a hundred or more cameras on a desk monitor, nobody notices one dark tile on the twelfth floor or a stairwell camera that has been pointing at a wall since a painter knocked it. These buildings are also concrete and steel, so cable runs are stable but a dead hallway camera is reached through a ceiling access panel or the fixture opening, not a new hole. Work follows the building's contractor rules: insurance certificate, freight elevator booking, sign-in at the desk, and sometimes a set work window. A tower repair often begins with a camera-by-camera audit: which ones are recording, at what quality, for how long, and which have died without anyone noticing.

PACT and public housing towers: who has to say yes

Independence Towers is six 21-story buildings between Clymer and Wilson Streets, completed in 1965; Taylor–Wythe Houses, completed in 1974, are five buildings of 8 to 13 stories with 525 apartments. Since the PACT conversions, the cameras in the converted developments belong to the private management, and repairs go through them. A tenant cannot hire us to work on the building's hallway or lobby cameras, and we will not touch a building system without the manager's approval. What residents can do is report the dead camera to management in writing and keep a copy. Inside your own apartment, a camera you own is yours to have repaired, and we are glad to help with that.

Los Sures co-ops and rentals: old cable, careful budgets

Many of the buildings near the bridge plaza were rehabilitated decades ago by Southside United HDFC and similar groups, and some of their camera systems date from that era: coax cable, a DVR, a separate multi-camera power supply in a box on the cellar wall. That power supply is often the first part to fail, and when it goes, every camera goes dark at once. Coax can usually be kept: a hybrid recorder or higher-resolution coax cameras give a much better picture on the same cable. For co-op boards and nonprofit managers watching every dollar, we price each fault on its own line, so the most important cameras can be fixed now and the rest scheduled.

Shuls, yeshivas and schools: busy days, locked offices

Institutional buildings here range from converted factories to purpose-built schools and small storefront shuls. A circa-1910 six-story building that the Central United Talmudical Academy bought in 2007 to turn into a school is one example the Brooklyn Eagle has written about. Their cameras tend to grow floor by floor, with recorders in an office that may be locked over Shabbos and Yom Tov, and logins held by a volunteer, a parent or a former administrator. We schedule around classes, davening and holidays, work through whoever the board or principal names, and keep cameras covering children, staff areas or the sanctuary pointed only where the institution's own rules allow. Field Notes

Field Notes: What We Check First on a Saturday-Night Call

This is the order our technicians work through when a South Williamsburg household or board calls after Shabbos to say something happened and the cameras may not have caught it. It is our working method, not a story about any one building. First, we ask whether the recorder is still running and tell them to leave it alone. Second, we ask for the date and time window and which camera should have seen it, and we ask them to read us, or send a photo of, what the recorder shows for that camera now: live picture, "no signal," or a frozen frame. Third, we ask whether the power or the internet went out during Shabbos, because a building that lost power at 2 a.m. may have a recorder that restarted without its drive, or a camera that never came back.

On site, the first job is always the clip. If the drive is healthy, we export the hours asked for in the recorder's own format before anything else. If the drive is failing, we copy what we can before testing anything. Only then do we look at why the picture was poor or missing, and what to change so it does not happen again: the angle, the light, the infrared, the recording schedule, the alerts, or the power. Most of the time the answer is not a new system. It is a setting nobody revisited, a camera mounted too high, a porch light on a timer, or a recorder whose warnings were going to an email address nobody reads.

Fault by Fault

Unwatched Hours, Hooded Faces, Glowing Infrared, Lobby Thefts and Power Blips: How South Williamsburg Camera Systems Go Wrong

Unwatched hours: a system that has to look after itself

Most camera advice assumes someone glances at the app every day and will notice a black tile. In a household that keeps Shabbos, there is a predictable gap of about twenty-five hours every week, longer over a two- or three-day Yom Tov, when nobody will. A camera that fails at three on Friday afternoon is first noticed on Saturday night. A recorder that freezes during a power dip at midnight sits frozen until someone looks. A drive that fills and stops overwriting, because of a setting, simply stops recording. None of this is a reason to change how anyone keeps Shabbos. It is a reason to repair the system so it needs no one during those hours.

In practice that means four things. The recorder is set to overwrite its oldest video automatically, so a full drive never stops it. It is set to restart and resume recording by itself after a power loss, and we test that by pulling its plug in front of you. Its health alerts, for video loss on any camera, a drive error or recording stopped, go to someone who will see them before candle-lighting, so a failure on Thursday or Friday morning can still be fixed before Shabbos. And the system's power is protected well enough to ride through the short dips that make recorders freeze. Whether alerts arrive during Shabbos at all, and on whose phone, is the household's decision; we set it up the way you ask.

Hooded faces: why a ceiling camera loses to a sweatshirt

When DNAinfo reported a rise in burglaries in the 90th Precinct in 2015, officers gave advice that still holds: mount cameras low and angled upward, because a camera on the ceiling looking down is easily beaten by a hooded sweatshirt. A camera high over a door, looking straight down, sees the top of a hood, a hat brim or the crown of a head. That is the most common reason a clip from a South Williamsburg lobby, stoop or porch shows that someone was there but not who. The fix is usually position rather than a new camera: lower and to one side, looking across the doorway at face height, or a second small camera at the door looking outward at people as they arrive. On a brick facade that is often a short extension along a mortar joint; in a lobby, a small camera beside the intercom panel or the mailboxes.

Hats are a particular case here. Brims shade the face from above as much as a hood does, so a camera looking down at a doorway sees brims, not faces, for a large share of the people walking through. That is not a fault of the camera; it is the wrong height. When we re-aim a door camera in this neighborhood, we test with someone standing where visitors stand, and check that the face, not the hat, fills the frame.

Glowing infrared: when night vision erases the face

The same 2015 advice said to leave lights on, because infrared night vision distorts faces. It does, in a specific way: at close range, a camera's own infrared lights the nearest object most strongly, and a face a few feet from a porch or door camera can turn into a bright white patch with no features. Pale walls, glass and metal railings close to the lens make it worse by bouncing the light back. A camera that looks fine on a quiet street can be useless for exactly the early-morning moment the September 2026 reports describe, when someone is at your door in the dark.

There are three repairs, in order of cost. First, settings: many cameras have a "smart" infrared mode that dims the light when something is close, and it is often switched off. Second, light: a small fixture over the door that stays on all night, set before Shabbos if needed, lets a color camera see a face with no infrared at all. Third, the camera: a model built for low light with its own visible light, or one that manages its infrared well, replaces a cheap one that does neither. We test at night, or simulate it by covering the scene, before we call the repair done.

Lobby and mailroom thefts: the clip that has to exist days later

Your Questions Answered

These are the questions South Williamsburg owners, boards, tenants and gabbaim bring to us about cameras that already exist and have stopped doing their job. Each answer is what we would tell you on the phone before a visit.

Shabbos, Yom Tov and the hours nobody checks

We put the porch light on a Shabbos clock that switches it off around midnight. From then until morning, the porch camera's picture of our door is a grey blur. Is the camera failing?

Probably not. While the lamp is on, the camera sees in color by the lamp's light. When the clock cuts it, the camera has to switch to its own infrared, refocus, and light the porch from a few feet away, which is the situation where faces wash out or turn to mush. The 90th Precinct's advice in 2015 was simply to leave lights on, and the September 2026 entries that Shomrim warned about happened in the early morning, after many timers have already turned porch lights off. The cheapest repair is often the clock setting: keep a low, warm fixture on until daylight. On the camera side, we set its day and night switching so it does not flip back and forth at midnight, and tune or replace it so the dark-hours picture is usable. How the light is set before Shabbos is your decision; we make the camera work with whatever you choose.

Most of the adults in our house carry kosher flip phones that cannot run a camera app. How will anyone know that a camera or the recorder has stopped?

A resident blog about life in South Williamsburg notes how many flip phones there are in the neighborhood, so this is a common setup, and it changes how a repair is finished. There are several ways to get a warning without an app. A small screen beside the recorder or in the kitchen can show every camera at once, so a black square is obvious at a glance. The recorder's built-in buzzer can be turned on for video loss and drive errors, if the recorder sits somewhere it will be heard. Its email alerts can go to a computer in the house or the office, or to a relative or managing agent who uses a smartphone. Some setups can pass an email alert on as an ordinary text message, depending on the phone carrier; we test what actually reaches your phone instead of promising it. Whichever you pick, a quick look at the screen on Thursday leaves time to call before Friday.

Our shul's lobby has a screen that shows the entrance cameras. Some members have asked that it not show their picture on Shabbos. Can the screen go dark on a schedule while the cameras keep recording?

Yes. The screen and the recording are separate: the recorder saves video whether a monitor is attached or not. A screen can be powered through a timer set before Shabbos, put to sleep by its own schedule, or fed from a recorder output that only shows chosen cameras. We set it up, switch the screen off in front of the gabbai, and then play back the same minutes to show that nothing was lost. Peninei Halakha, a rabbinic work, discusses standing where one's image appears on a monitor, and whether recording itself is acceptable on Shabbos is a question for your rav, not for a camera company. We will configure the system the way the shul decides and write down how it is set, so the next person who touches it does not undo it by accident.

Our yeshiva's recorder is in the principal's office, which stays locked through a three-day Yom Tov. If something happens on the first night, will the video still be there when the office opens?

Only if the drive keeps more days than the office is closed, plus the time it takes someone to realize they need the video. Many school systems put in years ago were set to keep a few days at reduced quality, which is barely enough. We check how many days the recorder actually holds at the quality that shows a face, and size the drive and recording settings so the entrances and halls keep at least two to three weeks. A locked office is a good place for a recorder, because a thief cannot carry it away, but a closed room in summer with the air conditioning off can overheat it, so we check its ventilation as well. If the school wants a second safeguard, the most important cameras can also record to a second recorder in another room or to an off-site copy, so one failure does not lose everything.

Tenants, landlords and building systems

Our building used to show the lobby camera on a channel on every apartment's TV. After the cable company rewired the building, the channel disappeared. Can it be brought back?

Often, yes. Older buildings sent the lobby camera into the building's TV cable through a small device called a modulator, so it appeared as one more channel. When a provider rewires the risers, that device is frequently disconnected or bypassed, and the camera itself is still working. The repair is to find the modulator, reconnect it to the building's distribution, and check the channel in a few apartments. This has come up before in New York: in a 2015 decision reported by landlordvtenant.com, the state housing agency upheld rent reductions after a building's vestibule camera feed to tenants' TVs stopped because the cable from the camera had been disconnected, noting the service had been provided for 19 years. That was one building's case, not a rule for every one, and we work for the owner. Where few tenants still watch cable TV, the owner may prefer to put the lobby view on apartment intercom screens instead.

Our landlord is having the hallway cameras repaired, and some tenants feel watched. What are the limits on where building cameras can point?

Courts in New York have generally allowed cameras in a building's shared spaces: in Otero v. Houston St. Owners Corp., decided in 2012, tenants' privacy claims over hallway cameras were dismissed. That does not mean anything goes. On every building repair we aim hallway, stair and lobby cameras at the common areas they are meant to cover, and we do not aim a camera so that it looks into an apartment when its door opens, through a window, or into a private room. If an existing camera does that, we tell the owner and recommend re-aiming or masking that part of the picture before we leave. We are not your lawyer, and a tenant with a specific complaint should take it to the owner, the managing agent or legal help; what we can do is make sure the cameras we repair cover the hallway and nothing more.

The balconies stacked above our building's street door keep the entrance in shadow all day. The door camera shows visitors as dark shapes against the bright sidewalk, even at noon. What fixes that?

Many newer South Williamsburg buildings have deep balconies stacked or staggered up the front, built so each family's sukkah is open to the sky, and the door underneath sits in shade while the sidewalk beyond it is in full sun. A camera mounted in that shade and aimed outward exposes for the bright street and turns faces into silhouettes. There are three fixes, usually combined. First, the camera's wide dynamic range and backlight settings, which are often off or set low. Second, the angle: a camera on the side wall looking across the entrance, or one by the door looking back in toward the building, sees faces against the dark doorway instead of the bright street. Third, light under the balcony slab, which evens out the scene day and night. If the camera cannot handle the contrast at all, replacing it with one built for backlit entrances is the last step, not the first.

The cable for our door camera runs up the doorframe right beside the mezuzah. If the camera is replaced, will the technician need to move the mezuzah?

No. Our technicians do not remove or handle a mezuzah. If the new camera needs a different cable route, we take it along the other side of the frame, above the lintel or through the wall beside the door, and we point out exactly where we plan to drill before we start, so you can say if it is too close. If other work on the door really does require the mezuzah to come off, that is for the family, a sofer or your rav to handle before we arrive and to put back afterward. In our experience the question almost never arises on a camera repair, because a camera at the doorway can usually be reached from the outer face of the wall without touching the doorpost at all.

The street, the season and the neighbors

Every fall the lulav and esrog stalls set up on the sidewalk in front of our Lee Avenue store. For two weeks the storefront camera sees only the back of a stall and sends an alert every few seconds. What can we do?

Brooklyn Magazine has reported that Lee Avenue is closed to traffic each fall for lulav and esrog stalls, so this is a seasonal problem rather than a broken camera. For those weeks, the motion zone can be redrawn to cover only the door and the strip of sidewalk the stall leaves clear, and alerts can be limited to people at the door instead of any movement. A varifocal camera can be zoomed over the top of the stall to the entrance, and a small indoor camera just behind the front window keeps the doorway covered whatever stands outside. Keep the storefront camera recording around the clock during the season, because crowded sidewalks are when things go missing. We save the normal settings as a preset, so after Sukkos they can be restored in a minute instead of being rebuilt.

After the Hooper Street break-ins, Shomrim asked everyone on our block to check their cameras for one early-morning window. How do we find the right minutes without watching hours of video?

Use the recorder's playback timeline rather than watching from the start. Most recorders mark recorded motion as colored blocks on the timeline, so you can jump straight to the moments when something moved on the camera facing the street. Many also let you draw a box over part of the picture, the sidewalk in front of your door for example, and search only for motion inside it. Play those stretches at four or eight times speed, then slow down when someone appears. Once you have it, export only those minutes. If your timeline shows no marks at all, the camera may be recording without motion detection or detection may be switched off, and that is something we fix and then teach on the visit. Whether you hand the clip to Shomrim or to the 90th Precinct is up to you as the owner.

A new building is going up on the lot next to ours, and the contractor's sidewalk shed now covers the view from our front camera. Is there a temporary fix?

New York YIMBY has reported new buildings filed in South Williamsburg on Wallabout Street and Williamsburg Street East, and each one brings a shed and months of work. Under a shed the sidewalk is dark by day and lit by the shed's own lamps at night, so a camera aimed over the top sees plywood. The usual temporary fix is to re-aim or lower the camera so it looks along the covered walkway toward your own door, adjust its settings for the darker scene, and add a small camera at the door if the shed hides the approach completely. We photograph the original position first, so the camera can go back exactly where it was when the shed comes down. We do not attach anything to a contractor's shed without the contractor's permission, and if the work next door has disturbed your cable, take photos before anything is repaired.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in South Williamsburg

Search summaries, chatbots and home-service directories such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr give camera advice written for an average suburban house. Some of it holds anywhere. Some of it fails on these blocks, where systems run unwatched every week, buildings are brick or concrete, and the burglaries people talk about happen before dawn. These are the general claims we see most often, and what South Williamsburg repairs actually show.

"Check your camera app every day and you will spot problems early"

The advice assumes someone opens an app daily. In many South Williamsburg households, nobody looks from Friday afternoon to Saturday night, and some adults carry phones that cannot run a camera app at all. A plan built on daily checking leaves a predictable gap every week, and a longer one over Yom Tov.

The repair has to replace the daily glance. That means a recorder that overwrites, restarts and resumes by itself, alerts sent to someone who will see them before Shabbos, and a screen in the house where a dead camera shows as a black square without anyone opening anything.

We test those things on the visit, including pulling the recorder's power, because a system that has to run alone for twenty-five hours should be proven to do it before we leave.

"Mount cameras high, out of reach, for the widest view"

High mounts do protect a camera and do show more of a yard. But the 90th Precinct's own advice, reported by DNAinfo in 2015, was to mount cameras low and angled upward, because a camera on the ceiling is easily beaten by a hooded sweatshirt.

On these blocks the problem is wider than hoods. Many men wear hats with brims, and a camera looking down at a doorway records brims rather than faces for a large share of the people walking through, whether they are neighbors or not.

The answer is usually two cameras with two jobs: one high for the overall scene and for tamper resistance, and one at about face height near the door, looking at people as they approach. Most of our "the camera saw nothing useful" repairs are really height repairs.

"Night vision lets you see clearly in total darkness"

Infrared night vision lets a camera see shapes and movement in the dark. It does not guarantee a recognizable face, and the same 2015 precinct advice warned that infrared distorts faces, which is why officers told owners to leave lights on.

The worst case is close range. A porch or door camera's infrared lights the nearest surface hardest, so a face a few feet away can become a white patch, especially next to pale walls, glass doors or metal railings that bounce the light back into the lens.

The early-morning entries reported in September 2026 are exactly that scene: someone at a door in the dark. A light left on, a camera that dims its infrared at close range, or a low-light color camera fixes it. A brighter infrared camera usually makes it worse.

"Your landlord has to keep the building's cameras working and give you the footage"

Not as a general rule. In a Brick Underground column about a Williamsburg renter's stolen packages, attorney Jennifer Rozen explained that city buildings are not required to have working cameras, and that landlords are not required to share footage outside a legal process.

There are exceptions that depend on the building's history. In the 2015 DHCR decision about a vestibule camera feed that had been provided to tenants for 19 years, rent reductions were upheld when it stopped, because it had become a service the landlord was required to maintain.

For a repair company, that means we take instructions from the owner or the board, not from a tenant about a building system. For a tenant, it means a written request to the owner, and a police report after a theft, is the path to footage; the police can request it.

"Just switch to wireless cameras and skip the wiring"

Wireless cameras are fine in the right spot, and we install and repair plenty of them. But Wi-Fi does not pass well through several brick walls, and the back of a walk-up is often too far from the router in the front apartment for a steady signal.

There is also the question of who can see them. Wireless consumer cameras are built around a smartphone app and a cloud account. In homes with flip phones or filtered internet, that app may not run, may be blocked, or may be on one relative's phone only.

Where a cable already exists, repairing the wired camera is usually more dependable and costs less over time than replacing it with a wireless one. Where it does not, an access point at the rear of the building often fixes the Wi-Fi camera that is already there.

"Businesses in New York City have to register their cameras with the police"

There is no such requirement. A 2019 State Senate bill, S487, would have created a mandatory camera registry, and it never became law, so no store on Lee Avenue or Broadway has to register its system.

What does exist is voluntary. In May 2024 the NYPD announced its Connect New York pilot, which lets businesses that choose to share their camera feeds with their precinct do so. Whether to take part is the owner's decision.

A repair can make either choice workable: cameras that record clearly, a recorder with its clock right, and someone on staff who knows how to export a clip when the 90th Precinct asks. That matters more than any registration.

FAQ

South Williamsburg Security Camera Repair FAQ

Our recorder sits in the cellar room we close off as a Pesach kitchen for most of the year. Is that a bad place for it?
+

It can be. A recorder needs air around it and someone able to reach it, and a room that is sealed and packed for eleven months often gives it neither. Boxes stacked against the vents let it overheat, and if a camera fails or the police ask for video, nobody wants to open the room in the middle of the year. On a repair we can move the recorder to a ventilated shelf or cabinet elsewhere in the cellar, extend the cables to reach it, and label where it went, so it stays reachable all year without disturbing the room.

Since our home internet filter was installed, the camera app on the one smartphone in the house will not connect. Is the camera system broken?
+

Usually not. Internet filters work by blocking services they do not recognize, and a camera app's connection to its cloud service is often one of the things blocked. The cameras keep recording to the recorder the whole time; only the remote view stops. The repair is to find which addresses the app needs and have them allowed by whoever manages the filter, or to set up viewing on the home network only. We test the app on site after the change and leave written notes of what was allowed, so the next filter update does not quietly undo it.

We would rather our cameras not be connected to the internet at all. Can a repaired system record and be watched only inside the building?
+

Yes. A recorder does not need the internet to record. We can disconnect it from the router, turn off its cloud and remote features, and connect a monitor where the family or the office can see the cameras locally. The trade-offs are that nobody can check the cameras from outside the building and alerts cannot go out by email or app, so a quick look at the monitor before Shabbos becomes the health check. Footage is exported to a USB drive on the spot when someone needs a clip.

Our indoor camera looks out the front window, and on Friday night the candles on the table beside it make the picture of the street glare. Can that be fixed?
+

Yes. What the camera sees is the candlelight reflecting off the inside of the glass, which on a dark street outshines everything beyond the window. The fix is to bring the camera right up to the glass with a soft shroud around the lens, so no light from the room reaches the pane in front of it, and to switch off its own infrared, which reflects the same way. A light outside over the door helps the street picture too. None of it requires moving the candles.

During the break-ins a camera was pulled right off the front of our house. Is replacing a stolen camera covered by the warranty?
+

No. Warranties on camera equipment cover parts that fail in normal use, and theft and physical damage are excluded, so a stolen camera is replaced at the normal price on our Brooklyn list. If you plan to claim it on your insurance, keep the police report number and photograph the mount before we start, and we will note on the invoice that the camera was missing. We usually replace it with a camera on a sturdier, tamper-resistant mount, and check that another camera covers the spot where the thief stood.

If our cameras are mounted low enough to see faces, won't someone just knock them down? How do you balance that?
+

With two cameras rather than one compromise. A camera high on the facade, out of reach, records the whole approach and anyone who tampers with the lower camera. A second camera at about face height near the door, in a vandal-resistant dome or a recessed housing, records faces. If someone hits the low camera, the high one has recorded who did it. On a brick walk-up both can usually share the cable route that is already there.

Neighbors on our street want Shomrim to be able to watch our street-facing cameras live. Can a repaired system be shared like that?
+

Technically, yes, and whether to do it is the owner's decision. If you choose to, we give the volunteer group its own login that can see only the street-facing cameras, cannot see indoor or rear cameras, and cannot delete or change anything, so it can be switched off in a minute if you change your mind. We do not share any camera without the owner's approval, and in a building with several owners or a board, all of them should agree first.

With a big family, our front door opens dozens of times an hour on a Sunday, and the door camera sends so many alerts that we have stopped reading them. What can be done?
+

Keep the recording and change the alerts. The door camera should go on recording everything, but its alerts can be limited to the hours when the house is asleep, to people rather than any movement, or switched off for that camera altogether while the rear and side cameras keep alerting around the clock, since those are the doors and windows nobody should be using at night. Health alerts, for a dead camera or a failing drive, stay on all the time, because those are the ones you cannot afford to ignore.

Our board wants recordings deleted automatically after a set number of days, so residents are not filmed and stored forever. Can the recorder do that?
+

Yes. Most recorders let you set a maximum number of days to keep, after which older video is erased even if the drive has room. We set it to the number the board chooses and confirm it in playback. When choosing, keep in mind that a theft is often discovered days later, and that a building which keeps too little video has nothing to give the police. Two to four weeks is a common choice, and individual clips can still be exported and kept when there is an incident.

Our camera system was put in by someone in the community who did it on the side and left no paperwork. Can you still work on it?
+

Yes, and it is one of the most common situations we meet. We start by mapping the system: which cameras feed which recorder, what model each part is, where the power comes from and which logins exist. That map, with labels on the cables and channels, becomes the paperwork you never had. Anything unsafe, such as cameras powered from an extension cord out a window, is pointed out and priced separately, and everything else is repaired as it stands.
