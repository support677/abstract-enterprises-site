The Neighborhood

Why Cobble Hill Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Cobble Hill is small, old and mostly protected, and every one of those facts changes how a camera system is laid out. Wikipedia puts the neighborhood at about forty blocks, bounded by Atlantic Avenue to the north, Court Street to the east, Degraw Street to the south and the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway to the west. Inside those lines, the Cobble Hill Historic District covers roughly sixty acres and 796 contributing buildings put up between the 1830s and the 1920s, in Greek Revival, Italianate and Queen Anne dress, according to its own Wikipedia entry. The city designated the district in December 1969 and extended it on June 7, 1988, and it joined the National Register of Historic Places in 1976. The Cobble Hill Association's history pages describe the goal of the landmark law here as preserving the neighborhood's nineteenth-century scale, and they name 122 Pacific Street, from 1832, as its oldest house. A camera on one of these fronts is an alteration to a protected facade, and the city has written rules for it.

Those rules are specific. Section 2-20 of the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules says security cameras must be as small and as few as feasible, with no more than two cameras for every twenty-five feet of street frontage on a residential or commercial building of six storeys or less with a frontage of forty feet or less. Larger buildings may carry three per twenty-five feet. The same section asks for attachment at mortar joints or through plain brick, stone or wood, a finish that matches the surrounding material, no more than about ten inches of visible conduit, and a location that is not visible or as little visible as possible, giving as examples beneath a band course, underneath a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence, or behind plantings. Brownstoner listed a Greek Revival house on Kane Street at twenty-five feet wide, which is about as broad as a single-family front here gets. For most owners the arithmetic lands on two cameras for the whole street face.

The second thing that shapes a plan is when trouble has actually happened. In January 2013, the Brooklyn Eagle reported, the NYPD released surveillance video of a man walking up to 360 Baltic Street, between Smith and Hoyt Streets, prying the front door open with a screwdriver at 4:38 in the afternoon, and taking a package from the hallway. In the first week of August 2015, DNAinfo counted at least seven home burglaries in Cobble Hill and Carroll Gardens, most of them with a door lock broken while the owner was away: a back door that did not lock on the 300 block of Smith Street, a broken lock between 2:45 and 5:45 p.m. on the 200 block of Baltic Street, and more than $10,000 in camera and digital equipment taken after a front lock was broken on the 400 block of Hicks Street. In January 2020, NY City Lens reported, a man police linked to six residential burglaries forced an apartment door near Smith and Degraw Streets in the afternoon. None of these were midnight jobs. A camera system that only wakes up after dark misses the hours the record points to.

The third thing is that people here have been told, more than once, that habits matter as much as hardware. The Cobble Hill Association passed along a message from the 76th Precinct's commanding officer, Deputy Inspector Natalie Maldonado, that a burglar arrested in the area said Carroll Gardens and Cobble Hill were easy targets because many residents leave their doors unlocked. In 2013, Gothamist reported, officers here even sent letters to thirty-five to forty residents who had left apartment doors open. Cameras do not lock a door. What they can do is show you, from your phone at work, that the garden door is standing open, record who walked through it, and give the precinct at 191 Union Street something to work with if a lock is broken anyway. We plan every Cobble Hill system around that honest, limited job.

The Front Budget

Two Cameras per Twenty-Five Feet: Spending the Landmarks Allowance on a Cobble Hill Front

Most camera companies start a quote by asking how many cameras you want. On a house inside the Cobble Hill Historic District, the better first question is how many the front is allowed to carry, because the answer is set by the Landmarks Preservation Commission and not by the budget. Rule 2-20 treats security cameras as health, safety and utility equipment, the same family as gas meters, intercom panels and light fixtures. LPC staff can approve them without a public hearing when the work meets the rule's criteria, and the count is the criterion people notice first: two cameras per twenty-five feet of frontage for buildings of six storeys or less with a front of forty feet or less, three per twenty-five feet for larger commercial, residential and institutional buildings.

On a rowhouse here, that turns a camera plan into a budget. A twenty-foot or twenty-five-foot single-family front has two cameras to spend, and every other camera the house needs has to live somewhere the rule does not count: on the rear facade, inside the house looking at the inside of a door, or in a shared hall. That is not a bad thing: two cameras placed close to the doors, each with one clear job, usually do more than four spread across a cornice line.

Where the two front cameras earn their place

The rule's own examples of discreet positions read like a description of a Cobble Hill stoop: beneath a band course, underneath a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence, behind plantings. Those are also the positions that see faces. A small dome under the stoop landing, finished to match the brownstone or painted to match the ironwork, looks out at the garden-level door and the areaway gate at about head height. A second small camera at the parlor door, mounted within or beside the door opening at a mortar joint, sees the person who climbs the stoop to ring or to try the lock. Between them, the two doors that open onto the street are covered, and someone walking up the stoop walks toward a lens.

What the front pair should not try to do is watch the whole block. A front camera zoomed out to take in both neighbors' stoops and the cars across the street turns a person at your door into a few pixels, and it records your neighbors' comings and goings, which on a row of attached houses is a fast way to lose goodwill. If the street itself matters to you, for example because your car is parked outside, we would rather set one of the two front cameras along your own frontage at a tight angle than add a third camera the rule does not allow.

Attachment, finish and cable on a protected front

Chapter 11 of the LPC Permit Guidebook spells out how equipment may be fixed: at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, metalwork, or concrete paving at the areaway or sidewalk, and never through cast iron or any decorative surface. Equipment is to be as small as possible and painted to blend, and the guidebook's own photographs show dome cameras finished to match the material around them. Cable follows the same logic. The rule asks for no visible conduit, or at most about ten inches of it, painted to match the background, secured into mortar joints and kept off decorative surfaces. In practice that means each front camera sits on a mounting plate anchored in the joints, its cable goes straight back through the wall behind it into the vestibule, the stoop vault or the cellar, and nothing runs across the face of the house.

Afternoons, Not Only Nights

Planning a Cobble Hill System Around the Hours the Break-Ins Actually Happened

Ask most people when burglaries happen and they say at night. The Cobble Hill cases that made the news say otherwise. The Baltic Street door was pried at 4:38 on a January afternoon in 2013. The 2015 burglary on the 200 block of Baltic Street happened between 2:45 and 5:45 p.m., and the one on the 100 block of Sackett Street just over the line in Carroll Gardens between 1:15 and 7:30 p.m. The 2020 door-forcing near Smith and Degraw was in the afternoon, and the pattern it belonged to included other mid-afternoon and noon break-ins in Park Slope. These were people who came while the house was empty and left before anyone returned. A system built only to light up and shriek after dark would have slept through all of them.

So on a Cobble Hill job we set two schedules, not one. The first is recording, which runs continuously on every camera, day and night, because a person walking calmly to a door may not trip a motion setting and because the seconds before a lock gives are the ones that show a face. The second is alerts, which are where the planning goes. While the household is out, usually weekday working hours, a person at the garden door, the rear door or inside the street door sends an alert with a short clip to the phones you choose. When the house is occupied, those same cameras keep recording but stay quiet, so you are not buzzed every time a family member comes home. At night, the rear and inside-the-door cameras alert again, and the front cameras alert only on a person who stops at a door, not on everyone walking past.

The dusk changeover

The Baltic Street door was forced as the January light was going, and that matters technically. Most cameras switch from a color day picture to an infrared night picture at a light level set in the software, and around that changeover the picture can hunt back and forth, blur moving people or show a smeared face. Under a stoop or beneath a band course, where the light falls off early, the switch can come half an hour before it does on the open sidewalk. We set the changeover point for each camera on site and check it at dusk before we sign the job off, and where a doorway has a fixture, we use that light to keep the camera in its sharper color mode for longer.

Brick, brownstone and the mortar joint

Brownstone is a soft sandstone that spalls when it is drilled carelessly, and the trim around Cobble Hill doors and windows is often brownstone even on a brick house. The Landmarks rule and the guidebook point mounts toward mortar joints and plain masonry for the same reason we do: a joint can be repointed, a cracked lintel cannot be put back. We drill into joints with masonry bits sized to the anchor, set stainless anchors, seal every penetration against water and keep the plate small enough that it sits within the joint pattern. When a camera comes off years later, the joint is raked and repointed and the face of the stone is untouched.

Business Security Camera Installation on Atlantic Avenue, Court Street and Smith Street

Cobble Hill's shops sit on three streets with three different rhythms, and the camera plan follows the rhythm. Wikipedia names Court and Smith as the neighborhood's main commercial streets, with Italian meat markets and barber shops next to newer shops and cafes, and the northern part of Smith known as Restaurant Row since the late 1990s and 2000s. Atlantic Avenue, the neighborhood's northern edge, holds one of the city's largest collections of Middle Eastern shops, mostly between Court and Clinton Streets according to 6 to Celebrate, and the Atlantic Avenue BID counts more than three hundred businesses along the corridor from Fourth Avenue to the waterfront.

Atlantic Avenue: groups, glass and the way out

Two Atlantic Avenue incidents in the sources show what a shop camera here has to catch. In October 2014, Brooklyn Paper reported, eight shoplifters walked into a clothing store between Clinton and Court Streets at about 1:45 in the afternoon and took $3,400 in sweatshirts, jeans, hats and gloves; the day before, a single thief had taken a $500 sweater from the same shop just before 6 p.m. In March 2024, News 12 reported, two people smashed through the glass of a convenience store on Atlantic Avenue and took goods while two others kept lookout, and all four ran down Atlantic toward Hicks Street. The first case is a busy-hours problem: several people at once, in daylight, spread across a small floor. The second is an empty-store problem: glass, a short visit and a fast exit. A shop camera plan here covers both with a camera on the door that sees each face as it enters, a camera behind the counter looking out over the floor, one in the stock room, and, where the facade allows, a view along the sidewalk in the direction people leave.

Court Street: long days and offices upstairs

Court Street mixes shops, banks, a cinema at 265 Court Street that opened in 1926 as the Lido, and second-floor offices reached by a separate street door. The ground-floor tenant usually controls the inside of the shop and the landlord controls the facade and the shared door to the offices above. We plan the shop's cameras inside its own walls and talk to the landlord before anything goes on the front, the sign band or the shared entrance, where the Landmarks count applies to the building as a whole if it sits inside the district.

Smith Street: late closings and back gardens

Smith Street's restaurants and bars close late and at different hours on different nights, and many have a garden or back room. An r/Brooklyn thread about break-ins in Brooklyn Heights and Cobble Hill included a resident's note that several restaurants around Columbia Street and the BQE had been robbed, one through a window at 3 a.m. That is the window a restaurant system has to watch: after the last staff member leaves and before the morning delivery. We cover the front door, the bar and register, the office where the safe or cash is kept, the back door and the garden, and we tie the after-hours alerts to the moment the system is set by the closing staff, not to a clock time that may be an hour too early on a Saturday.

Compliance notes for shops in Cobble Hill

Landmarks: a storefront inside the historic district falls under the same rule as a house, with the count and attachment rules applied to the building. Cast-iron storefront elements and decorative surfaces are off limits for mounts. Landlord and lease: the facade, awning and sign band usually belong to the landlord, and a lease may say who may fix anything to them. Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Cobble Hill Rowhouse, From the Tape Measure to the Back Fence

This describes how we work on these houses, not a story about any one customer. The first tool out of the bag on a Cobble Hill visit is a tape measure, because the width of the front decides how many cameras it may carry before anyone talks about models. We measure the frontage, count the storeys, and note the stoop, the band courses, the door hood, the areaway railing and anything carved, cast or decorative, because none of those can take a mount. Then we stand at the bottom of the stoop and look up at the parlor door the way a visitor would, and at the garden door the way someone stepping down into the areaway would. Those two lines of sight usually decide where the front pair go.

Inside, we go to the vestibule and look back at the street door, since a camera here is not counted on the facade and sees anyone who forces the door. Then the cellar: where the internet line enters, where the cable from the front can come through, whether the old wiring is live, and whether the cellar floods. Then the garden, the rear wall and the fence line, checking what the neighbors' windows look like from where a rear camera would sit, so the view can be masked before it is ever recorded. Last, we ask who will watch which cameras, and when the house is usually empty, because that sets the alert schedule. It is a slow walk, and it is the reason the quote rarely changes once we start.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Cobble Hill: Questions Answered

Doors, afternoons and the street entrance

The Baltic Street door was pried at 4:38 on a January afternoon, just as daylight was fading. How do cameras cope with the half hour between the day picture and the night picture?

They cope well if the changeover is set for the spot and checked at dusk, and badly if it is left at the factory setting. Most cameras decide when to drop from color to infrared by measuring the light, and a camera under a stoop or a door hood sees dusk earlier than the sidewalk does, so it may switch early, switch back as a car's lights pass, and record a flickering, smeared picture of exactly the minutes that mattered on Baltic Street. We set the switching point camera by camera, slow the switching so it does not flutter, raise the shutter speed so a person walking up is not blurred, and come back to the house at dusk to watch the change happen before we sign off. Where the doorway already has a light, keeping that light on a dusk-to-dawn sensor holds the camera in its sharper color mode for longer, which also helps a face come out in its real colors.

The 76th Precinct said a burglar it arrested called Cobble Hill an easy target because doors are left unlocked. If our budget covers either a better lock or another camera, which comes first?

The lock, nearly every time. A camera does not stop a door from opening; it tells you it opened and records who did it. If a door on the house does not lock reliably, or is often left unlocked by people coming and going, putting money into that door first prevents the loss, and the camera you add afterwards becomes the second line. Where the lock is sound, the next useful step is usually not another camera but a better alert from the ones you have, for example a notice to your phone when the garden door camera sees a person while the house is meant to be empty. We will tell you on the walk-through if we think a lock, a closer or a strike would do more than a camera, and we will not quote a camera to fill a gap a lock should close.

In one of the 2015 break-ins, thieves left a Hicks Street house with more than $10,000 in camera and digital equipment. How do we stop the recorder from leaving with everything else?

Put it where a burglar in a hurry will not find it, lock it, and keep a copy of the important moments somewhere else. A recorder on a shelf next to the television is the first box a thief grabs, and with it goes the video of their visit. On a rowhouse we usually put the recorder in a cellar closet, a utility cupboard or a locked cabinet on a floor the cameras cover, running on a small battery unit so it keeps recording if someone cuts the power. Then we set the system to send each alert clip to your phone as it happens, so the first few seconds of an intruder at the door are already off the premises even if the recorder is taken. For a household that wants more, an alert-only cloud copy can be added, and the quote says plainly whether that service carries a subscription.

Neighbors, households and what is inside

The 2013 Baltic Street video came from the building's own camera. If our camera records something that happens at the house next door, can we give the clip to our neighbors?

Yes, you can choose to share a clip you recorded on your own property, and on a row of attached houses it is often the most useful thing a camera does all year. Share the clip, not the login: export the minutes that matter as a video file and send that, rather than giving a neighbor access to your system, which would let them watch your door every day. Keep the original on your recorder or a USB stick until any police report is closed, because a detective may ask for a copy with its original time stamp, and write down the camera name and the time window so the clip can be found again. If the incident is serious, the better route is usually to give the clip to the 76th Precinct directly and let your neighbor know you have done so. And because your camera should be framed on your own stoop and doors, what it catches next door will usually be the edge of the frame, which is exactly why a clip from one house and a clip from the next are worth putting side by side.

In the 2015 and 2020 break-ins, the thieves went for laptops, jewelry and electronics inside the house. Should a camera watch the room where we keep valuables, and who in the household gets to see it?

An indoor camera in that room is worth having when the house is often empty in the day, as these afternoon cases suggest, but it should be set up so nobody feels watched at home. We put it high in a corner where it sees the door into the room and the place the valuables are kept, not the whole floor, and set it to record and alert only while the house is in away mode, so it is effectively off when you are home. The login for it goes to the adults who own the house, not to a tenant, a cleaner or a dog walker who has a login for the door cameras. If the room doubles as a guest room or a teenager's study, we would rather put the camera at the stair landing that leads to it, which records anyone heading that way without looking into the room itself.

Hicks Street and the expressway edge

Our house backs onto the BQE trench and the only spot for the rear camera is high on the back wall. Who keeps it clean and checks it, and how often?

Plan for it at the start, because a camera nobody can reach is a camera nobody maintains. We mount rear cameras on the trench side as low as the yard allows while still seeing faces, ideally where a short ladder from your own garden reaches them, and we choose a housing with a lip that sheds rain. Grime from the traffic below settles on everything, so we suggest wiping the dome or lens a few times a year with a soft cloth and plain water, and we show you how on the day of the install. The recorder can also send a daily snapshot or flag a camera whose picture has gone soft or dark, so you see the problem before you need the footage. If the camera has to be higher than you can safely reach, our service visit covers cleaning and a check of every view.

Shops on the three streets

The Atlantic Avenue shop that lost $3,400 in an afternoon had lost a sweater to one thief the day before. How can our staff quickly see yesterday's clip so they recognize a face if it comes back?

Make it a two-minute job instead of a project. We set up a staff login on the shop's phone or tablet that can play back the door camera by time and save a still, without access to settings or deletion. When something goes missing, the manager scrolls to the time, saves the clearest frame of the face from the door camera, and keeps it in the staff group for the next few shifts. The door camera is set at head height and close to the entrance precisely so that frame is usable. We would steer you away from posting those stills publicly; keep them among staff and give the full clip to the 76th Precinct if you report the theft.

After the 2024 convenience-store break-in on Atlantic Avenue, police said the group ran toward Hicks Street. Is a camera that looks along the sidewalk in the direction people leave worth one of our front positions?

Often, yes, if you have a position to spare and the landlord agrees. A door camera gives faces; a sidewalk view gives the direction of travel, the number of people, what they carried and sometimes a lookout standing apart from the group, which is what a detective needs to ask other businesses along the block for their video. It should be aimed along your own frontage toward the nearest corner, not across the street, and set wide enough to show bodies and clothing rather than faces. On a narrow shopfront inside the district, where the front allowance is small, a camera just inside the glass looking out along the sidewalk can sometimes do part of this job without using a facade position.

Our Smith Street restaurant closes at a different time every night. Can the cameras start alerting when the last person leaves instead of at a fixed hour?

Yes, and for a restaurant it is the better way. Instead of a schedule that switches alerts on at, say, 1 a.m., we tie the after-hours mode to an action the closing staff already take: setting the alarm keypad, pressing a button in the app, or locking the back door if it carries a contact. From that moment, a person on the bar camera, in the office or at the back door sends an alert with a clip to the owner and the manager. In the morning, the opening staff switch it back with the same action. The record shows exactly who set the system and when, which also settles the question of what time the last person really left.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Landmarks, Landlords, Neighbors and the State License: The Rules Behind a Cobble Hill Camera Job

Four sets of rules decide what a camera job in Cobble Hill may look like: the Landmarks rules for the outside of the building, the lease or the board for anything shared, privacy law for what the camera sees, and state licensing for who installs it. This is a plain-English summary of how they play out on the houses, walk-ups and shops in the neighborhood. It is not legal advice, and where a case is unclear we ask the owner, board or managing agent in writing before anything is ordered.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission. The LPC Permit Guidebook says that permits are needed for work that affects the exterior of a building inside a historic district, and the Commission's rule 2-20 lets its staff approve security cameras that meet a set of criteria rather than sending them to a public hearing. Those criteria are the ones described above: the count per twenty-five feet of frontage, attachment at mortar joints or plain masonry, a matching finish, minimal or no visible conduit, and a discreet position, with the secondary facade preferred where the primary facade is not necessary. Most applicants upload photographs and supporting documents through the Commission's Portico system. Interior work generally needs a Landmarks permit only where it affects the exterior or where the interior is itself designated, according to the Commission's permits page, so cameras inside a hall or a cellar are usually a matter for the owner and the board, not the Commission.

Owners, landlords and boards. In a rented apartment or shop, the outside of the building and the shared spaces belong to the owner. A tenant can usually put a camera inside their own home, aimed at their own rooms and the inside of their own door; anything in a hall, on the stoop or on the facade needs the owner's agreement. One r/AskNYC answer about package theft put it simply: check with the landlord before you put any camera up. In a condo or co-op, the board decides about common areas and the exterior.

Neighbors. New York's Civil Rights Law section 52-a gives a residential owner or tenant a claim against a neighbor who uses a camera to record them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy. Cobble Hill rear gardens sit side by side behind attached houses, often separated by nothing more than a fence, so every rear camera we install is framed on the owner's own garden, door and fence line, and any neighboring window or garden still in the picture is masked out in the camera's settings before recording starts.

Who may install. General Business Law section 69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. The Department of State's installer application adds that installing passive, non-detecting devices does not require licensure, so the line can look blurry for a simple camera. Most camera systems today detect people, send alerts and tie into alarms and door hardware, and we treat them as licensed work. We install under NYS license #12000287431, with insurance in place.

Police and registration. NYC 311 explains that businesses can register the locations of their security cameras with the NYPD at NewYorkCityConnect.org, so detectives know whom to ask for footage after an incident, and Connect New York states that registering a camera does not give the police any remote access to its video. Footage leaves your recorder only when you export it.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick, brownstone or a stoop is sealed against water, and every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in an apartment or mixed-use building gets rated firestop before we leave.

FAQ

Cobble Hill Security Camera FAQ

Who files the Landmarks application for front cameras on a Cobble Hill house, the owner or AESS?
+

The application is the owner's, because the permit is issued for the owner's building, but we prepare almost everything that goes into it. The LPC Permit Guidebook says most applicants upload photographs and supporting documents through the Commission's Portico system. We supply photographs of the front with each proposed camera position marked, the manufacturer's spec sheet with the camera's dimensions, a note on the finish, and a short description of how each camera is fixed at mortar joints and where its cable goes through the wall. If LPC staff ask for anything more, we answer it with you. The rear and interior work does not wait on the application, so it can be scheduled first.

How long should we expect Landmarks staff review to take before the front cameras can go up?
+

Plan on weeks, not days. One permit-expediting firm writes that in its experience LPC review takes a minimum of thirty days or longer depending on the project, and a complete application with clear photos and spec sheets is the best way to stay near the short end. Because staff can approve camera work that meets rule 2-20 without a public hearing, a modest front pair is usually among the simpler requests. We plan the job so the cellar runs, the rear and the inside-the-door camera are installed and recording while the application is in, and the front pair go up on a second short visit once approval arrives.

If Landmarks staff will not approve a camera where we wanted it, what are the fallback positions?
+

There are usually several, and the rule itself points to them. A camera that cannot sit beside the parlor door may be approved lower down, beneath a band course or under the stoop, where it is less visible. A camera wanted for the sidewalk can often be replaced by one just inside a window or the vestibule glass, which is inside the building rather than on the facade. And some coverage can move to the secondary facade or the rear, which the rule prefers anyway. When staff raise a concern, we revise the photographs and positions and resubmit with you, rather than mounting anything that has not been approved.

Do front cameras on a brownstone or a brick house have to match the color of the facade?
+

They should be finished so they do not draw attention. Rule 2-20 asks that equipment be finished to match the surrounding building material or not call undue attention to itself, and the LPC guidebook shows dome cameras finished to match the masonry around them. In practice we choose a small camera that comes in a neutral color close to the wall, and where none matches well, we finish the housing and mounting plate to match brownstone, brick or the black of painted ironwork using a paint suited to the plastic or metal, keeping the lens window and any sensors clear.

If our front can only carry two cameras, does choosing the 8MP C or D Series make up for the third camera we cannot have?
+

Partly. An 8MP camera records about four times the detail of a 2MP camera, so a single front camera with a slightly wider view can still give a usable face at the door and show a little more of the stoop and sidewalk. What it cannot do is look in two directions, so it does not replace a camera aimed somewhere else. On a Cobble Hill front we usually recommend spending on sharpness for the two front cameras and putting the money that would have bought a third into a rear or inside-the-door camera. The C Series starts at $1,292 on a DVR and the D Series at $1,292 as an IP system, against $850 for the 1080p A Series.

Is a camera with a built-in siren and flashing light a good idea on a block of attached houses?
+

On the front of a house, usually not; in a shop or a closed rear garden, sometimes. The F Series package, from $998, uses cameras that can flash a light and sound a siren when someone enters a zone. On a quiet residential block with neighbors a wall away, a siren set off by a cat, a delivery or a late visitor will make you unpopular quickly, and a flashing light at the front calls attention to equipment the Landmarks rule wants kept discreet. In a shop after closing, or in a rear garden that nobody should be in while the house is empty, a short warning can be useful. We set the deterrent to run only in away mode and only on a person, and to stay silent otherwise.

We are planning to redo the back garden next year. Can the system leave room for cameras we add then?

Yes, and it is much cheaper to plan for now. Our walk-up is five storeys with a twenty-five-foot front. Which Landmarks camera count applies to it?
+

Most likely the small-building count of two cameras for every twenty-five feet. Rule 2-20 applies that count to residential or commercial buildings of six storeys or less with a street frontage of forty feet or less, and the higher count of three per twenty-five feet to larger buildings. A five-storey building with a twenty-five-foot front falls within the first group. For a walk-up, that usually means one camera for the street door from outside, if any, and one for a ground-floor shop entrance or the cellar door, with the hall, mail area and stair covered from inside. It can be, with some care, and it is often still the best hiding place. If the cellar is truly wet or floods, we move the recorder to a closet on the ground floor or the garden level instead and run the cable a little further.
