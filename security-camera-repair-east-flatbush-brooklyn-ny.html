Why East Flatbush Properties Need Security Camera Repair

East Flatbush is the stretch of central Brooklyn covered by the NYPD's 67th Precinct, which describes its area as "East Flatbush and Remsen Village." It is mostly homes people own and live in: long rows of attached brick houses, freestanding and semi-detached two-families with a tenant upstairs, and small apartment houses on the side streets, with six-story elevator buildings and garden complexes along the bigger roads. Church Avenue, Utica Avenue, Linden Boulevard and the Nostrand Avenue edge carry the shops, bakeries, groceries, barbershops and storefront churches. Cameras are on a great many of these buildings already. What fails is rarely the decision to have them; it is the system quietly dying between the day it went up and the night it was needed.

A local example shows what a working system is worth. In October 2024, someone climbed into the garden at St. Therese of Lisieux Church on Avenue D and smashed the arm of a statue about a hundred years old. ABC7 New York reported that the church's surveillance footage showed the person entering the garden, climbing the statue, then covering a light with a backpack before using a brick. Father Liju Augustine had upgraded the church's security system when he arrived about a year earlier, and he said the video "laid out exactly what happened." Two details in that report matter for repair work. The garden gate is usually open around the clock, so the cameras were the only thing watching it at night. And the person went for the light first, which tells you what a night camera really depends on.

The second pressure is power. Just after 2:50 in the morning on January 14, 2026, overhead power wires caught fire on East 48th Street at Church Avenue. News 12 reported that Con Edison counted 76 customers without power and that residents of one building were evacuated because of high carbon monoxide readings. Nearby Flatbush had a manhole fire on Beverly Road in June 2025 that left at least 1,700 customers without power, and another near 650 Ocean Avenue in July 2026 that cut about 343. Camera systems seldom die in the dark. They are hurt when the power comes back unevenly, when a drive is writing at the moment it cuts out, or when a cheap adapter takes the surge. Many of the calls we get from this part of Brooklyn start the same way: everything came back on, except the cameras.

The third pressure is who is responsible. A lot of East Flatbush apartment buildings have changed hands, and some are badly run. In February 2025 News 12 walked into several buildings at Rutland Plaza on East 93rd Street without a key; one building there had 518 HPD complaints in two years, and HPD put it into its Alternative Enforcement Program. Resident Patricia Walters described "the sewage, the killing, everything the unlocked doors." In April 2026, tenants of a four-story building on Linden Boulevard told News 12 they could not reach a new landlord at all. In buildings like these, a broken camera is one problem among many, and no one person feels it is theirs to fix. Brick Underground has noted that city buildings "aren't required to have functioning security cameras at all," so a camera repair usually happens only when an owner, a managing agent, a church board or a tenant association decides it should.

Even large, professionally run systems slip. When the city's Department of Investigation tried to reach the CCTV cameras at 63 public housing senior buildings across New York in early 2025, it found that "44 of 63 of the Senior Buildings, approximately 70%, had CCTV cameras that could not be viewed remotely," citing "outdated or incompatible software or hardware causing login, connection, and data storage issues" along with damage. That is a citywide finding, not one about East Flatbush, but it describes the same three faults we find in private buildings here every week: the login is gone, the connection is broken, and the recording is not being saved.

So the job of a repair in East Flatbush is broader than swapping a dead camera. It is to get the system recording again, to make sure someone gets told the next time it stops, and to leave the owner with the logins, labels and backup power that keep it working through the next outage. Most row-house and two-family owners who call us for a repair also ask about the front door; a building with a camera at the entrance usually pairs it with a working intercom or access control, and we can look at both on the same trip.

Security Camera Repair for East Flatbush's Architecture

Six kinds of property make up nearly every camera repair call in East Flatbush. Each one breaks in its own way, and each one decides where the recorder ends up, how the cables run and who has to say yes before the work starts.

Attached brick row houses

The long rows on the side streets between Church Avenue and Linden Boulevard, usually with a recorder in the cellar and cables run up the back or the face of the brick. Faults cluster at the outside connectors and the cellar power strip.

Two- and three-family houses

Freestanding and semi-detached houses with an owner on one floor and tenants on the others. Cameras often run on one apartment's outlet or internet, and the questions are as much about who controls the system as what broke.

Small walk-ups

Four- to eight-family brick buildings with no super on site. Cameras cover the entrance, the vestibule and sometimes the yard, and they tend to go unchecked until something happens.

Six-story elevator buildings

The prewar and postwar apartment houses along the larger roads, with a recorder in the super's office or the cellar and long runs to the roof, the lobby and the halls. Building work and roof leaks are the usual causes of failure.

Garden complexes

Groups of buildings around courtyards, such as Flatbush Gardens and Rutland Plaza, where each building may have its own recorder or several are linked back to one management office.

New buildings

Recent construction such as Bethany Senior Terraces on East 40th Street and the Sparrow Square buildings on the former Kingsboro campus off Clarkson Avenue, where cameras are newer and often network-based, and the first faults are usually logins, firmware and handover gaps.

Attached row houses: the cellar is where most repairs start

On a typical East Flatbush row house the camera system was put in by one installer, often years ago, with the recorder on a shelf or table in the cellar near the electric meter and the internet modem. The cables come in through the cellar wall or a window frame, then climb the back of the house or the front facade to the cameras. The recorder is plugged into a power strip that also feeds the boiler controls, a freezer or a washing machine. When we arrive for a dark camera, we start there, because a loose plug, a dead strip or a surge-damaged adapter explains more row-house failures than any camera.

The outside half of the job is the brick. Older installs often have cables stapled along the mortar lines and connectors left bare under the camera. After a few summers facing south or west, the cable jacket cracks and water gets into the connector; after a few winters, the connector corrodes and the picture flickers, then disappears. The repair is usually a new connector in a weatherproof box or a short new run along the same path, not a new camera. When the cable is clipped neatly into the mortar joint and the box is sealed, the next repair is years away.

Two- and three-family houses: one system, two households

The two-family houses that line many East Flatbush blocks usually have one camera system serving the whole house. The questions that come up on a repair are practical. Whose outlet powers the cameras? Whose internet carries the phone app? Who gets the alerts? If the cameras are plugged into the upstairs tenant's outlet, they go dark when that apartment's power is off, and if the recorder runs through the tenant's router, phone viewing ends when the tenant moves out. We move power and network onto the owner's side where that is practical, so a change in one apartment does not take down the house.

The other recurring issue is inheritance. Many of these houses have passed from parents to children, and the person who set up the cameras, and knew the password, is no longer there. Getting a recorder back from a lost password is often possible on site, but some makers require proof of ownership or a reset that wipes settings. We tell you which route your recorder needs before anything is erased.

Walk-ups: the building nobody checks until something happens

The four- to eight-family brick walk-ups on East Flatbush side streets usually have a handful of cameras on the entrance, the vestibule, the stairs and the yard, wired to a recorder in the cellar or a locked hall closet. With no super living on site, the system is checked only when an owner visits or a tenant complains. That is why the most common walk-up repair call is not "the camera broke" but "we needed the footage and found out the camera had been dark for months."

The fix has two parts. First, the actual repair: the dead camera, the failed drive or the power supply. Second, a way to know next time. Most recorders can send an alert when a camera loses video or the drive reports an error, and we turn those on and send them to the owner's phone or email so the next failure is a message, not a surprise. For a building with frequent tenant changes, we also move the recorder somewhere only the owner or managing agent can reach.

Six-story elevator buildings: long runs and many hands

The six-story apartment houses along the bigger East Flatbush roads have longer runs from the cellar to the roof and the upper halls, and more people working in the building: roofers, plumbers, elevator contractors, cable and internet installers. Each can cut, unplug or bury a camera cable without knowing it. When a building's cameras fail one at a time over months, building work is usually the cause, and the repair starts with tracing each run from the recorder to its camera.

The other cause is water. Tenants of a four-story Linden Boulevard building told News 12 in April 2026 about collapsed ceilings and leaks. Water running down a wall or through a ceiling will find a camera, a junction box or a cable splice sooner or later. We repair the camera side once the leak is fixed, and we reroute or seal anything that sits where water travels.

Garden complexes: several buildings, one office

Complexes like Flatbush Gardens and Rutland Plaza are groups of buildings around courtyards and walkways, run from a management office. The cameras may be separate systems in each building or one network tied back to a central recorder through underground cable or rooftop wireless links. When all the cameras in one building disappear at once, the fault is usually in the link to that building, not the cameras. When one courtyard camera keeps dropping, the cause is often power or water in an outdoor box.

In complexes with management problems, the first question is who can authorize and pay for the work. We need a decision-maker, not just a tenant's permission, before touching a recorder that serves hundreds of apartments. Once that is clear, a whole-complex checkup with a written list of every camera's condition is usually the best first step.

New buildings: newer cameras, different faults

The newest East Flatbush buildings, such as Bethany Senior Terraces at 610 East 40th Street, which finished construction in 2026, and the Sparrow Square buildings rising on the former Kingsboro Psychiatric Center campus, use network cameras that rarely fail in the same ways as older coax systems. Their early problems are logins held by a contractor who has left, firmware that has not been updated, recorders set to keep only a few days, and cameras that were never added to the recorder at all. A repair in a new building often starts with documentation: what is installed, who has the passwords, and what each camera should be showing.

Field Notes: Working an East Flatbush Camera System From the Cellar Up

These notes describe how our technicians approach a typical East Flatbush row house, two-family or walk-up system when several cameras are out and nobody is sure why.

Save before diagnosing. If anyone needs footage from a recent night, the technician exports it first, before restarting or unplugging anything, and notes the recorder's clock against the real time.

Fault by Fault

Power, Weather, Light, Logins and Building Work: Why East Flatbush Camera Systems Fail

Five causes account for most of the camera failures we find on East Flatbush houses, buildings and storefronts. Knowing which one you are dealing with is the difference between a $150 visit and a new system.

Power: the outage is over, the damage is not

When power fails and returns, three things can happen to a camera system. The recorder may have been writing to its drive when the power dropped, which can corrupt the file system and leave it stuck on a setup screen or asking to format. A surge on return can kill adapters, power boxes or PoE switch ports, so some cameras come back and others do not. And settings stored in memory, including the clock and network address, can reset. The January 2026 wire fire at East 48th Street and Church Avenue and the manhole fires in nearby Flatbush in 2025 and 2026 are the kind of events that produce these calls. A battery backup on the recorder and switch, sized to ride through short outages and shut down cleanly in long ones, prevents most of them.

Weather: what years on a brick wall do to a camera

Outdoor cameras on East Flatbush row houses and two-families face sun, rain, snow and salt spray from the street. The camera bodies usually last; the connectors, cables and seals do not. Sun cracks the jacket of a cable stapled along the brick, rain gets into connectors left bare under the camera, and winter freezes the water inside. The picture flickers, then fails. Reolink's guide to video loss lists damaged or poorly routed cables and inadequate power among the top causes, and that matches what we see. Re-terminating a connector in a sealed box is a small repair on our Brooklyn list; a new camera is not needed until the camera itself has failed.

Light: a night camera is only as good as what it can see

Most cameras switch to infrared at night. When infrared fails, the night picture goes black; when the camera sits behind glass, the infrared bounces back and the picture goes white; when a camera relies on a porch light or a garden lamp, anyone who covers or switches off that light leaves it blind. The St. Therese video on Avenue D showed a person covering a light before striking, which is exactly the weakness to check. A repair here may mean replacing the infrared, moving the camera from behind glass, adding a camera with its own light, or putting the outside light on a circuit that cannot be switched from the street.

Logins: when the person who set it up is gone

A large share of East Flatbush repair calls are not hardware at all. The camera works and the recorder records, but nobody can see it, because the app account belongs to a relative, a former tenant, a former manager or an installer who has closed. The city's investigators found the same kind of login and connection failures behind most of the public housing senior buildings whose cameras could not be viewed remotely. The fix is to recover the recorder, set new passwords, put the account in the owner's name and write down who holds it.

Building work: the camera cable nobody saw

Roofers, pointing crews, window installers, plumbers and internet technicians all work where camera cables run. On East Flatbush row houses and apartment buildings, a camera that died the same week as a new roof, new windows or a new internet line was almost always cut, unplugged or buried by that work. We trace the run, repair or replace the damaged section, and tell you who was working there so you can raise it with them.

What a Church Avenue or Utica Avenue shop needs from a repair

Shop owners usually call after something has already happened: a robbery, a shoplifting pattern, a break-in, an argument at the counter or a slip and fall. The first need is the footage, saved and exported correctly. The second is to find out why a camera was dark, blurry or pointed at the wrong thing. The third is to make sure the cameras that matter most work every day: the register, the front door, the stock room and the back door. A recorder under the counter where anyone can unplug it, or in a back room where steam and grease from the kitchen reach it, is often part of the problem. We move it somewhere protected, put it on battery backup and set it to alert the owner if a camera drops.

For late-night counters like the Church Avenue deli robbed in 2024, the camera at the register has to show a face clearly under the store's own lighting, and the camera at the door has to catch people coming in, not the backs of their heads going out. Many repairs on these counters are re-aims and lens or setting changes rather than new cameras. We check every camera at the hours the store is actually busiest and darkest.

Barbershops, salons and offices

Night pictures and the light around a camera

Someone threw a bag over the light beside our church garden before vandalizing it, as happened at St. Therese on Avenue D. If the light near our camera is blocked, does the camera still record anything useful?

It depends on whether the camera can see without that light. A camera with working infrared keeps recording in black and white when a nearby lamp is covered; a camera whose infrared has failed, or that was set to rely on the lamp, goes nearly blind. On a repair we test each outdoor camera with the lights off, replace failed infrared, and where a garden or lot is large, add a camera with its own light mounted out of reach. Footage of the moment someone reaches for the light is often the most useful clip of all, so the camera should cover the light itself.

Our camera sits inside the front window of our row house looking out at the stoop, and at night it shows only a white glare. Is the camera broken?

Usually not. The camera's infrared lights bounce off the glass and flood the lens, so the picture goes white the moment it switches to night mode. The repair is to turn off the camera's infrared and rely on the street and porch lighting, press the lens flat against the glass with a shield, or move the camera outside in a weatherproof housing. Which one is right depends on how much light reaches the stoop at night, which we check after dark before choosing.

The camera in our barbershop on Church Avenue faces the mirrors, and the picture is full of reflections and bright spots. Can it be fixed without losing the view of the door?

Yes, in most shops. Mirrors and ring lights confuse a camera's exposure, and a camera aimed down the length of the shop picks up every reflection. We re-aim the camera so it sees the door and chairs at an angle to the mirrors, adjust exposure and backlight settings, and if needed add a second small camera just for the door. Most of that is labor and settings rather than new equipment.

Power, wiring and the block

Our block is fed by overhead wires like the ones that caught fire at East 48th Street and Church Avenue in January. Does that make our camera system more likely to be damaged?

It makes outages and surges more likely to reach it, since lines on poles are exposed to weather, branches and fire. The camera system is hurt less by the outage itself than by the moment power returns. A good battery backup on the recorder and the switch or power box, plus surge protection on the outlet, protects against most of that. If your system has already come back wrong after an outage, the repair usually starts with the power supply and the recorder's drive.

The cables for our cameras are stapled along the outside of the brick, and several have cracked in the sun. Do we have to redo the whole house?

Usually not. Sun-cracked cable lets water into the connectors, so the cameras at the end of those runs fail first. We replace the damaged runs along the same route, clip them into the mortar joints, and put every outdoor connector in a sealed box. Runs that are still sound stay. The written price lists each run so you can choose which to do now.

The little light on our camera is on, but the recorder shows no picture from it. What does that mean?

It means the camera has power but the video is not reaching the recorder, or the recorder is not accepting it. Common causes are a damaged cable or connector, a recorder set to the wrong channel or format, an IP address conflict on a network system, or a camera whose video board has failed while its power light still works. We test signal at the camera and at the recorder to tell which, so you pay to fix only the failed part.

Two-families and inherited systems

The recorder's maker wants proof of purchase before it will reset the password, and we never had a receipt. What else can we use?

Makers vary. Some accept the recorder's serial number with a code from its screen, some accept a deed or utility bill showing the house is yours, and some offer a reset that clears settings but keeps the recorder usable. We check what your recorder's maker allows, try the routes that keep your settings and footage first, and tell you before anything is erased. If the footage on the drive matters, we copy it off before any reset.

We have more cameras on our two-family than our recorder has inputs, so two were never connected. Can that be fixed without a new recorder?

Sometimes. If the recorder is a network model, a small switch can add cameras up to its license limit. If it is a coax recorder with all its inputs used, the choices are a larger recorder or moving the extra cameras onto a small second recorder. We count what the recorder supports, check the unconnected cameras still work, and give you both prices.

Apartment buildings and complexes

Not directly. Brick Underground has noted that city buildings are not required to have working security cameras at all, so a dead camera is not usually an HPD violation on its own. Camera power is low voltage, so the shock risk is small, but the water will ruin the camera, its connector and possibly the power supply it shares with others. Footage, police and security

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in East Flatbush

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers, help forums and national repair guides. Each one is partly true somewhere, and each one leads East Flatbush owners to the wrong repair or a lost clip.

"If the light on the camera is on, the camera is working"

Many quick answers treat a glowing status light or infrared ring as proof the camera is fine and point the owner at the recorder or the app. Reolink's own guide lists a case where there is no video even though the camera's lights are on.

A power light only shows that power is reaching the camera. The video can still be lost in a wet connector, a cracked cable along the brick, a wrong channel setting on the recorder, an address conflict on a network system, or a failed board inside the camera. On East Flatbush row houses, the connector at the camera end is the most common culprit.

The right test is signal at both ends: at the camera and at the recorder. That tells us which part failed and keeps you from paying for a new camera when the problem was a connector worth a fraction of the price.

"Unplug the recorder and plug it back in; that fixes most problems"

A restart is the first suggestion in almost every troubleshooting answer, and sometimes it does bring a frozen recorder back.

After an incident it is the wrong first step. A recorder with a failing drive may not come back from a restart at all, and one that does may write over the period you need sooner than you expect. A restart also clears the evidence of what failed, such as error logs or which camera dropped first. After a robbery on Church Avenue or a break-in on a side street, the footage is worth more than the convenience of a quick reset.

Save first, then diagnose. If you need a clip, leave the recorder running and call us; we export the footage with its timestamps, and only then restart, test or replace anything.

"Power outages don't damage camera systems; everything comes back when the lights do"

Answers about outages tend to assume the system simply waits in the dark and resumes.

East Flatbush has seen what happens instead. After events like the January 2026 wire fire at East 48th Street and Church Avenue, the calls we get are about recorders stuck on a setup screen, drives that will not mount, cameras that never came back because their switch port or adapter died, and clocks set back years. The outage did not cause most of that; the moment the power returned did.

A battery backup sized for the recorder and switch, surge protection on the outlet, and a recorder set to come back up cleanly prevent most of these failures. If yours did not come back, the repair usually starts with power and the drive, not the cameras.

"Once someone covers a camera, it's useless"

Some answers treat a covered or spray-painted camera as a total loss and suggest hiding cameras or buying many more.

The St. Therese video on Avenue D shows why that is too simple. The footage captured the person entering the garden and climbing the statue before they covered a light, and the priest said it "laid out exactly what happened." Covering a camera or a light takes time and usually happens in view of the camera itself. What matters is that the camera can see the person approaching it, that its night picture does not depend on a light within their reach, and that the owner is told when a camera stops.

A repair can add all three: re-aiming so cameras cover each other and the lights, fixing infrared so night views do not depend on a lamp, and turning on alerts for lost video. That is cheaper than doubling the number of cameras.

"Apartment complexes and public housing keep their cameras on a maintenance schedule"

AI answers about building cameras often assume that large landlords and housing agencies check their cameras routinely, so tenants can count on them.

The city's own investigators found otherwise. In early 2025 the Department of Investigation tried to reach the CCTV cameras at 63 public housing senior buildings and found about 70 percent had cameras that could not be viewed remotely, citing login, connection and data storage problems as well as damage. At an April 2025 City Council hearing, NYCHA said it aims to fix a reported camera within 24 to 72 hours, which depends on someone reporting it. Private complexes in East Flatbush have their own management problems; News 12 found unlocked doors and hundreds of HPD complaints at Rutland Plaza.

If you manage or own a building, the only schedule that counts is the one you set: a checkup at least once a year, alerts on lost video and drive errors, and a written list of every camera. If you are a tenant, put problems with building cameras in writing to management and keep a copy.

"Wireless cameras avoid the repairs wired systems need"

Some answers suggest replacing a broken wired system with Wi-Fi cameras to avoid cable problems for good.

In East Flatbush brick row houses and walk-ups, Wi-Fi brings its own repairs. Signal through brick and between floors is weak, routers get changed by tenants or internet providers, battery cameras drain faster in cold weather, and each camera depends on an app account that someone has to own. Every wireless camera still needs power from somewhere, so the outlet and adapter can fail just like a wired system's.

For most row houses and buildings, repairing the existing wired system is cheaper and steadier. Wi-Fi cameras make sense for a spot where cable truly cannot go, set up on the owner's network with the account in the owner's name.

"Buying the replacement part online is always the cheapest repair"

Many answers tell owners to order a replacement camera or recorder online and have someone fit it.

Sometimes that works, and we install customer-supplied equipment at labor-only rates. But a camera chosen by model number alone may not match the recorder's format or resolution, a recorder may not support the existing cameras, and a part bought online carries only whatever warranty its seller offers. The dead camera may also not be the fault at all; a connector or adapter may be.

Find the cause first. The $150 service call tells you exactly what failed and what will work with your system, and the written price lets you compare our part against one you buy yourself. East Flatbush Security Camera Repair FAQ

A technician told us our recorder has an ordinary desktop hard drive in it. Is that why it keeps failing?
+

It may be. Recorders write video around the clock, and drives built for desktop computers are not designed for that steady load, so they tend to wear out sooner and fail without warning. A drive made for surveillance recording is the right replacement. We read the drive's health on the visit, copy any footage you need, and replace it with the right type and size for how many days you want to keep.

After a break-in on our street, several neighbors found their cameras were not recording. Can one technician check several houses on the same block in one trip?
+

Yes. Each house is its own service call and gets its own written price, but booking them together lets one technician go door to door in the same arrival window instead of making separate trips. Each owner approves and pays for their own repair, and each system's logins stay with that household.

Every night at about the same hour, all of our cameras drop out for a few minutes. Is someone switching them off?
+

Usually not. Many recorders have an automatic maintenance restart set for the early morning, and some routers and power timers do the same. The recorder's log shows whether it restarted on its own. We check the log, move or turn off the scheduled restart if it falls during hours you care about, and look for a timer or power strip that might be cutting power on a schedule.

Our shop's 360-degree ceiling camera shows a stretched circle on the phone instead of a normal picture. Is it broken?
+

Usually it is a display setting. Fisheye cameras send a round image that the recorder or app has to flatten into normal views, and that setting can be lost after an update, a reset or a new phone. We restore the flattened views on the recorder and in the app, and set up separate views for the register, the door and the aisles.

Our cameras back up clips to the cloud, but the uploads stop every evening. Why?
+

Most often the home or shop internet connection is busiest in the evening and cannot carry several camera uploads at once, or the cameras record at a size too large to send in time. We check the upload speed and the camera settings, set the cloud copy to a smaller stream or to motion clips only, and keep the full recording on the local recorder.

We run a home daycare. Can the repair be done while children are in the house?
+

Yes, with planning. Tell us when you book so we can choose the quietest part of your day, keep ladders and tools out of the play areas, and do the cellar and outside work first. If a camera inside a play room needs work, we can do that part when the children are out or napping in another room.

Our complex links the cameras in its buildings with small rooftop wireless antennas, and one link keeps dropping. Can that be repaired?
+

Yes. Point-to-point wireless links fail when an antenna is knocked out of line, when a new obstruction such as a tree or a rooftop unit blocks the path, when the power at one end fails, or when the radio wears out. We check the alignment, the signal and the power at both ends, and replace the radios or re-aim them so the link holds in all weather.

Can the system send an alert the moment someone covers or blocks a camera?
+

Many recorders and cameras have a tamper or video blind setting that sends an alert when a lens is covered or the picture goes dark. We check whether your equipment supports it, set it so ordinary night changes do not trigger it, and send the alert to the phone or email of the person who should respond.

Motion alerts stopped arriving on our phones after a phone update, though the cameras still record. Is that a repair?
+

It is usually a settings fix that falls within the service call. Phone updates can turn off notifications for the camera app, sign the app out, or require a newer app version. We check the phone, the app and the recorder's alert settings together, and test an alert before we leave.

One of our cameras is hanging by its cable after a windstorm. Should we pull it down or leave it?
+

If it is hanging over a sidewalk, a stoop or a doorway where it could fall on someone, unplug its power at the recorder or power box and take a photo, then leave it if you cannot reach it safely. Do not pull it by the cable, which can damage the run inside the wall. We remount it on a sound bracket or replace it, and check the cable and connector it was hanging from.

Our recorder says there is an IP address conflict, and one network camera keeps disappearing. What causes that?
+

Two devices on the same network have been given the same address, often after a new router or a new device such as a printer or game console was added. The camera and the other device keep knocking each other off. We give each camera a fixed address outside the router's automatic range, or move the cameras onto the recorder's own network port, so the conflict cannot come back.
