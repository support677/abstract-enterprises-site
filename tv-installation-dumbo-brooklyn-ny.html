The Neighborhood

Why Dumbo Properties Need TV Installation

Dumbo is small. Wikipedia counts 4,975 residents in the 2020 census, in ZIP 11201 inside Community Board 2, while the DUMBO Improvement District, using its own wider boundary, puts the 2023 figure at about 10,200 people in 3,338 homes. What makes it unusual for a TV installer is not the size but the buildings. Almost everything here was built to make or store something: cardboard boxes, coffee, sugar, machinery, paint, soap pads. When the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the DUMBO Historic District on December 18, 2007, its report described three ways those buildings were put together. The oldest warehouses use slow-burning mill construction, with massive wooden columns, beams and joists inside brick walls. Some later buildings use a steel frame with terra-cotta tile floors. And the Gair company's factories were among the earliest large-scale reinforced-concrete factory buildings erected in the United States. A TV bracket has to be fixed into one of those three things, or into a partition that someone added inside them when the building became homes.

The conversions came in waves. The LPC report notes that artists moved into the empty factories from the late 1970s, often illegally, and that 1 Main Street, the Clocktower, became one of the earliest luxury residential conversions in the neighborhood in 1998. The Improvement District's building guide adds that the Eagle Warehouse at 28 Old Fulton Street was converted to condominiums as early as 1980, that 81–89 Washington Street and 135 Plymouth Street are now loft apartments, and that the 1950 Brillo factory at 200 Water Street is residential. CityRealty records that 70 Washington Street, built in 1910 and 1911, was converted in 2005 into 259 residences. Each conversion team made its own choices about what to keep exposed and what to cover, so two apartments of the same age a block apart can have completely different TV walls: one with raw concrete you can see form marks in, another with brick painted white, another with a smooth new partition hiding all of it.

Some of those interiors are dramatic, and drama is hard on a TV plan. A Compass listing team describes many Clocktower units as having concrete ceilings and mushroom-capped columns, deep multi-pane window bays set between concrete piers, and ceiling heights reported from 15 feet up to as much as 50 feet in the clock room. Rooms like that have very little ordinary wall. The windows take the long sides, the piers between them are narrow, the columns stand in the middle of the floor, and the one solid wall may be shared with a corridor or a neighbor. Ceiling height pulls people toward hanging the screen high, which is usually a mistake for comfortable viewing. Big glass brings glare at certain hours. And the heavy materials that make these buildings feel solid are the same ones that refuse to hide a cable. Deciding where the screen goes in a Dumbo loft is often a bigger part of the job than mounting it.

The working population is as important as the residents. The Improvement District counts 11,266 employees in 2022, more than 1,000 creative and tech companies, 6 million square feet of commercial space, more than 125 retail storefronts, more than 20 galleries and 152 artist studios. Wikipedia calls Dumbo the center of the Brooklyn Tech Triangle. In practice that means a large share of the screens we are asked about here are not living-room TVs at all: they are meeting-room displays for video calls, lobby screens, menu and sports TVs in restaurants on Front and Water Streets, and monitors in studios. Office buildings such as 45 Main Street advertise tech conference rooms, and the Dumbo Heights campus at 81 Prospect, 117 Adams, 77 Sands, 77 Prospect and 175 Pearl is built around offices and coworking. A display in one of those rooms needs the same care on the wall as a home TV, plus a connection to the network, a way for a laptop to reach it, and a landlord's approval before anyone drills.

Finally there is the street. Dumbo sits under and between two bridges, and the subway trains crossing the Manhattan Bridge are part of daily life; the MTA's 2023 noise report logged 503 trains passing during its measurements at a 68 Jay Street residence, with peaks above 90 decibels. Brooklyn Paper noted back in 2008 that new and renovated buildings here routinely install double-paned windows, and residents on Reddit describe the trains as audible but not shaking anything. For a TV that matters less for the mount than for the sound: a room that has to cut through train noise needs its soundbar set up with that in mind. The streets also changed recently. NYC DOT announced in November 2025 that a $108 million project had restored 26 blocks of Belgian block paving and the archway under the bridge, and the district's cobblestones, loading rules and frequent film shoots all affect where a van can stop. We plan the visit around them.

Same-Day Repair in Dumbo

Need Repair in Dumbo? A Screen Sagging Off a Concrete Wall, a Bracket Loose in Old Brick, or a Display Left Behind by the Last Tenant

The urgent calls we get from converted industrial buildings tend to look alike. The first is a screen that has started to sit lower on one side or lean forward, and in a concrete loft that almost always means the anchors were never right for the material. Somebody used plastic plugs, or drilled a hole slightly too large because the bit wandered on hard aggregate, and the anchor has been working loose every time the TV is pulled out on its arm. The second is a bracket on warehouse brick that was fixed into the mortar joints instead of the brick itself. Mortar in a building from the nineteenth century is often soft and sandy, and it crumbles around a fixing over time. The third is less dramatic but common in an office neighborhood: a company moves out of a suite and leaves screens, brackets and dangling cables on every wall, and the next tenant or the landlord needs them gone before the space can be shown.

If a screen is leaning, do not try to push it back up. If two people can lift it safely, take the weight off the bracket, unplug it and call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same screen goes back up on a properly anchored bracket on the same visit, it is priced as a new mount on that wall, with the take-down added. Old holes in concrete or brick that sit too close to where the new anchors need to go are a real constraint, because a new anchor next to a broken-out hole has less solid material around it, so we often shift the bracket a few inches and cover the old holes behind the new plate. If we find that the wall itself is the problem, such as a crumbling brick, a crack in old concrete or a timber that has split along its grain, we say so plainly and stop, so the building's engineer, the landlord or a mason can look at it. A TV can wait a week. A wall should not be asked to carry weight it cannot hold.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Dumbo's Architecture

Six kinds of building cover nearly every Dumbo TV call we get. What separates them is not the brand of TV or the size of the room, but three questions: what the solid walls are made of, what was added inside them during the conversion, and who in the building has a say before anything is fixed to a wall.

The Gair concrete factory

Early twentieth-century reinforced concrete: piers, deep window bays, concrete ceilings and, in many Clocktower units, mushroom-capped columns. Mostly condos and offices now.

The brick-and-timber warehouse

Nineteenth-century mill construction along the water: thick brick outer walls, heavy wooden posts and beams, wide plank floors. Lofts, offices, venues and shops.

The steel-frame loft

Buildings such as 68 Jay Street, which the Improvement District describes as brick with a steel frame and reinforced-concrete floors. Office floors and loft apartments.

The mid-century factory

Later industrial buildings like the 1950 Brillo factory at 200 Water Street, now residential, with different concrete and newer partitions than the early lofts.

The small early building

Pre-industrial and Civil War-era buildings on Old Fulton and Front Streets, some among the oldest commercial buildings in the city, often with restaurants below.

The newer tower

Towers in and beside the district, such as 60 Water Street and Front & York at 85 Jay Street: concrete structure, drywall partitions, glass walls and a management office.

The Gair concrete factory: piers, bays and a wall that may hide steel

The concrete buildings around Washington and Main Streets are what most people picture when they think of a Dumbo loft. Robert Gair built them for his box business, and the LPC report counts them among the earliest large reinforced-concrete factories in the country. Inside, the structure is a grid of concrete columns and floor slabs, with the outer walls largely given over to windows set between concrete piers. That grid is what makes the rooms feel so open, and it is also what makes a TV plan tricky. The surfaces that will carry a heavy screen are the piers, the columns and any solid concrete wall, and every one of them contains steel reinforcing bars. The bars are what the concrete hangs on; hitting one with a hammer drill does not usually damage the building, but it stops the hole dead, and you should never cut through a bar to make a bracket fit.

Our method in a concrete room is to scan, mark and drill slowly. A scanner set for concrete gives a reasonable idea of where the bars run, though old concrete with dense aggregate can confuse it. We mark the bracket's fixing points, check them against the scan, and drill the first hole with a small bit. If the bit stops on steel, the bracket moves a little, which is why we prefer brackets with slotted wall plates that allow a few inches of adjustment. The anchor is sized for the material and the load: a sleeve or wedge anchor rated for concrete, set to the depth its maker specifies, never a plastic plug. On our list, concrete is not one of the printed add-on lines, so we confirm the concrete charge from your photos and put it in writing before you book. You will not find out about it at the door.

The brick-and-timber warehouse: thick walls, heavy wood, soft mortar

The older warehouses toward the water were built for storage, not for show. The Improvement District describes 45 Water Street, the 1870 tobacco inspection warehouse that now houses St. Ann's Warehouse, as brick and timber, and the seven Empire Stores buildings along Water Street as brick warehouses from 1870 and 1885. In buildings of this kind the outer walls are thick, load-bearing brick, and the inside is a forest of heavy wooden posts holding heavy wooden beams. For a TV, the brick is the easier half. Solid old brick holds a bracket very well, provided the anchor goes into the brick and not into the mortar between bricks, which in a building this old can be soft enough to scratch with a key. We set fixings in the faces of whole bricks, keep them away from the edges, and on soft brick use more anchors at lower load each. Brick is +$65 on our list, and we tell you before booking which line applies if the brick has been plastered over.

The timber is a different question. Many people in these lofts want the TV on a post, because a post faces the seating area and the brick walls are taken by windows. A sound old-growth post is very strong, and structural screws or lag bolts into it hold well. But old posts often have deep drying cracks along the grain, old bolt holes from machinery, and in some buildings a fire-retardant coating or paint that should not be stripped. We look at the post closely, avoid the cracks, keep the screws well in from the edges and use a bracket whose plate fits the post's width. If the post is narrower than the bracket needs, we say so rather than hanging a 75-inch screen off a plate with half its fixings in the air. Cables on a post have nowhere to hide, so they run in a paintable cord cover or drop behind a console.

The steel-frame loft: brick outside, steel inside, concrete underfoot

Between the timber warehouses and the concrete factories came buildings with a steel skeleton. The LPC report names steel frames with terra-cotta floor tile as one of the district's three construction types, and the Improvement District lists 68 Jay Street, built in 1915 as a tea warehouse, as brick with a steel frame and reinforced-concrete floors. In a building like that, the outer walls are brick, the columns are steel, often boxed in with masonry or fireproofing, and the ceilings may show the arched terra-cotta tiles between steel beams. We do not fix TVs into structural steel or into fireproofing. A boxed steel column is not a TV wall, however convenient it looks. The usual answers are the brick outer wall, a conversion partition, or a floor-standing solution.

These are also the buildings where offices are most common, and office fit-outs leave partitions of light-gauge steel studs and drywall. Steel studs are thin, so a lag bolt that would bite into wood just strips. The right fixing is a toggle rated for the load, spread across at least two studs, and for anything on a full-motion arm, a backer behind or over the drywall. Because a partition is hollow, it is the one wall in these buildings where an in-wall power and cable kit can hide everything, provided the landlord allows the opening.

The mid-century factory: later concrete, newer partitions

Not everything in Dumbo is a century old. The Brillo factory at 200 Water Street dates from 1950, and buildings of that era use concrete in different ways from the 1900s factories, with different block, larger openings and, after conversion, far more drywall. In apartments in later buildings like this, the TV wall is often a framed partition rather than structure, which makes the job closer to an ordinary Brooklyn mount: drywall on studs at the base price, with an in-wall kit for hidden power. The exterior walls and any shafts are still masonry or concrete, and we find out which is which before choosing. It is also common for a conversion to put drywall directly over an original brick or concrete wall on furring strips, which leaves a narrow gap between the board and the masonry. That gap is too shallow for most in-wall kits, and a fixing has to go through the board and the gap into the masonry behind, so the anchor has to be long enough to reach.

The small early building: the oldest walls in the neighborhood

Around Old Fulton Street and the bottom of Front Street are buildings far older than the factories. The Improvement District's guide dates 5–7 Front Street to 1834 and calls it the oldest office building in New York City, gives 1 Old Fulton Street a date of 1835, and lists 8 Old Fulton Street, from 1860 and 1861, as apartments. Many of these buildings have restaurants or shops on the ground floor and homes or offices above. Their walls are often brick with plaster on the inside, and the interiors have been redone many times, so the wall behind your TV may be original plaster on brick, a later plaster skim, or drywall. We test before we drill. Because these buildings predate 1978 by more than a century, any work that would disturb a significant area of old paint falls under the lead-safe rules HPD publishes for older housing; a few bracket holes do not reach that threshold, but cutting openings for an in-wall kit in a rental apartment is the kind of work where we ask about the building's lead records first.

The newer tower: glass, drywall and a management office

Dumbo's towers are a different world. Wikipedia describes 60 Water Street as a 17-story mixed-use building, and Front & York at 85 Jay Street is one of the large newer residential complexes at the district's edge. Buildings of this generation are concrete structures with drywall partitions, and in many apartments the living room has a wall of glass on one or two sides. The TV usually goes on the one long partition, which may be shared with a corridor or a neighbor. Drywall on metal studs is the usual surprise here, and so is a concrete column or shear wall buried behind the drywall exactly where the bracket wants to go. We scan for both. Towers also bring a management office that sets contractor hours, wants a certificate of insurance and may have rules about opening walls. A paintable cord cover needs only a few small screws; an in-wall kit opens the drywall in two places, which most offices accept when it is installed to code but some want to approve first.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The First Fifteen Minutes in a Dumbo Loft With a Concrete Wall and a Wall of Windows

These notes describe how our technicians approach a converted industrial apartment or office. They are our practice, not a story about any particular address or customer.

Find the light first. Before the scanner comes out, the technician stands where the customer will sit and looks at the windows. In a loft with large windows, the wall the customer picked on a cloudy morning can be washed out in the late afternoon. If the screen will face that light, we raise it before any hole is drilled, because the fix for glare is placement, and placement is free until the anchors go in.

Name the material. Next comes the wall itself. Concrete in these buildings is often painted, and the paint can hide whether a surface is poured concrete, block, brick with a skim coat, or a partition. A knock, a look at the corners and a scan usually settle it, and a single small test hole where the bracket plate will cover it confirms it. Grey dust that comes slowly is concrete; red dust is brick; a quick drop through a thin board means a partition; pale wood shavings mean a timber or a furring strip.

Decide where the cables will go before the bracket goes up. On concrete or brick, there is no cavity to fish cables through. The choice is a cord cover painted to match, a console directly below, or moving the screen to a partition. Settling that first avoids the classic mistake of a perfect mount with a tangle of cables hanging under it.

Tape, sit, then drill once. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen, the customer sits down, and only then do we drill, in one short burst so the noise is over quickly for neighbors on the other side of thick but sound-carrying walls.

Wall by Wall

Concrete, Brick, Timber or Partition: What Holds a TV in a Dumbo Loft and How the Wires Get Hidden

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on a flat or tilting bracket on drywall over wood studs, labor only. In a Dumbo loft that exact wall is the exception, so the material behind the paint decides most of the plan. Each add-on exists because that material needs different bits, different anchors and more time, not because an old building is an excuse to charge more.

Poured concrete (confirmed in writing before booking). The walls, piers and columns of the early concrete factories are dense, often made with coarse aggregate, and full of steel. Drilling is slow and loud, with a hammer drill and carbide bits, and the anchor has to be a type rated for concrete and set to the depth its manufacturer specifies. Concrete is the one material in Dumbo where we expect to move a bracket an inch or two after the first hole, because a bar sits where the plate wanted to go. Concrete is not printed as an add-on line on our list, so we price it from your photos and put that price in writing before you book.

Solid brick or stone (+$65). The outer walls of the warehouses and steel-frame lofts, and many party walls, are solid brick, sometimes exposed, sometimes painted, sometimes under plaster. Brick holds a TV very well if the anchor goes into a whole brick. Old lime mortar is weaker and should never carry the load, so we place fixings by the brick pattern, which on a painted wall means finding the courses first. On softer brick we use more anchors so each one carries less.

Plaster (+$45). In the older small buildings and some conversions, plaster sits on brick, on metal lath or on wood lath. Plaster is strong when pressed and weak when pulled. Once a test hole tells us what is behind the plaster, we choose the fixing for that material and tell you which line applies before we continue.

Hiding the wires when the wall is solid

"Make the wires disappear" is the second request on almost every call, and in Dumbo it is harder than the mount. There is a right way and a wrong way. The wrong way is to push the TV's own power cord through one hole in the wall and out of another. Mike Holt's summary of the National Electrical Code for EC&M is blunt: flexible cords "are not permitted to be run through holes in walls" and "are not permitted to be concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." A cord hidden inside a wall can be crushed, can overheat and cannot be inspected. The right way, on a hollow framed wall, is an in-wall power and cable kit, which puts a recessed power inlet low on the wall, carries power inside the wall in its own rated cable, and gives a recessed outlet behind the TV with a separate pass-through for HDMI and network cables. On our list it is $169 including the kit.

The difficulty is that an in-wall kit needs a hollow cavity running from behind the TV down to the outlet, and concrete, brick and timber have none. A contributor on a StreetEasy Talk thread about mounting a TV in a prewar building described exactly this, a "cement-like wall" that could not be fished, and another poster said a cord cover is "not ideal but it isn't bad" and can be painted. That matches our experience. On masonry the honest options are a paintable cord cover ($59), run straight and tight and painted to match; a console directly under the screen so the cables drop out of sight behind it; or moving the TV to a partition wall where an in-wall kit is possible. Cutting a channel into original concrete or brick and plastering over it is a mason's job, not a TV installer's, and in a condo it is almost certainly something the board will want to approve.

On exposed brick, a cord cover is more visible than on painted concrete, because it crosses the courses. Two things help. One is placing the screen so the cover runs along a mortar line or the edge of a pier rather than across the middle of the brick. The other is choosing a low console and mounting the TV just above it, so the cable run is a few inches long instead of a few feet. We sketch both with tape before deciding.

Boards, Landlords and Landmarks

Who Signs Off on a TV Wall in Dumbo: Condo Boards, Rental Managers, Coworking Operators and the Landmarks Commission

Most Dumbo TV jobs involve at least one person besides the customer who has a say about the wall. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. What follows is what the public guidance says and what we ask before booking, so you can ask the right question first.

Condo owners: read the alteration agreement. Many condo and co-op buildings require an alteration agreement for work inside apartments. Brick Underground's guide to these agreements says they exist to protect building-wide structures and typically prohibit tampering with things like load-bearing walls; the sample it describes requires contractors to carry general liability insurance of at least $1 million; and agreements may also dictate the days and hours contractors can work. In a converted factory, the concrete piers, columns and outer walls are exactly the kind of building-wide structure those clauses protect. A few anchor holes for a bracket are usually treated as minor, but some boards want to know about any drilling into structure, and opening a wall for an in-wall kit can be treated as an alteration. Ask the managing agent which applies, send us their requirements, and we will send the certificate of insurance and a short description of the work before the visit.

Renters: the lease decides. In Brick Underground's "Realty Bites" column, a New York real estate attorney said the first place to look is the lease's alteration provision, and that a ban on wall hanging that is not in the lease would not be enforceable. A property manager in the same piece said a nail-size hole is fine but a larger one is considered damage, and that a tenant who removes a mounted TV and leaves significant damage must fix it before moving out. The column also noted that landlords must itemize deposit deductions and return the rest within two weeks of the tenant vacating. In a rental loft, holes in original concrete or brick are harder to hide than holes in drywall, so it is worth getting the landlord's agreement in writing, including which wall and how the cables will run. If the answer is no, a TV on its stand at $119.99 is a proper job with no holes at all.

Coworking and private offices. The Improvement District lists coworking operators at 68 Jay, 155 Water, 195 Plymouth, 10 Jay and 55 Water Streets, and the Dumbo Heights buildings house others. If your company rents a private office from an operator, the operator usually controls the walls, and its house rules may forbid drilling or require its own contractor. Ask before booking. A display on a rolling or floor stand avoids the question entirely.

The Landmarks Commission. The DUMBO Historic District is a city landmark district, and people often ask whether mounting a TV inside needs the Commission's approval. The LPC's own guidance says most exterior changes to building facades in historic districts require its review, but that a permit for interior work is needed only when the work also needs a Buildings Department permit, when it affects the exterior, or when the interior itself is designated. It adds that ordinary repairs and maintenance do not need an LPC permit. NYC DOB's guidance says the code defines minor alterations and ordinary repairs that do not need a permit. A TV on an interior wall falls on that side of the line. Things that change the outside of the building are different: a satellite dish or antenna on a roof, a screen meant to be seen through a storefront window, or a new hole through the facade for a cable. The LPC's guidebook says rooftop installations should not be visible, and that staff may approve a minimally visible one only if that is impossible. If any part of your project touches the outside, we stop and tell you to ask the building and the Commission before we go further.

Lead paint in older buildings. HPD's safe work practices apply to housing built before January 1, 1978, and are triggered by work that disturbs two square feet or more of paint in a single room. Nearly every converted building in Dumbo predates 1978. A bracket's anchor holes disturb far less than that, but cutting openings for an in-wall kit, removing a large old bracket or patching a wall can come closer, so in a rental we ask whether the landlord has lead test results for the apartment before planning that kind of work.

Compliance notes for Dumbo businesses and buildings

  • Keep screens out of the walking path. The 2010 ADA Standards limit wall-mounted objects whose leading edge sits between 27 and 80 inches above the floor to 4 inches of projection into a circulation path. A full-motion arm in a corridor or reception area can break that rule when it is pulled out, so in public areas we use low-profile brackets or mount above 80 inches.
  • No power cords inside walls. NEC 400.12 bars flexible cords run through holes in walls or concealed by walls, floors or ceilings. Hidden power behind a business screen goes through an in-wall power kit with its own rated cable, or a cord cover on the surface.
  • Low-voltage cable and permits. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 says an electrical permit is not required for wiring under 50 volts used for data and similar circuits, which covers HDMI and network cable to a display. Line-voltage power is different and belongs to a licensed electrician.
  • Exits, sprinklers and alarms stay clear. We do not mount a screen where it blocks an exit sign, a sprinkler head, a fire alarm device or a door swing, and we keep cables off the floor along any route people use to leave.
  • Facades are Landmarks territory. A screen meant to be seen from the sidewalk, a sign, or a cable through the front wall of a landmarked building is an exterior question for the LPC and the landlord, not an interior mount.
  • Insurance paperwork first. Commercial landlords and coworking operators often require a certificate of insurance naming the owner and the managing agent before work starts. Ours is sent ahead of the visit.
The Signal Behind the Screen

Fiber in the Street, Concrete in the Walls: Getting a Reliable Signal to a Dumbo TV

A TV is only as good as what reaches it, and in Dumbo the gap is rarely the internet service. One provider tracker reports Verizon business fiber reaching about 90.7 percent of Dumbo, with other fiber carriers present too. The weak link is usually the last forty feet inside the apartment or office, through walls that block Wi-Fi as effectively as they block cables. Concrete and thick brick are hard on wireless signals, and a router in a closet at one end of a loft may give a streaming TV at the other end a picture that stutters every evening.

There are three practical fixes, and we talk through them before the screen goes up, because each one changes what goes behind the TV. The first is a wired connection: if the building or a previous tenant left a network jack near the TV wall, we test it and use it. The second is moving the router or adding a mesh point closer to the TV, which is your provider's or your IT person's equipment, not ours, but we can tell you where it would help. The third is choosing a streaming device that handles weak signals better and placing it where the signal is strongest rather than hiding it behind the screen. A setup-only visit ($195 for the first hour, then 30-minute blocks) covers getting the TV, streaming device and apps working once the screen is up.

Your Questions Answered

TV Installation in Dumbo: Questions Answered

These are the situations people in Dumbo lofts, towers and offices describe when they call: rooms with more window than wall, posts and columns where the TV wants to go, boards that guard original structure, landlords who want the brick left alone, and offices that need the screen to work on a call, not just hang straight. Each is answered once here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Rooms with more window than wall

Our loft in one of the old concrete factories has a ceiling well over fifteen feet, and the only solid surface facing the sofa is a concrete pier between two window bays. Is a pier wide enough to carry a 65-inch screen?

Often it is, but measure first. A 65-inch TV is roughly 57 inches wide, and the bracket's wall plate is far narrower than the screen, so what matters is whether the pier is wide enough for the plate's fixing points to land in solid concrete well away from its edges, and whether the screen will overhang the window reveals on either side. Overhanging a reveal is fine if nothing opens into it. The bigger issues are light and height. A pier between two bays gets daylight from both sides, so the screen sees glare at the hours the sun comes through those windows; we check it with tape at the time of day you watch most. And a tall ceiling should not pull the screen up. We set the center of the picture near your seated eye level and leave the concrete above it bare. Cables on a pier have nowhere to hide, so the plan includes a painted cord cover or a console directly below.

Our open loft has the windows along one side and the kitchen along the other, and there is no wall facing the sofa at all. What can we do?

There are four honest answers. The first is turning the seating so it faces a wall you do have, which in many lofts means the wall shared with the corridor or the neighbor. The second is a column or post, if one stands in a sensible place and its surface is sound. The third is a floor stand or media console with the TV on its own feet, which on our list is a $119.99 stand setup with the devices connected. The fourth is a new partition or built-in, which is carpentry and, in a condo, usually an alteration the board approves, so it is not something we build. What we would not do is hang a screen from the ceiling slab of a historic concrete building to float it in the middle of the room without the building's engineer agreeing to it. A stand is the simplest answer in most open lofts, and it is the only one that leaves the concrete untouched if you sell or move.

Posts, columns and the original structure

The only thing facing our sofa is one of the building's original wooden posts, and it has long cracks running down it. Will it hold a 65-inch TV?

Usually yes, as long as the fixings stay out of the cracks. Long splits along the grain are normal in old heavy timber; they open as the wood dries over decades and do not mean the post is weak. They do mean that a screw driven into or beside a crack can push it wider and hold less. We look at all four faces, choose the face with the fewest checks, keep every fixing well in from the edges and away from old bolt holes, and use structural screws long enough to bite deep into sound wood. The bracket plate has to fit the post's width so that all its fixing points land in timber. If the post is narrower than the plate, we use a smaller plate or suggest a different spot rather than leaving half the holes empty. If the timber carries a fire-retardant finish or the building treats the posts as protected, ask the building before we drill.

Our office is in a steel-frame building, and the column in front of the desks is boxed in with plaster or board. Can the display be fixed to the box?

Not to the box itself, and not to the steel inside it. In a building like 68 Jay Street, which the Improvement District describes as brick with a steel frame and concrete floors, a boxed column usually hides a structural steel column wrapped in fireproofing. The board or plaster around it is a thin cover, not a mounting surface, and drilling through it can damage the fireproofing that protects the steel in a fire. That is not a risk worth a convenient screen. The good answers are the brick outer wall, a fit-out partition, or a display on a floor stand placed in front of the column. If the landlord's engineer specifically designs a clamp-on column mount, that is a different conversation, and we would install to their design. Otherwise the column stays as it is.

Our condo's alteration rules talk about load-bearing walls and structure. Is a concrete pier something the board has to approve before we put a TV on it?

Ask, because only your board can answer that for your building. Brick Underground's guide to alteration agreements says they typically prohibit tampering with load-bearing walls and similar building-wide structure, and in a converted concrete factory the piers, columns and outer walls are exactly that structure. Many boards treat a few anchor holes for a TV bracket as minor work that needs no formal approval; others want to know about any drilling into concrete. A short email to the managing agent saying which surface, how many holes, roughly what diameter and depth, and that the installer is licensed and insured, usually settles it in a day. We provide those details and the certificate of insurance. If the board says no to the pier, the partition wall or a stand is the fallback.

Freight, renters and leaving the brick alone

Our building only lets contractors use the freight elevator, and it has to be booked in advance. Who books it, and what happens if the slot is short?

Usually the resident books it, because the building wants the request from the apartment, but we tell you the time window to ask for and we confirm it before the visit. Many Dumbo conversions kept their freight elevators; CityRealty notes that the Clocktower has three. Ask for a slot that starts at the beginning of the arrival window and covers the carry up and the carry down, and ask whether the building wants the elevator padded. A single mount does not tie up the freight for long, since most of the visit happens in the apartment, but large screens need the freight for the box on the way up and the packaging on the way out. If the building's slot is shorter than the job, we bring the TV and tools up in one trip and take the empty box down at the end, or leave it for your recycling if you prefer.

Our lease allows a TV on the wall, but the landlord wants the original exposed brick left unmarked when we move out. Is there a mount that leaves nothing behind?

Not on brick. Every bracket that actually holds a TV on brick leaves anchor holes, and holes in old brick can be filled by a mason but rarely disappear completely. There are three ways to meet the landlord's wish. One is a partition wall in the loft, if there is one, where the holes can be patched and painted invisibly. Another is a TV on its own stand or a floor stand, which leaves nothing at all and is $119.99 for the stand setup on our list. The third is getting written agreement from the landlord that a mount in a specific spot is acceptable and how the holes will be dealt with at move-out. Brick Underground's attorney pointed out that the lease's alteration clause is what decides these questions, so put the agreement in writing and photograph the wall before and after.

Sound, signal and offices

Our apartment looks onto the Manhattan Bridge, and we reach for the remote every time a train goes over. Is there a TV or soundbar setting that helps?

Yes, and it is worth setting up properly. The MTA's own measurements near the bridge in 2023 recorded peaks above 90 decibels at some Dumbo residences, though the report does not say whether windows were open, and residents on Reddit describe the trains as clearly audible indoors even with good windows. Two features help. Dialogue enhancement or a voice mode on the soundbar or TV raises speech relative to music and effects, so a passing train covers less of what people say. Automatic volume leveling or a night mode narrows the gap between quiet and loud scenes, so you are not turning the sound up for a whisper and then down for an explosion. Mounting the soundbar directly under the screen ($129) keeps the voices where the picture is. We set these up with you at your usual volume, ideally while a train goes by, and show you where the settings live so you can change them later.

The wall jack where our meeting-room display is going has no label, and nobody at the company knows where it leads. Can you find out before the screen goes up?

Yes. We trace it with a tone generator and probe from the wall jack back to the patch panel or closet, which tells us whether it reaches your own network, the landlord's, or nothing at all. If it reaches your switch and tests clean, we label both ends and the display uses it. If it is dead or goes somewhere you do not control, we tell you before mounting, because putting a screen in front of a useless jack only to move it later wastes a wall. In older Dumbo conversions, offices often inherit cable from several previous tenants, so this check matters more than it would in a new building. Repairing or replacing the run is a cabling job we quote separately; the TV visit itself covers the mount, the connection and making sure the display works on your network.

Our landlord will not allow anything drilled into the brick, so our team's 75-inch display is going on a rolling stand. How do we keep it from tipping in a busy office?

Choose the stand for the screen, not the other way round. The stand's maker lists the maximum screen size and weight it is rated for, and a 75-inch display should sit well inside both. A wide base, locking casters and a low center of gravity matter more than looks. The CPSC's 2023 release on tip-overs counted about 19,400 emergency-room-treated injuries a year from furniture, TV and appliance tip-overs, and its chair, Alex Hoehn-Saric, said that "Furniture and TV tip-overs remain a major safety hazard in American households." Offices are not homes with toddlers, but a tall screen on a stand that gets pushed into a doorway is still a risk. We assemble the stand, mount the display to it, check that the casters lock, and route the cables so nobody can pull the stand over by tripping on them.

"Find two studs and screw the bracket in"

Generic guides start every TV job with a stud finder, as if every wall were drywall on wood.

The code-compliant options are an in-wall kit on a hollow partition, a paintable cord cover on masonry, or a console under the screen.

"Big rooms need the TV mounted high"

Some advice scales the mounting height to the wall, so a tall loft gets a high screen.

Your eyes do not move when the ceiling does. A screen hung to fill a 15-foot concrete wall leaves people looking up all evening.

We set the screen from the seat, with tape, and leave the space above it empty if that is what comfortable viewing needs.

"TV mounting costs about the national average"

Cost guides publish averages from many cities; TaskRabbit's 2026 guide quotes an hourly figure for the whole U.S., and one installer's Yelp answer quoted $150 to mount a TV.

"Fiber service means streaming will be perfect"

FAQ

Dumbo TV Installation FAQ

If the first hole in our concrete wall hits a steel bar right where the bracket should go, does the job or the price change?
+

The price does not change. We stop, shift the bracket an inch or two using its slotted plate, and drill again clear of the bar. We never cut reinforcing steel. If the bars are so dense that the bracket cannot sit where you want it, we show you the nearest workable position before going further.

We are renovating our loft with an interior designer. When in the project should the TV go up?
+

After the walls are finished and painted, and after the furniture plan is fixed, because the seat decides the height. If an in-wall kit is planned on a new partition, tell us before the drywall closes so the openings can be placed sensibly. We are happy to agree positions with the designer by photo beforehand.

Our window wall has metal mullions between the big panes. Can a TV be fixed to a mullion?
+

No. Mullions hold the glass and are part of the window system, and drilling them can affect how the windows seal and move. A TV belongs on a pier, a solid wall, a partition or a stand. If the window wall is the only option, a floor stand placed in front of it is the safe way to do it.

Do Dumbo's cobblestone streets and loading rules add anything to the bill?
+

No. Dumbo is billed at the same Brooklyn rate as the rest of the borough, and getting the van, the tools and the TV to your door is our problem to plan. Tell us about any loading entrance, courtyard or freight door when you book so we can use the easiest way in.

Our loft has a sleeping mezzanine. Can the TV be placed so it can be watched from up there as well as from the sofa?
+

Sometimes, with a compromise. A screen set for the sofa will look low and angled from a mezzanine. A tilting or full-motion bracket helps, and so does placing the TV on the wall the mezzanine faces. We tape the outline and check the view from both places before choosing, because one position rarely suits both perfectly.

At night the big windows behind our sofa reflect in the TV screen. Can mounting fix that?
+

Partly. Night reflections come from lights and glass behind the viewer, so a slight downward tilt, a screen with a matte or anti-glare finish, shades on the windows behind you and lamps placed to the side all reduce them. We check reflections with you during the visit and adjust the tilt before tightening the bracket.

Our soundbar is already on the wall, but the new TV will hang higher. Can the soundbar be moved to sit under it?
+

Will wall mounting affect our TV's manufacturer warranty?
+

Check your TV maker's warranty terms, since they set the rules. We mount using the TV's own mounting points and bolts of the type and length its manual specifies, on a bracket rated for its size and weight, which is how manufacturers expect their TVs to be wall-mounted.

How can I tell from a photo whether my loft wall is painted concrete or a partition?
+

Knock on it and look at the corners. A partition sounds hollow and usually has a straight, sharp corner bead. Painted concrete sounds dead and solid, feels cold and often shows faint board marks or small air pockets under the paint. Send us a photo taken at an angle with a light across the surface, which makes those marks show.

The wall we want has old electrical conduit running across it. Can the TV still go there?
+

Usually yes, if the bracket can sit between the conduit runs and the screen does not cover a junction box that has to stay reachable. We do not move or alter electrical conduit; that is an electrician's work. We place the bracket around it and route the TV's cables so they do not ride on top of it.

Our concrete wall has a special plaster or lime finish. Will drilling chip it?
+

There is always a small risk at the edge of a hole in a hard finish. We put tape over each spot, start with a sharp bit without hammer action until it is through the finish, then switch to hammer mode for the concrete. Any small chip sits behind the bracket plate, which is why every hole is placed where the plate will cover it.

The only free wall in our loft has a radiator under the window. Can the TV go above it?
+

It can, but it is not ideal. Warm air rising from a radiator flows over the TV, and many TV manuals advise keeping sets away from heat sources. If there is no other wall, we leave a generous gap above the radiator and check the manual's guidance for your model before agreeing the height with you.
