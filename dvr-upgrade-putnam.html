The Neighborhood

Why Putnam County Properties Need DVR Upgrades

Putnam is small on the map and big on distance. The county's own hazard-mitigation profile describes a little over 240 square miles split between six towns — Carmel, Kent, Patterson, Philipstown, Putnam Valley and Southeast — and three villages, Brewster, Cold Spring and Nelsonville. More than nine-tenths of that land sits in the Hudson Highlands, on gneiss and granite, and about two-thirds of it was still forest in the last land-cover count. Roughly three-quarters of the county drains into New York City's reservoirs. On a camera job, those numbers turn into a simple fact: the cameras are far apart, the ground between them is rock, wetland or City watershed land, and the cable that links them was laid once, at some expense, and nobody wants to lay it again. That is why a recorder upgrade — changing the box and keeping the runs — is the most common camera job we do in the county.

The housing explains where the old recorders came from. The Census Bureau counts about 38,700 homes in Putnam, with 83.8 percent owner-occupied, and most are detached houses on their own lots. Many began as something smaller. Putnam Lake in Patterson, Lake Carmel in Kent and Lake Peekskill in Putnam Valley were laid out from 1928 onward as summer bungalow colonies; the Patterson historical society records 11,000 lots mapped at Putnam Lake alone, twenty feet by a hundred, sold for fifty dollars each to city families who left after Labor Day. Those cottages have since been winterized, extended and rewired in stages, and the camera systems in them were added the same way — a kit from a big-box store in one decade, a second recorder for the garage in the next, a camera on the dock because the boat was there. The American Community Survey still counts about 1,750 Putnam homes held for seasonal or occasional use, so a fair share of the systems we upgrade watch a house that is empty for weeks at a time.

When owners call, it is usually because something happened and the recorder was not there for it. In January 2025 the New York State Police issued a public safety alert about targeted residential burglaries across Dutchess, Westchester and Putnam, advising residents to secure second-floor entry points and not to post absences online. In September 2024 the Highlands Current reported break-ins on Old Albany Post Road and Indian Brook Road in Philipstown, with the Sheriff reminding residents to lock doors and windows. The Putnam County Sheriff's Office runs a Dark House Check for residents who will be away for an extended period, with deputies looking over the outside of the house for signs of forced entry — and says plainly that it is no guarantee. A recorder that keeps a month of clear, correctly dated video, and tells you when it stops recording, is the part of that picture you control.

The other trigger is the weather, and here Putnam has a record few places match. When Tropical Storm Isaias came through in August 2020, Daily Voice reported the day after that NYSEG had 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers without power, with Central Hudson's Philipstown, Cold Spring and Nelsonville customers out as well; four days later more than 1,700 NYSEG customers in the county were still waiting. On Christmas Day that same year NYSEG's Brewster division counted more than 27,000 customers affected after gusts reached 71 miles an hour. An old DVR plugged into a wall outlet stops when the power stops, and too many come back with a reset clock, a damaged disk or cameras that stay dark until someone power-cycles them. Our Putnam upgrades are built around that: battery backup for the recorder and modem, camera power that restarts cleanly, and a test where we switch everything off before we leave. If you are thinking about the house as well as the cameras, a monitored alarm for a Putnam home can be linked to the recorder at the same visit, and the wider choices across the six counties are on our Hudson Valley DVR upgrade hub.

How the work changes with the building

In a converted bungalow, we start under the house. The recorder is often in a crawlspace or a closet against an outside wall, both of which swing from damp to dry with the seasons, and the cable is usually stapled to joists alongside old knob-and-tube remnants or newer Romex. We lift the recorder into conditioned space, often a bedroom closet or a utility cupboard, keep the camera runs, and replace only the lengths that have been chewed, kinked or left lying on a damp floor. Where a run has to be extended to the new location, we join it in a dry box rather than in the crawlspace.

On a lakefront lot, the work is about the slope and the water. The camera on the dock or the boathouse is the one most likely to have failed, because its cable crosses ground that heaves with frost and is soaked every spring. We test that run first. If it is sound, it gets new outdoor-rated connectors and a drip loop at each end; if it is not, the replacement goes in conduit where the slope allows, or across the yard by a pair of small wireless radios when trenching would mean disturbing the shoreline buffer.

On acreage with outbuildings, the question is electrical. A barn or a pump house on its own meter is effectively a separate building with a separate ground, and coax running between two grounds carries hum, surges and, in a thunderstorm, sometimes enough current to take out a camera input on the recorder. We bond what can be bonded, fit isolators or surge protection at the building entry, and where the distance or the lightning exposure is high, change the link to fiber with a small media converter at each end, which carries no electrical path at all.

In an old farmhouse or stone house, we follow the existing cable rather than starting new routes. A fieldstone foundation can take a week of patience to get a cable through; the coax from the last system has already done that, so we reuse it as a pull string if it has to be replaced, and keep it if it tests good. The recorder goes somewhere with air around it, never in the cellar beside the oil burner.

In a village house, the rules are the neighbours and the street. In Cold Spring, exterior changes visible from the street within the historic district need the Historic District Review Board's approval, so we favour keeping existing camera positions, mounting replacements in the same holes, and placing anything new where it is not visible from the street. Owners who are adding cameras to an older house often pair the upgrade with new camera installation in Putnam for the parts of the property the old system never covered.

Rules and credits Putnam owners ask about

  • County contractor registration. Putnam County's Chapter 135 says no one may run a home improvement business in the county unless registered, and the Consumer Affairs office describes the programme as making sure contractors on residential property are insured, bonded and covered by workers' compensation. Ask any installer, including us, for their registration details.
  • County low-voltage licensing. Putnam's Electrical Board licenses “Limited Data Communication Technicians”, and the county code's definition covers video surveillance as well as networking, intercom and fiber. The county publishes a list of licensed low-voltage technicians; it is worth checking.
  • Electrical permits in three towns. The county notes that electrical permit applications for locations in Carmel, Patterson and Putnam Valley are filed with those towns' building departments. A like-for-like recorder swap plugs into an existing outlet; new circuits for a barn or a generator are an electrician's work and permit.
  • Cold Spring's historic district. Since 1976 exterior changes to buildings in the village's historic district have needed approval from its five-member Historic District Review Board; the village's guidance exempts work that is not visible from the street.
  • Wetland and watershed setbacks. The Town of Carmel regulates a 100-foot buffer around wetlands of an eighth of an acre or more, and the Town of Kent flags projects within 100 feet of ponds, streams and watercourses. New trenches for camera cable near water are planned around those buffers before anyone digs.
  • Camera and audio law. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a neighbour a civil claim against cameras placed to record their back yard without consent, and recording a conversation you are not part of without consent is eavesdropping under Penal Law §250.05. We mask neighbouring yards and set cameras to video only unless you have a clear reason and the consent to record sound.
  • Small-business security tax credit. New York's Commercial Security Tax Credit, created in the FY2025 budget, offers up to $3,000 per retail location to businesses with 50 or fewer employees that spend over the threshold on items including surveillance cameras; applications for the certificate close on October 31 each year.

Your Questions Answered

DVR Upgrades in Putnam County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Putnam owners put to us about barns and pump houses, lake houses that close for the winter, a phone signal that comes and goes, storms that take the power for days, long private driveways, watershed land and the animals that wander through every camera's view. They are grouped by the problem behind them, and each answer is the one we would give on the phone.

Barns, Pump Houses and Outbuildings

The barn is about 400 feet from the house. Is that too far for a high-definition camera on the old coax?

Usually not, if the cable itself is decent. The analogue high-definition formats that hybrid recorders use were designed for long coax: Dahua's data sheets for its HDCVI recorders quote up to 700 metres for 4K and 4-megapixel video and 800 metres for 1080p over coax, which is well beyond any barn in the county. The limits in practice are the cable and the joints, not the format. Thin premade cable with molded connectors, copper-clad aluminium coax, or a run that was spliced in the field three times will lose the fine detail long before the distance does. We test the run with a high-definition signal at the barn end, look at the picture on the recorder, and decide from what we see. Where the cable is the weak point, the fix is often one new length rather than a different camera.

Does a camera on the well pump house need its own power, or can it run off the cable from the house?

It can usually run off the cable. Many current analogue HD cameras and recorders support power over coax, where the recorder sends the camera's power down the same coax that brings its video back, so there is no need for an outlet or a plug-in supply at the pump house. The trade-off is distance: power over coax is limited to a few hundred feet depending on the camera's draw and the cable, and a camera with a big infrared array may need its own supply. Pump houses are cramped and damp, and the pump motor's own wiring is line voltage, so we keep the camera cable away from it and mount the camera outside the door rather than inside the housing. If you want to know whether the pump-house door has been opened, a simple door contact on the recorder's alarm input does that without a second camera.

The barn has its own electric meter. Does that change how the camera cable should connect to the house?

Yes, and it is one of the most common reasons outbuilding cameras fail in Putnam. Two buildings on two services have two separate earth grounds, and a coax shield connecting them gives stray current an easy path between them. On a good day that shows up as a faint bar rolling through the picture; in a nearby lightning strike it can arrive as a surge that damages the camera, the recorder input or both. Depending on the distance and the exposure we fit a ground-loop isolator, add surge protection where the cable enters each building, or replace the link with fiber and a small converter at each end, which carries the video with no electrical connection at all. Any change to the barn's electrical service itself is for a licensed electrician; we coordinate with yours.

There is no power and no cable out at the far field gate. Can a solar camera there show up beside the wired cameras?

Often it can, with two checks first. A solar camera needs real winter sun on its panel, which rules out a spot under hemlocks or on the north side of a ridge, and it needs a way to get its video home: either a cellular data plan, which depends on the carrier's signal at that exact spot, or a wireless link back to a point near the house. If both check out, most such cameras record to their own memory card and stream through the maker's app; some can be added to a recorder as a network camera. We will tell you honestly which kind you will get. Where the field is within reach of a buried conduit, a wired camera is still cheaper to own over ten years, but for a gate a long way from any building, solar is often the sensible answer.

Lake Houses and Seasonal Homes

The dock camera fogs over every morning from late summer into autumn. Is that the camera or the recorder?

Neither, as a rule; it is the lake. When cold night air meets water that is still warm from the summer, fog forms over the surface and condenses on anything near it, including a lens or a dome bubble. A recorder cannot clear it, and a better recorder will only show the fog more clearly. What helps is the camera: a turret or bullet with a flat lens window and a short hood collects less moisture than a dome bubble, and a camera mounted higher on the boathouse or under an eave sits above the worst of it. Many cameras have a defog or heater function on their housings; we check whether yours does and switch it on. We also make sure the camera's own enclosure is sealed, because moisture inside the housing is a different, permanent problem.

We close the lake house from November to April. Should the recorder stay on all winter?

Yes, if the house keeps its heat and power on, and most owners do. A recorder left running records the empty months that matter most and reports problems as they happen; one switched off saves a few dollars of electricity and leaves you blind. What changes for winter is the setup: we lengthen retention so a whole month is kept, set alerts for people only at the doors and the drive, make sure the recorder notifies you if a camera or the disk fails, and confirm the modem and recorder are on a battery so a short outage does not take the system down. If the house is fully shut down with the power off, we would rather see a cellular camera at the door than a recorder that cannot run.

How will we know the recorder at the weekend house has stopped working if nobody is there to look?

The recorder can tell you. Current recorders raise events for a lost camera signal, a disk error, a full disk, a failed login and, through the app, the recorder itself going offline, and each can be set to push a notification to your phone. We switch on the ones that matter, test them by unplugging a camera before we leave, and show you what each alert looks like. Pair that with a once-a-week glance at the playback, and a failure is spotted in days rather than discovered in spring. For houses with patchy internet we also set the recorder to keep recording normally when the connection drops, so a lost link means a gap in alerts, not a gap in footage.

Weak Phone Signal and Rural Internet

Our cell signal at the house is one bar. Can we still pull up the cameras on our phones while we're home?

Yes. When your phone is on the house Wi-Fi, the app can reach the recorder over the home network, and many apps will use the local connection automatically when you are on the same network as the recorder. Cell signal does not come into it. The weak link is usually the Wi-Fi itself in a long house or on a deck down by the water, which is fixable with a properly placed access point. If the only way your phones work at home is Wi-Fi calling, that is common in parts of the county, and it does not stop the camera app working on the same Wi-Fi.

Our home internet is a capped cellular or satellite plan. Will the cameras eat through the monthly data allowance?

Not on their own. A recorder stores video on its own disk and sends nothing out unless someone is watching live or playing back remotely, or unless cloud backup has been switched on. What does use data is long sessions of live viewing in full resolution. We set each camera's second, lower-resolution stream as the default for remote viewing, which is typically a small fraction of the main stream's data, and you can switch to full detail for a single camera when you need it. We also check that no cloud upload is quietly running. On a capped plan, a week of normal checking in on the cameras is unlikely to be noticed; streaming eight cameras in high definition for an hour every evening will be.

Ice Storms, NYSEG Outages and Generators

Our standby generator takes about a minute to start after the power fails. Does the recorder still need a battery?

Yes. In that minute the recorder loses power, stops mid-write, and then reboots when the generator comes on; it can do the same thing again when the transfer switch puts you back on the grid. A recorder that is cut off while writing can corrupt the file it was saving or, after enough of these, the disk. A small battery backup between the recorder and the outlet covers the generator's start-up gap and the return to grid power, so the recorder never notices either. We size it for the recorder, the modem and any camera power supply on the same circuit, and set the recorder to resume recording on its own if the battery ever does run flat.

An ice-laden branch brought down the cable to the garage camera that runs on our poles. Is fixing it ours or NYSEG's?

If the camera cable was strung on your own poles or between your own buildings, it is yours, and it is ours to fix if you want us to. Utility poles and the lines on them belong to the utility or the communications companies that lease space on them, and a private camera cable should not be lashed to them. If yours was, we will move it. For the repair we usually replace an overhead span with direct-burial cable or conduit where the ground allows, because ice and falling limbs are the main thing that breaks aerial runs here. Where an overhead run has to stay, we use a messenger wire to carry the weight and a drip loop at each building, and keep it clear of the power service drop.

After the power came back, several cameras stayed on “no video” until someone unplugged their power supply. Can the upgrade stop that?

Usually, yes. That symptom is common on older systems where each camera has its own small plug-in power adapter or where several share a cheap power box: a brownout as the power returns can leave a camera hung until it is power-cycled. The fix is to power the cameras from one well-regulated supply on the battery backup, or from the recorder itself over the coax where the cameras support it, so the cameras ride through the dip instead of hanging. We then test it the only way that counts — switching the power off at the breaker and back on — and make sure every camera comes back by itself.

Long Driveways, Private Roads and Plows

Can a camera at the bottom of our wooded driveway warn us someone is coming before they reach the house?

Yes, and it is one of the most useful things a rural system can do. A camera near the road end of the drive, with a vehicle-detection line drawn across the driveway on the recorder, can send an alert the moment a car turns in, which on a long drive gives you a minute or more before it reaches the house. Getting the video back is the main task: if there is no cable to the bottom of the drive, a pair of small point-to-point radios with a clear line of sight usually does it, and we survey the trees before promising that. At night, headlights shining straight into the lens can wash out the picture, so the camera is aimed to see vehicles side-on as they pass a fixed point, not head-on.

We share a private road with five other houses. Can we put a camera where it meets the town road?

Only with the agreement of whoever owns that land and the right to use it, which on a shared private road is often a road association or the neighbour whose deed includes that strip. Ask first and get it in writing. In practice, neighbours on a shared road often agree to one camera at the entrance that all of them can view, which is easy to set up on a recorder with separate limited logins. If agreement is not possible, a camera on your own property aimed along your own frontage, with the neighbours' houses masked out, is the alternative. We will not mount a camera on land you do not control.

The town plow keeps knocking over the post with our driveway camera on it. Where else can it go?

Higher and further back. A post at the road edge sits right in the plow's path and in the snowbank it throws, and a short one gets buried even when it is not hit. Better spots are a tree well inside the property line, using a mount that is strapped rather than screwed in so the tree can grow, a tall post set a few feet behind the snowbank line, or the gate pillar if you have one. A slightly longer lens lets the camera sit further back and still see the same stretch of road. The cable to the old post should come up out of the ground inside a protective sleeve, not exposed at the edge of the pavement.

Watershed Land, Wetlands and Neighbours

The back of our lot meets New York City watershed land. Can we aim a camera that way, and can cable run near the stream?

A camera on your own building can look out over your own yard toward the boundary; we keep its view on your property and mask what lies beyond it, which also keeps out the footpaths that cross some City lands. City-owned water-supply land is not yours to dig in or mount on, and the City's Department of Environmental Protection issues separate permits for temporary use of it. Near streams and wetlands on your own land, town wetland rules apply: Carmel regulates a 100-foot buffer around wetlands, and Kent flags work within 100 feet of watercourses. For that reason we route new cable along the house side of the property, use a wireless link across a wet area rather than trenching through it, and talk to the town before any digging in a buffer.

Can the Sheriff's Dark House Check and our cameras work together while we're away?

They complement each other. The Sheriff's Office describes the Dark House Check as extra patrols and, when appropriate, a look at the outside of the house for signs of forced entry, with deputies contacting the owner if anything is wrong. Your cameras cover the hours between patrols and record what a deputy might later need to see. Two practical steps help: give the Sheriff's Office a phone number you will actually answer while you are away, and set the recorder to alert you for people at the doors, so you know before a deputy does. If a deputy or detective later asks for video, a clip exported straight from the recorder, with the correct time on it, is what they need.

Wildlife, Weather and the View

Black bears set off the yard camera most nights. Can the system tell a bear from a person standing up?

Mostly, but not perfectly, and we tell owners that up front. Bears are a real presence in the county: in May 2024 the Putnam County News & Recorder reported a large bear in a Carmel backyard less than half a mile from the historic courthouse. A recorder's person-detection is trained to recognise human shapes, and it ignores most animals most of the time, but a large animal upright at the edge of the frame at night can still be classified as a person now and then. We reduce the misses by drawing detection zones around doors, decks and the drive rather than the tree line, setting a minimum target size, and making sure the night image is lit well enough for the analytics to see shape rather than shadow. Keeping bird feeders and garbage away from the house, as the state's wildlife agency urges, cuts the visits themselves.

Spiders spin webs across the night cameras every summer. What actually keeps them off?

Nothing keeps them off entirely, but a few things help a lot. Infrared lights on a camera attract insects at night, insects attract spiders, and a web strand a few centimetres from the lens glows in infrared and fills the frame — Swann's own support guidance describes insects close to the camera triggering motion and false recordings. We reduce it by using external or white-light illumination a short distance from the camera where possible, so the insects gather at the light and not the lens; by choosing cameras whose infrared can be turned down where there is other light; and by showing you how to clean the housing with a soft brush. A once-a-month wipe in July and August is part of owning cameras in the woods.

Branches have grown into half the camera views since the system went in. Do we move the cameras or trim the trees?

Usually a bit of both, decided camera by camera. Trees in Putnam grow fast into a view that was clear ten years ago, and branches moving in the wind are the single biggest source of false motion on older systems. Where the camera is in the right place for what it needs to see, a trim is cheaper. Where the tree is the neighbour's or a prized specimen, we move the camera, often lower and closer to the thing it watches, or change it for a narrower lens that looks past the branches. We review every view on the new recorder before we leave and draw masks over any branch that cannot be avoided.

Cottages, Crawlspaces and Older Wiring

The camera cable in our converted bungalow runs through a crawlspace that gets wet every spring. Does it all need to come out?

Not all of it. Coax with an intact outer jacket tolerates damp air reasonably well; what fails is the connectors and any length lying in standing water or on soil, where water wicks into the cable and corrodes the shield over time. We pull the runs up off the floor onto hangers along the joists, replace any length that shows a damaged jacket or a high-resistance shield, and move every connection point out of the crawlspace into a dry box or into the house. The recorder itself never goes in the crawlspace. If the space floods rather than just gets damp, that changes the plan: cable there goes into sealed conduit or is rerouted through the house.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About DVR Upgrades in Putnam County

Our build environment could not display a live Google AI Overview for Putnam DVR searches, so we took seven claims from the press releases, data sheets, aggregator sites and forums those answers draw on, and checked each against what Putnam properties show us.

“The coverage map shows signal, so a cellular camera will work at your property”

Coverage maps are the first thing an online answer reaches for when you ask whether a cellular or solar camera will work somewhere rural. In Putnam that is a weak foundation. In October 2015 Senator Schumer called on federal regulators to investigate carriers whose maps showed quality reception in northern Westchester and Putnam, saying “There are more cell phone dead zones in Northern Westchester and Putnam Counties than post-winter potholes on the West Side Highway.”

The county itself has spent years trying to close gaps. In May 2016 the Highlands Current reported a county plan to place towers on county-owned land to improve emergency radio coverage, which might also help phone users “aggravated by dead zones”, with possible sites in Philipstown, Cold Spring, Putnam Valley, Mahopac, Carmel and Patterson. Hills, hollows and tree cover still make signal a property-by-property matter.

So before we recommend a cellular camera, we stand where it would go with a phone on each carrier and look at the real signal, and we try an upload. If it is not there, the answer is a wireless link back to the house or a buried cable, not a camera that will spend its life reconnecting.

“HD-over-coax reaches hundreds of metres, so distance is never a problem”

The data sheets are right about the format. Dahua's HDCVI recorder documentation quotes up to 700 metres for 4K and 4-megapixel video and 800 metres for 1080p over coax, and Hikvision's Turbo HD announcements made similar claims. On paper, no Putnam barn is too far away.

In the field, those figures assume good solid-copper cable with sound connectors, run in one piece. The long runs we meet here were often laid on the surface or stapled along fences, joined two or three times, and exposed to years of frost and sun. They also run between buildings that may sit on separate electrical grounds, in a county where Earth Networks counted 75,093 lightning pulses in 2020 alone. Distance is rarely the limit; joints, cable quality, grounding and surges are.

That is why every long run is tested with a high-definition signal before we commit to keeping it, why we re-terminate with compression connectors, and why outbuildings on their own meter get isolation or fiber rather than trust.

“Put the recorder on a battery backup and an outage won't matter”

A battery backup is essential, and online answers are right to recommend one. What they leave out is scale. A typical small battery unit will keep a recorder and modem running for tens of minutes to perhaps an hour or two, depending on its size and the load.

Putnam outages are often measured in days. After Tropical Storm Isaias in August 2020, NYSEG had 34,150 of its 38,693 Putnam customers out the next morning, and 1,733 were still waiting four days later. The cameras also need power, the modem needs the internet provider's equipment up the road to be working, and many houses here have a well pump and heat that make a generator the real plan.

So the battery's job on our upgrades is to bridge: the first minutes of an outage, the generator's start-up and the transfer back. For long outages we put the recorder, modem and camera supply on a circuit the generator carries, and the recorder keeps recording locally even when the internet is down.

“Smart detection ends false alerts”

Person and vehicle detection on a current recorder is a real improvement over the pixel-change motion detection of older DVRs, and it is one of the best reasons to upgrade. It is not magic, and rural properties test it harder than suburban ones.

Manufacturers' own support pages acknowledge the limits: Swann's guidance explains that insects flying close to a camera can trigger motion detection and false recordings. Here the list is longer — deer at the tree line, bears like the one the Putnam County News & Recorder reported in a Carmel backyard in May 2024, branches moving in a storm, headlights on a wet road, snow falling through the infrared.

Detection works best when the zones are drawn tightly around the places people actually approach, the night image is properly lit, and the camera is aimed so a person fills a reasonable part of the frame. We set it up that way and review the first week's alerts with you.

“New York has no contractor licence, so anyone can install cameras”

Contractor-licensing guides summarise New York State as having no statewide general contractor licence, and AI answers often carry that through to “no licence needed”. That stops being true at the county line.

Putnam County's Chapter 135 requires home improvement businesses working in the county to be registered, and its electrical licensing chapter covers low-voltage and limited data communications work, defined to include video surveillance alongside networking, intercom and fiber optics. The county's Consumer Affairs office publishes a list of licensed low-voltage technicians.

Before anyone works on your system, ask for their registration and licence details and check them against the county's lists. AESS works under New York State low-voltage licence #12000287431, and we are glad to be asked.

“Almost everyone has broadband now, so remote viewing is solved”

The headline numbers support the claim: the Census Bureau's QuickFacts show 94.8 percent of Putnam households with a broadband subscription. Aggregator sites go further, describing near-total satellite availability and widespread fiber in places like Putnam Valley.

The edges tell a different story. In September 2025 the state announced more than $11.4 million for Comcast, with a matching contribution, to bring fiber to 390 Putnam County locations that did not have it. In 2023 the Highlands Current reported that Verizon was reviewing a fiber build in Cold Spring but could not commit to a timeframe. Many of the properties we work on are exactly the ones at the end of the line.

So we set up remote viewing for the connection you actually have — a lower-resolution substream by default, alerts with snapshots rather than video, and local recording that does not depend on the internet at all.

“An old DVR just needs a new hard drive”

It is the most common forum advice for a recorder that has stopped saving video, and sometimes it is right: a drive is the part that wears out first, and replacing it can restore a recorder that is otherwise healthy and still supported.

On a ten-year-old Putnam DVR it is often the wrong economy. Older recorders may not take the drive sizes needed for a month of retention, may record only at the low resolutions their cameras were sold with, and often have firmware that no longer gets fixes. Fortinet's FortiGuard Labs reported widespread exploitation of CVE-2024-3721, a command-injection flaw in a family of DVRs, with no vendor patch available — the advice was to isolate or replace.

We will tell you honestly when a drive is all your recorder needs. When it is not, the money is better spent on a current hybrid recorder that keeps your cameras and cable, closes the old internet port forwards and runs the app you already have on your phone.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walk the Property Before Touching the Recorder

On a Putnam upgrade I walk the property before I open the recorder. I want to see where every cable leaves the house, how it gets to the barn or the dock, what it passes on the way, and whether it goes up a pole, across a lawn or along a stone wall. Half the failures out here are visible from outside: a mower-nicked coax across a path, a span sagging after an ice storm, a shed-wall connector packed with mud and wasps.

Then I ask which buildings have their own meter. That one question decides whether a barn camera can share the coax with the house or needs fiber, and it explains more burnt-out recorder inputs than anything else.

Last, before I leave, I cut the power at the panel or the battery and wait. If every camera does not come back on its own with the right time on the screen, the job is not done. In a county where the power can be off for days, that test is the one that matters. — Anwar Timothy, NYS licence #12000287431

FAQ

Putnam County DVR Upgrade FAQ

Can you swap the recorder while we're away and walk us through it on a video call afterwards?
+

Yes, if someone can let us in and the house has power and internet. Many Putnam owners are at work in the city or living elsewhere while the weekend house waits, so we often do the swap with a caretaker, neighbour or contractor opening the door. Before we leave, we send photos of the new recorder and its battery, and once you are free we go through the app with you on a video call: live view, playback, alerts, export and adding the rest of the family. Logins are created in your name, and the administrator password is given only to you.

Can a camera keep an eye on the propane tank, the oil fill and the generator?
+

Yes, and on a rural property they are worth watching. Fuel deliveries happen when nobody is home, generators sit in the open, and tank areas are out of sight from the house. One camera on the side of the house can usually cover the tank, the fill pipe and the generator pad, with a detection zone around them so you are alerted to a person there but not to the delivery truck on the road. If the generator has an alarm or status output, an electrician can wire it to the recorder's alarm input so a fault shows up as an event alongside the video.

Will a camera's infrared light bother the horses or other animals in the barn?
+

Infrared illuminators on security cameras work at wavelengths people see only as a faint red glow at the lens, and most owners report that horses, dogs and livestock take no notice of them. What animals do notice is visible light switching on and off, so inside a barn we avoid cameras with white-light or flashing deterrence features, or switch those features off. We mount barn cameras high and out of reach of a curious head, run the cable in protective conduit where animals can get at it, and aim them along the aisle so each stall door is in view.

Can the recorder warn us if the heat fails in the empty house in January?
+

Not with a camera alone, but the recorder can carry the warning. Many recorders have alarm inputs that accept a simple dry contact, and a low-temperature switch or the alarm output of a freeze sensor, wired to one of those inputs, can make the recorder push an alert to your phone just as it does for a person at the door. An internet-connected thermostat or a monitored alarm with a temperature sensor does the same job independently, which is better still. In a house left empty through a Putnam winter, frozen pipes cost far more than any recorder.

Should the cameras record around the clock or only when something moves, on a property where the nights are long and quiet?
+

We usually set the cameras near the house and the drive to record continuously at a moderate quality, with full quality and an event marker when something is detected. Continuous recording means nothing is missed if detection fails to trigger, which can happen at night at the edge of a camera's range. On quiet rural nights video compresses well, so continuous recording costs less disk space than it would on a busy street. Far cameras that mostly watch trees can record on events only to save space. Either way, the retention figure is read off the recorder after the first full day, not guessed.

Can a copy of important clips be kept off the property in case the house itself is broken into?
+

Yes. The simplest protection is putting the recorder somewhere a burglar is unlikely to look, rather than in plain sight under the TV. Beyond that, many recorders can send a snapshot or a short clip of each detection to your phone or to an email account, which leaves a copy off the property even if the recorder is taken. Some makers offer cloud backup of event clips on a paid plan, which we can set up if you want it, bearing in mind your internet's upload speed. A full continuous copy off site is rarely practical on a rural connection.

Mice got into the barn and chewed through a camera cable. What protects the new runs?
+

Rodents chew cable where it runs through warm, sheltered places they already use, such as feed rooms, wall cavities and the space under a floor. Inside barns and sheds we run camera cable in conduit, either rigid where it is exposed or flexible where it bends, and keep it off the floor and away from feed storage. Where cable enters a building, the hole is sleeved and sealed so it does not become a doorway. Outside, buried runs go in conduit at a sensible depth. Rodent-deterrent cable jackets exist, but conduit is the more reliable defence.

Our house has no number visible from the road. What should we tell you so the crew finds it?
+

Give us the address, the name of the private road or driveway it is on if it has one, and a short description such as the colour of the mailbox or the gate, and say which turn-off to take if the map pin lands on the wrong side of a lake. A dropped pin from your phone while standing at the end of your drive is ideal. Tell us too if there is a gate code, a steep or unpaved stretch, or a weight limit on a bridge. While we are there, it is worth fixing the house number at the road, because emergency crews need it as much as we do.

Will you take down old cameras we no longer use and seal the holes?
+

Yes. Properties that have had two or three systems often carry dead cameras on the eaves and the barn, and each one is a hole in the siding and a cable that leads nowhere. During the upgrade we identify which cameras are live, take down the ones you no longer want, and seal each mounting hole and cable entry with an exterior sealant or a cover plate. If a dead camera's cable could be useful later, we leave it labelled and coiled inside rather than pulling it out, because an existing path to an outbuilding is worth keeping.

Can a camera let us check the dock and the ice after a winter storm without driving up from the city?
+

Yes. A camera on the lake side of the house or the boathouse, aimed down at the dock and the shoreline, lets you see whether the dock has shifted, a tree is down across the path or the ice has pushed up against the boathouse, all from the app. Set it to send a snapshot each morning, which works even on a weak connection. Mount it under an eave so snow does not cover the lens, and keep its cable off the frozen ground where ice heave can stretch it. If the view needs to swing between the dock and the drive, a pan-tilt camera with saved positions does both.
