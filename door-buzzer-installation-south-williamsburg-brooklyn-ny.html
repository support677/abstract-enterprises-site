The Neighborhood

Why South Williamsburg Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

South Williamsburg is the part of Williamsburg south of Division Avenue and Broadway, running to Flushing Avenue and the East River, with the Hasidic community centered on Lee and Bedford Avenues and a long-standing Puerto Rican community in the blocks once called Los Sures. Wikipedia puts its 2020 population at about 47,000. For anyone planning a door buzzer, though, the neighborhood is best described by what is happening to its front doors right now, and three things stand out.

The first is construction. Vacant lots are being filled with buildings designed for very large households. In November 2025 New York YIMBY reported permits for a six-story building at 293 Wallabout Street, between Harrison and Throop Avenues, with just three residences averaging 2,794 square feet each. In May 2026 it reported permits for a seven-story building at 170 Williamsburg Street East with 20 residences, a 7,072-square-foot community facility and 6,797 square feet of commercial space. And the six-story rental at 100 South 9th Street opened its housing lottery in April 2026 with 45 apartments. Every one of these buildings needs an entry system designed before its walls close, and each needs a different one.

The second is the city's own record of broken intercoms. When we queried NYC Open Data on November 11, 2026, HPD had recorded 107 Housing Maintenance Code violations mentioning an intercom across the three ZIP codes that cover South Williamsburg since January 1, 2025: 55 in 11206, 38 in 11211 and 14 in 11249. Tenants filed 320 complaints in the same period whose category mentioned an intercom. Those ZIP codes also take in north Williamsburg, East Williamsburg and parts of Bushwick and Bedford-Stuyvesant, so the numbers describe the wider area rather than South Williamsburg alone, but the individual records name streets right in the middle of it: Keap Street, Hooper Street, Clymer Street, Wilson Street, Union Avenue and Broadway.

The third is that many of the busiest doors in South Williamsburg are not apartment doors at all. Synagogues, yeshivas, community halls and offices fill the blocks around Lee and Bedford Avenues, and those buildings are now buying door security with public money. On September 8, 2026 the Governor announced a record $70 million in Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes grants to 290 nonprofits, 147 of them in New York City for a total of $35,792,289, with locks and access controls among the eligible uses. "As the Holiday season approaches, our schools, synagogues, and institutions are feeling more vulnerable than ever," Rabbi Yeruchim Silber of Agudath Israel of America said in the same announcement. A buzzer at the front door of such a building is a security device that must also let a crowd out in seconds.

Behind all three is the older housing stock: brick walk-ups, the rehabilitated buildings and co-ops of Southside United HDFC, known as Los Sures, founded in 1972, which says it has been involved in rehabilitating and building more than 5,000 units in over 340 buildings and co-ops, and the public housing towers of Independence Towers and the Taylor–Wythe Houses.

Repair or Replace in South Williamsburg

Need Repair in South Williamsburg? Fix the Part, Replace the Panel or Install the Whole System

A good share of the installation calls we get from South Williamsburg start as something else: a tenant's complaint to 311, an HPD violation with a correction deadline, a board that has paid for one repair too many, or a building that changed hands and came with a panel nobody has the password for. Before any equipment is chosen, we decide which of three paths fits the building, and we say so plainly.

The first path is a targeted repair, when one part is the whole problem: a power supply in the cellar that sags under load, a strike that has come loose in the frame, a single apartment's station or the pair that feeds it. The second is a partial replacement: a new street panel and power supply on a riser that meters sound, with only the failed apartment stations replaced. The third is a full installation with new cable, for buildings whose risers have been spliced so often that every repair causes the next fault, or for buildings that never had more than a row of bell buttons. If a repair is all your building needs, we price it as a repair, and our Brooklyn door buzzer team handles both kinds of work.

They differ in how many households sit behind the street door, how cable can reach them, who has the authority to order the work and which part of the law applies. Each also has one mistake that shows up when the job is rushed.

Family-size new condos

Six-story buildings on former vacant lots with a handful of very large apartments, like the three homes averaging 2,794 square feet planned at 293 Wallabout Street.

Mixed-use new buildings

Seven-story buildings that combine condos with a community-facility floor and stores, like the 20-residence building planned at 170 Williamsburg Street East near the Marcy Avenue station.

Prewar walk-ups

Rehabilitated and HDFC buildings

Buildings and co-ops rehabilitated through groups like Los Sures, run by a nonprofit manager or a volunteer co-op board with a fixed budget.

Mid-century and later towers

Taller buildings toward the waterfront and around Clymer, Wilson and Kent, where HPD records name apartments on the eighth, ninth and tenth stories.

Family-size new condos: three apartments, six floors and a station on every level that people use

A building like the one planned at 293 Wallabout Street is the clearest example of how South Williamsburg's new construction differs from the rest of Brooklyn. Six stories, 74 feet tall, and only three residences, each averaging 2,794 square feet according to the permit filings New York YIMBY reported. Apartments that size are usually duplexes, two full floors each, built for households with many children, a large kitchen and a dining room that seats a crowd. The street door station needs only three buttons. The work is inside the apartments.

The mistake here is the one developers make everywhere: one indoor station per apartment, mounted by the apartment's entry door because that is where the drawings put it. In a duplex, the entry door is on one floor and half the family lives on the other. We plan two stations per apartment at minimum, one in or near the kitchen, which is where the household spends the day, and one on the bedroom floor, both ringing at once. A video station on the kitchen level lets whoever is cooking see the visitor and release the door with wet hands still at the sink.

A building with three apartments sits well below the eight-apartment line in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, so no intercom law forces the choice. That is exactly why it gets left off the plans until the last minute, and why we prefer to be called while the framing is open. Cable to a second station on the upper level of a duplex costs little before the sheetrock goes up and a great deal afterward.

Mixed-use new buildings: condos, a community floor and a store behind one facade

The 170 Williamsburg Street East filing describes a seven-story building with 24,556 square feet of residential space for 20 residences, 7,072 square feet of community facility and 6,797 square feet of commercial space. In South Williamsburg, a community facility in a new building often means a place of worship, a school room, a hall or offices, used by people who do not live there. That changes the entry plan completely. With 20 apartments the building is inside §50-a, so the residents' entrance needs talk and release to every home, while the community facility and the store each get their own door station, release and power, planned before the slab sleeves are cast. The questions below cover how the three groups are kept apart.

New lottery rentals: 45 apartments, elevators and a lobby that works without a doorman

100 South 9th Street, a six-story building that YIMBY places in South Williamsburg, opened its housing lottery in April 2026 with 45 residences, elevators, bike storage, laundry and a rooftop terrace. That is a typical new rental here: enough apartments that a row of buttons becomes a directory or code panel, amenity rooms that tenants reach with a fob, and no doorman. The lobby panel does the doorman's job.

For buildings like this we usually plan a directory panel with video at the street door, apartment stations or phone answering for each unit, and a keypad or reader for residents, always with a key or another non-app way in, as the city's tenant data rules require.

Prewar walk-ups: the riser has been patched for decades and HPD has the record

The walk-ups on streets like Keap and Hooper are where the city's violation records cluster. In July 2026 HPD wrote a class B violation at 332 Keap Street to "properly repair or replace the broken or defective intercom system" for apartment 3K on the fifth story. In December 2025 it wrote one at 178 Hooper Street for the "bell buzzer/intercom from building vestibule" serving an apartment on the fifth story. The wording tells you what tenants live with: a fifth-floor apartment with a station that does not ring or does not release, in a building with no elevator.

Many of these buildings predate the 1968 line in §50-a, so the duty to install an intercom where none existed arises only when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask in writing. Once a system is there, though, the Housing Maintenance Code section cited in every one of these violations, §27-2005, requires the owner to keep it in good repair. For an owner tired of repairing the same riser, a full replacement is usually cheaper over five years than another round of patches, and we price it both ways.

Rehabilitated and HDFC buildings: a board, a regulatory budget and residents who have waited

Los Sures, the community housing organization founded on the Southside in 1972, describes its work as the rehabilitation and construction of more than 5,000 units in over 340 buildings and co-ops, and CBS New York reported that its work has grown to 44 properties. Buildings of that history are now reaching the age at which their entry systems were last replaced a generation ago. Some are managed by a nonprofit, some are HDFC co-ops run by volunteer shareholder boards.

These are careful buyers, and rightly so. We write the quote so a board can read it at a meeting: what is reused, what is new, what the system costs over ten years including any subscription, and how residents without smartphones, who are often the older shareholders, will answer the door. Where a board can only fund part of the work this year, we tell it which part keeps the building safe first.

Towers: the eighth floor is a long way from a street door that will not release

HPD's records show the other end of the scale. In November 2024 it wrote a violation at 73 Wilson Street for apartment 8E on the eighth story; in August 2024 one at 450 Kent Avenue for apartment 9K on the ninth story; in June 2026 one at 126 Clymer Street for apartment 10D on the tenth story, describing a "return buzzer" that did not work. In a tall building a dead station is not an inconvenience; it means a resident takes the elevator down every time a visitor or a delivery arrives.

Towers need a panel with a directory, two-wire or IP apartment stations depending on what the risers will carry, battery-backed power in a locked room and, very often, a second station at a side or garage door. Public housing towers such as Independence Towers and the Taylor–Wythe Houses are managed by NYCHA or its private PACT partners and order their entry systems through those managers, not through individual tenants; residents there should report a broken intercom to management.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Violation Line, Ring the Top Floor, Then Find the Last Splice

This is how our technician spends the first part of a visit to an existing South Williamsburg building, before anything is priced.

First, the record. Every HPD intercom violation names the apartment, the story and the wall where the station hangs, as in "apartment 3K, 5th story." Before the visit the office looks the building up, so the technician arrives knowing which stations the city has already found dead. Those are tested first, from the street panel, with someone standing in the apartment.

Second, the top floor. The technician presses the button for an apartment on the highest floor and walks up to it, listening on the way for the chime and checking whether voice carries both ways and the strike releases. On a walk-up, the top floor is where a weak power supply or a long run of tired cable shows first.

Third, the splices. In the cellar the technician follows the riser cable from the power supply to the point where it rises, and counts the splices. Every patch a previous repair left behind is a place the next fault will start. A riser with one or two clean splices is worth keeping; one with a splice on every floor is usually the reason the building keeps getting violations, and it goes on the quote as new cable.

The City's Intercom Record

What 107 HPD Intercom Violations Say About South Williamsburg Doors, and What Owners Should Do With Them

HPD's open data shows what actually happens at doors here, and it is worth reading before anyone spends money. Our November 11, 2026 query found 107 intercom violations and 320 intercom complaint problems since January 1, 2025 across ZIP codes 11206, 11211 and 11249, with the complaints split 159, 106 and 55. Those ZIP codes reach beyond South Williamsburg, so treat the totals as the wider area's; the street-level records below are inside the neighborhood.

Three things stand out when you read the individual records. The first is the wording. Nearly every violation reads "§ 27-2005 HMC: properly repair or replace the broken or defective intercom system from building entrance to" a named wall in a named room of a named apartment. HPD is not citing the building for lacking an intercom; it is citing the owner for failing to maintain one. The duty that matters for most South Williamsburg buildings is the duty to keep what is there working.

The second is where the failures are. The records inside the neighborhood name apartments on high floors again and again: the fifth story at 332 Keap Street and 178 Hooper Street, the sixth story at 186 Clymer Street, the eighth at 73 Wilson Street, the ninth at 450 Kent Avenue and the tenth at 126 Clymer Street. That matches what we see. Faults accumulate at the far end of a riser, where voltage drop, old splices and the longest runs combine, and they are felt most by residents who have the longest walk to the door.

The third is the status. Of the South Williamsburg records we read, several are marked "violation dismissed," one at 381 Hooper Street is marked "violation closed," and the newest, at 332 Keap Street and 360 Bedford Avenue, still read "NOV sent out," meaning the notice has gone to the owner and the correction is pending. A dismissed or closed violation tells you the city's file on that item ended; it does not tell you whether the same riser will fail again next winter, or whether the repair was a new station on the same tired cable.

How we use the record on a South Williamsburg job

  • Before the visit. We look up the building's violations and complaints so the technician knows which apartments the city has already found without a working station.
  • When pricing. If the record shows intercom violations at several apartments on the upper floors, we price the riser as well as the stations, because replacing stations alone on a failing riser is how buildings end up back in the record.
  • When scheduling. An open class B violation comes with a correction period. We schedule against it and tell you honestly if a special-order panel will not arrive inside it, so a repair can hold the door until the new system goes in.
  • At handover. We leave a test sheet that lists every apartment with its result for call, voice and release, the document an owner needs when certifying a correction to HPD.

One honest limit: we install and document the system; we do not file certifications with HPD or represent owners before it. That is the owner's or managing agent's job, and our test sheet is what they file with.

New Construction

Planning the Buzzer on a New South Williamsburg Building Before the Walls Close

South Williamsburg's new buildings are going up on lots that New York YIMBY describes as vacant, so there is no old system to reuse and no excuse for a bad one. The entry system on a new building is cheapest and best when it is designed alongside the rest of the low-voltage work. Here is the order we work in with developers, architects and general contractors.

At design: count the households, not the apartments

The drawings show units. The entry system has to serve people. A three-unit building of family duplexes needs three buttons and six or more indoor stations. A mixed-use building with a community facility needs a second door station for the people who use that floor and a third for the store. We mark every door station, indoor station, release, power supply and riser route on a single plan sheet the architect can drop into the set.

At framing: sleeves, boxes and the riser path

Before the slabs are poured or the shafts are closed, the riser needs a dedicated sleeve or conduit from the cellar to every floor, separate from the electrician's power risers. Back boxes for indoor stations go in while the studs are open, at the height we agree with the owner, usually near a kitchen doorway and at the top of the internal stair of each duplex. The street door frame is ordered prepared for the electric strike the door hardware schedule calls for, so nobody cuts a new steel frame on site.

At finishing: panel, stations and the test

The panel goes on once the facade is finished, the stations once the walls are painted, and the system is programmed and tested before the temporary certificate of occupancy inspections. We test every apartment from the street, with the doors closed, and leave the developer a wiring record and a programming record to hand to the condo board or managing agent.

A synagogue, a yeshiva or a community hall has a different door problem from an apartment building. It is locked most of the time and open to hundreds of people at a time a few times a day. Staff need to see who is outside before opening it, and anyone inside needs to be able to leave instantly. A video door station with a release, a camera view on the office screen and a lock that always opens from inside is the core of most of the door projects we plan for these buildings.

Many of these projects are now paid for with grants. The Governor's September 8, 2026 announcement said the latest Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes round distributed a record $70 million to 290 nonprofits, with 147 grants totaling $35,792,289 in New York City, and that eligible organizations could request up to $250,000 each. Eligible organizations include religious organizations and houses of worship, community and civic centers, cultural museums and daycare centers. What that means in practice for a door project here:

Rules and Responsibilities

Which South Williamsburg Buildings Must Have a Buzzer, What It Has to Do and Who Pays

  • Newer buildings of eight or more apartments must have one. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires a self-locking main entrance and an intercom that allows voice communication and remote release in Class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments.
  • Older buildings install one when a majority of tenants ask. For buildings put up before that date, the duty arises when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing, with installation started within 90 days and completed within six months.
  • Every existing system must be kept working. The intercom violations written in South Williamsburg cite Housing Maintenance Code §27-2005, the owner's duty to keep the building and its systems in good repair. As the New York Times put it in January 2024, "A working intercom isn't just a convenience when visitors or food deliveries arrive — it's a legal requirement in most buildings."
  • Small buildings are the owner's choice. A building of fewer than eight apartments, like a three-unit family condo or a two-family row house, is outside §50-a's mandate, which is why tenants on r/AskNYC regularly ask whether a four-unit or a brand-new three-unit Brooklyn building has to have a buzzer at all.
  • Keyless systems bring data rules. HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) covers owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems: consent "in writing, or through a mobile application," authentication data destroyed "no later than 90 days" after collection unless anonymized, and no requirement that tenants use a smart access system to enter their own apartments.
  • Magnetic locks need the full package. Electromagnetic locks are made only fail-safe, and the building code's sensor-release provisions require a sensor on the egress side, a manual "PUSH TO EXIT" device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within 5 feet of the door that keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, release on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, and UL 294 listed equipment.
  • Fire doors stay latched. Allegion's guide notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes, so a rated door into a stair or cellar latches in a fire; the release must carry the right listing for the door.

None of this is legal advice for a particular building. An owner answering a tenants' request, a board planning a keyless lobby or a developer reading §50-a against a new filing should confirm the details with a housing lawyer, the managing agent or the architect. What we provide is a system that meets the requirement that applies and paperwork showing what was installed.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in South Williamsburg: Questions Answered

New buildings and family-size apartments

We are developing a six-story building with three family duplexes. Should a second indoor station on the upper level be standard in every apartment, or an option each buyer pays for?

Make the cable standard and the station a choice. The expensive part of a second station is not the station itself but the cable from the riser to the upper level of the duplex, and that cable costs very little while the framing is open and a great deal once the apartment is finished, painted and lived in. If every duplex gets a back box and a pulled cable on the upper level during construction, a buyer who wants the second station later pays only for the station and an hour of work.

In practice most developers building family-size apartments in South Williamsburg find the second station is expected, not optional. In a home of nearly 2,800 square feet over two floors, a station by the apartment's entry door on the lower level is out of earshot of the bedrooms, and the kitchen, where the household spends most of the day, may be on either floor. We would put both stations in the base specification, price them per apartment on the quote so the developer can see the cost, and leave the buyer to choose video or audio for the second one.

Our new building will have condos upstairs, a community facility on the lower floors and a store at street level. Can people who come for the community floor be kept out of the residential stair?

Yes, and it is far easier to plan now than to fix later. The principle is that each group of users is admitted only to the doors it needs. Residents' visitors are buzzed through the residents' entrance into a lobby that leads to the elevator and residential stair. Visitors to the community facility arrive at its own entrance, are buzzed in by its own staff, and reach only its floors. Where the building's design means the two groups share a stair for exit, the doors from that stair back into the residential floors are kept locked from the stair side while always opening from the residential side for anyone leaving.

The hardware is ordinary; the planning is what matters. It needs a door schedule that says, for every door, who may enter from which side and when, agreed by the developer and whoever will run the community facility. We draw that schedule, the architect puts it on the plans, and the fire-safety rules on exit doors are respected at every step.

The city's record and older buildings

Several intercom violations on our block show "violation dismissed" in HPD's records. Does that mean those intercoms were fixed?

Not necessarily. A dismissed status means HPD's file on that violation was closed without the violation standing, and the public dataset does not say why. Among the South Williamsburg records we read, violations at 126 and 186 Clymer Street, 178 Hooper Street, 208 Broadway, 268 Union Avenue, 73 Wilson Street and 450 Kent Avenue all carry that status. Some will have been repaired; the dataset alone cannot tell you which.

For an owner, the useful question is not the status but the pattern. If the same building has had intercom violations at several apartments on its upper floors over two or three years, whatever the status, the riser or the power supply is probably the common cause, and another round of station-by-station repairs will lead to another round of complaints. That is the situation in which we price a full replacement alongside the repair, so the owner can compare five-year costs.

HPD wrote our walk-up for one dead station on the fifth floor. Can you replace just that apartment's station, or does the riser have to be replaced too?

Often just the station, if the cable to it meters sound. The first test is at the cellar end: we meter the pair that serves the fifth-floor apartment and compare it with a pair to a working apartment on the same line. If it reads the same, the fault is in the station or its connection, and a matching station is a quick fix. If the reading is wrong, the fault is in the cable, and we follow it floor by floor until we find the failed section.

What decides whether the whole riser should go is what we find on the way. A riser with clean conductors and one damaged section is worth repairing. One where each floor has a splice from an old repair, or where the cable's insulation has hardened and cracks when it is moved, will keep producing violations like the ones written at 332 Keap Street and 178 Hooper Street, and we would say so in writing with both prices.

Tenants on the top floors of our building keep calling 311 about the intercom. Can a group of tenants hire you directly to put in a new system?

Not for the building's own system. The street door, the panel and the riser belong to the building, and only the owner, the board or the managing agent can order work on them. What tenants can do is use the routes the law gives them. In a building with an existing system, a complaint to HPD leads to an inspection and, if the intercom is broken, a violation under Housing Maintenance Code §27-2005. In an older building without one, Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires the owner to install a self-locking door and an intercom once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request it in writing.

If the owner then calls us, we are happy to meet the tenants' representatives as part of the walkthrough, because they know which apartments fail and when. Tenants on r/AskNYC describe the same frustration everywhere in the city, a top-floor walk-up resident who "can't allow guests, packages, food delivery" into the building, and the fastest fix is usually an owner who sees a written price for solving it once.

Shuls, schools and community buildings

Our shul received a Securing Communities Against Hate Crimes grant that includes the entrance. What does your quote need to show for the grant file?

It should match the approved project exactly and break the cost into parts a reviewer can check. Our quote for a grant-funded door lists each door separately, then each item on it: the door station, the indoor or office station, the electric strike or lock, the power supply, any exit device, the cable and the labor, with any work by a licensed electrician shown as its own line. It names the make and model of each piece of equipment, and we attach the cut sheets.

After installation you receive a completion record for the file: photos of each door before and after, the test sheet showing each release and exit tested, and the wiring record. The Governor's September 2026 announcement describes eligible improvements, including locks and access controls, and notes that applicants submit vulnerability assessments; what was approved for your organization is in your award documents, and we price to those, not to our own idea of what the building needs.

Hundreds of people leave our hall at once after an event. Does an electric release on the main doors slow them down?

It should not, and if it would, it is the wrong hardware. On a door with an electric strike, the strike only controls the outside: from inside, people leave by pushing the bar or turning the lever exactly as they would without it, and Allegion's guide confirms that exit works the same whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. On assembly doors we pair the release with the exit hardware the door already needs, so leaving the building is a single push.

Our yeshiva's office is on the second floor. How can staff see and admit a parent at the street door without walking down every time?

With a video door station at the street entrance and a video station in the office with a release button. The parent presses the call button, the office sees and speaks to them, and staff release the door from their desk. If more than one room needs to answer, such as the office and an administrator's room, each gets a station or a release button, and every one rings when the street door calls.

Two details make this work in a school. First, the camera at the door should show the whole person and the space behind them, so staff can see whether anyone is following the parent in. Second, the release time should be short, just long enough for one person to enter, and the door's closer set so it shuts and latches firmly behind them. Children leaving the building go out by the exit hardware as normal; the buzzer only controls who comes in.

Co-ops, owners and shops

Our HDFC co-op wants video, but many of our shareholders do not use apps. Is a wired video system without phone answering a sensible choice in 2026?

Yes, and for many co-ops it is the best one. A wired video system puts a station with a screen and a release button in every apartment, works without anyone's phone or Wi-Fi, carries no monthly subscription and is easy for an older shareholder to use: the screen lights when someone rings, and one button opens the door. Systems such as Aiphone's GT Series are made for multi-tenant buildings and, in Aiphone's words, offer "the advantage of using existing wire and infrastructure," which keeps the cable cost down where the risers test sound.

Phone answering can be added for the shareholders who want it on many systems, but it should be an extra, never the only way to answer. That also keeps the co-op clear of a per-apartment software fee it would have to pay every year whether residents use the app or not.

Our Lee Avenue store wants a buzzer on its back door, which opens into the building's cellar corridor next to the apartments' meters. Whose permission do we need, and should it join the building's system?

You need the building owner's written approval, because the door, the corridor and the cellar are part of the building, and the work touches shared space the residents' system also runs through. Once approved, the store's buzzer should be its own system: its own call station outside the back door, a release controlled from inside the store, and its own power supply on the store's circuit. It should not share a power supply or a cable with the apartments.

Keeping it separate protects everyone. A delivery buzzed in by the store reaches the store, not the residential stair, a fault in the store's equipment never takes out the apartments' door, and when the store changes tenants the next one can remove or replace its buzzer without anyone touching the residents' system.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in South Williamsburg

Ask an AI assistant or a search summary what a buzzer costs in New York and you get confident numbers pulled from installers' cost guides, many of them written for Manhattan towers or for a single house. Some are right somewhere and wrong for a three-family condo on Wallabout Street or a walk-up on Keap Street. Here are seven claims we see repeated, each checked against its source and against what we find at doors here.

"Basic audio buzzers cost $500 to $1,500 per unit installed"

That range comes from Vertex Security's guide to intercom upgrade costs in New York City, which prices the entry-level hardware tier per unit. It is a reasonable figure for some buildings, but "per unit" does a lot of work in that sentence.

On our Brooklyn list, an audio station with a new release for a small building or a single door runs $400 to $900 for the whole job, and a full multi-unit entry system starts at about $2,500. A 20-apartment audio system on sound risers lands near the bottom of the multi-unit range, not at twenty times a per-unit figure.

The lesson is to ask any installer, us included, for the whole-building price with the parts listed, not a per-unit rate.

"Intercom installation costs $1,500 to $5,000 per unit or more"

ASM Integrators' guide to intercom costs in New York and New Jersey gives that range, "depending on the system's complexity and the building's infrastructure." For a high-end IP system in a luxury building it may hold. For a South Williamsburg lottery rental or HDFC co-op it would put a 45-apartment system somewhere between $67,500 and $225,000.

That is far above what wired systems cost on our list, where a multi-unit building runs from about $2,500 and a larger video system can pass $10,000.

Where a building truly needs new cable to every floor, we price it as cable, by the riser, so the board can see exactly what it is paying for.

"A video intercom for a small building with one entrance costs about $4,000 to $5,000"

Douglas Security's 2026 NYC price guide gives that figure, and separately puts IP or cloud intercoms at about $2,500 to $7,000 per door fully installed.

A three-family condo with one street door needs a video station, three or six indoor stations and a release. On our list that is a $700 to $1,800 job when the cable is roughed in during construction, and only buildings with many apartments move into the multi-unit range.

"Small building" covers everything from three apartments to thirty, so a single figure for it tells an owner very little.

"Wireless systems cost $350 to $1,100, so they are always the cheap option"

NYHLS's door buzzer guide does give wireless systems at $350 to $1,100 installed against $450 to $1,900 for wired ones.

In an apartment building the release in the frame still needs wire and power, the panel still needs dependable power, and wireless apartment stations must reach through brick walls and concrete floors. ButterflyMX markets its cellular intercom as needing "no building wiring," but that describes the panel's connection, not the strike, and residents answer on phones or added stations.

Wireless is a tool for awkward runs, not a way to skip the door hardware, and it is rarely the cheapest choice for a walk-up whose risers still test sound.

"New York law requires every apartment building to have an intercom"

Close, but not quite. The New York Times was careful to say a working intercom is "a legal requirement in most buildings." Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires one automatically in Class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments; older buildings must install one when a majority of tenants ask in writing; smaller buildings are outside the mandate.

That is why tenants on r/AskNYC ask whether a four-unit Brooklyn building, or a brand-new three-unit one with "no buzzer and one mailbox," is allowed. In South Williamsburg the same split runs through the new construction: the 20- and 45-apartment buildings are inside the rule, the three-residence condo is not.

And wherever a system exists, the Housing Maintenance Code requires it to be kept working, which is what nearly every HPD intercom violation here cites.

"Your old wiring can carry any new system"

Aiphone's own upgrade guidance is more careful: "If the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused." The condition is the whole point.

In the walk-ups that keep appearing in HPD's records, the riser has often been spliced so many times that it is the cause of the failures. Reusing it saves money only until the next violation.

So the old cable is metered before the quote, pair by pair, and if any section is not usable, the new run and its price are on the quote before you approve.

"A cloud intercom costs less because there is no wiring to run"

The installation can cost less, but the monthly bill is the part summaries leave out. Swiftlane's apartment intercom guide says software subscriptions commonly add five to fifteen dollars per unit per month, or thirty to one hundred dollars per door per month.

Cloud systems also bring HPD's Tenant Data Privacy Law into play in multiple dwellings, with its rules on consent and on destroying authentication data within 90 days, and the requirement that tenants are never forced to use the app to reach their own apartments.

For an HDFC co-op or a family condo board watching every dollar, a wired system with no subscription is often the better buy over ten years, and we quote both so the board can compare.
