The Neighborhood

Why Orange County Properties Need CCTV Planned Around the Crossroads

Orange County is where two of the busiest freight routes in the Northeast cross. Interstate 84 runs east to west from the Newburgh-Beacon Bridge to Port Jervis, the Thruway runs north to south past Harriman and Newburgh, and Route 17 (the future I-86) cuts diagonally through Monroe, Goshen and Middletown. That geography explains most of the camera work we do here. Trucks, shoppers and commuters all pass through the same handful of interchanges, and the properties that sit beside those interchanges, from distribution buildings to outlet stores to the hotels and gas stations that serve them, have very different problems from a farmhouse in Pine Island or a brick row house in Newburgh's East End.

So the first question on every Orange County CCTV job is not "how many cameras?" It is "what kind of loss are we trying to stop, and where does it happen?" A warehouse loses money to freight that leaves the wrong door or arrives damaged, with no footage to settle the claim. A store at an outlet center loses it to shoplifting. A city landlord loses it to break-ins through rear doors and to vehicles damaged or taken from a back lot. A farm loses fuel, tools and equipment from fields nobody watches overnight. A good closed circuit television system for each of those looks different, even when the cameras in the box are the same.

When you break it down by the department that took the report, a few agencies along the shopping corridors account for most of the theft, while the City of Newburgh carries a share of the burglaries far larger than its size.

The warehouse belt along I-84

The fastest-growing building type in the county is the distribution center. In February 2025, JLL announced the start of the 146,075-square-foot I-84 Orange County Logistics Center in Montgomery, with 36-foot clear ceilings, 31 dock doors, two drive-in doors and 16 trailer parking spaces, and listed Amazon, FedEx, UPS, Staples, Medline and XPO Logistics among its neighbors. In July 2026, HGAR reported that Walmart had filed plans with the Town of Wallkill for a distribution center of about 1.5 million square feet on roughly 200 acres near Route 17K and I-84, still in early planning board review.

“Montgomery offers a prime logistics location that has seen significant growth thanks to its deep talent pool and business friendly programs.”

Buildings like that are a different scale of CCTV system from anything residential. Thirty-one dock doors means at least thirty-one places where freight changes hands, plus a truck court, a trailer yard, a guard booth, the employee lot and a warehouse floor with racking aisles under a ceiling more than three stories up. The cameras have to read trailer numbers in the yard, show which pallet went onto which truck, and keep the footage long enough to answer a freight claim weeks later. We cover how we wire that in the commercial section below.

Two power companies, one county

Orange County is split between two electric utilities, and a recorder that loses power loses footage no matter whose lines failed. Orange & Rockland Utilities lists Middletown, Wallkill, Goshen, Chester, Monroe, Woodbury, Warwick, Deerpark, Port Jervis and most of the southern and western towns among the New York communities it serves. Central Hudson covers the northeast corner, including the Newburgh and Montgomery areas. Both report storm outages by town, and both have long outages on their record. In O&R's December 20, 2025 storm update, 238 Orange County customers were still without power the morning after the storm, including 70 in Chester, 50 in Deerpark and 32 in Warwick.

For a home that is an inconvenience. For a warehouse or a store, the hours after a storm are exactly when doors are propped open, alarms are offline and freight sits waiting, so the recorder and the PoE switch go on a battery backup on every commercial job we do here, and a generator circuit where the building has one.

Three cities, many villages, a lot of farmland

Outside the highway corridors, Orange County is three small cities, two dozen villages and a great deal of open land. Newburgh has blocks of 19th-century brick row houses and storefronts, many inside the East End Historic District. Middletown has a downtown of brick commercial buildings along North Street and Victorian houses on the hills around it. Port Jervis sits where New York meets New Jersey and Pennsylvania along the Delaware. Villages such as Goshen, Warwick, Monroe, Chester, Walden and Washingtonville have Main Street storefronts with apartments above. And from Florida and Pine Island west toward the New Jersey line, the black dirt region grows onions and other vegetables on dark, flat, drained farmland, with packing sheds, equipment barns and fuel tanks scattered across fields that can be a long way from the farmhouse.

The cities each bring their own wrinkle. In Newburgh, the useful camera on a row house is usually at the back, because the alleys and rear lots behind Broadway and Liberty Street are where cars sit overnight and where a forced door is out of sight of the street. In Middletown, many big old houses have been split into apartments, so one building may have three entrances, a shared driveway and a detached garage, and the owner wants all of it on one recorder while each tenant's own door stays private. In Port Jervis, downtown buildings near the rail yard and the river are older and often damp at the foundation, which decides where a recorder can safely live.

The suburban towns are a different job again. Developments in Monroe, Chester, Blooming Grove, Montgomery and the Town of Wallkill are mostly sided houses with attached garages, and many of their owners commute to New York City or northern New Jersey. For them the question is usually remote viewing and sensible alerts during the work day: a driveway camera that tells them when a delivery arrives, a back door camera that stays quiet unless a person steps onto the deck, and a recorder that keeps going when the power flickers.

Each of those calls for its own approach to CCTV installation, and the rest of this page goes through them one at a time.

How the work changes by building

Brick row houses. Old brick is soft and the mortar is softer. We anchor into mortar joints rather than the face of the brick wherever the mount allows, use a junction box so the camera sits flat on an uneven wall, and bring the cable through one sealed hole straight into the house behind the mount. On a row house the most useful cameras are often at the back: one on the rear door, one on the yard or the alley gate, and one aimed down the back lot where cars park. If the house is inside a City of Newburgh historic district, see the note on facade approval in the questions below before anything is fixed to the front.

Victorians and two-families. Balloon framing in older wood houses can actually help: open stud bays run from the basement to the attic, so a cable can often be dropped inside a wall with no visible run. Porches complicate camera angles, because a camera under a deep porch roof sees the porch but not the walk. We usually put one camera on the porch ceiling for faces at the door and another on the corner of the house for the walk and the driveway. In a two-family, the owner decides who sees which camera, and the tenant's unit stays off camera entirely.

Raised ranches and colonials. Vinyl siding over sheathing is the most common exterior we mount on. The camera goes on a mounting block so the siding can still expand and contract, the hole goes through the block, and the cable runs through the basement rim joist or the attic. A raised ranch has its front door half a level above the driveway, which changes the mounting height for a face camera; we test the angle with the camera in hand before we drill.

New construction. In a house that is still open studs, we run Cat6 from each camera position to a central closet before insulation and drywall, label both ends and leave a coil at each location. The cameras can go up on move-in day or a year later. This is the cleanest way to get CCTV installation with concealed wiring, and it costs far less than fishing cable through finished walls afterward.

Garden apartment complexes. The cameras are spread across several buildings and a parking lot, but the recorder should be in one locked room. We use the buildings' own walls and soffits for mounts, run cable through common basements or attics, and link separate buildings with outdoor-rated cable in conduit, fiber or a short wireless bridge. Owners and managers can read more on our apartment building security camera page for Orange County, which covers resident privacy and common-area rules in more depth.

Farm buildings. Steel pole barns and packing sheds are dusty, damp and full of machinery. Cameras go on framing, high enough to stay clear of loaders and tractors, in housings rated for dust and water. Where a barn has its own electric service, the run from the house to the barn is where we use fiber or a bridge rather than long copper, because the two buildings sit on different grounds and a storm surge can travel down a long cable.

Warehouse and distribution center CCTV installation

A distribution building is really four camera jobs on one recorder. The first is the gate: a camera aimed at the truck entrance with a narrow lens and a fast shutter, so the cab, the plate and the trailer number are all readable as the rig rolls through, day or night. The second is the dock face: one camera per door or per pair of doors, mounted inside above the door so it sees the trailer's open end, the dock plate and the first few pallets going on or coming off. The third is the yard: wide cameras along the trailer rows and the fence line, high on the building or on light poles, with smart detection that ignores trucks moving on schedule and flags a person walking the fence at 2 a.m. The fourth is the floor: cameras down the main aisles, at the cage where high-value product is kept, at the shipping and receiving desks, and at the employee entrance.

The cable work is what separates a warehouse job from a store job. Ceilings of 36 feet or more mean the crew works from a lift, cables ride in the building's cable tray or on J-hooks along the steel, and runs from the far end of a 500-foot building back to one recorder can pass the 100-meter limit of copper Ethernet. On larger buildings we place a network switch in an intermediate closet, fed by fiber from the main recorder room, and keep every camera run under the limit. Every camera is labeled at both ends and on the recorder by dock number, so when a driver disputes a load, the manager pulls up "Door 14" instead of "Camera 37".

Outlet, mall and shopping center stores

The Woodbury police numbers make the point: almost every index crime in that town is a larceny. For a store, retail CCTV installation is about the moments theft happens. A camera at the entrance at face height catches everyone coming in, framed tight enough to identify them. Cameras over the high-value tables and racks show hands, not just bodies. A camera at the register sees the cash drawer, the screen and the customer. A camera on the back door and the stockroom covers the losses that happen after the doors close, which in many stores are larger than shoplifting. In a mall or outlet center, the common areas and the parking lot usually belong to the landlord's own system, so a tenant's cameras stay inside the leased space and at its own entrances.

Holiday shopping season is when gaps show. Woodbury Common and the Route 300 and Route 211 stores see their heaviest traffic from Black Friday through Christmas, so we try to finish store jobs by early November and spend the last visit tuning recording quality at the registers and doors before the rush.

Hotels, gas stations and the traffic in between

New York rules every Orange County business should know

These are the state rules that come up most often on business jobs. This is general information, not legal advice; your attorney should confirm what applies to you.

  • Some rooms in a workplace never get a camera. A warehouse or store can record its floor, docks and counters, but New York Labor Law §203-c forbids an employer from video recording toilets, locker rooms or rooms for changing clothes unless a court has ordered it.
  • Microphones raise a separate question. New York is a one-party consent state, and Penal Law §250.05 makes it eavesdropping to record a conversation none of whose participants agreed. A camera's built-in mic at a sales counter can cross that line, so ours ship to businesses with audio switched off.
  • No recording where people undress or expect privacy. Penal Law §250.45 makes secret recording in such places unlawful surveillance.
  • Historic districts in the City of Newburgh. City Code Chapter 300, Article VI requires a certificate of appropriateness from the Architectural Review Commission for exterior alterations to designated properties; a facade-mounted camera with conduit can fall under it.
Your Questions Answered

Orange County CCTV Installation: Questions Answered

These are the questions Orange County owners and managers ask us most, grouped the way the calls come in: freight and warehouses first, then stores and lots, then homes, farms and the older cities.

Freight, docks and distribution buildings

How long should a warehouse keep dock footage to answer a freight damage or shortage claim?

Longer than most people set it. A recorder left on factory settings often keeps one or two weeks, while freight claims arrive much later. Under the federal Carmack rules for interstate motor carriers, 49 U.S.C. §14706, a carrier may not give a shipper less than nine months to file a loss or damage claim. By the time a customer reports that pallets arrived crushed or short, the footage of them leaving your door may already be overwritten.

We ask each warehouse how far back its claims usually reach and size the storage to match: 30, 60 or 90 days on the dock and yard cameras, and less on quieter interior views. Dock cameras do not need to record at full frame rate all night; recording on motion with a short pre-event buffer stretches the same drive much further. When a claim does come in, managers export the clip for that door and that time right away, so it is kept no matter how long the recorder holds the rest.

How many cameras does a mid-size distribution building need, and how big a recorder?

Count from the building, not from a box. A useful starting rule is one camera per dock door or per pair of doors, one or two at the truck gate, two to four along each side of the trailer yard, one at every personnel door, one or two at the shipping and receiving desks, one per main aisle and one at any high-value cage. On a building of around 150,000 square feet with 30 dock doors, that easily reaches 40 to 60 cameras.

The recorder follows the count and the retention. A 64-channel NVR with several large surveillance drives is common at that size, and we leave spare channels for the doors and yard lights added later. Very large buildings are often split across two recorders, one for the dock and yard and one for the interior, so a single failure never blinds the whole site. Every camera is quoted after we confirm the layout, since a dock camera inside the door and a pole camera at the far end of the yard are very different runs.

How do cameras stay readable when truck headlights or a low winter sun shine into the lens?

Two settings and one decision about placement. Wide dynamic range, usually labeled WDR on the spec sheet, lets a camera hold detail in the bright and dark parts of the same picture, so a driver's face stays visible against open dock doors or the glare of a headlight. A faster shutter keeps a moving cab or a plate from smearing at night. Most fixed cameras default to a slow shutter in the dark to brighten the picture, which is the right choice for a hallway and the wrong one for a gate.

Placement does the rest. We avoid aiming a camera straight down a lane where every truck's headlights hit the lens, and set the gate camera at an angle to the approach instead. On east- or west-facing docks, where the morning or evening sun sits right in the frame for part of the year, we shift the camera a few feet or use a hood. We check every outdoor camera after dark before the job is closed.

Can CCTV show which forklift hit a rack, a door or a pallet?

Yes, if the cameras are placed for it. Forklift damage happens at the ends of aisles, at the dock plates and at the overhead doors, so a camera at each end of a main aisle, looking down its length, catches most rack strikes, and the dock cameras catch doors and plates. Many forklifts carry a unit number on the mast or the counterweight; a camera at a sensible height and distance can read it.

The footage is only half of it. The recorder's clock has to be right so a supervisor can match the time against shift logs, which is why we set every recorder to sync its time automatically. And because the point is to find what happened, not to watch people all day, most managers set these cameras to record continuously and only review them when something is damaged.

How long does a CCTV installation take in a large warehouse that keeps working during the job?

Usually several days to a couple of weeks, staged so the building never stops. On a working warehouse we pull cable and mount cameras section by section, working from a lift in aisles the manager closes for us, and save the dock face for the slowest hours or a weekend. The recorder and network gear go in early, so cameras come online as each section is finished rather than all at the end.

A small store or a house is a different scale: a 4- to 8-camera residential job is often finished in a day, and a typical shop in a day or two. Every quote includes a realistic schedule. We book within 2 hours of your request and schedule 7 days a week, first come first served once the deposit is paid, but we never promise same-day installation on a building of this size.

Can cameras under a 36-foot ceiling be cleaned and re-aimed later without closing an aisle?

That is something we plan for at the install, because a camera nobody can reach does not get maintained. Where we can, we mount high-bay cameras on columns or walls at a height a standard lift can reach from the main aisle, rather than in the middle of a rack row. Varifocal lenses that can be zoomed and refocused from the recorder let us adjust a view from the ground without touching the camera.

For cleaning, the dusty spots are the dock doors and anywhere near propane forklift exhaust or a battery charging area. We note on the camera list which cameras need a lift and which are reachable from a ladder, so a maintenance visit can be scheduled for a quiet shift and finished quickly.

What should cameras cover at a bus depot, fleet yard or truck terminal where vehicles park overnight?

The entrance first, then the fuel island, the maintenance bay doors and the parking rows. The entrance camera should show every vehicle in and out with its number or plate. The fuel island needs a camera that sees the pump and the vehicle together, because fuel theft is the most common loss at yards like these. Catalytic converters, batteries and tools are the other targets, so cameras along the parking rows should be low enough to see under and between vehicles, not just over their roofs.

Yards are dark and big, so we lean on pole-mounted cameras with color night vision where the lot lights allow it and infrared where they do not, and on smart detection that alerts on a person walking among parked vehicles after hours while ignoring the drivers on the morning schedule.

Stores, lots and the highway strips

Can a camera on my building read the plates of cars going by on Route 17K or Route 300?

Not reliably, and it is the wrong goal. A plate at highway speed, at an angle, in headlight glare at night, crosses the frame in a fraction of a second. Even a dedicated license plate recognition camera is built to work where vehicles slow down and pass through a known spot, such as your own lot entrance, a gate or a drive-through lane.

So we put LPR camera installation where it works: at your entrance and exit, aimed along the lane at a shallow angle, with a narrow lens and a fast shutter. That catches the plate of every vehicle that actually comes onto your property, which is what you need after an incident. A separate wide camera shows the car's color, make and direction, which helps when a plate is covered or missing.

What camera layout helps a store prove a group shoplifting incident, not just one person?

Overlapping views. When several people work together, one distracts staff while others fill bags in another aisle, and a single camera rarely shows the whole thing. The layout that holds up is an entrance camera that identifies each person coming in, cameras over the high-value areas that show hands and merchandise, and a wide view of the sales floor that shows how the group moved together, all on one recorder with matching time stamps.

The exit camera closes the loop: the same people leaving with the merchandise. Exported together, those clips tell the story from arrival to departure, which is what police and loss prevention teams ask for. We name every camera on the recorder by location, so a manager can export "Entrance", "Fragrance table" and "Exit" for the same five minutes without guessing.

Should a closed business use cameras with a speaker or siren to warn people off the lot?

Often it is worth it. Active deterrence cameras switch on a light, a siren or a recorded warning when a person enters a zone you set, such as the lot after closing or the area behind the building. Some models also let you speak through the camera from your phone. On a dealer lot, a contractor yard or the back of a store, that is often enough to send someone on their way before anything happens.

The trick is the zone and the schedule. A deterrent that fires every time a customer walks past at 7 p.m. annoys everyone, so we set the zone tightly and arm it only after hours. Our F and D Series house-brand systems include active deterrent cameras with siren and lights, and we can add them to other systems too.

Homes, farms and the older cities

Does a camera on a historic Newburgh facade need approval from the city?

It can. The City of Newburgh's Code, Chapter 300, Article VI, has the Architectural Review Commission approve exterior alterations to designated properties and properties in the city's historic districts, including the East End Historic District, through a certificate of appropriateness. The city's own guidance on its land use process notes that a certificate is needed for material changes in appearance, including light fixtures. A camera with visible conduit on the front of a designated building is the kind of change to ask about first.

In practice we avoid the question where we can. Most of the useful cameras on a row house go at the back, in the alley and on the side, out of view from the street. Where a front camera is needed, we choose a small, dark camera that matches the trim, mount it in a mortar joint or under a cornice, and run the cable inside rather than up the face of the building. If you are unsure whether your property is designated, call the city's planning office before we drill. This is general information, not legal advice.

What should cameras watch on a black dirt farm with fuel tanks and equipment parked in open fields?

Start where things are taken: the fuel tank, the equipment barn, the packing shed doors and the lane in from the road. A camera on the fuel tank should see the nozzle and the vehicle filling up. A camera on the barn should see the doors and the yard in front of them. A camera at the end of the farm lane, where the property meets the road, records every vehicle that comes in, which on open flat land is the only way in.

Fields are the hard part, because there is nothing to mount on and no power. Where equipment sits out in a field for weeks, a camera on a pole with a solar panel and a cellular connection is the practical choice, with the understanding that it records clips rather than the round-the-clock video a wired camera gives. Everything near the buildings stays wired to the recorder in the house or the office, with fiber or a wireless bridge to the far barns.

Can alerts run only on a schedule, such as after closing or while we are at work?

Yes. The recorder can keep recording around the clock while sending phone alerts only during the hours you choose. A store can get alerts for people near the back door from closing to opening. A family that commutes from Monroe, Middletown or Harriman can get driveway and door alerts during the work day and turn them off in the evening. Each camera, and each type of detection, can have its own schedule.

The recording itself never pauses. If something happens at 6 p.m. on a camera whose alerts are off, it is still on the drive. We set the schedules with you at handover and adjust them after the first week, when you can see which alerts are useful and which are just noise.

Is a CCTV system the same as a monitored alarm, and do we need both?

They do different jobs. A monitored alarm detects a break-in with door contacts and motion sensors, and a central station calls you and the police. A CCTV system records what happens and lets you see it live and afterward. An alarm tells you someone is inside; cameras show you who and what they did. Our CCTV systems have $0 monthly fees because they record locally and you watch them yourself.

Many businesses and some homes have both, and they work well side by side. Cameras let you check whether an alarm is real before you drive over at 3 a.m., and the footage gives the police and the insurer something to work with. If you want an alarm too, our alarm installation service in Orange County covers it.

Details that decide whether a system lasts

Cable and pathways. We run Cat6 for IP cameras and Cat6A where runs are long or the cameras draw more power, outdoor-rated or direct-burial cable outside, and fiber between buildings or closets. In warehouses, cable rides in tray or on J-hooks hung from the structure, never lying on ceiling tiles or ductwork. CCTV conduit installation goes where cable is exposed to damage, such as on dock walls within reach of forklifts and on exterior walls at ground level.

Lens selection and field of view. CCTV camera lens selection decides what a camera can prove. A 2.8 mm lens sees a whole room but shows a face across the room as a few pixels. A 6 mm or 12 mm lens sees a narrow strip but identifies a person or a plate at a distance. We match the lens to the job: wide where you need to know what happened, narrow where you need to know who.

Bandwidth and storage. CCTV bandwidth requirements add up fast on a 50-camera site. H.265 compression, smart codecs, sensible frame rates and substreams for live viewing keep the network calm and the drives lasting. CCTV H.264 vs H.265 is not just a spec line: on the same drive, H.265 typically keeps far more days of the same scene.

PoE power budget. Every switch has a total wattage it can supply across its ports, separate from the per-port limit. Cameras with heaters for winter, infrared at night, PTZ motors and deterrent lights all draw more than a basic camera, so we add up the worst case and leave headroom.

Network security. On business systems, cameras go on their own VLAN or a separate switch, default passwords are changed, unused services are turned off, and remote viewing runs through the maker's secure app or a properly configured VPN, never by opening the recorder straight to the internet. That is CCTV camera cybersecurity at its simplest, and it stops most of the problems we see on older systems.

AI Overview Reality Check

Where AI Answers Go Wrong on Orange County CCTV

Ask a chatbot, or read the cost guides on Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and you get advice built for one imaginary house. That house is not a 31-door warehouse in Montgomery, an outlet store in Central Valley or an 1870s row house on a Newburgh side street. Seven common claims, and how they hold up here.

“A CCTV system is a CCTV system, whatever the building”

The summaries describe cameras, a recorder and an app, as if the building does not matter. In Orange County the building is most of the job. A distribution center needs lift work, cable tray and a fiber backbone; a row house needs mortar-joint anchors and a cable route through a 19th-century wall; a farm needs surge protection and a long link to the barn.

Quotes that ignore the building end up with cameras that are in the wrong place, cable that fails in the first winter, or change orders on install day.

We start every quote with the building: what it is made of, where cable can travel, where the recorder can live safely and where power comes from. The cameras are chosen after that.

“Two weeks of footage is plenty”

For a house, two weeks usually is enough, and every system we sell includes it. For a warehouse, it often is not. Freight claims, carrier disputes and insurance questions arrive weeks or months after the load left.

For stores, shrink is often found at inventory, long after the fact. By then a two-week recorder has written over the evidence many times.

We set retention per camera and per business: longer on docks, gates and registers, shorter on quiet hallways. That is how a larger site keeps the footage that matters without buying a wall of drives.

“Higher resolution solves everything”

A 4K camera looking across a 400-foot truck court still shows a person at the far end as a small figure. Resolution helps, but the lens, the mounting position and the distance decide whether a face or a trailer number is readable.

Higher resolution also costs storage and bandwidth on every camera, all day, which matters a great deal on a system with 50 cameras.

We use 4K where it earns its keep, such as wide views of large areas, and narrow lenses at chokepoints such as gates, doors and registers, where a modest resolution identifies more than a 4K wide shot ever will.

“Put the cameras on the outside of the building and you are covered”

Outdoor cameras are essential, but in a store or a warehouse much of the loss happens inside: at the register, in the stockroom, at the dock, in the cage. A building with ten cameras on the outside and none inside can show a truck arriving and leaving without ever showing what went onto it.

The reverse is true on row houses and farms, where the outside is everything and the inside is private.

We split coverage by where the loss happens for that building, which is why the police agency numbers earlier on this page matter: larceny towns get more inside and register coverage, burglary-heavy blocks get more doors and alleys.

“Cloud cameras are simpler than a recorder”

For one camera on a front door, maybe. For a building, cloud-only cameras mean a monthly fee per camera, an upload stream for every camera running all day, and no recording when the internet drops, which is exactly what happens in a storm or when someone cuts the line.

A local recorder keeps recording through an internet outage, and with a battery backup it keeps going through a short power outage too.

Our systems record locally with $0 monthly fees. If you want a copy of important events off site, we add it on top of local recording, not instead of it.

“Motion alerts will tell you about every intruder”

Basic motion detection reacts to anything: trucks moving on schedule, headlights on the road, rain, snow and the shadows of a flag. A warehouse yard or a busy lot set to plain motion produces hundreds of alerts a day, and people stop looking.

Smart detection that recognizes people and vehicles, with zones drawn around the fence line, the back door or the fuel tank, and schedules that switch alerts on only after hours, cuts that to the handful that matter.

We tune detection during the first week and adjust zones once we see what the site actually does at night.

“Any handyman can put up a camera kit”

Anyone can screw a camera to a wall. The parts that fail are the ones nobody sees: an outdoor run with indoor cable that cracks in a year, a long run past the Ethernet limit that drops on cold nights, a recorder on a shelf with no battery, a system left on the default password, a camera with the sun in its lens every afternoon.

Professional CCTV installation is worth it when the footage has to hold up for an insurer, a carrier, a court or the police.

Can you mount cameras on the light poles in our parking lot?
+

Usually, yes, if the poles are yours or your landlord agrees. Light poles give height and a view down the lot rows, and they often carry power. We mount with brackets made for poles, bring the cable in through the pole base or in conduit, and add surge protection. Poles owned by the town or a utility are not ours to use, so cameras for those areas go on your building or on a pole of your own.

Can I put cameras on the warehouse floor or in the break room to watch employees?
+

Work areas such as the warehouse floor, the docks, the shipping desk and the cash office can be covered, and many employers tell staff in writing where cameras are. New York Labor Law §203-c bars employers from video recording in restrooms, locker rooms and rooms set aside for changing clothes, and we leave audio off in workplaces unless the owner decides otherwise. This is general information, not legal advice.

Will wired cameras interfere with the Wi-Fi our scanners and forklift tablets use?
+

No. Wired cameras send their video over Cat6 or fiber, not over the air, so they do not take up the radio space your handheld scanners and tablets depend on. We also keep the cameras on their own network or VLAN, so heavy video traffic does not slow down the warehouse system or the office computers.
