The Neighborhood

Why Bath Beach Properties Need Access Control

Bath Beach is a small, dense corner of southwest Brooklyn. Wikipedia puts its edges at 86th Street on the north, 14th Avenue and Dyker Beach Park on the west, Stillwell Avenue on the east and Gravesend Bay on the south, with about 33,000 residents at the 2020 census across ZIP codes 11214 and 11228. It sits in Community District 11 and is policed by the 62nd Precinct from its station house at 1925 Bath Avenue. Those are the only background facts this page needs. What matters for a door is what Wikipedia says next: the housing is mostly semi-attached houses and small apartment buildings. Almost every property here has a shared entrance of some kind, and almost every shared entrance still runs on metal keys.

The houses come first because there are so many of them. The city’s Department of Finance counted 74 two-family sales and 32 three-family sales in Bath Beach in 2022 against only 19 one-family sales, and in 2024 the two-family median was $1,252,000. A two-family in Bath Beach is very often an owner downstairs and a tenant or a grown child upstairs, sharing one front door, one vestibule and one cellar. Over a few decades the keys to that front door travel: former tenants, home attendants, a contractor who redid the bathroom, a cousin who stayed one summer. Nobody can say how many copies exist. Replacing the cylinder solves it for a month, until the next copy is cut. Giving each person a code or fob that can be cancelled on its own is the part of access control that matters most to a house, and it is the part generic guides tend to skip because they are written for offices.

The apartment buildings are the second story, and they are more varied than the word suggests. Brick Underground’s guide to the neighborhood describes co-ops converted from rental four-story walk-ups, older Victorians, one- to three-family houses, and newer three-story condos of six to twelve units built on lots where houses were knocked down. Along Cropsey Avenue and Bay Parkway there are post-war elevator buildings: a 1960 six-story co-op at 30 Bay 29th Street is listed with “a part-time doorman, virtual doorman, live-in super,” and a co-op at 8721 Bay Parkway is listed as an “elevator building with live in super” and a garage with a waiting list. Each of those descriptions is an access control question. When the doorman goes home, what happens at the door? Who holds the garage credentials when a space changes hands? Who can open the laundry room and the bike storage? We plan around the answers.

Then there are the big rental complexes near the water. Openigloo’s building records for Shore Haven, off Cropsey Avenue near 21st Avenue, show a 41-unit, six-story building at 2064 Cropsey Avenue built in 1948, and the same tax lot listed under a run of other street numbers on Cropsey Avenue and Bay 25th Street, with neighboring buildings on 20th Lane and 21st Drive. Apartments.com’s listing for the complex mentions elevators, a garage, a courtyard, an on-site manager and 24-hour security. A property like that does not have a front door. It has many front doors, each with its own street number, all owned and maintained by one company. Access control on a complex of that shape is a question of scope: which credential opens which entrance, and how one office keeps track of hundreds of households across all of them.

New construction adds a fourth kind of door. New York YIMBY has reported filings for a three-story, twelve-unit building at 2325–2335 Bath Avenue on the site of an auto-body shop, twelve residences at 122 Bay 26th Street near the 20th Avenue station, and a six-story, ten-unit mixed-use building at 16 Bay 25th Street with a day care on the first floor and retail in the basement. Small new condos like these are usually handed over by the developer with one keypad and one shared code, or a video panel on someone else’s cloud account. A day care in a building with apartments above it is a different problem again, because the city’s child care rules require its entrance to be secured while parents keep unrestricted access to their children.

We do not sell any of this on fear, and we would rather quote the facts than the headlines. Bath Beach does have break-ins in the local news, from a home on Bay 37th Street near 86th Street that five men forced their way into in February 2026, as News 12 reported, to a grocery on Bath Avenue named in a 2022 burglary spree the Brooklyn District Attorney prosecuted. But most of the calls we get here are not about a crime. They are about a street door that no longer closes by itself, a lost key nobody can account for, a store owner who wants the back door locked at 10 p.m. whether or not the last employee remembers, and a co-op board that wants to know who still has the cellar key. Access control fixes those everyday problems, and that is how we describe it.

  • Mostly shared doors. Two- and three-family sales outnumbered one-family sales by more than five to one in 2022 (NYC Department of Finance), so most house jobs serve two or more households at one street door.
  • Converted walk-ups. Many co-ops here began as rental four-story walk-ups (Brick Underground), which means an original street door, a buzzer riser that has been patched for decades, and keys cut long before the conversion.
  • Part-time attendants. Post-war elevator co-ops near Bay Parkway list part-time doormen, virtual doormen and live-in supers (CityRealty; Brownstoner), so the door has to behave differently at different hours.
  • Complexes with many addresses. Shore Haven’s records list one owner across many street numbers on Cropsey Avenue and the nearby lanes (Openigloo), so one system has to run many separate entrances.
  • Small new buildings. Three-story, ten- to twelve-unit filings on Bath Avenue and the Bay streets, including one with a first-floor day care (New York YIMBY).
  • Elevated stores. The West End Line runs above 86th Street, with D stations at 18th Avenue, 20th Avenue, Bay Parkway and 25th Avenue (Wikipedia), so storefront back doors face narrow alleys and service entrances rather than open lots.

Most Bath Beach owners who ask about a front door reader also ask about a camera covering the same steps, and most co-op boards that replace a buzzer want video calling to residents’ phones at the same time. Building Specialists

Access Control for Bath Beach’s Architecture

The building decides the hardware long before anyone picks a brand. These are the six kinds of property that make up most of the Bath Beach calls we get, what each usually needs at the door and why.

Semi-attached brick two- and three-families

Brick houses on the Bay streets with a stoop or a few steps up to one front door, a vestibule, and a cellar under the whole house. Usually one controlled street door with a call button per household, the controller in the cellar, and keypad deadbolts on the apartment doors if the owner wants them.

Surviving frame Victorians

Wood-frame houses more than a century old, some still detached with porches, others split into two or three apartments. Doors are often original wood in wood frames bored by several generations of locks, so the frame is repaired before a strike goes in.

Walk-ups and walk-up co-ops

Four-story brick buildings, many converted from rentals to co-ops, with a street door, an inner vestibule door, a cellar door and a roof door. A reader on the street door tied into the old buzzer, and later readers on the cellar and package areas.

Post-war elevator co-ops and rentals

Six-story buildings from about 1948 to the 1960s along Cropsey Avenue, Bay Parkway and the lower Bay streets, with elevators, live-in supers, laundry rooms and often a garage. Networked readers, lobby schedules around attendant hours, and separate garage credentials.

Multi-building rental complexes

Groups of six-story buildings under one owner, each with its own street number and lobby, sharing courtyards, a garage and a management office. Many readers on one network, with each tenant’s credential scoped to their own entrance and the shared spaces.

New small condos and mixed-use buildings

Three- to six-story buildings of six to twelve units on former house or garage lots, some with a ground-floor day care or store. Video entry with fobs, an aluminum or steel entrance, and a separate door system for the commercial space.

What the materials mean for the install

Brick is the forgiving case, and it is most of what Bath Beach is built from. Readers and stations are fixed into the mortar joints rather than the face of the brick, so if the device ever comes off, the wall needs a joint repointed, not a brick replaced. Cable from the cellar comes up inside the wall where a chase exists, or through one small sealed hole behind the device. On a two-family the cellar almost always runs under the front door, which keeps the cable run short and cheap.

Wood is harder. A frame Victorian or an older two-family may have a front door jamb that has been drilled for four or five different locks over a century. A strike needs solid wood around it to hold when someone leans on the door, so a damaged section of jamb is filled or spliced first. Glass panels in the door or the sidelights also matter: if someone can break a pane and reach the inside lever, the lock choice changes, and we say so at the visit.

Post-war buildings bring aluminum and steel. Lobby doors from the 1950s and 1960s are often aluminum with narrow frames and glass sidelights, and the lock has to go where the frame has room, usually in the header or the panic bar. Garage doors under those buildings are steel overhead doors with their own motors; we add a reader beside the pedestrian door and a vehicle reader or tag reader for the overhead door rather than replacing the operator. Older cellars in buildings close to the bay can be damp, so controllers and batteries go high on a dry wall, never on the floor.

Many Entrances, One Owner: Access Control for the Cropsey Avenue Complexes

The rental complexes near the bay are the most interesting access control problem in Bath Beach, and the one generic advice handles worst. Openigloo’s records for Shore Haven list a single tax lot behind a long run of street numbers: 2034, 2036, 2038, 2044, 2064, 2074, 2076 and 2078 Cropsey Avenue, 235 Bay 25th Street, and neighboring buildings on 20th Lane and 21st Drive. The building at 2064 Cropsey Avenue alone has 41 apartments on six floors, built in 1948. Multiply that across a complex and one office is responsible for many street doors, many lobbies, a garage, a courtyard, laundry rooms and storage, all used by hundreds of households who come and go every day.

The first decision is scope. On a complex like this, a resident’s fob should open their own building’s street door and the shared spaces they pay for, and not the lobby of every other building on the lot. That sounds obvious, but it is the single most common mistake we see on older systems: every fob opens every door because it was easier to program that way. The result is that a lost or copied fob is a key to every lobby in the complex, and residents of one building cut through another building’s hallway because it is shorter. Scoping each credential to one entrance costs nothing in hardware and closes that gap.

The second decision is how the office keeps up. A complex turns over apartments every month. Each move-in needs credentials on the lease start date, each move-out needs them cancelled on the last day, and each lost fob needs to stop working within minutes, not at the next office visit. That calls for a networked controller in each building, all managed from one account at the management office, with a named credential for every adult in every household. A cloud-managed multi-door system runs $4,000 to $15,000 on our Brooklyn list, and a full building system with lobby, package, laundry and garage doors runs $15,000 to $40,000; on a complex, the price is set building by building once the doors have been walked.

The third decision is privacy. Every one of these buildings is a multiple dwelling, so the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to any fob, app or keyless system on its doors. HPD’s page summarizes what that means: tenants receive a written privacy and retention policy, the owner collects only the minimum data the system needs, authentication data is destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized, the data is never sold, and the system may not be used to track tenants outside the building or to harass them. On a complex where the management office can see every door, those limits need to be set in the software from the first day, not added later after a complaint.

Finally, rollout. A changeover that leaves residents locked out is worse than no system at all. CBS New York reported in April 2025 that when Queensbridge Houses in Queens moved its doors to fobs, residents said the fobs did not work, some doors jammed, and people propped doors open with duct tape; the residents’ association president counted 78 door complaints on one side of the complex in a single month. On a complex in Bath Beach we change one building at a time, keep the old key working at every door until the new credentials have been handed out and tested, and leave a working battery backup at each controller before the keys are retired.

When the Doorman Goes Home: Post-War Co-ops Near Bay Parkway

Several of the post-war co-ops between Bay Parkway and the bay are staffed for part of the day. CityRealty’s listing for the 1960 six-story co-op at 30 Bay 29th Street, a short walk from the Bay Parkway D station, describes “a part-time doorman, virtual doorman, live-in super.” That combination is common in buildings of this age and size: a doorman during the busiest hours, a remote attendant service for the rest, and a super who lives on site and handles the cellar, the roof and the garage. Each of those people needs something different from the door system, and each change of shift changes what the street door should do.

During staffed hours the lobby door can stay on the doorman’s control: residents use fobs, visitors are announced, and deliveries are taken at the desk. When the doorman leaves, the door has to lock by itself and visitor calls have to go somewhere. In buildings with a virtual doorman, the call panel routes visitors to the remote attendant, who can speak to them through the panel and, where the board allows it, see them on the lobby camera. Where there is no remote service, the panel calls the resident’s phone or apartment station directly. The schedule that switches between those modes lives in the access controller, so it happens on time every night whether or not anyone remembers.

The board’s job is to decide who can do what, and to write it down. Who may release the door remotely: only residents, or the virtual doorman too? Who may see the lobby camera from off site? Who keeps the administrator account when the managing agent changes? What happens to a shareholder’s fobs when an apartment is sold, or sublet under the building’s sublet rules? We bring a short written list of those questions to the board meeting, and we set the system up to match the answers rather than the other way round.

The live-in super usually gets the widest credential in the building: street door, cellar, boiler room, roof, garage and storage. That credential should still be personal, logged and revocable, not a master key that a former super might still have. The garage is a separate list. In a co-op with a garage waiting list, spaces change hands as shareholders move or give them up, and the garage tag or fob for that space should pass with the space, not stay with the person who used to hold it.

What we keep from the existing building

Most post-war lobbies already have a working intercom of some kind, a door closer that can be adjusted, and an aluminum entrance that is still sound. We reuse what is sound. The intercom handsets can often stay in the apartments while the lobby gets a new panel and reader, and the street door keeps its key cylinder for emergencies. The roof door stays exactly as the Multiple Dwelling Law requires, self-closing but never self-locking, openable from the inside without a key, and we do not put it on a schedule.

A Day Care Downstairs, Apartments Above: New Mixed-Use Buildings on the Bay Streets

New York YIMBY’s report on a filing at 16 Bay 25th Street describes a six-story, ten-unit building with a 3,638-square-foot day care on the first floor and 2,115 square feet of retail on the basement level. It is a useful example because it brings together three sets of users who must never share a door system by accident: residents going up to their apartments, staff and parents going into the day care, and customers and workers going into the store. Whether or not that particular building has opened, buildings of this kind are being filed on the lots of old houses and garages across Bath Beach, and each one needs its doors planned before the walls close.

The day care has its own rules. The NYC Health Department’s compliance guide for group child care centers says entrances must be “secured with passkey identification or other means of limiting access to staff, parents and other authorized persons” and “monitored by staff and/or electronic or other surveillance allowing unobstructed views.” The same guide says parents must have unrestricted access to their children at all times, that a child may be released only to an authorized escort, and that every exterior door needs a panic bar on the inside mounted between 34 and 48 inches from the floor. Those requirements fit together well with access control: staff and enrolled parents get credentials, the door relocks every time, a camera or a staff member sees the entrance, and the panic bar lets everyone out instantly.

What the door system cannot do is decide who is an authorized escort. That is the program’s list, kept by the program. We set up the day care’s door so that its administrator, the director, adds and removes parents and staff on their own account, separate from the residential building’s account. A parent whose child leaves the program loses access on the last day. A grandparent added as an escort for a month gets a credential that ends on that date. None of those entries appear in the residents’ system, and no resident’s fob opens the day care door.

The store in the basement is the third system, usually the simplest: a staff door with a schedule, an alert if it opens after hours, and its own administrator. The residential lobby stays self-closing and self-locking around the clock. A developer who plans all three at the hardware-schedule stage pays for one set of cable runs; adding the day care’s door after the building is finished usually means opening finished walls. If you are building one of these, send us the door schedule before the framing inspection and we will mark which openings need power, a door contact and a reader.

Twelve Owners, One Shared Code: Taking Over a New Condo’s Front Door

The small condos going up on Bath Avenue and the Bay streets are the newest buildings in the neighborhood and, in our experience, often the ones with the weakest door setup. New York YIMBY’s reports describe a three-story, twelve-unit building filed at 2325–2335 Bath Avenue on the site of an auto-body shop, and twelve residences at 122 Bay 26th Street with a cellar and nine parking spaces. Brick Underground quotes a local broker, Dan Gallogly, explaining why buildings like these appear where houses used to stand: “There’s no vacant land.” Each one replaces a house that had one front door and a family with a building that has one front door and a dozen households.

What the developer leaves at that door varies. Sometimes it is a keypad with one shared code that every owner, every tenant and every contractor knows. Sometimes it is a video panel registered to the developer’s own cloud account, which the condo association cannot fully administer. Sometimes it is a perfectly good system nobody has the login for. The first job after the sponsor hands over control is to find out which one you have, and to move the administrator account into the association’s name.

The second job is to end the shared code. Every owner and every tenant gets their own code, fob or phone key, so that a unit sale, a lease ending or a lost phone changes one credential and not the whole building’s. Contractors and cleaners get credentials that end on a date. The association’s board, usually two or three people in a building this size, gets administrator access, with at least two administrators so the building is never dependent on one person’s phone.

The third job is to check the parts nobody sees: is the controller on a battery backup, does the door release when the fire alarm sounds if the building has one, does the entrance relock if the power fails, and is the cellar door, where the meters and the water service are, on the system at all? In a twelve-unit building those questions can usually be answered in one visit, and most of the fixes are configuration rather than new hardware.

Roofs, cellars and back doors: what the 86th Street break-ins teach

News 12 reported in October 2025 that on one night in September a thief got into a Planet Fitness on 86th Street in Bensonhurst through the roof and took $450 from the register, and hours later someone broke through the door of a business on 86th Street in Bay Ridge and left through the basement. Neither of those stores is in Bath Beach, but 86th Street is Bath Beach’s northern edge, and the pattern is familiar: the front gate held, and the way in was somewhere else.

That is worth saying plainly because it limits what access control can promise. A reader on the back door does not stop someone coming through a roof. What it does do is make every door that is supposed to be locked actually locked, on a schedule, with an alert when one opens outside business hours. Add a door contact to the roof hatch and the cellar door, and the same system reports those openings too, even though nobody uses a fob there. Combined with an alarm and a camera on the back passage, that covers the routes the front gate does not.

For a store with apartments above, the residents’ door is the other half. Under the Multiple Dwelling Law the street entrance of a class A multiple dwelling has to be self-closing and self-locking, and the store’s staff should not be holding it open for deliveries. We put the shop door and the residents’ door on separate schedules and separate administrators, even when they share one controller, so the store owner and the building owner each manage their own users.

Rules that apply to Bath Beach business doors

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, a door on an exit path that is electrically locked has to release when the power fails and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and a magnetic lock needs a release built into the door hardware that works with one hand. Exit doors from shops, offices and clinics always open from inside without a key, card or code.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294 for access control; we fit listed equipment and keep the listings in the job file.
  • Apartments above shops. Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires the street entrance of a newer class A multiple dwelling to be self-closing and self-locking, kept locked unless an attendant is on duty, and buildings with eight or more apartments need an intercom with voice and remote release. A shop cannot leave the residents’ door on a daytime unlock schedule.
  • Tenant data. Where a building with apartments uses fobs, apps or other smart access on residential doors, HPD’s Tenant Data Privacy Law page lists the duties: a written policy, consent, minimum data, destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and no sale of the data. A store’s own staff door is not a residential door, but a shared street door is.
  • Child care. A day care’s entrance must be secured with passkey identification or another way of limiting access to staff, parents and authorized persons, with inside panic bars on exterior doors, under the Health Department’s group child care guide.
  • Licensing. New York State requires a Department of State license to install, service or maintain security systems for others. AESS works under license #12000287431.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Watch the Street Door Close Before Anything Else

This describes how we start every Bath Beach walk-up and two-family job, not a particular customer. Before we look at readers or brands, we stand on the sidewalk and open the street door all the way, let go, and watch. On a building that has had the same door for decades, the answer is often the whole problem: the closer has lost its oil and the door stops a few inches short, or the door has dropped on its hinges and the latch rides over the strike plate instead of into it. A reader on a door like that just records who walked into an unlocked building.

Then we go down to the cellar, because it decides the price. We look for the intercom power supply, the electrical panel and a dry wall above the floor for the controller and battery. In houses close to the bay we check the floor and the walls for tide lines from past water and keep everything well above them. We trace the old buzzer cable to see whether it can carry the new reader’s signal or whether a new run is cleaner. Last, we ask the owner or the super for the key count: who has a street door key today, and who used to. That list, almost always longer than expected, is what the credentials will replace.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Bath Beach: Questions Answered

Complexes and rental buildings

Residents of one building in our Cropsey Avenue complex use their fobs on other buildings’ doors to cut through. Should a fob open only the tenant’s own entrance?

Yes, and it is a settings change, not a hardware change. On a complex where one owner runs many entrances, each tenant’s credential should open their own building’s street door and the shared spaces their lease includes, such as the courtyard, the laundry room and, for those on the list, the garage. It should not open every lobby on the lot. When every fob opens every door, a single lost or copied fob becomes a key to the whole complex, and the management office cannot tell from the log whether someone in Building A had any reason to be in Building C.

The change is made in the access software, building by building, with a notice to residents first. Staff such as porters and the super keep wider credentials, but each one is personal and logged. If some residents have a real reason to use another entrance, for example a parent who lives in the next building, that can be added to their credential on request.

We own a rent-stabilized building and want to move the street door from keys to fobs. What has DHCR expected owners to provide?

DHCR has allowed fob systems in rent-stabilized buildings, with conditions. In one published ruling from July 2016, it approved a change from keys to fobs with no rent change on the basis that occupants, including children, receive fobs free; tenants can get up to four more free fobs for guests or employees; lost-fob replacement costs no more than $25; the system has a 48-hour battery backup; the building stays accessible by metal key in an emergency; and the system records entrances but not departures. Brick Underground’s summary of DHCR practice reports similar rules: fobs free to all lawful occupants, extra cards free and a capped replacement fee.

Every building’s order is its own, and an owner should apply to DHCR before the change rather than after. What we provide is a system that can meet those conditions: a controller with battery backup sized for the outage period, a key override on the street door, logging configured for entry only, and the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law paperwork. We do not give legal advice on the filing itself.

Our building’s new fob system stopped working when the internet went out after a storm. Shouldn’t the door have kept working?

It should have. A well-designed system keeps its list of valid credentials in the controller inside the building, so the door keeps reading fobs and logging entries when the internet drops, and syncs the log when the connection returns. Systems that check every fob against a remote server can lock people out the moment the network fails. Brick Underground printed a reader’s complaint about exactly this in 2018, a fob system that failed when the internet went out or it rained, and a tenant lawyer, Sam Himmelstein, told them: “If you’re being deprived of the ability to get into your own apartment, your lease is being breached.”

When we install or take over a system, we test it with the internet unplugged and with the power off, and we keep a mechanical key override at the street door. Readers on outward-facing doors near the bay are outdoor-rated and sealed against driving rain. If an existing system fails that test, we tell the owner whether a firmware setting fixes it or whether the controller has to change.

Our walk-up became a co-op decades ago and the street door still opens with the original keys. Does it matter how many copies are out there?

It matters more than anything else about the door. A key cut thirty years ago for a rental tenant still opens a street door that has never been rekeyed, and in a converted walk-up nobody has a complete record of who received one. Rekeying the cylinder solves it until the next copy is made at a hardware store. A reader gives each shareholder and each sublet tenant a personal credential that cannot be copied at a key kiosk, especially if it is an encrypted fob rather than the old 125 kHz kind, and the board can cancel any single one without touching the rest.

We keep the cylinder on the street door as an emergency override, rekeyed at the changeover, with those few keys held by the board and the super. In a four-story walk-up the cable route is usually simple: from the cellar up behind the door frame, sharing the old buzzer’s path.

Co-ops with staff, garages and shared rooms

Our co-op near the Bay Parkway station has a doorman for only part of the day. What should the lobby door do when he is off?

It should lock by itself and route visitors somewhere useful. During staffed hours the door can run under the doorman’s control. When the shift ends, the controller switches the door to fob-only and the call panel to its overnight mode: calls go to the virtual doorman service if the building has one, or straight to residents’ phones or apartment stations if it does not. Deliveries go to a package room that couriers can open with a one-time code, or wait for the next shift.

The switch happens on a schedule in the controller, not by someone flipping a switch, so it is on time every night and on holidays. The board decides what the remote attendant may do, such as whether they can release the door or only announce the visitor, and we set the permissions to match.

When a garage space passes to the next shareholder on our waiting list, how is the previous holder’s garage access ended?

Garage access should belong to the space, not the person. We set up garage credentials, a fob for the pedestrian door and a tag or long-range credential for the overhead door, as a separate group from the lobby fobs. When a space changes hands, the managing agent or the super removes the old holder from the garage group and adds the new one in a minute, and the old holder’s lobby fob keeps working as before.

The log then shows the garage door opened by people actually on the garage list. Buildings that hand out garage remotes without a record usually find, years later, that more remotes exist than spaces, and the only fix then is to change every one.

Our laundry room and storage cages are in the cellar. Should those doors be on the same fobs as the street door?

Usually yes, with their own rules. Residents use one fob, but the laundry door can be limited to the hours the laundry room is open, and the storage room to residents who rent a cage. That keeps the cellar, where the boiler, the meters and often the intercom power supply are, closed to anyone who walks in behind a resident at the street door. The super and porters get all three doors at all hours.

If the laundry machines take a payment card from a laundry company, that card usually works on the laundry company’s own system and cannot double as a door fob. Residents carry one of each; we do not try to merge them.

Houses and new condos

We bought a three-family on a Bay street and the seller handed over a bag of unlabeled keys. Where does a switch to codes start?

With the street door and the cellar door, in that order. The street door is shared by all three households and is the one whose keys have traveled furthest; a strike, a keypad or station beside it, and a separate code or fob for each adult in each apartment ends the unknown-key problem there. The cellar door, if it opens to the yard or the street, gets a lock on the same system and a contact so you know when it opens. Apartment doors can follow later, with keypad deadbolts that keep the tenant’s own key as well.

Meanwhile, rekey the street door cylinder the day you close, so the bag of keys stops being a risk while the system is planned. When the reader goes in, that rekeyed cylinder stays as the emergency override.

We are one of twelve owners in a new Bath Avenue condo and the developer left a keypad with one code everyone shares. What should the board change first?

First get the administrator account into the association’s name, then end the shared code. Ask the sponsor or the installer for the controller’s login, the app account and any cloud subscription, and transfer them before the sponsor’s involvement ends. Then give each owner and tenant a personal code or fob, give contractors and cleaners credentials with end dates, and turn the shared code off on a date everyone is told about in advance.

If the keypad cannot hold individual codes, or the account cannot be transferred, it is usually cheaper to replace the keypad than to live with it. A single controlled door on our Brooklyn list runs $1,500 to $2,500, and in a building this new most of the cable is already in place.

Day care, shops and offices

Our day care has to keep its entrance secured, but the rules also say parents must always be able to reach their children. How do both fit on one door?

They fit well. The Health Department’s group child care guide asks that entrances be secured with passkey identification or another way of limiting access to staff, parents and other authorized persons, and that they be watched by staff or by a camera. Enrolled parents get their own credential, so they can enter at any time the center is open without waiting for staff to buzz them in, while strangers cannot. The panic bar on the inside, which the same guide requires on exterior doors, lets everyone out at once.

The director administers the list, separate from the residential building’s account, and a parent’s access ends on the day their child leaves. Who counts as an authorized escort is the center’s decision and its records, not the door system’s; the door only makes sure the people on the list are the ones who can open it.

A thief came through the roof of a gym on 86th Street last year. Does a reader on our back door do anything about the roof?

Not by itself, and we will not pretend otherwise. The September 2025 break-in News 12 reported on 86th Street went in through the roof and out the same way. What an access system can do is put every opening on one panel: the back door on a reader and a schedule, and the roof hatch and cellar door on contacts that report the moment they open after hours. An alarm on the same contacts calls you or your monitoring company.

In a building with apartments above, the roof door cannot be locked against the people inside; the Multiple Dwelling Law requires it to open from the inside without a key. A contact that reports it opening, combined with a hatch that is secured from the outside, is the usual answer.

Our restaurant on Bath Avenue has a sidewalk cellar hatch for deliveries. Can that hatch go on the access system?

Usually as a monitored opening rather than a controlled one. Sidewalk cellar doors are steel leaves that open from below or with a key from above, and they are not built to take a strike and reader the way a personnel door is. What we can do is put a contact on the hatch so the system reports when it opens and how long it stays open, and put the door at the bottom of the cellar stairs, inside the building, on a reader and schedule so staff use their own credentials to reach the stockroom.

If deliveries arrive before opening, a delivery credential with a time window on that inside door lets the driver reach the cellar and nowhere else, and the log shows when.

Our medical office is on the ground floor of a co-op on Bay Parkway, and the side entrance opens into the building’s hallway. How do we keep patients out of the hallway?

Give the office its own entrance and lock the connecting door. The street-side entrance to the suite gets a video station that calls the front desk, so patients are buzzed into the waiting room. The door between the suite and the residential hallway gets a reader on the office side that only staff can use, and stays locked to everyone else; from the hallway side it opens only for residents or building staff who need it, or not at all.

The co-op and the office each keep their own users. The board approves the hardware on its walls, the office manages its own staff list, and neither side sees the other’s logs.

“A fob system means you never have to worry about keys again.”

A fob system changes which keys you worry about. The street door still needs a mechanical override for emergencies, and DHCR’s 2016 fob ruling made emergency key access one of its conditions for a rent-stabilized building. Those override keys have to be few, counted and held by the right people.

“Access control is an office product.”

A shared street door on a two-family has the same problems an office has, in miniature: people who need to get in, people who should not anymore, and nobody sure who has a key. A personal code for each adult solves it the same way it does for an office.

“Cloud systems are always better.”

Cloud management is convenient for a complex with many entrances and a management office that needs to issue fobs from a desk. It is not automatically better for a two-family or a twelve-unit condo.

We set logging, retention and administrator access to match those rules before the first fob is handed out, not after a tenant complains.

National guides often price the lock off the shelf and call it a door. That is one part of the job.

A virtual doorman is a person somewhere else, watching a camera and answering a call panel. Several post-war co-ops near Bay Parkway list one alongside a part-time doorman and a live-in super.

That service needs a door that locks by itself, a panel that routes calls to it at the right hours, and a controller the attendant can release only within the limits the board sets. Without those, there is nothing for the remote attendant to control.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover a two-family’s front door and a complex of many lobbies. What changes in Bath Beach is the second step: in a neighborhood of co-ops, condo associations, rent-stabilized rentals and shared houses, settling who controls the door comes before choosing any hardware.

1. Send photos and the address

A photo of each door from outside and inside, the address and cross street, how many households or users, and who approves work on the building.

2. Settle who controls the door

We confirm who signs and who will administer the system: the owner, the board, the managing agent, the condo association or the business, and which rules apply to the building.

3. Survey the door, the cellar and the power

A technician watches each door close, finds a cable route through the cellar, picks a dry spot high on a wall for the controller and battery, and checks the power source and the network.

4. Write the door plan and Brooklyn price

A door-by-door plan naming the lock, the reader, who gets which credential and what each door does in a power cut or fire alarm, priced from the published Brooklyn list.

5. Fix the door before electrifying it

Closers, hinges and latches are adjusted or replaced so the door relocks every time, and damaged jambs are repaired before a strike goes in.

6. Install cable, controller, lock and reader

Cable in the cellar or concealed paths, the controller and battery mounted high and dry, then the strike or lock, reader, door contact and exit device.

7. Load credentials and schedules

A named credential for each person, start and end dates where needed, doorman and overnight schedules, garage and laundry groups, and the privacy settings the building requires.

8. Test with the power off and hand over

Every door tested on battery, with the internet unplugged and with the fire alarm signal where there is one, then the administrator account, a door map and a written condition note.

Twice a year: a five-minute street door check

When the clocks change, open each controlled door all the way and let it close by itself: does it latch without a push? Check the battery level of the controller and any keypad in the app before winter. Read the user list and remove anyone who no longer needs access: a tenant who moved, a contractor who finished, a garage space that changed hands. If the door drags or stops short, a call now costs less than a door that sits unlocked for a month.

Can you add a fob reader to a Bath Beach building that already pays for a virtual doorman service?
+

Yes. The virtual doorman company provides the remote attendant and usually its own call panel and camera; we install or replace the lock, reader, controller and door hardware so the door relocks every time and fobs work for residents. If the building later drops the service, the reader and controller stay in place and the call panel is switched to call residents directly.

How much notice should Bath Beach residents get before their street door changes from keys to fobs?
+

We recommend written notice at least a few weeks before the changeover, with the date, how fobs will be handed out, what the privacy policy says, and what to do if someone is away on the day. In rent-stabilized buildings the owner should have DHCR approval in hand first, and in any multiple dwelling the Tenant Data Privacy Law paperwork goes out before the fobs do. The old key keeps working until every household has a tested fob, so nobody is locked out by a short notice.

Our Bath Beach building has no internet service in the cellar. Can a fob system still go in?
+

Yes. A controller that stores its credentials locally does not need the internet to read fobs and open the door; it needs power and a battery backup. Without a connection the system is managed on site or by a short cable run to a router elsewhere in the building, or by a cellular modem if the board wants remote management. Many small Bath Beach buildings run perfectly well on a local-only system with no subscription.

Do encrypted fobs cost more than the old 125 kHz fobs on your list?
+

The ten fobs included in a single controlled door are encrypted at no extra charge, because we no longer recommend the old 125 kHz kind for new systems; they can be copied with an inexpensive device. Where a building keeps an existing reader that only reads 125 kHz fobs, we say so in the plan and price an encrypted reader as an option, so the board can decide with the numbers in front of it.

Can the cable from our Bath Beach building’s old buzzer be reused for the new reader?
+

Sometimes. Old buzzer cable in walk-ups and post-war buildings is often thin, multi-pair wire that was spliced many times over the years. It can sometimes carry a door release or a door contact, but a modern reader and a networked panel usually need new cable. We test the old cable at the survey, reuse it where it measures sound, and price a new run where it does not, so you know which applies before the job starts.

What should a Bath Beach owner have ready before the site visit?
+

Access to the cellar and the electrical panel, the location of the intercom or buzzer power supply if you know it, a rough count of who holds keys today, and the name of whoever signs for the building, whether that is the owner, the board president or the managing agent. If there is an existing keypad or panel, have its brand and any login you were given. Photos of each door from both sides help us bring the right parts.

Do you fill in a Bath Beach day care’s Health Department paperwork for its entrance?
+

No. The program and its licensing staff handle the Health Department’s requirements and inspections. What we provide is the door hardware and documentation: the lock and reader specifications, how the panic bar is mounted, how the door behaves in a power cut or fire alarm, and how the director adds and removes authorized parents and staff. Programs often give that summary to their inspector or consultant.

Will a reader hold up on a Bath Beach door that faces the wind and damp off Gravesend Bay?
+

Yes, if it is an outdoor-rated device mounted with sealed fixings. Street doors on the lower blocks near the bay take wind-driven rain and damp air, so we use readers and stations rated for outdoor use, seal the cable entry behind each device, and keep controllers and batteries indoors, high on a dry wall. Stainless or coated screws are used outside so rust does not stain the brick.

Who do Bath Beach residents call when a fob we installed stops working?
+

The building’s administrator first, usually the super, the managing agent or a board member, because most fob problems are a credential that was switched off or never added and can be fixed in the app in a minute. If the reader or the door itself is at fault, the administrator calls us, and a visit is booked within two hours of the call. Residents do not need to call us directly unless the building has asked us to handle them.
