The Neighborhood

Why Windsor Terrace Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Windsor Terrace's camera problems are small, specific and close to home. This is a quiet residential pocket, wedged between Prospect Park on the east and Green-Wood Cemetery on the west, with Prospect Park West and Park Slope to the north, Caton Avenue and Kensington to the south, and the Prospect Expressway cutting through its middle. Most of what people here ask us to watch is not dramatic. It is a front door that someone tried once, a car on the block that was rummaged overnight, a shed behind the row that turned out to be easy to open, and a mailroom where boxes keep vanishing. Those are exactly the cases where a camera either produces one clear, usable picture or produces nothing worth having, and the difference is almost always placement.

The neighborhood's own record shows what that difference looks like. In May 2017, Patch published a video, shared by a neighborhood Facebook page, from a house on Prospect Avenue between Ninth and 10th Avenues. A man in a hooded sweatshirt and red hat walks up and works at the door lock for a few seconds, then seems to notice the camera, knocks it down and runs. The page that shared it said "he cased the block for an hour before tried to get in a few houses." Two years earlier, DNAinfo reported that the 72nd Precinct arrested a package thief in a building entryway at Greenwood Avenue and Sherman Street after packages were stolen on East Third Street. Locals had filmed him and posted the video on YouTube, but the arrest came because "a local recognized him from a wanted poster and called 911." A recognizable face, sharp enough to print, is what turned a clip into an arrest.

The houses shape the work as much as the incidents do. Windsor Terrace was built up largely between 1902 and 1919, when the builder William Calder put up roughly 700 row houses, so many that Brownstoner says the area was nicknamed Calderville. Those rows are attached on both sides, with a stoop or a porch in front, a cellar below, and a deep, narrow yard behind that can only be reached through the house. Earlier wood-frame houses and cottages survive on a few blocks, East Fifth Street among them; prewar co-ops line Prospect Park Southwest facing the park; apartment houses stand along Prospect Avenue and, from a later wave of building, near Fort Hamilton Parkway. On an attached row, a yard camera's cable has nowhere to go but through the house, and a front camera on brick or limestone has to be anchored into masonry that is more than a century old.

Then there is the street. On a block of attached rows with no driveways, the family car spends the night at the curb, and the latest precinct numbers say the car is now the bigger worry. The 72nd Precinct, which covers Windsor Terrace along with Sunset Park and the surrounding blocks, reported 99 grand larcenies of autos in 2026 through November 8, against 89 at the same point in 2025, a rise of 11.2 percent. Burglaries were essentially unchanged, 89 against 90, and are down 39.5 percent over two years. In May 2025 the KARMABrooklyn neighborhood blog passed on the precinct's warning of "an uptick in the theft of catalytic converters in Sector A," which it said "includes Windsor Terrace, Greenwood Heights, and South Slope." A doorbell does not see a car three houses down. A camera built for the curb does.

The back of the house and the inside of the bigger buildings fill out the picture. In October 2016, the Brooklyn Reporter wrote that thieves "broke in through the property's fence" near East Fourth Street and Vanderbilt Avenue, opened a backyard shed and took two bikes worth nearly $2,000. In October 2024, News12 reported that tenants in a building on Prospect Park Southwest had watched packages disappear from the mailroom for years, with the building's own footage showing several people taking them. One resident said: "I made a report, but they said unless the package is worth $1,000, they won't take further action." So a Windsor Terrace camera plan has to answer three practical questions. Will the picture identify someone? Will it still exist if the camera is attacked? And does it cover the yard, the curb and the shared spaces, not just the front step?

What the Footage Taught

A Poster, a Knocked-Down Camera and a Rummaged Car: What Windsor Terrace's Own Incidents Say About Where Cameras Go

We would rather design from what has actually happened on these blocks than from a brochure. Three reported incidents, spread over a few years and a few streets, between them show almost every mistake a home camera can make and the fix for each. None of them is a reason to panic; the precinct's burglary count is far below where it was a generation ago. They are simply the clearest local evidence of what a camera has to capture to be worth the money.

The 2015 package case: the face mattered more than the video

When the East Third Street package thief was caught in December 2015, DNAinfo noted two things side by side. Residents had filmed him stealing and put the footage on YouTube as a warning. And the arrest itself happened because a neighbor recognized him from a wanted poster. A poster is a still frame. That tells us what a front camera in Windsor Terrace is really for: a single frame, taken as the person faces the door, sharp enough that someone on the block would know the face on paper. Video of a figure walking away with a box, filmed from above the door, shows a hood and a back. So our front cameras are set at a height and angle that catch people as they come up the stoop or the porch steps, facing the lens, and we check the paused frame on the day of the install, not just the moving picture.

The 2017 Prospect Avenue video: seconds at the lock, then the camera came down

The Patch video from Prospect Avenue between Ninth and 10th Avenues is a short lesson in timing and reach. The man worked at the lock for a few seconds before he appeared to notice the camera. Then he knocked it down. Two things follow. First, the useful seconds happen before a person notices anything, so the camera's view has to begin on the walk or the steps, where people are still looking ahead, rather than at the lock, where they are already busy. Second, a camera a person can reach will sometimes be reached, so the camera that matters most should be out of arm's length from the stoop, the porch rail and the top step, and the recording has to live somewhere else. A camera that streams to a recorder in the cellar has already sent its picture before anyone touches it.

The 17th Street car break-ins: nobody knew when it happened

In October 2015, Brownstoner described a run of odd car break-ins on 17th Street between 10th and 11th Avenues and on 18th Street, where change was taken, glovebox contents were dumped across the seats, and one neighbor's sunglasses ended up in another neighbor's car. "They left my neighbor's GPS but took his change," one resident wrote. The story does not mention any camera, and what stands out is how little anyone could say about when it happened or who did it. A curb camera on a row house cannot stop a car window from breaking. What it can do is turn "sometime overnight" into a time, a direction of travel and a picture, which is what a police report and an insurance claim both ask for first.

Where Losses Happen

The Curb, the Back Yard and the Mailroom: The Three Spots a Windsor Terrace Doorbell Never Sees

A video doorbell is often the first camera a Windsor Terrace household buys, and it is a fine camera for the one thing it is built to see, the person standing at the door. The trouble is that the neighborhood's recent losses mostly happen somewhere else. Here is how we cover the three places that come up again and again, and one place no home camera should try to cover.

The curb: a camera that knows where the car sleeps

Car thefts in the 72nd Precinct are up 11.2 percent for the year, and the precinct's 2025 converter warning named Windsor Terrace's sector. On a row with no driveways, the car is rarely in front of its own house; it is wherever there was a space, often several doors down or across the street. So the first question on a curb camera is not which camera but where the car usually ends up. We aim a dedicated camera at that stretch of curb, not the whole block, set its lens so a person beside the car fills enough of the frame to recognize, and check the picture at night under the street light that is actually there.

The back yard and the shed: the side of the house nobody walks past

The 2016 shed break-in near East Fourth Street and Vanderbilt Avenue began at the fence. On an attached row the yard is invisible from the street, the neighbors' yards run side by side behind low fences, and a shed at the back holds bikes, tools and strollers. A camera at the rear of the house, mounted under the deck or at the parlor-floor window line, can see the whole length of a narrow yard, including the shed door and the back fence where someone would come over. Its cable has to reach the cellar without crossing finished rooms, which usually means drilling down through the rear wall into the cellar ceiling, and its view is framed to stop at your own fence line so it records your yard and not the yards beside it.

The mailroom and lobby: where a building's cameras earn their keep

The Prospect Park Southwest building News12 visited in 2024 already had cameras, and they had recorded the thefts. The super was still hoping for a locked mailroom door or gate, and the building was reduced to posting signs asking tenants not to buzz in strangers. That is a familiar pattern: footage that proves what happened without changing what happens. In a co-op or apartment house, a mailroom camera works best as part of a plan, placed to record faces at the point where packages are picked up, paired with a door or gate that limits who gets in, and set so the super or the managing agent can find a given afternoon in a minute rather than an hour. We plan building cameras that way, and we tell a board frankly when the camera is the second fix, not the first.

The street mailbox: not yours to watch

In March 2025, a local report documented checks washed and rewritten after being dropped in blue mailboxes, including the box at Ballard Pharmacy on the Park Slope and Windsor Terrace border and one at 4th Street and Prospect Park West. Representative Dan Goldman was quoted saying New York City is the worst metro area for package theft, with $945 million in losses the year before. Owners sometimes ask whether a house camera can watch the corner mailbox. It is public property and a home camera's view should stay on your own building, so we do not aim cameras at it. The practical advice is simpler: mail checks from inside a post office, and keep your own camera on your own door, where packages and mail actually land.

Calder row houses: one cellar, three directions

On a Calder row the cellar is the camera system's spine. The front camera, the curb camera and the yard camera all sit on different faces of an attached house, and the only space that connects the front wall to the back wall without crossing a finished room is the cellar ceiling. So we plan every run to drop straight into the cellar behind its camera and travel along the joists to one recorder location, usually on a high shelf away from the boiler and the laundry. On the front, the brick above the porch roof or the stoop's parlor door gives a high, solid mount; on houses where the porch was closed in decades ago, we mount outside the porch glass rather than behind it, because a camera inside a glazed porch sees its own reflection at night. At the back, the underside of a rear deck or the wall between the parlor and cellar windows gives a sheltered spot that sees the full length of the yard.

Limestone bow fronts: the stone stays whole

The curved limestone fronts on some Windsor Terrace rows are the hardest facades on the block to repair and the easiest to damage. Limestone chips and stains around a careless anchor, and a crack in a carved sill is permanent. On a bow front we keep fixings out of the stone itself wherever the house allows: the camera goes on a brick return, in the mortar joint of a plain section, under the porch or cornice soffit, or on the wooden door surround, and the cable enters behind the mount rather than running across the face. Where the only good view really does need a fixing in stone, we tell the owner before drilling, use the smallest anchor that will hold, and seal it with a material that will not stain.

Wood-frame houses and cottages: friendlier walls, more ways in

A frame house is easier to wire than a brick row because its walls are hollow, and harder to protect because it often stands with a side path, more windows near the ground and a back door off a side yard. Cable can usually be fished inside the wall from the cellar to a mount just under the eave, so very little shows outside. Mounts go onto a solid mounting block screwed into the framing, never straight into clapboard, shingle or vinyl, which split or crack under a camera's weight. Because frame houses have more openings than rows, the plan usually adds a camera for the side path and one for the back door, and we check whether any low roof or porch roof gives a person a step up to an upper window.

Prospect Park Southwest co-ops: the board, the mailroom and the park side

In a park-facing co-op, cameras in the lobby, the mailroom, the service entrance and the elevators are the board's decision. Habitat magazine's 2023 article on video doorbells quoted a board lawyer saying a shareholder's camera in a common area is "done with the permission of the board," and noted that some co-ops limit camera angles, what may be recorded and how long footage is kept. We plan those systems with the board and the managing agent: which entrances matter, who gets live view and who gets playback, where the recorder is locked, and how long recordings are kept. The park side adds its own issue. At night the park across the street is very dark, so the entrance camera is set for a face lit by the building's own entry light, not for the dark trees beyond, and its motion zone stops at the curb.

Apartment houses on the avenues: stairs, cellars and the store below

The apartment houses along Prospect Avenue, many built in the 1920s, and the smaller postwar buildings near Fort Hamilton Parkway are mostly walk-ups, often with a store at street level. Their camera plans revolve around the street door, the stair, the mailboxes and the cellar door, with cable run up a stair hall in a plain, painted raceway or through an existing chase rather than across tenants' walls. Where a store occupies the ground floor, the building and the business usually keep separate systems with separate recorders, because the landlord and the tenant need different things recorded and different people watching. In a building with three or more apartments, every camera stays on common areas and the outside; none looks into an apartment door.

Newer two-families and small buildings: wire it at the right moment

Windsor Terrace still adds buildings now and then. Wikipedia records 17 two-family houses built in 1983 on the site of the old Pilgrim Laundry, and in April 2026 a permit was filed for a three-story, six-unit building with commercial space at 1259 Prospect Avenue. Newer houses have drywall, accessible ceilings and sometimes spare conduit, which makes camera cable easier. For a building still under construction, the right time to talk about cameras is before the walls close: one cable to each camera position and to a recorder location costs very little while the framing is open and a great deal afterward. We work from the plans with the owner or contractor, then mount and set up the cameras once the facade is finished.

Cafes and bars on Prospect Park West: the hours after the last customer

A cafe or bar on Prospect Park West has two lives a camera has to cover. During business hours the cameras that matter are the register, where they show hands, cash and the card terminal, and the front door, where they record faces coming in. After closing, the picture that matters is the front window and the back or side door, recording continuously, because a break-in at a quiet storefront is over in minutes and motion alerts that fire all day on a busy counter are useless at night. We set separate alert hours for after closing, send those alerts to the owner's phone, and make sure the recorder sits in a locked back room or office rather than under the counter, where anyone who comes over the bar can unplug it.

Prospect Avenue shops: doors, windows and the stock room

Bklyner's 2017 report on a pattern of 11 commercial burglaries in Kensington and Windsor Terrace listed two on this avenue, at 1233 and 1241A Prospect Avenue, and said the thieves got in "through windows and using crowbars to force doors or break locks." For a shop here that means the plan covers every way in, not just the front door. A camera inside, aimed at the front door and window from behind the counter, records anyone who forces either. A camera at the back or side window covers the second way in. If the stock is in a cellar reached from inside or from the sidewalk, that door gets its own camera too. For hardware and supply stores with expensive tools on display, a high camera over the aisles shows what was taken, and a camera at the register shows each sale.

Fort Hamilton Parkway storefronts: the 2 a.m. list

The same 2017 pattern list included three businesses on Fort Hamilton Parkway, at 2826, 2902 and 2923, each hit between about 2:10 and 3:30 in the morning. Those times are the case for continuous recording on a storefront, rather than motion-only recording, and for a camera that can see a face in near darkness inside a closed shop. We usually pair an inside camera with low-light performance or a small light left on in the shop, an outside camera on the gate or roll-down shutter, and alerts that go to the owner from closing time until opening. If a shop has a roll-down gate, we check that the outside camera still sees the door when the gate is down, because a camera that only sees closed steel at night records very little.

Parishes and schools: busy entrances, empty evenings

Field Notes: Starting a Windsor Terrace Walk-Through Where the Car Was Parked Last Night

This describes how we approach properties here, not one particular job. On a Windsor Terrace walk-through we usually start at the curb, not the front door, and we ask a question owners rarely expect: where was your car parked last night, and where is it usually? On a block of attached rows, the answer is often three or four houses away, sometimes across the street, and that single fact decides where the curb camera goes and what lens it needs. Then we walk from the nearest corner toward the house the way a stranger would, noting the point on the sidewalk where a person first looks toward the door, because that is where the front camera should catch the face.

Next we go through the house to the back, since on an attached row there is no other way. In the yard we stand at the back fence and the shed and look back at the house, picking the spot under the deck or between the windows that sees the whole strip. Finally we go down to the cellar and find the route: where each cable can drop in, where the recorder can sit dry and out of sight, and where the internet comes in.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Windsor Terrace: Questions Answered

The 2015 package thief was caught after a neighbor recognized him from a wanted poster. Which settings make one paused frame from our door camera sharp enough to print on a flyer?

Resolution, a fast enough shutter and the right distance. A 4MP or higher camera, set to record its main stream at full resolution, with the face filling a good part of the frame at the top of the stoop, gives a still you can print. Motion blur is the usual enemy at night, so we set the night shutter so a person walking up the steps stays sharp, and we test it by pausing the recording on the day of the install. A wide camera high above the door may record the whole stoop and still give you a face too small to recognize on paper.

The Prospect Avenue intruder in 2017 worked at the lock for a few seconds before he noticed the camera. Should our door camera be placed so people see it before they reach the door?

Placed so it sees them before they reach the door, which is not quite the same thing. The view should start on the sidewalk and the steps, where people are still looking ahead and their faces are toward the house. Whether the camera itself is obvious matters less than where its view begins. A visible camera can end an attempt, as it did in 2017, but it should also be mounted high enough that it cannot be swatted down from the top step, with a second view that records anyone who tries.

Catalytic converter thefts were reported rising in the 72nd Precinct's Sector A in 2025. Can a camera on a row house see someone working under a parked car at night?

Only if it is aimed for it. A camera mounted high to watch the stoop looks down at car roofs, and someone lying beside or under a car is hidden by the body of the car. A curb camera mounted lower, around the parlor-floor window line, and aimed along the curb shows the gap under the cars and anyone crouching there. It needs good low-light performance, because the underside of a car is the darkest part of the street, and an alert set for people within the curb zone between midnight and dawn.

Our shed holds two bikes, much like the one broken into near East Fourth Street and Vanderbilt Avenue in 2016. Should the yard camera watch the shed door or the fence?

Both, and on a narrow Windsor Terrace yard one camera can usually do it. A camera at the back of the house, aimed down the length of the yard, sees the fence at the far end and the shed door along the way, so it records a person coming over the fence and then going into the shed. If the shed sits against the back fence and faces sideways, a small second camera on the shed itself, wired through protected conduit, catches the door face on. The view is framed to stop at your fences on either side.

Our house is on the north side of Prospect Avenue, facing the expressway cut. With no houses across the street, what does our front camera actually need to see?

Your stoop, the sidewalk in front of the house and the stretch toward each corner. Without houses opposite, there is no neighbor's window to keep out of the frame, but there is a steady stream of traffic and headlights below. We draw the motion zone around your steps and sidewalk only, so the camera records everything but alerts only for people at your door, and choose a camera that handles headlights against a dark background without washing out the face at the door.

Our co-op on Prospect Park Southwest already has mailroom footage of people taking packages, and the thefts keep happening. What would make the cameras change anything?

Pairing them with control of the door. A camera records who took a box; it does not stop the next person who is buzzed in. The changes that work together are a mailroom door or gate that only residents can open, a face camera at that door and another at the pickup shelves, and a search setup that lets the super pull a given afternoon in a minute. With the door controlled, the cameras narrow suspects to people who had access, which is when footage starts to change behavior.

A Prospect Park Southwest tenant was told police would not follow up on a stolen package unless it was worth $1,000. Is a home camera still worth having for thefts that small?

Yes, but for different reasons than an arrest. A clear clip lets a retailer or carrier replace the item, settles whether a delivery really arrived, shows a neighbor or a building manager who was involved, and adds to a pattern if the same person keeps coming back. Police interest grows when the same face appears in several reports. And a camera at the door changes where couriers leave boxes, because you can see the delivery happen and ask them to bring it inside the porch or vestibule next time.

Calder Place is named for William Calder, the state senator who sponsored the first daylight-saving law. Will our recorder change its own clock in March and November?

It will if it is set up properly, and it should be. We set every recorder to New York time with daylight saving switched on and synced to an internet time server, so it moves its clock forward in March and back in November and corrects any drift. That matters because a clip stamped an hour off can make a report or an insurance claim look wrong. At the handover we show you where the clock setting lives, and after the next change you can check it in a few seconds on your phone.

Our cars on 17th Street were gone through overnight and nobody knew when. Can the recorder find the moment without someone watching the whole night?

Yes. Modern recorders mark motion events on a timeline, and the cameras in our IP packages can tag people and vehicles, so you can ask for every person beside the curb between midnight and six and get a short list of clips. For that to work, the curb camera needs its detection zone drawn around the parked cars, and the clock has to be right. We show you how to run that search on your phone at the handover, using a few minutes of real footage from your own block.

Our internet comes in at the front of the parlor floor, but the only out-of-sight place for a recorder is the cellar. Does the recorder have to sit next to the router?

No. The recorder needs one network cable to the router, not to be beside it. On a Calder row we usually put the recorder and its switch in the cellar, where every camera cable arrives anyway, and run a single cable up to the router on the parlor floor. If you ever unplug or replace the router, the cameras keep recording in the cellar; you only lose phone viewing until it is back. A small battery unit on the recorder keeps it running through short outages.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Windsor Terrace

The claims below come up again and again in AI-written answers, cost guides and directory sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Thumbtack when people ask about cameras for a Brooklyn house. Where a claim has a named source, we say so. Each is followed by what we find on Windsor Terrace properties.

"Security camera installation in Brooklyn costs about $1,416."

That is HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn average for camera installation and repair, with a typical range of $680 to $2,145 and a spread from $160 to $3,500. As a borough-wide snapshot of everything from a single doorbell to a small system, it is reasonable.

A Windsor Terrace row house with a door camera, a curb camera zoomed several houses down and a yard camera wired through the cellar is a specific job, and a park-facing co-op's lobby and mailroom is a different one again. An average tells you little about either.

So we publish package starting prices, beginning at $850 for four cameras, and quote each property in writing with every camera, mount and cable run on its own line, so you can see what moves the number.

"A visible camera scares burglars away."

Sometimes. The 2017 Prospect Avenue video is the local proof: the man stopped once he noticed the camera. But he had already spent several seconds at the lock, and his response was to knock the camera down.

A visible camera is a deterrent and a target at the same time. Relying on its presence alone means that the one person who is not deterred may also be the one who takes the camera with him.

The fix is placement and backup: a view that starts on the steps before anyone notices it, a mount out of reach, a second camera that sees the first, and a recorder indoors that already has the picture.

"Wireless cameras are fine for a small row house."

For one camera near the router, often. For a yard camera at the back of an attached brick row, through a party wall's worth of masonry, plaster and a floor, Wi-Fi is the weak link. A Brooklyn owner on Brownstoner's forum got blunt advice from another member: "Wired. Always wired. With these old brick buildings, even the best wireless systems are spotty."

Battery cameras add a second problem: they need recharging, and they tend to go flat in the cold months when nobody is in the yard to notice.

A wired camera gets power and data through one cable, records to the recorder whether the internet is up or not, and never needs a ladder to recharge. We use wireless only where a cable genuinely cannot go, and say so on the quote.

"Give the footage to the police and they will catch the thief."

Footage helps, but the local record is more mixed. A Prospect Park Southwest resident told News12 in 2024 that after filing a report about a stolen package, "they said unless the package is worth $1,000, they won't take further action."

And in the 2015 East Third Street case, the arrest came from a neighbor who recognized a face on a wanted poster, not from police reviewing the video.

So a camera's value here depends on producing a recognizable face and a correct time, which serves the police, a carrier claim, an insurer and your neighbors alike. We set every system up for that, rather than for a clip that only looks good on the app.

"Break-ins are rising, so every house needs cameras right away."

Not according to the 72nd Precinct's own numbers. Burglaries in 2026 through November 8 stood at 89 against 90 a year earlier, down 39.5 percent over two years and 91.1 percent over 33 years.

What is rising is car theft, up 11.2 percent for the year, alongside the 2025 converter warning for Windsor Terrace's sector. That changes the order of priorities more than the urgency.

For many row-house owners the honest recommendation is a door camera and a curb camera first, with the yard added if there is a shed or a rear door worth protecting. We would rather sell you three cameras you need than eight you do not.

"You need a monthly cloud subscription to watch your cameras on your phone."

Only with cameras built around a cloud service. Many consumer doorbells and Wi-Fi cameras keep recordings in the maker's cloud and charge for history beyond a short window.

A recorder in your cellar stores the footage on its own drive and streams live view and playback to the phone app without a subscription. Your ongoing cost is the internet you already pay for.

If you want an off-site copy as well, for example for a house that sits empty for a season, we can add one and say on the quote whether it carries a fee from the provider. It is an option, not a requirement.

"A video doorbell covers the front of the house."

It covers the person standing at the door. On a Windsor Terrace row it usually does not cover the curb where the car is parked, the sidewalk toward the corner, or the stoop seen from the side.

Doorbells are mounted at button height beside the door, which on a house with a high stoop or an enclosed porch can mean a face seen from below or through glass, and a frame too wide to recognize anyone at the gate.

A doorbell is a good first device and pairs well with a camera system. What it is not is the whole front of the house, and we plan the door, the curb and the sidewalk as separate jobs.
