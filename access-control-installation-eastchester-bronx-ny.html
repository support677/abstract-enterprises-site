The Neighborhood

Why Eastchester Properties Need Access Control

Start with the name, because it causes more confusion than anything else on this page. The Eastchester we cover here is a neighborhood of the Bronx, not the town of Eastchester in Westchester County a few miles north. Wikipedia’s entry explains the overlap: the area belonged to the old Town of Eastchester until New York City annexed it in the 1890s, which is why the neighborhood, the town and Eastchester Road elsewhere in the Bronx all share a name. Residents rarely use it; the same entry notes that people more often describe the area as east of White Plains Road, south of Mount Vernon, near Pelham or simply East 233rd Street. When you call us, any of those descriptions will do.

Bronx Community Board 12 describes Eastchester as about half a square mile bordered by the Westchester County line, the New England Thruway, Baychester Avenue and East 233rd Street, with Boston Road as the main thoroughfare and Dyre Avenue as the main commercial strip. The board’s page adds two things that matter for anyone thinking about doors and gates: the neighborhood contains an industrial district, and it contains the NYCHA Boston Secor Houses. Wikipedia fills in the rest of the picture. It describes Eastchester as dominated by one- and two-family homes of various types, with one large public housing project and an industrial district, on low-lying, flat ground with the Hutchinson River running through it. Edenwald, to the southwest, is treated as part of Eastchester in some sources and as its own neighborhood by the community board; we keep a separate Edenwald access control page for its blocks.

That mix sets the agenda. In a district of walk-ups, access control is mostly about one street door shared by dozens of tenants. In Eastchester it is mostly about many separate front doors, side doors, garage doors and yard gates, each with its own owner. The NYU Furman Center’s 2026 profile of the community district that contains Eastchester, Williamsbridge and Baychester records a homeownership rate of 38.6 percent in 2024, above the 32.7 percent citywide rate, and a rental vacancy rate of 3.2 percent. Those numbers describe a district where a large share of households own the door they want to secure, and where a rental unit in a two-family house is often the owner’s main financial asset. Owners here make their own decisions about locks, and they pay for them directly.

The commercial side has the same ground-floor character. Dyre Avenue is the shopping street, and at its north end sits Eastchester–Dyre Avenue, the last stop of the 5 train, which recorded 696,740 riders in 2024 and which the MTA said in July 2025 it plans to make accessible with elevators. Westchester’s Bee-Line buses also run into the neighborhood along Boston Road. A terminal station and a bus transfer bring steady early-morning and late-night foot traffic past the delis, takeout counters, salons and pharmacies on the avenue, and the shops that serve those commuters keep long hours with small staffs. Their weak points are rarely the front door during business hours; they are the staff door, the stock room and the moment of opening and closing.

Then there is Boston Road east of the elevated line. Forgotten New York’s 2018 walk through the area described the stretch near the Hutchinson River as mostly given over to car repair shops, junk dealers and tire shops, with scrapyards, storage facilities and other industrial uses along the river and a security fence manufacturer among them. Businesses like these live behind fences and gates, not storefront glass. Their access problem is a vehicle gate that has to open for a tow truck at two in the morning, a lot full of customers’ cars, service bays with overhead doors, and remotes and keys that former employees may still have in a drawer at home.

And there is the odd corner that only a city-line neighborhood has. The same Forgotten New York posts describe a small slice of Bronx Eastchester on the far side of the Hutchinson River, around Ropes, McOwen and Hollers Avenues and the dead-end Good Place, where the city line cuts across streets. A reader commenting on that post described the pocket, sometimes called Bronx Manor, as having Pelham Manor mailing addresses and Westchester’s 914 area code while paying city taxes, and a 2023 follow-up repeats that description and notes a 1949 agreement under which the Pelham school district educates its children. For an access control job, the practical question there is which city’s building rules apply. We answer it in the questions further down.

One more fact frames the neighborhood’s largest buildings. NYCHA’s September 2024 community plan for Boston Secor, the 1969 development of 538 apartments off Boston Road that moved into the city’s PACT renovation program that month, lists a keyless, layered access control system for building entries: key fobs at front lobbies, rear entrances, laundry rooms, package rooms and community spaces, intercoms in each apartment and new cameras and lighting. Boston Secor is not our client and we have no role in that work. We mention it because it is the most visible access control project in the neighborhood, and it shapes what owners of nearby small buildings ask us for. In January 2026, Norwood News reported a fatal gas explosion and fire at one of the complex’s buildings during that renovation, a reminder that every locked door we install has to let people out instantly and let firefighters in.

Eastchester at a glance, from the sources we read

  • Boundaries and size: about half a square mile, bordered by the Westchester County line, the New England Thruway, Baychester Avenue and East 233rd Street (Bronx Community Board 12).
  • Housing: dominated by one- and two-family homes, with one large public housing development and an industrial district; the Hutchinson River runs through the neighborhood (Wikipedia).
  • Owners, not just renters: the Furman Center’s 2026 figures for Community District 12 show more than a third of homes owner-occupied in 2024 (38.6 percent, above the city’s 32.7) and a 3.2 percent rental vacancy rate.
  • Largest complex: Boston Secor Houses, built 1969, 538 apartments for about 1,180 residents, converted to PACT in September 2024 with a keyless fob system in its plan (NYCHA community plan).
  • Transit: Eastchester–Dyre Avenue and Baychester Avenue on the 5; Bx16 and Bx30 city buses; Bee-Line routes from Mount Vernon and Westchester (Wikipedia).
  • Civic map: Bronx Community Board 12; the 47th Precinct at 4111 Laconia Avenue; ZIP codes 10466 north of Boston Road and 10475 and 10469 south of it (Wikipedia).
Repairs

Gate Stuck Open or Keypad Dead in Eastchester Today?

A gate that will not close leaves a whole lot open, and a house keypad that refuses every code leaves a family on the stoop, so repairs go to the front of our schedule. Call (646) 490-0629 and tell us exactly what you see: a gate that opens but stops halfway, a keypad that lights up but never clicks the lock, a reader that beeps red for every card, a strike that buzzes without releasing, or a door that releases on its own. Those symptoms point to different parts — a tripped safety sensor or a worn gate chain, a dead lock battery, a controller that lost its card list after a power cut, a strike that has drifted out of line with the latch — and a clear description means the technician arrives with the right part.

We repair systems that other companies installed as readily as our own. If you have the installer’s paperwork, the gate operator’s model plate or the name of the keypad app, photograph them before we come; if you do not, we will identify the equipment on site. Most single faults are fixed within an hour or two of arrival, and anything that needs a part we do not carry is made safe the same day: the gate chained shut, the door returned to its mechanical lock, or the keypad bypassed to a key until the part arrives.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Eastchester’s Architecture

Eastchester’s buildings are low and varied: houses from several decades on streets laid out early in the last century, brick two-families, small apartment buildings, shops with apartments above them, and industrial sheds behind fences. Each one puts the controller, the cable and the lock in a different place.

Detached houses on 1920s streets

Forgotten New York dates many of the side streets near Boston Road to the 1920s, some still without sidewalks. Houses here often have a front door, a side door off the driveway, a rear door to the yard and a detached or attached garage, so the first decision is which of those doors matters most.

Brick and frame two-family houses

The most common building in the neighborhood, according to both the community board and Wikipedia. Two households share some doors and not others, and the owner usually lives in one unit. The system has to give each household its own credentials without letting either see the other’s comings and goings.

Low apartment buildings

Small walk-ups and garden-style buildings scattered between the houses. These are multiple dwellings under state law, so the street door, the intercom and any keyless system come with legal duties that a house does not carry.

Shops with apartments above

Two- and three-story buildings on Dyre Avenue and East 233rd Street with a store on the ground floor and residential units upstairs. The residential door is usually beside the shop door, and the two often share a cellar or a rear yard.

Sheds, yards and service bays

The industrial district and the Boston Road strip: single-story repair shops with overhead doors, tire shops, storage buildings and scrapyards behind chain-link or steel fences. Power is often at one end of the lot and the gate at the other.

Churches, schools and community spaces

Houses of worship, after-school programs and community rooms along Boston Road and the avenues, with entry schedules tied to services, classes and meetings, and with doors that must open freely for a hall full of people.

How the work differs door by door and gate by gate

Wood and fiberglass house doors. Most house entry doors in the neighborhood take a keypad deadbolt or keypad lever without any change to the frame, which keeps the job to an hour or two. Where the owner wants a reader and an electric strike instead, so the door can be released from an indoor monitor or a phone, we check that the frame is solid enough to cut, that the door closes against its weatherstrip without being shoved, and that there is a path for a thin cable through the hinge side or up through the cellar ceiling.

Aluminum storm doors and outer security doors. Many houses have a second door in front of the main one. We decide with the owner which of the two is the controlled door. If the outer door is the one that keeps people off the porch, it gets the hardware; if it is mainly for weather, the main door does, and the outer door simply closes on a closer.

Steel doors on sheds and bays. Personnel doors on repair shops and storage buildings are usually hollow-metal doors in steel frames, which take electric strikes and surface-mounted readers well. The questions there are panic hardware, which some occupancies require on the inside, and whether a door that faces the lot needs a heavier reader housing to survive being bumped by vehicles and cleaned with a pressure washer.

Swing and sliding gates. A gate is not a door on a larger scale. A motorized gate has its own controller, its own safety sensors and its own limits, and the access control system should tell that controller when to open rather than try to replace it. We connect keypads, readers and call boxes to the gate operator’s input terminals, leave its photo-eyes and edges in charge of safety, and confirm that the gate still reverses when something is in its path.

Roll-down gates on storefronts. On Dyre Avenue a roll-down gate covers many shop fronts at night. A keypad or reader for the shop door has to sit where it can be reached when the gate is up and is protected when the gate is down, which usually means inside the gate line or on the side wall beside it.

Cross-sell that fits. Most Eastchester owners who add a keypad to the side door or a reader to a yard gate also want to see who used it; see our Eastchester security camera installation page for the camera side of the same visit.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Eastchester

Commercial work in Eastchester divides neatly by street. Dyre Avenue and East 233rd Street are storefront corridors that serve commuters and neighbors; Boston Road mixes a shopping plaza, houses of worship and the long strip of vehicle businesses; and the land along the Hutchinson River holds the industrial uses. Each corridor has a different idea of what the “door” is.

Dyre Avenue

The main commercial strip, running down to the 5 train’s terminal at Dyre Avenue and Light Street. Delis, takeout counters, salons, laundromats and pharmacies with long hours and small staffs need staff-door codes, stock-room readers and schedules more than front-door hardware.

East 233rd Street

A major thoroughfare that crosses Dyre Avenue and carries the Edenwald branch of the public library at 1255 East 233rd Street. Mixed-use buildings here combine shop doors, residential doors and shared cellars.

Boston Road and Shopwell Plaza

Boston Road is the neighborhood’s primary thoroughfare. Wikipedia places Shopwell Plaza, named for the old supermarket chain, on Boston Road beside the Boston Secor houses, and the Hillside post office at 3482 Boston Road. Plaza tenants share service corridors and loading areas.

The vehicle strip near the river

Repair shops, tire shops and junk dealers along Boston Road near the Hutchinson River, as Forgotten New York described them. Gates, fenced lots, overhead bay doors and after-hours drop-offs are the access questions here.

The industrial district

The community board’s description of Eastchester includes an industrial district, and Forgotten New York found scrapyards, storage facilities and a fence manufacturer along the river. Long lots with power at one end and a gate at the other shape every design.

Houses of worship and programs

Churches, after-school programs and community rooms along Boston Road and the side streets want doors that unlock themselves for services and classes and lock again afterwards, with staff codes in between.

Compliance notes for Eastchester properties

  • Egress first. The NYC Building Code allows electrically locked egress doors only with specific release arrangements. Section 1010.1.9.8 covers sensor-released locks: a sensor on the egress side, unlocking on loss of power, a manual “PUSH TO EXIT” device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within 5 feet of the door that keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds, and release when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate. Section 1010.1.9.9 covers magnetic locks released by door hardware with a built-in switch. Every door we lock electrically follows one of those patterns or uses hardware that already allows free exit.
  • Multiple dwellings. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, an intercom with door release in buildings of eight or more apartments, and the same equipment in older buildings once a majority of tenants request it in writing. A fob reader sits beside that intercom; it does not replace it.
  • Keyless entry in apartment buildings. The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (Local Law 63 of 2021) applies to owners of multiple dwellings using fobs, cards, apps or biometrics: express consent, a written retention and privacy policy, minimum data, destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and no requirement to use a smart credential to enter one’s own apartment.
  • Low-voltage permits. The city’s Administrative Code (§28-105.4.7) exempts low-voltage signaling, communication and data wiring under 50 volts from the electrical permit requirement when it is done by a licensed electrician or a qualified person, with exceptions for life-safety systems. The 120-volt circuit that feeds a gate operator or a lock power supply is electrical work for a licensed electrician.
  • Gate safety. As general AESS practice, we never disable or bypass a gate operator’s entrapment protection when adding a keypad or reader, and we test the gate’s reversal with an obstruction before we leave.

This is practical guidance, not legal advice. Owners with a question about a specific building’s classification, a lease or a regulatory agreement should check with their attorney or the relevant city agency.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Eastchester: Questions Answered

These are the questions homeowners, shopkeepers and lot operators in Eastchester bring to us, grouped by the kind of property they come from. The short answer comes first; the reasoning follows.

Houses Near the City Line

Our house has a front door, a side door off the driveway and a door from the garage into the kitchen. Which one should get the keypad first?

The door the family actually uses every day, which on most Eastchester houses is the side door off the driveway, not the front door. People park, walk a few steps and go in the side; the front door is for visitors and mail. Putting the keypad where the habit already is means it gets used, codes replace keys for the trips that matter, and the front door can stay on its deadbolt with a video doorbell for visitors.

The garage-to-house door deserves its own thought. Many families leave it unlocked because the overhead garage door is “the lock”, and an overhead door opened by a remote that was copied, lent or left in an unlocked car then becomes a way into the kitchen. If the garage door is opened by remotes, either the house door from the garage gets a keypad lock too, or the garage door itself moves to a wall keypad and an app, so there are no stray remotes to worry about.

We usually suggest one controlled door at the start and a second later if the first one earns its keep. A single keypad deadbolt on the right door is a modest job; trying to put every door on a system in one visit is how owners end up paying for hardware nobody uses.

Our teenagers keep losing house keys. For children, is a code, a fob or a phone app the better credential?

A personal code is usually best for children, with a fob as a backup for the youngest and a phone app only for those old enough to keep a phone charged. A code cannot be dropped on the bus or left in a coat at school, and it can be changed in seconds if a friend learns it. A fob suits a child who cannot yet be trusted to keep a number private, because a lost fob can be deleted from the lock without changing anything for the rest of the family.

Phone apps are convenient for adults and a poor choice as a child’s only way in. A phone at five percent battery at the end of a school day is a locked-out child on the stoop. If the family likes the app, we set each child up with the app and a code, so the code is always there when the phone is not.

Our house east of the Hutchinson River has a Pelham Manor mailing address and a 914 phone number. Do New York City’s rules or Westchester’s apply to the entry system?

If the property is in the Bronx, the city’s building and electrical rules apply, whatever the mailing address says. Forgotten New York’s posts on the area describe a small pocket of Bronx Eastchester across the river, around Ropes, McOwen and Hollers Avenues and the western end of Good Place, where the city line cuts across streets, and a reader comment there describes Pelham Manor postal addresses and 914 numbers on homes that pay city taxes. Postal routes and phone exchanges do not move municipal boundaries.

Before quoting a job in that pocket we confirm the borough and block from the city’s property records, so there is no doubt about which code governs the door hardware and which utility rules apply to any new circuit. Forgotten New York notes that Con Edison serves both sides of the line, so the power company does not change either.

The back of our lot meets the Thruway fence, and the yard gate is the only other way in. Should that gate be on the same system as the house?

Yes, if the yard gate is something people use; no, if it only exists for the occasional delivery of a load of mulch. A rear or side gate that family members use daily belongs on the same keypad codes as the side door, so one person’s code works at both and one deletion removes both. A gate that is opened twice a year is better served by a good padlock and a camera that watches it.

Wikipedia notes that the New England Thruway runs along the neighborhood’s eastern edge, and houses on the blocks beside it often have narrow yards against a highway fence or embankment. Gates there tend to be chain-link or welded steel on a post set in soil. A battery keypad lock made for gates, or a gate latch with a low-voltage electric release fed from the house, are the two practical options; we choose between them by how far the gate is from a power source and how much the post moves in winter.

Whichever option we use, the gate has to open from the yard side without a code. A yard where someone could be trapped by a locked gate during a fire in the house is worse than one with no lock at all, so free exit from the inside is designed in from the start.

Storefronts at the End of the 5 Line

Can a door behind the counter keep anyone from following a cashier into the back room while the shop is open?

Yes. A self-closing door with a code or card reader and a lock that releases freely from the back-room side is one of the most useful changes a small shop can make. The front door has to stay open during business hours, so it can never be the security boundary while customers are inside. The boundary that can hold is the door between the sales floor and the back room, where the cash, the safe, the stock and the staff’s belongings are.

amNY reported in November 2023 that two men robbed a deli at 3805 Dyre Avenue shortly before 1 a.m. on October 31 of that year, taking phones and cash from people inside even though the store was full. Nothing on a door would have stopped a robbery at the counter of an open store, and we will not pretend otherwise. What a controlled back-room door does is limit how far such an incident reaches: staff can step behind it and it latches, and nobody can push through to the safe or the stock room after them.

The details matter. The door needs a closer strong enough to latch every time, a reader or keypad on the sales-floor side only, and hardware on the back-room side that opens with a single push, so staff are never trapped. A panic button or a phone in the back room completes the arrangement; a camera over the door records who came and went.

Can the lock on our late-night store’s front door change over on a timer at midnight, so nobody has to remember to flip it?

Yes. An electric strike or electrified lock on the front door, a schedule in the controller and a release button behind the counter let the door run open by day and locked by night, with staff buzzing customers in after the cut-off. Many late-night stores in the city do this with a mechanical latch and a button; doing it with a scheduled lock means nobody has to remember to switch it, and the change happens at the same minute every night.

The door must still open from the inside with a single motion at every hour, which is why we use either a strike that leaves the inside lever free or a sensor-released lock with the push-to-exit device the Building Code requires. A camera at the door and a small monitor at the register let the cashier see who is outside before pressing the release.

Schedules can follow the business rather than the clock: an earlier lock on Sunday nights, a later one on Fridays, and holiday overrides loaded in advance. The owner can change the schedule from a phone, and every release from the register button is logged with the time.

We rent the shop below our apartment on Dyre Avenue to a tenant who wants a keypad on the back door. Who should own the codes?

The shop tenant should control the codes for the shop’s own doors, and the building owner should hold an administrator code for emergencies, written into the lease. A commercial tenant needs to add and remove employees without asking the landlord every time. A landlord needs a way in when a pipe bursts in the cellar at 4 a.m. and the tenant is on vacation. Both needs fit in one system if the roles are set up correctly from day one.

In practice that means a lock or controller with separate user groups: the tenant administers the shop group, the owner holds one master credential that is used only for emergencies and is logged when it is. When the lease ends, the tenant’s group is deleted in one step and the next tenant starts clean.

Yards, Gates and Service Bays on Boston Road

Our repair shop keeps customers’ cars in a fenced lot overnight. How should the lot gate handle tow trucks that arrive at all hours?

Give each towing company its own gate code or card that works only at the hours you choose, add a call box that rings the owner’s phone for anyone without a code, and keep a camera on the gate. A single shared gate code given to every driver is the most common arrangement on Boston Road and the least controllable: it spreads, it never changes, and when something goes missing from the lot the log says only that “the code” was used.

Separate credentials per company mean the gate log shows which tow operator came in at 2:14 a.m., and a company you stop working with is removed in seconds. Where drivers change often, a call box with video lets the owner see the truck, talk to the driver and open the gate from bed, with the release logged under the owner’s name.

The gate itself must keep its safety devices in charge. We connect the keypad, reader or call box to the gate operator’s inputs and leave its photo-eyes and sensing edges untouched, and we confirm the gate stops and reverses for an obstruction before we leave. A car that is towed into a gate that closes on it costs far more than any break-in.

Can a keypad or reader be added to a sliding gate that already has a motor, or does the whole operator have to be replaced?

Usually it can be added. Almost every gate operator has input terminals for an external open command, and a keypad, reader or call box connects to those. What we check first is whether the operator is healthy: whether it opens and closes smoothly, whether its safety sensors work, and whether its control board is still made. An operator that is already failing will fail with a new keypad on it too, and we tell the owner so before adding anything.

The second check is power and cable. The keypad post usually stands a car length in front of the gate, and the cable from it has to reach the operator without being driven over. Where the lot surface makes a trench impractical, we use a wireless link between the keypad and the operator, or mount the keypad on the fence post beside the gate with the cable running along the fence.

The third is the exit. Vehicles leaving the lot should not need a code, so we use the operator’s free-exit input with a vehicle detector or a button inside the lot, and we set the gate’s close timer so it does not shut on the next car in line.

Former mechanics may still have remotes for our bay doors. How do we take back control without changing every door?

Erase every remote from each door opener’s memory, re-learn only the remotes you can physically account for, and move day-to-day opening to a wall keypad or reader with personal credentials. Most overhead door openers store their remotes in a memory that can be cleared, and clearing it costs nothing but an hour. The problem is that you then have to know where every legitimate remote is, which is rarely true in a shop that has had staff come and go for years.

The lasting fix is to stop treating remotes as the credential. A reader or keypad by each bay door, wired to the opener’s wall-button terminals, lets each employee use their own code or card, and a departed employee is removed in one step. The owner keeps one or two remotes for vehicles that must open the door from inside the cab.

If the bay doors are also the building’s exits, nothing about the access system may stop them from being opened from the inside in an emergency. We leave the manual release and any personnel doors exactly as they were.

Our storage yard backs onto the river and the gate is far from any power. Can a reader still work there?

Yes, with one of three approaches: a battery-powered gate lock with its own keypad or reader, a low-voltage cable run from the building along the fence, or a small solar-charged power supply at the gate. Which is right depends on how often the gate is used and whether it is motorized. A manual pedestrian gate used a few times a day is well served by a battery gate lock; a motorized vehicle gate needs a steady supply.

Forgotten New York describes scrapyards, storage facilities and other industrial uses along the Hutchinson River, and lots there are often long and narrow, with the office at the street and the back gate hundreds of feet away. Running cable along an existing fence line in a protective conduit is usually cheaper and more reliable than trenching across a lot that sees heavy vehicles.

Whatever powers the gate, its status should reach the office. A door-position contact on the gate, reported back to the controller, tells the owner when the back gate is open at an hour it should not be, which is the information a yard owner most often lacks.

Small Buildings Near a Big Fob Project

The NYCHA complex nearby is getting fobs at its lobbies, laundries and package rooms. We own a twelve-unit building. Which parts of that plan make sense at our scale?

The street door, the cellar or laundry door and the intercom make sense in a twelve-unit building; a package-room reader and campus-wide monitoring usually do not. NYCHA’s 2024 Boston Secor plan lists fobs at front lobbies, rear entrances, laundry rooms, package rooms and community spaces across four residential towers, plus 24-hour camera monitoring by management. That scale of system suits 538 apartments with on-site staff. A twelve-unit owner gets most of the benefit from one well-hung street door with a reader, the required intercom, and a second reader on whichever back or cellar door is the weak point.

The part of the big plan that applies fully is the legal frame. A twelve-unit building is a multiple dwelling, so the Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to its keyless system exactly as it applies to a tower: consent forms, a written retention and privacy policy, minimum data, and a 90-day limit on keeping authentication records unless anonymized. We hand owners a template policy and set the controller’s log retention to match.

How do firefighters and EMS get through a street door that only opens for fobs?

The same way they always have: the door keeps a mechanical key cylinder for the building’s emergency key, fire alarm activation releases locks that are required to release, and nothing about the reader stops anyone leaving. As general AESS practice we never install a street door lock that has no mechanical override. Power fails, controllers fail and fobs get lost, and a door with no key path at all is a door that has to be broken in an emergency.

The Building Code’s sensor-release rules require electrically locked egress doors to unlock when the fire alarm or sprinkler system activates and when the lock loses power. On a small building without a fire alarm system, we use hardware that lets people out with a single motion regardless of power and keep the lock fail-secure only on the outside, so the door stays locked to the street but never to the people inside.

The January 2026 fire at Boston Secor, which Norwood News reported followed a gas explosion during renovations, is a sober reminder of why these rules exist. When people bang on doors in the middle of the night telling neighbors to get out, every exit has to work without a code, a card or a thought.

Installers of security and fire alarm systems in New York State need a state license under General Business Law §69-m, which covers work anywhere in the state; local electrical rules then apply on each side of the line. Our license, #12000287431, is issued by the state, so it covers an Eastchester house and a Mount Vernon store alike. What changes at the line is the local layer: New York City’s Building Code and electrical rules on the Bronx side, and the city or village rules of Mount Vernon or Pelham Manor on the other.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Eastchester

We could not view Google’s AI Overview for Eastchester searches while writing this page, so we do not quote it. What follows are claims we hear repeated by callers who have read general cost guides, national retailer pages and automated answers, each followed by what we find on the ground in this neighborhood.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars.”

This claim usually comes from smart-lock pricing. Fixr’s cost page puts an installed smart lock at around $300 on average, and that is a fair figure for a battery keypad deadbolt on a house door. It is not the price of a wired reader with an electric strike, a controller and a battery-backed power supply, which is what a shop’s back room or a building’s street door needs.

Installers who publish their own figures put wired access control at roughly $1,500 to $5,000 per door including cabling and the panel, and our own single-door range is $1,500 to $2,500. Gate work is priced after a site visit because the gate operator’s condition, the distance to power and the safety devices vary so much from lot to lot.

The honest summary for an Eastchester homeowner is that a keypad lock on the side door can be a small purchase, and a business that needs logs, schedules and a lock that releases from a counter button is buying a system. Both are reasonable; they are just different things.

“A smart lock is the same thing as access control.”

A smart lock is one kind of access control, and for a house it is often the right one. Where the comparison breaks down is when several doors, several people with different schedules, and a need for records are involved. A shop with a back door, a stock room and a cash office needs one place to add and remove staff, not three separate apps.

Smart locks also stop at the door. They do not open a gate operator, raise a bay door, report a door left ajar to a central panel or release on a fire alarm. Those jobs belong to a controller with wired locks and inputs.

Our rule of thumb: one or two doors used by one household, a good keypad or smart lock; more doors, more people or any business use, a controller-based system that can grow.

“A gate is just a big door.”

A motorized gate is a machine with its own controller, motor, limits and entrapment protection. The access control system’s job is to tell that machine when to open; it is not to replace the machine’s own logic. Treating a gate as a big door is how keypads end up wired around safety devices, which is dangerous and leaves the owner liable.

On the Boston Road lots, the gate questions are about vehicles: how a tow truck gets in at night, how cars leave without a code, how long the gate stays open, and what happens if a car stops in the gate’s path. None of those come up at a house door.

“Eastchester means the town in Westchester.”

Automated answers mix up the Bronx neighborhood, the Westchester town and Eastchester Road, which runs through other Bronx neighborhoods. Wikipedia’s entry for the Bronx neighborhood begins by warning readers not to confuse it with the town or its census-designated place, and notes that the town is markedly more affluent than the neighborhood that shares its old name.

The confusion has practical consequences. Westchester pricing, Westchester permit rules and Westchester contractors’ service areas do not apply to a house on a street off Dyre Avenue, and Bronx rules do not apply to a house in the Westchester town. A quote that talks about the wrong Eastchester is a sign the installer has not looked at your address.

“Biometric entry is banned in New York City apartment buildings.”

It is not banned, but it is regulated. HPD’s summary of the Tenant Data Privacy Law lists biometric identifiers among the data a smart access system in a multiple dwelling may use, subject to express consent, minimum data, a written policy and destruction of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, and owners may not require a smart credential to enter a tenant’s own apartment.

For houses and businesses with no apartments, that law does not apply in the same way, though a business still has to think about its employees. New York’s 2022 electronic monitoring law, as described by employment lawyers, requires employers to give notice before monitoring employees’ phone, email and internet use; it does not mention door logs, but we suggest employers tell staff plainly what the door system records.

“You need a monthly subscription for remote access.”

Some systems are sold only with a cloud subscription, and some features, such as phone-based visitor calls, may depend on one. Many controllers, keypad locks and gate call boxes work fully on site and offer remote access without a monthly fee.

We specify systems that run without a subscription by default and price any optional service separately, so an owner can decline it. Whatever the system, the administrator login is set up in the owner’s name and handed over at completion.

“Every keypad needs a city permit,” or “none ever does.”

Both are wrong. The city’s Administrative Code exempts low-voltage signaling, communication and data wiring under 50 volts from the electrical permit requirement when it is done by a licensed electrician or a qualified person, with exceptions for life-safety systems. A battery keypad lock on a house door involves no permit at all.

The 120-volt circuit that feeds a gate operator or a lock power supply is a different matter: it is electrical work for a licensed electrician. Work that changes how a building’s exits operate also has to meet the Building Code’s egress requirements, whatever the voltage.

We tell owners at the estimate stage which parts of a job are low-voltage work we do ourselves and which parts need an electrician, and we coordinate the electrician when one is needed.

We leave every Eastchester owner with a short routine. Open each controlled door fully and let it close on its own; it should latch without a push. Five minutes a month catches most failures before they leave a door or a lot open.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Test the Gate Before You Touch the Keypad

This is a note on method, not a story about a particular lot. When an owner asks for a keypad on a motorized gate, the first thing we do is not look at keypads. We stand beside the gate and run it through a full cycle, then run it again with a soft obstruction in its path. A gate that does not stop and reverse is a gate that can injure someone or crush a car, and adding a keypad to it only makes it open and close more often.

Then we open the operator’s cover. We look for the model plate, the input terminals, the state of the wiring, any jumpers someone has added to bypass a sensor, and signs of water in the housing. We check the chain or rack for wear and the gate’s rollers or hinges for drag, because an operator straining against a stiff gate trips its own overload and looks like an access control fault later.

Only after that do we choose the keypad or reader, its post location and its cable path. If the operator needs repair first, we say so in writing before quoting the access side. It is the gate version of a rule that applies to every door we touch: the opening has to work mechanically before any electronics can make it secure.

FAQ

Eastchester Access Control FAQ

If the controller in our shop office is damaged or stolen, do we lose every code and the gate log?
+

Not if the system is set up properly. As part of handover we export the user list and settings and give the owner a copy, and on systems with cloud or on-site management software the database lives off the controller as well. A replacement controller can then be loaded with every user, schedule and door setting in one session instead of re-enrolling people one by one. The event log is a different matter: entries stored only on a destroyed controller are gone, which is why we mount controllers in a locked enclosure away from the door they serve and, for businesses that care about the history, set the log to copy to the management software as events happen.

Can a gate call box ring my cellphone when a customer or a delivery is waiting at the gate?
+

Yes. Many gate call boxes can place a call or send a video call to one or more phones, so the owner or the person on duty can see the visitor, talk to them and open the gate remotely. The release is logged under the name of whoever opened it. We set up a call order, for example the shop phone first and the owner's cellphone second, and decide with you what happens after hours, such as sending calls straight to voicemail while keeping codes working for drivers you trust.

Can the cleaner's code open our side door without also turning off the house alarm?
+

Yes, provided the lock and the alarm are set up to treat users differently. Some keypad locks and access controllers can tell an alarm panel which user just opened the door, and the panel can then disarm for the family's codes while ignoring the cleaner's, so the cleaner still has to enter a separate alarm code or the alarm stays armed in the rooms they do not use. Whether that split is possible depends on the specific lock and alarm brands, so we check both before promising it. Where it is not, the simpler answer is a cleaner's door code that only works on cleaning days and an alarm partition that leaves the work areas disarmed.

Do you install keypads and readers on garage doors?
+

Yes. An overhead garage door can be controlled by a wall keypad or a reader wired to the opener's push-button terminals, or by a smart garage controller with an app. That replaces handheld remotes, which are easy to lose and hard to account for, with personal codes that can be deleted. The garage door's own safety sensors stay in charge of closing. Because the door from the garage into the house is the next barrier, we look at that door too and often suggest a keypad lock there as well.

Who should hold the administrator account for a family home system?
+

One adult owner, with a second adult as a backup administrator. The administrator can add and delete codes, see the log and change settings, so the account should belong to the person who owns the house, not to the installer, a relative who set it up once or a tenant. We create the account in the owner's name and email, hand over the recovery details in writing, and show the backup administrator how to remove a code in case the owner is away. Children and tenants get user codes, never admin rights.

Do the city's tenant data rules apply to a door system at a business with no apartments?
+

Not in the same way. HPD describes the Tenant Data Privacy Law as applying to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access systems, so a shop, repair garage or storage yard with no apartments falls outside it. Employers still have duties to staff, and New York's 2022 electronic monitoring law requires notice before monitoring employees' phone, email and internet use; it does not mention door logs, but telling employees plainly what the door system records is good practice. A mixed-use building with apartments upstairs is a multiple dwelling, so its residential doors are covered.

Can a new keypad or door station use the wire from our old doorbell?
+

Sometimes. Many older houses have a thin two-wire doorbell cable from the front door to a transformer in the basement, and some video doorbells and door stations are designed to run on exactly that wire and power. A reader with an electric strike usually needs more conductors and a dedicated power supply, so the old doorbell wire is rarely enough for a full access control door. We test the existing wire and transformer during the survey, tell you which devices it can support, and only run new cable when the old one cannot do the job.

Is a Wi-Fi smart lock enough for our house, or do we need a wired controller?
+

For one or two doors used by one household, a good Wi-Fi or Bluetooth smart lock with a keypad is usually enough. It needs no cable, runs on batteries and keeps a mechanical key override. A wired controller makes sense when there are several doors or a gate, when the house is a two-family with separate households, when the door needs to release from an indoor monitor or a phone call, or when the owner wants a door-left-open alert. We are happy to recommend the simpler option when it fits.

What should we have ready before the survey of a gate on our lot?
+

Three things help most. First, the gate operator's model plate, photographed with the cover closed, and any manual or installer paperwork you have. Second, a list of who needs to come through the gate and when: staff, towing companies, customers, deliveries and the owner. Third, the location of the nearest electrical panel and internet connection. With those, the survey takes less than an hour, and we can tell you on the spot whether the operator can take a keypad or reader as it is or needs work first.

Can access control be added to a new fence and gate that a fence company is still building?
+

Yes, and it is the cheapest time to do it. If we talk to the fence company before the posts are set, they can leave a conduit or a sleeve to the keypad post, a mounting plate on the gate frame and a path for cable to the operator, and we can agree which side of the gate the reader sits on. Coordinating at that stage avoids cutting a finished driveway or drilling a new powder-coated post. We work alongside the fence contractor rather than replacing them, and we put the division of work in writing.
