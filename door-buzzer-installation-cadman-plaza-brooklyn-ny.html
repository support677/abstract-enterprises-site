The Neighborhood

Why Cadman Plaza Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Cadman Plaza is a park before it is a neighborhood. Named for the Reverend S. Parkes Cadman, it runs from the Brooklyn Bridge south toward Borough Hall between Cadman Plaza West and Cadman Plaza East, and the people who live and work around it are spread across very different buildings. To the west, between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street, stands the residential complex the city built on the old bridge trolley terminal site in the late 1960s and early 1970s: Cadman Plaza North at 140 Cadman Plaza West, the Whitman Close tower and townhouses at 75 Henry Street, and Cadman Towers at 101 Clark Street and 10 Clinton Street. On the old library site at the south end of that strip, One Clinton now rises over the new Brooklyn Heights branch. East and south of the park are the Theodore Roosevelt federal courthouse, the old post office and bankruptcy court, the state Supreme Court at 360 Adams Street, Borough Hall and the office towers of Court Street.

That mix is what makes door buzzer installation here unusual. Most of the homes around the park are in elevator towers with a doorman at a front desk, so the first job of an entry system is not to let a visitor talk to an apartment from the sidewalk. It is to let the desk reach every apartment quickly, to cover the doors the desk cannot see, and to keep working when the desk is unstaffed for a break or a round of the building. A 2025 decision of the Kings County Supreme Court involving the co-op at 140 Cadman Plaza West describes doormen there, including a relief doorman reached on a building phone. CityRealty lists a full-time doorman at 75 Henry Street and a 24-hour attended lobby with a concierge at One Clinton. In buildings like these, the house phone between the desk and the apartments is the buzzer system, and when it fails the desk has no clean way to announce a guest.

The second thing that sets Cadman Plaza apart is the townhouses. The planners added low duplex houses at the base of the towers to bring the complex down to the scale of the brownstones across Henry Street. A widely cited architectural history counts 50 duplex townhouses at Cadman Towers, and Brownstoner's 2012 profile of Whitman Close counts "only around 16" two-story houses there. Brownstoner described those houses as "an odd mix of public and private space, with the entrances and backyards right on public walkways always crowded with commuters and tourists." A townhouse door that opens onto a walkway used by people heading for the bridge has its own needs: a station that a visitor can find and a passer-by will not play with, a camera that shows who is actually at the door rather than the crowd behind them, and, in many houses, a way for the household to answer from the second floor without coming down.

The third is ownership. These are co-op and condo buildings, so every decision about the front door goes through a board, and the boards here have been busy. THE CITY reported in April 2024 that Cadman Towers became the first Mitchell-Lama co-op approved to convert to a Housing Development Fund Corporation, with more than three-quarters of shareholders in favor, $62 million of debt to manage and resale prices capped, and new buyers now coming in through the city's Housing Connect lottery. A building where apartments change hands through a lottery, often to people who have never lived in a doorman tower, needs an entry system that is simple to learn and easy to update when a household moves in or out. One Clinton is a much newer condominium, completed around 2020, with equipment chosen by its developer. And the shops along Pineapple Walk, the strip between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street that the 75 Henry Street co-op owns, need doors that serve customers and deliveries without opening into the residential side of the building.

The city's own records suggest the towers are not in the kind of trouble that drives entry work in many Brooklyn neighborhoods. When we pulled NYC 311 complaints filed under bell, buzzer and intercom for ZIP 11201 from January 1 to November 2, 2026, we found 22 across the whole ZIP, from Downtown to Cobble Hill, and none at 140 Cadman Plaza West, 75 Henry Street, 101 Clark Street, 10 Clinton Street or One Clinton. HPD's violation records for the ZIP hold roughly 110 intercom violations going back to 1984, again with none at those addresses. So installation here is usually a choice made by a board or an owner, not a repair ordered by an inspector: replacing a desk phone system that has outlived its parts, adding video so the desk and residents can see who is at a side door, giving townhouses their own stations, or bringing the garage and service doors into one plan. That is good news for a building, because it can plan the work around its budget and its residents instead of a 14-day correction deadline.

Six Cadman Plaza facts that shape a buzzer or entry installation

  • Tall co-op towers. CityRealty lists 140 Cadman Plaza West at 27 floors and 250 apartments, built in 1967, and 75 Henry Street at 32 stories, completed in 1973; Cadman Towers' two towers rise 32 and 13 stories. An entry system has to carry calls up a long riser to hundreds of apartments.
  • Doormen at the desk. Court records and building listings describe doormen at 140 Cadman Plaza West and 75 Henry Street and a 24-hour attended lobby at One Clinton. The desk console and the house phone are part of the entry system, not a substitute for it.
  • Townhouses on public walkways. Brownstoner described the Whitman Close townhouse entrances as sitting right on walkways crowded with commuters and tourists. Each townhouse door needs its own station, placed and aimed for a busy path.
  • Garages and plazas. The complex was built with garages and raised plazas, so residents arrive by car, on foot from the plaza and from the street. Each of those routes ends at a door that someone has to be able to open.
  • Resales through a lottery. After its 2024 conversion, Cadman Towers' new buyers come through Housing Connect, so households change and new residents need to be added to the system without a service call each time.
  • Next to a historic district. The Brooklyn Heights Historic District, designated in 1965, is bounded by Cadman Plaza West on the north, and the complex was left out of it. Buildings across Henry Street are inside; the towers are not.

Repair or Replace in Cadman Plaza

Need Repair in Cadman Plaza? A House Phone That Rings the Wrong Floor, a Townhouse Bell Gone Quiet, or a Garage Door That Will Not Release

A good share of the calls that start as installation questions in Cadman Plaza turn out to be repairs. A desk system where a handful of apartments stopped ringing is often a single damaged pair or a loose connection in a riser closet, not a reason to replace the whole building. A townhouse bell that went silent may be a worn button or a failed transformer. A garage or service door that buzzes but does not open is usually a strike, a closer or a door that has dropped on its hinges. Before we recommend a new system, we look at what is there and tell you plainly whether fixing it is the better value.

If a door is stuck open or cannot be locked right now, say so when you call (347) 934-8335. A diagnosis is the $250 service call booked online, and the $250 is credited toward the repair or toward a new system if replacement turns out to make more sense. You get both options with prices and a straight recommendation. If a part has to be ordered, we leave the door able to be locked and opened with a key before we go, so the desk or the household is never left with a door that cannot be secured.

The doorman co-op tower: the desk is the hub, not the front panel

In a doorman tower the visitor's first stop is a person, so the entry system is built around that person's desk. A visitor gives a name, the doorman calls the apartment, the resident says yes or no, and the doorman lets the guest through. Every part of that exchange needs equipment: a console or handset at the desk that can call any apartment by number, a station in each apartment that rings loudly enough to be heard in a bedroom down the hall, and, ideally, a camera view at the desk of every door the desk is responsible for but cannot see from where it sits. In the older towers around the park, that equipment is often the original house phone system or a replacement from a later decade, and the parts that wear out first are the desk console and the apartment handsets, not the cable.

Because the desk is not staffed every minute, a tower also needs an entrance panel that works without it. Doormen take breaks, sign for deliveries in the package room and walk the building. During those minutes a visitor at the door should still be able to call an apartment, or call the desk's handset wherever the doorman is. That is the panel at the street or plaza door, and in towers with more than one public entrance, the panel at each of them. The design question for a board is which doors stay locked to the street at all times, which are opened only by the desk, and which residents can open with a code, a card or a phone.

The cable in these towers runs up a riser beside the elevator core and branches to each floor. Over a building of thirty floors that is a long run, and an older riser has often been spliced and re-terminated more than once. We test it pair by pair before deciding anything. Where the pairs are sound, a two-wire system can reuse them; Aiphone describes its GT Series as a multi-tenant system for "new builds and retrofit projects" that supports up to 5,000 tenant stations and can use existing wire. Where whole sections are bad, we pull new cable in those sections only and reuse the rest. Our multi-unit range on the Brooklyn list starts at about $2,500 and passes $10,000 for large video or app systems, and in a tower this size the riser test and the number of apartments decide where in that range the building lands.

The duplex townhouse at the tower base: a private door on a public path

The townhouses that sit at the foot of the Cadman Plaza towers are part of the same co-op as the tower above them, but they do not share its front door. Each has its own street entrance, and Brownstoner's description of Whitman Close, entrances "right on public walkways always crowded with commuters and tourists," fits what owners tell us about the whole complex. That changes how a doorbell or door station is chosen. The station needs a lit button and a clear label so a visitor can tell which house they are at, a camera that looks at the person standing at the door rather than down the length of the walkway, and housing that holds up to being touched, leaned on and occasionally pressed by people who are only passing by.

Inside, a duplex means two floors to hear the door on. A single chime in the entry hall is easy to miss from a bedroom upstairs, so most townhouse installations add a second answering station on the upper floor or ring the household's phones. Many owners also ask whether the townhouse can call the tower's desk, or whether the desk can see the townhouse door. Both are possible if the townhouse station is part of the building's system rather than a stand-alone doorbell, and whether that makes sense depends on how the board wants the desk to serve the townhouses. We price both ways so the owner and the board can decide together.

The newer condominium: inheriting the developer's system

One Clinton, completed around 2020 above the new library, is the newest residential building on the park. CityRealty lists a full-time doorman, a concierge and a 24-hour attended lobby. A building of that age usually came with an entry system the developer chose and installed during construction, and the condo board inherits it along with whatever service plan or software contract came with it. When condo boards call us, it is usually to add something the developer left out, such as a station at a second door, video at the desk for the service entrance or a panel for after-hours entry, or to understand what they actually own before the original contract renews.

The smaller walk-up or elevator building: the classic vestibule panel

Across Henry Street and along Clark and Middagh Streets are older brick apartment buildings without doormen, the kind a Reddit user once asked about as "one of those small brick apartment buildings on Henry Street by Cadman Plaza." These are the closest thing near the park to a standard Brooklyn buzzer job: a panel in the outer vestibule, a release on the inner door, a cable run up the stairs or a small riser, and a station in each apartment. A resident of a roughly 20-unit Brooklyn Heights building wrote on Reddit that the front door there "hydraulically locks when pushed open", in a thread about a door left propped late at night. A door that closes and locks on its own is the point of the whole system, and we check the closer and latch on every door we wire.

Many of these buildings sit inside the Brooklyn Heights Historic District, which the city designated in 1965 and which is bounded by Cadman Plaza West on the north. A panel on the front of a building inside the district goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission's staff rules described further down this page. Buildings in the residential complex west of the park were left out of the district, so the towers and townhouses follow the co-op's rules instead.

The office tower suite: a buzzer behind someone else's lobby

The office towers near Borough Hall, including the 35-story Montague-Court Building at 16 Court Street, completed in 1927, have their own lobbies, their own security desks and their own rules about what tenants may install. A law office or medical practice on an upper floor usually wants a door station at the suite entrance with a camera, a release button at the reception desk and a closer that shuts the door behind every visitor, so that clients, couriers and process servers are seen before they reach the front office. The work stays inside the suite, but the landlord still approves it, and the building often specifies when contractors may work and how they reach the floor.

The shop under the tower: customers, deliveries and a co-op landlord

Pineapple Walk, the strip of shops between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street, belongs to the 75 Henry Street co-op, according to the Brooklyn Paper's 2016 report on the shareholders' vote against a proposed tower deal there. A shop in a strip like that has two masters for its doors: the shop, which wants customers and deliveries in easily, and the co-op, which wants nothing that opens into the residential side of the building. A buzzer or video station at the shop's delivery door, answered at the counter or on the owner's phone, does the first job. Keeping that station entirely separate from the building's entry system, on its own power and its own cable, does the second. We plan shop entry so a shop key, code or release never opens a residential door.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Walking a Cadman Plaza Tower With the Super Before Pricing an Entry System

These notes describe how our technicians approach a doorman tower and its townhouses like the ones around Cadman Plaza. They are our standard practice for this kind of building, not an account of any single address.

Start at the desk, not the door. The first stop is the doorman's desk, because that is where the system lives in a staffed building. We ask the staff on duty how they announce a guest today, how long it takes to reach an apartment, which apartments never seem to answer and which doors they cannot see from the desk. Doormen know more about a building's entry problems than anyone, and what they describe usually points straight to the faults in the cable or the stations.

Count every door that ends a journey. Then we walk every route a person can take into the building: the street door, the plaza door, the door from the garage, the service entrance, the door from the package room, the townhouse doors. Each one gets a line on the plan with who uses it, who should be able to open it and whether anyone sees it. Boards are often surprised by how many doors there are once they are listed in one place.

Open the riser closets. With the super, we open the closets on several floors, top, middle and bottom, and look at how the cable is terminated, whether it has been spliced and whether there is room for new equipment. We test a sample of pairs on the spot so the first estimate is based on the building's real cable rather than a guess.

Let every door go. At each door the system will open, we push it fully open and let go. If it does not close and latch on its own, it needs a closer adjustment, new hinges or a realigned strike before any release is wired to it. HPD treats a hallway or entrance door that fails to self-close and latch as an immediately hazardous violation, so this check protects the building as well as the system.

Stand on the walkway. At the townhouses, we stand where visitors stop and look at the camera's picture with people walking past behind them. On a busy path, a camera aimed straight out shows strangers more than callers, so the station is angled and the button is placed where only someone facing the door will reach it.

The Doorman Desk

Building the Entry System Around a Cadman Plaza Doorman Desk

In most of Brooklyn a buzzer system has two ends, the door and the apartment. In the Cadman Plaza towers it has three: the door, the desk and the apartment. Getting the desk right is what separates an installation that the staff like using from one they work around with their own cell phones.

Calling from the desk. The desk needs a console or master station that dials any apartment directly by number, with a display that shows which apartment is being called and whether it answered. In a building of 250 to 400 apartments the staff place a great many calls a day, so speed matters: one press to call, a clear ring in the apartment, and no hunting through menus. Many current systems let the desk keep a call log, which helps a managing agent see when an apartment repeatedly does not answer and its station may need attention.

Seeing the doors the desk cannot. A doorman at the main lobby desk usually cannot see the garage door, the service entrance or a second lobby door off the plaza. Video door stations at those entrances, displayed on the desk console, let staff see who is calling before releasing anything. Security Sales described a 148-unit Park Slope co-op that took this approach in 2015, putting video intercoms at both lobby entries and the service entrance with a concierge station on the superintendent's desk. The building's property manager said the board chose video "to give them a better idea of who's asking to enter the building."

When the desk is empty. A doorman on a break or in the package room should not mean a visitor waits at a locked door with no way to call. The entrance panel can call apartments directly whenever the desk is unattended, or forward desk calls to a handset or phone the doorman carries. Some buildings set the panel to call the desk first and fall back to the apartment after a short delay. We set this up with the staff so it matches how the building actually runs at night and on weekends.

Remote management or a closed system. Habitat Magazine reported in 2018 on a Manhattan co-op that turned down a smartphone system and installed a hardwired Aiphone video system for $20,000 on its existing wiring; its board secretary said, "Most of the board felt more comfortable staying with a closed-circuit, hardwired system versus a cloud solution." Either choice is reasonable, and we price both.

Concrete and Risers

Running Buzzer Cable Through Cadman Plaza's Poured-Concrete Towers

The Cadman Plaza towers are built of poured concrete, with the saw-toothed concrete walls that architectural histories of the complex describe. That is excellent for a building's durability and hard on anyone who needs to run a new wire. A brownstone has hollow walls and a cellar ceiling to route through. A concrete tower has a riser shaft, hung ceilings in the corridors, and apartment walls that are often solid concrete or block with a skim of plaster. The cable plan has to be worked out around that before any price is final.

The riser is the backbone. Every floor's cable comes off the riser in a closet near the elevators. If the existing riser cable tests sound, it carries the new system and the cable work in the building drops to almost nothing. If parts of it are bad, new cable is pulled through the same sleeves between floors, which the super can usually open from the closets. Brick Underground's NYC intercom guide quotes an installer saying that, with older systems, "while they may be outdated, the core wiring is typically fine," and that matches what we usually find in buildings of this age. A different installer in the same article said "a 100-unit building could be done in about two days," which gives a sense of scale; a tower of 250 or more apartments takes longer, and we schedule it floor by floor.

Townhouses and Walkways

Door Stations for the Cadman Plaza Townhouses on Public Walkways

The duplex townhouses are the homes around Cadman Plaza most like a private house, and they are also the ones most exposed to the public. Their doors face walkways and plazas that carry a steady stream of people toward the Brooklyn Bridge, the courts and the High Street subway station. A door station that would be unremarkable on a quiet brownstone block needs more thought here.

Camera angle. A camera aimed straight out from a townhouse door on a walkway shows the walkway: commuters, tourists, strollers and bikes. The owner wants to see the person at the door. A slight downward angle and a narrower view keep the picture on the doorstep, and many stations and doorbells let us mask parts of the picture so the camera ignores the path behind the visitor. That also cuts the number of motion alerts a household gets from people who never stop.

Answering on two floors. A duplex usually has its living space on one floor and bedrooms on the other. A second station upstairs, or phone answering for each member of the household, means the door can be answered from wherever people are. If the household wants to release the door from upstairs, the door gets a release that fits its frame, which we check on the walkthrough.

Historic District Edge

Which Landmarks Rules Apply to a Door Station Near Cadman Plaza

Cadman Plaza sits on the northeast edge of the Brooklyn Heights Historic District, the city's first, designated in November 1965. The district is bounded on the north by Cadman Plaza West, and its eastern line partly follows Clinton and Henry Streets. The residential complex built west of the park in the late 1960s and early 1970s was left out of the district. In practice that means two buildings facing each other across Henry Street can follow completely different rules for a new door station.

For a building inside the district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook sets the staff-level rules for intercoms and doorbells. Intercom panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening," and an intercom may also go on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway. "A maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening." The design, size and finish must be compatible with the building, equipment must not be attached through cast iron or to decorative surfaces, and conduit must be hidden behind the installation, with about ten inches of visible conduit allowed when that is impossible, painted to match. Applications are filed through the LPC's Portico system with photos of the building, close-ups of the mounting location, manufacturer cut sheets and paint cards for visible surfaces.

The same chapter limits security cameras, which matters when a building adds a camera with its new panel. Small residential buildings may have no more than two cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage, and large residential buildings no more than three, well spaced to avoid a cluttered look. A door station with a built-in camera on a district building should be planned with those numbers in mind.

For the towers, townhouses and shops of the complex, which sit outside the district, the rules that matter are the co-op's own. Most boards require approval for anything mounted on the outside of the building, and many have standards for color and size. We prepare the same kind of packet for a board that we would for the LPC: the cut sheet, a marked photo, dimensions and finish. On a district building, we do not mount anything on the facade until the owner tells us the permit has been issued.

Compliance points that apply to Cadman Plaza entry installations

  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a and the age of the tower. Class A multiple dwellings with eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968 must have a self-locking main entrance door with an intercommunication system allowing voice contact and remote release. CityRealty dates 75 Henry Street to 1973, and Cadman Towers was completed in 1971, so both fall under that rule. 140 Cadman Plaza West is listed as built in 1967; for older buildings, the law requires the door and intercom when tenants in a majority of apartments request or consent in writing, with work started within 90 days and finished within six months.
  • Keeping it working. Once a building has an intercom, the city's Housing Maintenance Code expects it to be kept in good repair. Habitat Magazine's 2024 coverage of a co-op with a broken intercom quoted attorney Nancy Kourland of Lasser Law Group: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom."
  • Self-closing doors. HPD's guidance says apartment and hallway doors "are required by law to swing closed and latch by themselves." A defect that stops a door closing or latching is a class C immediately hazardous violation with 14 days to correct, and Local Law 71 of 2022 has HPD choose 300 buildings a year for proactive inspections. Every door we wire is checked for closing and latching first.
  • Tenant Data Privacy Law. Local Law 63 of 2021 covers multiple dwellings that use smart access systems such as key fobs, apps or biometric readers. Owners need express consent in writing or through an app, must give residents a data retention and privacy policy, must destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, and may not require a smart access system to enter a resident's own apartment.
  • Electrically locked doors. Where a magnetic lock is used instead of an electric strike, the International Building Code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, unlocking on loss of power and on fire alarm activation, and a manual push-to-exit device near the door. We use fail-secure strikes on most residential and office doors and fit magnetic locks only with the full set of releases.
  • Accessible reach. The 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach at 48 inches maximum, which we use as the upper limit for buttons on lobby panels, office suites and shop doors.
  • Landmarks next door. Buildings inside the Brooklyn Heights Historic District follow the LPC's staff rules for intercom panels and doorbells described above. The towers and townhouses of the complex west of the park are outside the district and follow their co-op's rules.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Cadman Plaza: Questions Answered

These are the questions boards, shareholders, townhouse owners and office managers ask about new entry systems around Cadman Plaza, together with ones raised in Habitat Magazine, Brick Underground, Security Sales and the manufacturer guides we checked, answered for the buildings here.

Doorman towers

Our tower has a doorman around the clock. Can the staff at the desk take the place of the intercom the law calls for?

Not on paper. The Multiple Dwelling Law's intercom rule for post-1968 buildings of eight or more apartments describes equipment: a self-locking main door and an intercommunication system that lets a resident talk to a visitor and release the door. A doorman is a very good service, but staffing levels change with budgets, and the rule does not say a staffed desk satisfies it. In practice, the desk-to-apartment system most towers here already have, together with an entrance panel that works when the desk is empty, covers both the law and the way the building runs.

For a tower built before 1968, such as one dated 1967, the intercom becomes required when tenants in a majority of apartments ask for it in writing. Most co-op boards install and keep a working system long before that point, because residents expect it and because a broken one becomes a repair obligation once it is installed.

When the doorman is in the package room, how does a visitor at the plaza door reach anyone?

Through the entrance panel at that door, set up to match the building's routine. The simplest setting has the panel call the apartment directly whenever the desk does not pick up. Another sends the call to a portable handset or phone the doorman carries, so staff can answer from the package room, the mail room or a floor. Some buildings use both: the panel tries the desk, then the doorman's handset, then the apartment.

Which order works best depends on how the building is staffed at different hours. We go through the day and night shifts with the super or the managing agent, set the panel accordingly, and test it with the desk empty before we leave.

Can the desk console show the garage and service door cameras and open them, without residents being able to?

Yes. Release rights are set per station. The desk console can be given the camera view and the release for the garage pedestrian door, the service entrance and the main lobby, while apartment stations release only the door their visitor is calling from. Residents arriving at the garage door use their own code or card, which opens that door and nothing else unless the board decides otherwise.

We write the release rights for every station into the hand-over record, so the managing agent can see at a glance who can open each door and change it later if the building's policy changes.

Our tower has more than thirty floors. Does the height limit which entry system can run up the riser?

Height matters less than the condition and type of the cable. Multi-tenant systems are built for large buildings: Aiphone states that its GT Series supports up to 5,000 tenant stations on a two-wire bus. What limits a tall tower is usually the length of a run on thin or spliced cable, which can cause weak audio or video on the top floors, and the number of stations each power supply can drive.

We test the riser at the top, middle and bottom before choosing equipment, and where a long run needs it, we add a distribution unit or power supply partway up the building. If the old cable cannot carry video reliably to the highest floors, we say so and price either new riser cable or an audio system for those floors.

Townhouses at the tower base

Our duplex townhouse opens onto the walkway, not the tower lobby. Should its door station join the building's system or stand on its own?

It depends on what you want the desk to do for you. A stand-alone video doorbell or door station is cheaper, can be done for one house at a time, and keeps the townhouse independent of the tower's equipment. A station that joins the building's system lets the desk see your door, lets you call the desk, and puts your door on the same plan and maintenance as every other door in the co-op.

If several townhouse owners want the connected option, it is usually cheaper to wire them together while the tower's system is being replaced. Either way the board normally approves anything on the outside of a townhouse, so we prepare a packet for the board with the station's size, finish and mounting spot.

Couriers keep walking past our townhouse because it faces the plaza rather than a street. What helps them find and ring the right door?

A station that is easy to see and clearly labeled. A lit button visible from the path after dark, the house number or name printed on the station itself, and a station mounted where someone walking along the plaza notices it all cut down on missed deliveries. If the co-op has a standard style for townhouse numbers, we match it.

A video station or doorbell also lets you talk to a courier who is at the wrong door and guide them to yours, or tell them where to leave a parcel. If parcels are a regular problem, the building's package room and the desk are usually the better answer, and the station can tell couriers to go there.

Concrete walls and signal

Our apartment walls are solid concrete. How does a new station get its cable without channels cut into the wall?

By using the cable and the opening the old station already has. In the Cadman Plaza towers, the apartment station is usually fed from the corridor near the entry door, and replacing it in the same position uses the same cable and the same box. That is why we recommend keeping new stations where the old ones were unless there is a strong reason to move them.

If you want the station somewhere else, the choices are a small painted surface raceway from the old position, which is tidy but visible, or a second station that pairs with the first where the system allows. We do not patch or paint walls ourselves; if a route would need an opening in finished plaster, we show you where before the price is set so you can decide.

Our lobby is poured concrete and phones barely get a signal there. Can an app-based panel still reach residents?

It can, as long as the panel does not depend on a cellular signal at that spot. Some app-based panels connect over a built-in cellular radio, and those need decent reception where they are mounted. Others connect over the building's wired internet, which concrete does not affect. On the walkthrough we measure the signal at the panel location and choose accordingly.

Residents' own phones still need a signal or Wi-Fi to receive the call wherever they are. In a concrete tower that usually means apartment Wi-Fi. For residents who would rather not depend on that, the system can keep a wired station in the apartment as well.

Boards, condos and resales

Who in our building should be able to update the directory and codes, and how is that set up?

Usually the managing agent and one or two people at the building, such as the super or the resident manager, with the board deciding who. Most current systems have an administrator role that can add and remove households, change directory entries and issue or cancel codes and cards, and a lower role for desk staff that can only see and release doors.

Our board wants the managing agent's office to update the system remotely, but some shareholders do not want the building online. Is there a middle ground?

Yes. A wired system can run entirely on the building's own cable for calling and releasing, with only the management software connected to the internet, so an outage never stops a resident from answering the door. Habitat Magazine's 2015 report on virtual doormen noted that phone and app services stop working when a building's internet goes down; keeping the core system wired avoids that.

The opposite arrangement, a fully closed system with no remote access, is also common in co-ops. The trade-off is that changes have to be made at the building. We price both so the board can weigh convenience against independence.

Our condo's entry system came from the developer. Before the service contract renews, how do we find out what we actually own?

Start with an inventory. We identify the make and model of every panel, station and controller, check whether the system needs a paid software subscription to keep working, find out who holds the administrator login, and confirm whether the equipment can be serviced by any qualified installer or only through the original vendor. That tells the board what it is paying for and what it would lose if the contract lapsed.

Swiftlane's cost guide says software subscriptions on some cloud systems add $5 to $15 per unit per month. In a building of about 130 units, that is a real line in the budget, so knowing whether the building's system carries one is worth an hour of a technician's time.

Many do. Keypad panels commonly have a raised dot on the five key, and several makers offer raised or Braille labels for call buttons and directory panels. Touchscreen panels are harder for a blind visitor to use, so where that matters we suggest a panel with physical buttons, or one with both a screen and a keypad.

Can a visitor who uses a hearing aid hear the panel clearly?
+

Some door stations can be fitted with an induction loop, which sends the speaker's sound straight to a hearing aid set to its telecoil mode. On panels without that option, a louder speaker setting and a video picture, so the resident can see and gesture, help. If residents or frequent visitors use hearing aids, tell us and we include a panel that offers a loop.

When the building changes managing agents, who should hold the system's admin logins?
+

The board should, with the managing agent given access as its agent rather than as the owner of the account. Can the desk tell which entrance a visitor is calling from?
+

Yes. Each entrance panel is named in the system, so the desk console shows a call from the plaza door, the garage door or the service entrance by name, with that door's camera picture. Staff then release only the door the visitor is standing at, which matters in a tower with several ways in.

How long does it take desk staff to learn a new console?
+

The basic tasks, calling an apartment, seeing a door and releasing it, are usually learned in one shift. At hand-over we demonstrate the console to the staff on duty and leave a short written guide at the desk for relief doormen and overnight staff. Can the entrance panel show instructions when the desk is unattended?
+

Many panels can display a short message or change their screen at set hours, for example telling visitors to dial an apartment code or call the desk handset after the desk closes. Panels with a screen also let the building post the directory instructions in plain words. We set the wording with the managing agent when the panel is programmed.

Can the garage door use a different code from the lobby door?
+

Yes. On a keypad or card system, each door can have its own codes or card permissions, so a resident who parks can open the garage pedestrian door while someone who does not park cannot. Codes for staff and contractors can be limited to particular doors and hours, and the administrator can change any of them without a service call.

Can the system warn the desk when a door is held open too long?
+

Yes, if the door has a contact that reports whether it is closed. The system can sound a local alert or flag the door at the desk console when it stays open past a set time, which helps staff catch a propped service door or a garage door that did not close.
