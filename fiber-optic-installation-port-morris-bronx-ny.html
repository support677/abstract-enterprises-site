The Neighborhood

Why Port Morris Properties Need Fiber Optic Installation

Port Morris was planned as a seaport and grew into a factory district. The Department of City Planning's own history of the neighbourhood describes how Gouverneur Morris II hoped to build a harbour to rival Manhattan's, how piano and furniture makers filled the blocks instead, and how the Third Avenue Bridge (1898), the Willis Avenue Bridge three years later and the Harlem River train yards (1906) tied the area to the rest of the city. In the 1950s a large share of the housing was cleared for the Triborough Bridge and the Bruckner and Major Deegan expressways, and manufacturing drifted away for decades. What was left was a stock of heavy brick and concrete buildings — the City counts them at one to six storeys, mostly along Bruckner Boulevard — that were built for machines and freight, not for networks.

Since the late 1990s those buildings have been changing use at speed. In 1997 the City Planning Commission created the city's first special mixed-use district here, along five blocks of Bruckner Boulevard, so that housing could sit beside light industry. In 2005 the City Council extended it across eleven more blocks between Park and Willow Avenues, and in 2017 the Special Harlem River Waterfront District opened the western end to high-density towers. On the ground that means piano-factory lofts, a bakery warehouse turned creative office, a Locust Avenue warehouse turned sound stages and a new two-level logistics depot. Residents notice the change too: on r/bronx, one thread asks whether Port Morris and Mott Haven are really gentrifying or whether locals are simply renovating "abandoned industrial or commercial buildings," and a thread on r/nyc describes the area's new towers as offering luxury apartments at a lower price than Manhattan or Long Island City.

Every one of those conversions creates the same fiber problem. The building's pathways were laid out for its first life: a freight elevator that no longer runs, a telephone closet on a floor that is now somebody's apartment, a printing plant's cable trays that end in a room nobody leases. The carriers' glass reaches the block — the Center for an Urban Future reported in July 2025 that nearly 99.8 percent of the Bronx is wired for high-speed service — but it lands in a cellar or a sidewalk vault, and the owner has to carry it to the people who need it. Meanwhile the City Council's data team ranks Melrose–Mott Haven–Port Morris, Community District 1, as the district with the lowest broadband adoption in the city, with 44 percent of households lacking a broadband subscription. Coverage at the curb and connection at the desk are two different things, and the gap between them lives inside the building.

That is the work on this page: fiber that follows a building through its change of use. Sometimes it is a new riser in a loft building whose old one was a dumbwaiter. Sometimes it is a link from a dispatch office to a warehouse bay two floors away, or a backbone between a studio's stages and its machine room. Often it begins with an audit of glass a previous occupant left behind, because the cheapest strand in Port Morris is the one already in the wall that still tests clean. Most owners add copper drops, cameras or door access in the same visit, which is why our Port Morris structured cabling team usually walks the building with us.

Port Morris facts that shape a fiber plan

  • 129 lots, 38 of them industrial: when the City studied the 2005 rezoning area, light manufacturing, warehousing and open storage occupied 29 percent of the lots, residential buildings 34 percent, and one-third of all lots were vacant or underused — the raw material for today's conversions.
  • 1885–86: the Estey Piano Company Factory at 112–128 Lincoln Avenue was built; with its corner clock tower it is the oldest known piano factory still standing in the Bronx, and City Planning recorded 50 lofts already converted inside it by 2004.
  • 96 acres: the size of the Harlem River Yards waterfront property, leased by New York State in 1991 for 99 years and since developed with a FedEx distribution centre, a FreshDirect facility, waste transfer stations and other industrial users.
  • 36-foot clear heights and 4,000 amps: the warehouse bay and electrical service at 307 Bruckner Boulevard, a roughly 100,000-square-foot two-level last-mile facility that New York YIMBY reported in August 2026 was scheduled to open in the third quarter of the year.
  • Four sound stages with 52-foot ceilings: Silvercup Studios' North Lot at 295 Locust Avenue totals 148,140 square feet, with stages from 8,030 to 16,220 square feet and 33,900 square feet of production offices.
  • 44 percent: the share of households in Community District 1 — Melrose, Mott Haven and Port Morris — without a broadband subscription in the City Council's internet-access data, the highest of any district.

Studio and Stage Backbones

Building Specialists

Fiber Optic Installation for Port Morris's Architecture

Within a few blocks of the Cypress Avenue station you can find six very different kinds of building. Each one hides its pathways in a different place, so each starts from a different question on our walkthrough.

Nineteenth-Century Factory Lofts

Brick bearing walls, heavy timber or iron framing and tall windows, like the Estey building on Lincoln Avenue. Floors were open workrooms; today they are divided into lofts or studios, and the original stair and freight shafts are spoken for. Our first question: where can a riser go that no one lives in?

Early-Twentieth-Century Concrete Warehouses

Reinforced-concrete frames with deep slabs and big column bays, such as the 1916 A&P bakery building at 825 East 141st Street that became Union Crossing. Coring is slow but predictable, and the old freight routes are wide. Our first question: which floor did the building's network used to serve?

Rowhouses and Small Walk-Ups

Two- and three-storey rowhouses on East 133rd and East 134th Streets near Willow Avenue, and three- to five-storey multifamily buildings along Bruckner Boulevard, many with a shop on the ground floor. Our first question: who controls the cellar?

New Waterfront and Corridor Towers

Concrete residential towers along the Harlem River and Third Avenue — Bankside's seven towers, the 27-storey Maven at 2413 Third Avenue — and mid-rise buildings on Bruckner Boulevard. Our first question: is the telecom closet stack already sleeved, and who is the building's network managed by?

Last-Mile and Fleet Buildings

Steel-framed logistics buildings with high-bay floors, mezzanines and covered fleet parking, of the kind now finishing at 307 Bruckner Boulevard. Our first question: where do the dispatch desk, the docks and the vehicle chargers sit relative to the carrier's entrance?

Rail-Yard Industrial Park Buildings

Distribution, food and processing buildings on the state-owned Harlem River Yards land, reached through a master lessee. Our first question: whose approval does a new entrance or a new trench need, and in what order?

Factory lofts: following the old shafts without using them

Nineteenth-century factory buildings almost always have a stack of small spaces that line up floor to floor — a former toilet stack, a closet beside the stair, a disused chimney or an abandoned belt or pipe chase. Those are our first candidates for a riser, because the alternative is coring through old timber or masonry floors that may be carrying finished lofts. Where nothing lines up, a short run of conduit up a rear or light-well wall, entering each floor through a sleeve, is often less disruptive than cutting interior floors. Open, exposed ceilings are common in these lofts, so horizontal runs are dressed in neat raceway or painted cable tray rather than hidden, and fastened into the brick joints or the framing, never into decorative ironwork.

Concrete warehouses: thick slabs, wide bays, long runs

Warehouse buildings from the 1910s and 1920s were designed for heavy loads, so slabs are thick and sometimes ribbed. The bigger issue is distance: a floor that was one bakery or one printing operation may now be thirty suites, and the far suite may be well beyond the 100-metre reach of a copper channel. In these buildings we usually set one fiber enclosure per floor near the centre of the plate and serve suites from there, so no tenant's connection runs through another tenant's space.

Rowhouses: small runs, careful entries

For a rowhouse or walk-up, a fiber job is usually one riser of a few strands from the cellar to the top floor, plus a short link to a ground-floor shop. The work is in the details: entering through an existing service sleeve rather than a new hole in a party wall, keeping the cellar enclosure above the floor and away from the boiler, and using small surface raceway painted to match on stair halls where there is no chase.

New towers: the closet stack is the opportunity

A new tower's advantage is its telecom closet stack. When the contractor has set sleeves through every slab, mounted backboards and provided a dedicated outlet in each closet, a fiber riser can be pulled, spliced and tested during fit-out in a fraction of the time an older building takes. The risks are the opposite of a factory loft's: sleeves that were filled by other trades before we arrive, closets used as storage during construction, and coring requests in slabs that need scanning first (see the questions below). We ask to see the closets before the drywall closes, so missing sleeves can be added while it is still cheap.

Logistics buildings: height, steel and vehicles

In a high-bay logistics building, everything happens overhead. Cable rides in basket tray fixed to the roof steel, comes down to devices only in steel conduit along columns that forklifts do not strike, and crosses dock doors high enough to clear the tallest trailer.

Commercial Fiber Optic Installation

Business Fiber Optic Installation in Port Morris

Port Morris has no single main street. Its businesses cluster along a handful of corridors, each with its own building type and its own reason to want fiber.

Bruckner Boulevard and Alexander Avenue

The neighbourhood's mixed-use spine, running beneath the Bruckner Expressway. City Planning's history records the antiques dealers who made it a South Bronx "Antique Row"; today restaurants, bars and new mid-rise apartments share it with older shops. Fiber here is usually a short link from a shared cellar to a ground-floor business, or a riser for a renovated walk-up.

East 132nd to East 135th Streets

The side streets south of Bruckner Boulevard, including the Estey building at Lincoln Avenue, newer rentals such as The Arches at 224–228 East 135th Street, and the industrial lots near the Bronx Grit Chamber. Expect a mix of lofts, live-work units and small manufacturers, often in the same block.

East 138th to East 141st Streets

Larger converted buildings, including Union Crossing at 825 East 141st Street and Per Scholas's technology-training campus at 804 East 138th Street. These floors need backbones that can serve many tenants or classrooms from one telecom room.

Locust, Walnut and Willow Avenues

The eastern, more industrial half of Port Morris, guided by the Port Morris Industrial Business Zone and running toward the Hell Gate Bridge approach: warehouses, contractors' yards, food businesses and Silvercup's North Lot stages at 295 Locust Avenue. Fiber links offices to floors, yards and stages.

Harlem River Yards

The waterfront industrial park south of East 132nd Street, home to the FedEx and FreshDirect facilities, waste transfer stations and other distribution users. Long, flat buildings with yard links and strict access control.

Third Avenue and the Harlem River Waterfront

Where Port Morris meets Mott Haven at the Third Avenue Bridge: the Bankside towers, Maven and other new rental buildings, with ground-floor retail, amenity floors and parking that all need a planned backbone.

Code and safety points that come up on Port Morris jobs

  • Exit stairs are not cable routes. Section 1023.5 of the 2022 New York City Building Code prohibits penetrations into or through interior exit stairways except for a short list of items that serve the stair itself, so a loft building's enclosed stair cannot double as its riser.
  • Nothing hangs from sprinkler pipe. NFPA 13 states that sprinkler piping and hangers must not be used to support non-system components. In warehouses with dense sprinkler mains, cable gets its own supports from the structure.
  • Listed cable for the space. Under the electrical code's Article 770, riser-rated (OFNR) cable is the minimum between floors and plenum-rated (OFNP) cable is required in spaces used for return air; we specify the listing in every proposal.
  • Every penetration sealed. Holes through rated floors and walls are closed with a listed firestop system and photographed for the owner's file, which matters in buildings where a conversion is still under inspection.
  • Low-voltage permit status. As our structured cabling pricing pages state, low-voltage cabling under 50 volts does not need its own DOB permit — but it still has to follow the building and electrical codes, and work inside an active alteration is coordinated with the general contractor.
  • Elevators stay elevator-only. Hoistways and machine spaces carry only the elevator's own wiring under the electrical code, including the freight elevators of converted warehouses.

Many owners combine the fiber work with door access and cameras in the same visit; our Port Morris access control and Port Morris security camera crews can share the pathways the fiber opens.

Stages and Depots

Fiber for Port Morris Studios, Depots and Distribution Floors

Two newer kinds of Port Morris building put unusual demands on a network. Production studios are one. Silvercup's North Lot on Locust Avenue lists four sound stages of 8,030 to 16,220 square feet with 52-foot ceilings, production offices with suites and open bullpens, and on-lot parking for more than 60 vehicles. A building like that carries footage, playback, lighting control and the office network for whichever production is in residence, and it changes hands between productions every few months. Fiber suits the links between stages and machine room: it is immune to interference from dimmers and generators, and a labelled patch field lets each new production plug in on day one.

Last-mile logistics buildings are the other. The facility New York YIMBY described at 307 Bruckner Boulevard stacks a 46,000-square-foot warehouse with 36-foot clear heights above 46,000 square feet of covered fleet parking, with an 8,200-square-foot mezzanine, four loading docks and 4,000 amps of power. In a building arranged like that, the dispatch desk, the handheld scanners on the warehouse floor, the dock cameras, the gate and the vehicle chargers can each be hundreds of feet and a full storey from the room where the carrier's circuit lands. Copper cannot reach most of those points in one run; fiber between a few well-placed enclosures can, and the switches at each enclosure feed the devices nearby.

Both run around the clock, so we put coring, lift work over aisles and the final cutover into windows the operator picks, and splice and test while the building keeps working.

  • Stage-to-machine-room tie lines: single-mode strands in armored cable along the stage perimeter, terminated in wall enclosures beside the stage doors.
  • Production-office distribution: one enclosure per office floor, so each production's suites are served from the building's backbone rather than temporary cable.
  • Dock and yard links: outdoor-rated fiber to gatehouse, dock doors and pole cameras, with sealed entries where the cable crosses from yard to building.
Your Questions Answered

Fiber Optic Installation in Port Morris: Questions Answered

Sixteen questions from Port Morris owners, tenants, studio managers and depot operators, sorted by building type. The answers assume the building has changed use at least once, because almost every building here has.

Converted factories, lofts and creative offices

The provider's cable reaches our building through an old sidewalk vault that takes on water — can the entrance be moved?

Often, yes, and in a waterfront neighbourhood it is worth asking. Older New York commercial buildings often have vaults under the sidewalk, built for coal, boilers or freight, and carriers sometimes brought cable in through them because the opening already existed. If the vault leaks, the splice case or terminal down there sits in damp air or standing water. The fix is a new entrance: a sleeve through the foundation wall above the usual water line, or through a front or side wall into a dry room, with the carrier re-pulling its cable to the new point. We design the sleeve and the room, and the carrier moves its own facilities, so the change has to be coordinated with each provider in the building.

Before we sign a lease on an older industrial building, how can we find out which carriers can already serve it?

Ask three questions, in this order. First, ask the landlord which providers have equipment in the building today and where it sits; a provider with glass already in the cellar can usually serve a new tenant quickly. Second, put the exact address into each carrier's business-availability check, because coverage in this part of the Bronx changes from one block to the next. Third, have someone walk the building to see whether there is a clear path from the carrier's equipment to the space you would lease. We do that walk: we trace the route, note what has to be built, and give you a written estimate you can use in lease negotiations, including whether the landlord should pay for a riser that will serve future tenants as well.

Our lease may end when the owner redevelops the site — which parts of a fiber installation can a tenant take along when it moves?

The electronics travel; the glass usually stays. Switches, media converters, optics and patch cords belong to the tenant and come out in an afternoon. Cable that has been pulled through walls, sleeves and risers, and the enclosures fixed to the structure, generally become part of the building, and most leases say so. With redevelopment in mind, we design the job to keep the tenant's investment in portable equipment and keep the fixed part small: a short link from the building's existing riser rather than a new riser of your own, pre-terminated cables where they suit the route, and enclosures you can unbolt.

Can the fiber equipment share the main electrical room in a building with very large power service?

It is rarely the best place. Buildings converted from industrial use often have big switchgear rooms, and the electrical code requires clear working space in front of that equipment and limits what can be stored or mounted there. Fiber itself is not affected by the electrical fields around switchgear, but the switches and optics at each end are, in the sense that they need clean, dedicated outlets, moderate temperatures and a locked space the building's electricians do not need to open. We usually recommend a separate small room or a lockable cabinet on an adjacent wall, with the fiber passing the electrical room in its own pathway, and we confirm with the building's electrician before fixing anything nearby.

Studios, stages and event spaces

Our stage has to stay silent during takes — will fiber enclosures and switches add noise the microphones pick up?

The cable makes no sound at all; the active equipment can. Passive fiber enclosures and patch panels have no fans or moving parts, so they can sit on a stage wall without any acoustic effect. Switches and media converters with cooling fans are the issue, and they belong in a machine room or a closet outside the stage, connected to the stage wall panels by fiber. Where a device must sit on the stage itself, we use fanless models or mount them in an insulated, ventilated box away from the set. Because fiber carries no electrical current, it also cannot introduce hum or ground loops into audio equipment, which is one reason productions prefer it between stages and the control area.

We rent our loft for events a few nights a month — can visiting vendors get a network connection without touching ours?

Yes. The simplest arrangement is a dedicated vendor port: a labelled patch point, fed by its own strands or its own network segment from the building's enclosure, placed where caterers, sound engineers and ticketing staff usually set up. Vendors plug into that port and nowhere else. On the switch, that port sits on a separate network from your own equipment, so a visiting laptop or mixing desk cannot see your cameras or office systems.

Warehouses, depots, yards and shops

Our delivery fleet parks on the ground floor and the warehouse is upstairs — how does fiber get from dispatch to the upper bay?

Through a protected vertical route planned with the building's layout, not around it. In a two-level depot the ramp, the vehicle lanes and the racking all compete for the same columns and walls, so the riser goes where none of them will reach: typically a column line or wall at the building's edge, inside steel conduit up to a height above any vehicle, then into tray on the upper level. A pair of enclosures, one beside the dispatch office and one on the warehouse floor, holds the splices and the switches.

The electric-van chargers in our fleet garage need a network connection — is fiber worth running to them?

For a row of chargers far from the office, often yes. Networked chargers report usage, take software updates and are controlled by the fleet's management system, and they work far better on a wired connection than on cellular modems in a concrete garage. Because chargers are usually spread along a parking level, a single fiber link to an enclosure at the middle of the row, with short copper drops from a small switch to each charger, covers them all and stays well clear of the chargers' heavy power cables. The electrical work for the chargers themselves is a licensed electrician's job; we provide and test the data link and coordinate the routes so the two never share a raceway.

Can fiber run through a metal fabrication or welding shop where sparks and hot slag fly?

Yes, if it is kept out of the hot zone. Fiber jackets melt like any plastic, so cable in a fabrication shop runs high on the walls or overhead, away from welding bays and cutting tables, and drops to devices only inside steel conduit. Interlocking-armored cable adds protection along routes near grinders and plasma cutters. Enclosures are metal, mounted on walls that spark showers do not reach, and closed with sealed entries to keep out metal dust, which can foul connectors.

Does fiber get stolen for scrap the way copper cable does?

Very rarely, because it has little scrap value. Copper theft from vacant and industrial buildings is a well-known problem, and thieves strip it for the metal. Optical fiber is glass with a plastic jacket and, in armored versions, a thin steel or aluminium wrap, none of which is worth much to a scrap buyer. In yards and vacant sections of a building we run it inside conduit, label it clearly as fiber optic cable, and keep enclosures locked, which together discourage anyone looking for metal.

We sublease from a company that itself leases the land from the State — how many sign-offs does a new fiber entrance need?

Usually two or three, and they come in a set order. On land like the Harlem River Yards, the State leased the property to a master developer, which in turn subleases parcels and buildings to businesses. A new hole in your building's wall may need only your landlord's consent, but a trench across the yard, a link to a neighbouring parcel or a new conduit from the street can need the master lessee's approval and sometimes the State's as owner. We prepare one scope with drawings, cable types and a timetable that your landlord can forward up the chain, which keeps everyone reviewing the same document and avoids starting work that has not been approved.

Will exterior conduit on our street wall get tagged or broken, and how is it protected?

Anything on a street-facing wall in a busy neighbourhood can attract paint or damage, so we keep exterior runs as short and as high as practical. Where fiber has to run outside — up a rear wall, along a yard fence or to a pole camera — it goes in rigid steel or heavy-wall conduit with tamper-resistant fasteners, and any pull box is a lockable metal enclosure rather than a plastic one. Conduit can be painted to match the brick, which also makes it less of a target.

Is there any help for industrial businesses in the Port Morris Industrial Business Zone that are planning a network upgrade?

There is a place to ask. The South Bronx Overall Economic Development Corporation, SoBro, describes itself as the Industrial Business Solutions Provider for all five of the Bronx's Industrial Business Zones, Port Morris among them, and offers business services to companies inside those zones. What it can offer changes over time, so contact SoBro directly about current programmes rather than relying on any figure you read online. If a programme needs a written scope or quote, we can provide one that lists each item separately, so the business can submit it without reworking our proposal.

New towers and brownfield sites

Our new building sits on a cleaned-up brownfield with a vapour barrier under the slab — can fiber conduit pass through it?

Only with the environmental engineer's approval and a sealed detail. Port Morris has a long industrial history, and some new buildings sit on sites cleaned up under city or state programmes, which can leave engineering controls such as a vapour barrier or a sub-slab ventilation system under the ground floor. Every pipe and conduit that passes through that layer has to be sealed in the way the engineer specifies, or the barrier stops working. Wherever possible we bring fiber in above the slab, through a foundation or exterior wall, so the barrier is never touched. If a below-slab conduit is unavoidable, it is installed before the slab is poured, with the engineer's boot or seal detail, rather than cored in later.

Our new tower has post-tensioned concrete floors — how do you core through them without hitting a tendon?

By not coring until the slab has been scanned and the location approved. Post-tensioned slabs carry steel tendons under high tension, and cutting one is dangerous and expensive. Before any new penetration in a newer building we ask for the structural drawings, scan the area with ground-penetrating radar to map tendons and reinforcing, mark a clear spot, and get the building's engineer or management to approve it in writing. In practice we prefer to avoid new cores altogether by using the sleeves the contractor set in the telecom closets during construction; when those are in place, a riser goes in quickly and the slab is never touched.

Our rental building has a resident co-working lounge — should it run on the building's own fiber or on a resident's internet account?

On the building's own connection, kept separate from residents' service. A shared lounge used by many residents should not depend on one resident's account or router, and the building's managed network for cameras, access control and elevators should not be shared with lounge users. The usual design is a building-owned circuit for amenity spaces, fed over the building's fiber to a small switch serving the lounge's access points, on a network segment of its own.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fiber Optic Installation in Port Morris

Search for fiber installation in the Bronx and an AI summary will usually stitch together a homeowner cost guide, a per-foot materials price and a provider count. Each figure below is quoted as its source publishes it. None is wrong in its own context; each is misleading if you apply it to a converted Port Morris building.

Claim 1: "Fiber optic cable installation costs average $4,500"

Angi, the home-services marketplace that also runs HomeAdvisor, says in its fiber guide that "fiber optic cable installation costs average $4,500 for most homeowners, with most installations ranging from $1,500 to $7,000." That figure is about bringing fiber into a house. It says nothing about a seven-storey warehouse with thirty tenants or a stage building with a machine room.

In Port Morris the range is wider in both directions. A single single-mode link from a building's main room to one loft, with OTDR traces on its two strands, comes to $527 using our published prices. A new twelve-strand riser through a converted factory sits in our published $4,500–$7,000 band, and a link between two buildings on a yard is $8,000–$15,000 or more.

Our research turned up no comparable Fixr page, so Angi's figure is the one AI answers lean on. Use it for a house; for a Port Morris building, use a walked route and a line-by-line proposal.

Claim 2: "Commercial fiber optic cabling services can scale into six figures"

Ring and Ping's 2026 cost guide says that "residential projects typically range from $1,500 to $6,000, while commercial fiber optic cabling services can scale into six figures for complex builds." That is true of campuses and data centres, and AI summaries tend to repeat it as if every business job were that size.

Most Port Morris commercial jobs are nowhere near it. A shop on Bruckner Boulevard may need one link from the cellar; a depot may need an office-to-floor link and a few drops; a creative-office floor may need one enclosure and a handful of strands per suite. Those are four- and low-five-figure jobs in our published pricing.

Large totals appear only when one building needs a new entrance, a riser, full floor distribution and cable removal at once — and each of those is a separate line that can be phased.

Claim 3: "Single-mode fiber is cheap at roughly $0.09 to $1.49 per foot"

Unio's installation-cost article puts single-mode cable at about $0.09 to $1.49 per foot and multimode OM3/OM4 at $1.50 to $6.00, and AI answers often multiply a building's length by those numbers to get a project cost. That is the price of the cable on the reel.

In a 1910s concrete warehouse the cable is the smallest part of the bill. The money goes into scanning and coring thick slabs, building a riser where none exists, firestopping every penetration, supporting cable on the structure rather than on sprinkler pipe, splicing each end and testing every strand.

So a per-foot figure is useful for one thing: showing that extra strands in the same pull are cheap. Specifying twenty-four strands instead of twelve barely changes the cable cost, but it saves a second pull when the next tenant arrives.

Claim 4: "There are an estimated 31 dark fiber providers in The Bronx"

Lightyear's February 2026 guide to dark fiber in the Bronx estimates 31 providers and rates the borough's connectivity as "High," while adding that availability "depends on fiber infrastructure near a specific address." AI summaries tend to drop that caveat.

The caveat is the whole story in Port Morris. A carrier with fiber on Bruckner Boulevard still needs a way into your building, a place to land its equipment and a riser to reach your floor. In a converted factory the entrance may be in a leaking vault, the old telephone room may now belong to a tenant, and there may be no riser at all.

Provider count matters when you negotiate a circuit. The building's own entrance and backbone decide whether any of those providers can actually deliver it to you, and that part is the owner's to build.

Claim 5: "Nearly 99.8 percent of the Bronx is now wired for high-speed internet"

The Center for an Urban Future's July 2025 report on the Bronx digital divide makes this point and immediately qualifies it: problems remain, "including individual buildings not yet wired for broadband and limited competition in some areas." AI overviews often quote the first half alone.

The City Council's data team adds the other side. Its internet-access data shows 44 percent of households in Melrose–Mott Haven–Port Morris without a broadband subscription, the highest share of any community district. Wired streets and connected households are very different measures.

For an owner, the bottleneck is not the street but the stretch between the provider's equipment and the people who need service.

Claim 6: "Fiber installation takes four to six hours"

AT&T's support pages describe a home fiber installation as taking four to six hours, and T-Mobile says its in-home install "can take up to 4 hours." Those figures appear in AI answers to almost any question about how long fiber takes.

They describe a provider's technician connecting one home to fiber that already reaches it. A riser in a Port Morris loft building is a different job: walking the building, getting management approval, scanning and coring, pulling and supporting cable floor by floor, splicing each end and testing every strand. Inside one floor that may be a day or two; a whole building takes longer, and approvals often take longer still.

Start the paperwork early: we can walk the building and send the scope to management while you are still choosing a provider.

Claim 7: "5G home internet needs no landlord or co-op board approval and no wiring"

A Bronx internet-provider guide promotes fixed wireless with exactly this line: "no landlord or co-op board approval required, no wiring, plug-and-play setup in 15 minutes." For one apartment with good signal, that can be a sensible choice.

It does not solve a building's problem. Signal fades through the brick bearing walls of factory lofts and the steel and concrete of warehouses, and a wireless router in the front office does nothing for a scanner on a warehouse floor, a camera on a yard pole or a stage's machine room. Those still need cable from somewhere.

Wireless works well as a backup circuit, with fiber carrying its signal from a roof or window to the main room. It cannot replace the backbone inside.

Siting the Equipment

Water, Highways and Air: Where Fiber Equipment Belongs in Port Morris

Port Morris is a low-lying peninsula with water on three sides and highways on the fourth, and both shape where fiber equipment should sit. South Bronx Unite, the community group behind the Mott Haven–Port Morris Waterfront Plan, writes that after Hurricane Sandy the neighbourhood's waterfront was "upwards of 3.5 feet underwater." The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' draft harbour-protection study, as reported by the Mott Haven Herald in March 2023, proposes flood walls and deployable gates along the Bronx side of the Harlem River, describing the Port Morris walls as an "induced" measure because protecting East Harlem would push water toward the Bronx; the paper noted that a final plan was unlikely to be built before 2044. The same reporting points to heavy-rain events such as Hurricane Ida as a separate risk to storm surge.

For a fiber plan that means the building's main room and its active equipment should sit above the lowest occupied level wherever the building allows it, with only passive cable and sealed splice cases in cellars and vaults. Cable entering from outside comes in through a sleeve that is sealed against water as well as fire, and indoor/outdoor cable is used through damp sections so there is no splice at the wall. Where a carrier insists on terminating in a cellar, we keep its terminal and our first enclosure on the wall well off the floor, and design the riser so the equipment upstairs can be fed from a second carrier entering higher in the building if one is added later.

The highways matter in a different way. Bruckner Boulevard runs beneath the Bruckner Expressway, and the neighbourhood is ringed by the Major Deegan and the approaches to three bridges. South Bronx Unite also describes waterfront waste transfer stations, peaker power plants and around 1,000 daily diesel truck trips associated with the FreshDirect warehouse. Fine dust and soot are hard on fiber connectors, which must be clean to work, so enclosures in Port Morris are closed units with sealed cable entries, every connector is inspected and cleaned before it is mated, and dust caps stay on unused ports.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Port Morris Building's Past Lives

What our installers look for first in a converted building (our usual practice, not one job's story)

The first thing we look for in a converted building is old labels. Cable left by earlier occupants often carries a company name, a room number or a stencilled code, and those clues tell us which tenant ran which cable long before any drawing does. We photograph every label we find, because they disappear when walls are opened.

The second is the original freight route. Factories and warehouses moved goods from the street to the upper floors along one path, and the building's first wiring usually followed it. Even when the freight elevator is gone, the shaft, the chase beside it or the dock below it often points to the most direct way through the building.

The third is who uses the space at night. Studios, depots and live-work lofts run on schedules that a daytime visit misses. Asking when the building is quietest, and who will be there, saves a second trip and keeps our work out of somebody's shoot or delivery run.

What happens if the ceiling or attic space we need is fouled with pigeon droppings?
+

We do not work in it until it has been cleaned. Droppings that build up in old lofts, rooftop bulkheads and abandoned ceiling spaces can carry health risks when the dust is disturbed, so heavy accumulations should be removed by a cleaning or abatement contractor using proper protection before any cable work begins. Light, isolated deposits we can work around with protective equipment. We point out fouled spaces on the walkthrough so the cleaning can be booked ahead of installation day.

Can fiber work go ahead while the building is still on temporary construction power?
+

Yes, for most of it. Pulling, supporting and splicing fiber need only temporary lighting and battery tools, and our splicers and test sets run on their own batteries. What has to wait is anything that needs finished conditions: active switches that need a dedicated outlet, and final testing in rooms where other trades are still sanding, painting or cutting, because dust ruins connectors. We often pull and splice during construction, cap and protect the ends, and return for final cleaning and testing once the rooms are finished.

Our loft building has no working elevator — does carrying materials up the stairs change the price?
+

Only slightly, and the proposal says so. Fiber is light compared with copper, and a reel of riser cable and a few enclosures go up stairs easily. What adds time in a walk-up loft building is repeated trips with ladders, drills and test equipment, and the care needed not to mark finished stair halls. We plan the work floor by floor to keep trips down, and where the extra labour is meaningful it appears as its own line rather than being buried in the price of the cable.

Our construction lender pays out in draws — can your proposal be broken into the line items its inspector checks?
+

Yes. Construction lenders and their inspectors usually release money as stages of work are completed, so it helps if the low-voltage scope is divided the same way. We can split the proposal into stages such as entrance and sleeves, riser installed, floors distributed, and testing and closeout, each with its own amount, and provide photos and test results at each stage so the inspector can confirm it. The deposit set out in our service agreement still applies to the first stage.

Can fiber carry the monitoring link for rooftop solar panels or a battery system?
+

Yes. Solar inverters and battery systems report production and faults over a network connection, and on a large roof, or in a building whose battery room is in the cellar, the distance and the electrical surroundings favour fiber. A short fiber link from the roof bulkhead or battery room to the building's network, with a media converter at each end, keeps the monitoring data entirely separate from the electrical side. The solar or battery installer still configures its own equipment; we provide the link and its test results.

Can fiber be pulled in an unheated building during a cold snap?
+

Usually, within limits. Every fiber cable has a minimum installation temperature set by its manufacturer, and below it the jacket can stiffen and crack when it is bent, so in a building open to the weather in winter we check the cable's rating against the conditions and, if needed, keep reels in a heated space until they are pulled. Splicers and test sets work in the cold but need time to warm up. Many Port Morris buildings under conversion are unheated in winter, so we plan pulls for the warmer part of the day.

Do you supply the hybrid fiber cables that broadcast cameras plug into?
+

No. Those camera cables, which combine fiber and power in one rugged jacket, are specialist broadcast equipment that productions rent along with their cameras. What we install is the building's side: single-mode tie lines between stages, control rooms and machine rooms, terminated in panels where a production's own equipment can patch in. That gives each production a clean, tested path through the building without running its own cable across the floor.

Can we see a sample test report before we hire you?
+

Yes. We can send an example closeout package with the client's details removed, showing the strand labels, the loss result for each strand, OTDR traces and the route sketch. It shows exactly what you will receive when your own job is finished, and it is a fair way to compare proposals: ask every bidder for the same thing before you choose.

Can the old fiber enclosures and patch panels a previous tenant left behind be reused?
+

Often, once they have been checked. Wall enclosures, rack panels and splice trays do not wear out the way electronics do, so a sound enclosure with intact doors, cable entries and trays can usually be cleaned, relabelled and given new pigtails. We replace hardware that is cracked, missing its trays or full of dust and debris, adapters whose ports are damaged, and anything mounted where it now blocks a door or a working space. Reused hardware is listed on the proposal as reused, so it is clear what is new.

Do we need to tell our property insurer before fiber work starts?
+

Usually not for routine low-voltage work, but check your policy and your lease, because some require notice of any work in the building. What insurers and landlords most often ask for is our certificate of insurance, naming them where required, and evidence that every opening we make through a fire-rated floor or wall has been sealed. We provide both, with photographs of each sealed penetration in the closeout file, which is also what you would want to show after any later claim.
