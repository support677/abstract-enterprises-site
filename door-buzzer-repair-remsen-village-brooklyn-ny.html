The Neighborhood

Why Remsen Village Properties Need Door Buzzer Repair

A Remsen Village block can hold four different kinds of door buzzer within a few hundred feet, and each one breaks in its own way. The neighborhood is the eastern part of East Flatbush. Brownstoner's architecture writer Suzanne Spellen places it between East Flatbush, Northeast Flatbush and Canarsie, and notes that it was largely farmland until the Nostrand Avenue subway reached the area in the 1920s, after which it filled in with houses through the 1940s. CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes what is there now: brick rowhouses, semi-detached houses and garden apartments, many in red or yellow brick with small front yards. Along the avenues are four-story walk-ups, apartments over storefronts, churches and, more recently, new mid-rise buildings.

The houses explain much of the repair work. On East 91st Street, Spellen describes a row of brick two-family houses built in 1932 in which each house holds two apartments and each apartment has its own doorway under a peaked hood, with a two-car garage behind the house, one space for each apartment. That is a very different buzzer from the one most people picture. There is no lobby panel and no list of names. There are two buttons on two doorways, two bells or chimes, and often two separate wire runs down into one basement, sometimes from one transformer and sometimes from two. When one household says "the bell is dead" and the other's works fine, the fault is almost always somewhere on the half of the circuit they don't share.

The rental buildings bring a different set of problems. In April 2026 News 12 Brooklyn visited a four-story rent-stabilized building on Linden Boulevard in East Flatbush where tenants were not sure who owned the building after their longtime landlord died. HPD listed a new registered owner from December 2025, the building carried more than 900 HPD violations, and the city had enrolled it in its Alternative Enforcement Program. One tenant, Sharon Lee, told the station: "We don't mind paying our rent, but we want to know we are sending it to the right people." That story is not about an intercom. It is about the thing every intercom repair in a rental building depends on: someone with the authority to approve the work. Brick Underground's intercom guide makes the same point from the repair side, noting that building authorization is needed before any repair is made.

And the door itself matters more than the electronics. In May 2025 News 12 reported on a building on Nostrand Avenue, on the western side of East Flatbush, where tenant Cephas Bradley said: "Many instances where people just come in and urinate in the hallway because the door does not lock." A buzzer that rings perfectly is worth very little if the street door it controls doesn't close and latch. HPD treats a defective self-closing entrance door in a multiple dwelling as a class C immediately hazardous violation with 14 days to correct, so in a walk-up the strike, the closer and the latch are part of every buzzer repair we do.

Put together, Remsen Village buzzer work is less about one kind of system and more about reading which kind you have. We look at the doorway first, then follow the wire to wherever its power comes from, and we repair the part that failed rather than the whole system.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Two-Doorway House Before Touching a Wire

This is how our technicians approach a house where each apartment has its own front doorway, written as our standing practice rather than as a story about one particular address. The first thing we do is press both buttons, one after the other, and listen from both apartments. If both bells are weak, the shared part is suspect: the transformer, or the single pair of wires that leaves it. If only one is weak or dead, we stop thinking about the transformer and start thinking about that doorway's button, that apartment's chime and the run between them.

Then we go down to the basement and look for where the bell power actually starts. In houses of this age it can be a small transformer screwed to a joist next to the electrical panel, a second one added years later on the other side of the basement, or a plug-in adapter that someone fitted when a video doorbell went in. Owners are often surprised to learn their house has two. We measure each one with the bells pressed, label which doorway it feeds, and only then decide what to replace. Most of the time the answer is one part, not the system.

Two Doorways, Two Circuits

Door Buzzer Repair for Remsen Village Two-Families Where Each Apartment Has Its Own Door

The two-family with a doorway for each apartment is one of the most common houses in the neighborhood, and it creates repair problems that lobby-style buildings never have. The East 91st Street houses Brownstoner describes are a clear example: each house, built in 1932, holds two apartments, and each apartment has its own doorway under its own peaked hood. Variations of that plan, with one doorway at the front and one at the side, or one up a short flight of steps and one at ground level, appear on many Remsen Village blocks.

Why one household's bell fails and the other's doesn't

In a house like this the two bells are two separate circuits that may share only their power. Each doorway has a button. Each button has its own pair of wires running through the door frame, into the wall and down to the basement. Each pair ends at a chime, a buzzer or, in some houses, a simple bell mounted inside the apartment it serves. The only shared part may be the transformer, and sometimes even that isn't shared. So when the downstairs family's bell stops working and the upstairs family's still rings, we are almost certainly looking at the downstairs button, the downstairs chime, or the downstairs wire run. A new transformer would not change anything.

The opposite pattern, where both bells go weak or quiet at once, points the other way. Then the shared power is the first suspect: a transformer that has aged out, a loose screw terminal on it, a breaker that was switched off during electrical work, or a splice where the two runs join a single feed. We measure voltage at the transformer with nothing pressed, then with each button held in turn, and the readings tell us which part of the circuit is losing power.

When one apartment adds a video doorbell

A very common Remsen Village call starts like this: one household replaced its old button with a video doorbell, and since then either the new doorbell keeps restarting, or the other household's bell has become unreliable. Video doorbells draw far more power than a plain button, and the transformer in a 1930s or 1940s house was usually chosen for one or two simple bells. ButterflyMX notes that typical intercom and bell systems run in a low range of about 6 to 16 volts. Many video doorbells want the top of that range or more, continuously. When they share a small transformer with a second bell, both can suffer.

The fix is usually one of two things, and we price both in writing before doing either. The first is a properly sized transformer that can carry both doorways comfortably. The second is to give each doorway its own supply, so the two households' choices don't affect each other. The right answer depends on how the two runs are wired in the basement, which we only know after we've traced them. We also check the chime on the video doorbell side, because a chime that hums instead of ringing after one of these upgrades is often just being fed the wrong way, not broken.

The side doorway and the garage behind

Houses with a garage in back often have a third door that nobody thinks of as part of the bell system until it fails: a rear or side door that family members use every day, with a button that was added years after the house was built. These later additions are where we find the most improvised wiring: thin wire stapled along a fence or a garage wall, splices wrapped in tape, and runs that pass through damp ground. When a back-door button rings only in dry weather, that run is the first thing we test. If it has to be replaced, the quote offers a weather-rated cable on a protected path rather than another run along the fence.

Who pays when two households share a house

In an owner-occupied two-family the owner normally pays for the bell or buzzer at the tenant's doorway as well as their own. We bill whoever books and authorizes the visit, and we tell both households when we will be there, because we often need to press a button at one doorway while listening inside the other apartment. If a tenant calls us directly about their doorway, we ask them to confirm the owner has approved the visit before we book it, so nobody ends up with a bill they didn't agree to.

Rental Buildings

Apartment Buzzer Repair in Remsen Village Walk-Ups When the Owner Has Changed

The four-story walk-ups along Linden Boulevard, Church Avenue and the other avenues are where apartment buzzer repair in Remsen Village looks most like the rest of Brooklyn: a street door with an electric strike, a call panel with one button per apartment, a power supply in the cellar, and a handset in each apartment. What makes them different right now is how many of them are changing hands, and how often a tenant with a dead buzzer has no clear person to call.

What the Linden Boulevard story tells a tenant

The News 12 report from April 2026 described tenants who did not know who owned their building after the landlord died, a new owner listed with HPD from December 2025, and more than 900 violations. HPD told the station it had placed the building in its Alternative Enforcement Program and could use its Emergency Repair Program to fix hazardous conditions. For a tenant whose buzzer has stopped working in a building like that, the useful lesson is about the route, not the repair. The 311 apartment maintenance complaint explicitly covers doorbells, buzzers and intercoms that are broken or missing. A complaint creates a record that an inspector can act on, and in a building already under city enforcement, that record matters.

We can't be hired by a tenant to repair a landlord's building system without the owner's approval, and we won't pretend otherwise. What we can do is work for whoever does have authority: a new owner who has just taken over and wants to know what condition the entry system is in, a managing agent, or a court-appointed administrator. Brick Underground's repair guide notes that building authorization is required before any repair, and that a tenant can handle the communication. In practice that means a tenant can pass our number to the owner or agent, and we deal with them directly.

The first visit after a sale

A new owner of a Remsen Village walk-up usually inherits an entry system with no paperwork. Nobody knows the brand of the handsets, whether the strike is fail-secure or fail-safe, which apartments' buttons have been rewired, or where the power supply was moved during the last renovation. Our first visit in that situation is a diagnosis of the whole entry: we test every button on the panel, every handset, the voice in both directions and the release from each apartment, and we write down what passed and what failed. The owner gets a dated record that shows exactly where the system stands on the day they took over. Any repair beyond that diagnosis is priced separately, in writing.

This matters for violations as well as for tenants. HPD writes buzzer faults under the Housing Maintenance Code with wording like "properly repair or replace the broken or defective bell buzzer intercom system from the building entrance door to panel", and its records show these violations in classes A, B and C. An owner who knows which apartments fail, and why, can repair them in the right order instead of chasing one violation at a time.

Older handsets and the wire behind them

Many of these walk-ups still have audio handsets that were installed decades ago. Brick Underground's guide notes that the wiring inside the walls is usually fine even in older systems such as Lee Dan, and that the likely fault points are the lobby connections, the apartment station, the door strike and the power supply. Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith put it simply in that article: "It's all in the wire connections." That matches what we find. A building where four apartments in one line have gone quiet usually has one failed connection where that line meets the riser, not four broken handsets. We test from the panel and the cellar before we order a single station.

The Door Itself

Electric Strike Repair in Remsen Village: When the Buzzer Works but the Door Doesn't

The most common walk-up complaint we hear is not about the bell at all. It is that the buzzer sounds upstairs, someone presses the door button, and the visitor still can't get in. Or it is the reverse: the door doesn't lock, so the buzzer barely matters. Both are door problems, and both are part of the repair.

A strike that hums but holds

An electric strike is the part of the door frame that lets the latch go when the release button is pressed. When it hums but the door stays shut, the cause is usually one of three things. The first is pressure on the latch: a door that has sagged or a frame that has moved pushes the latch hard against the strike's keeper, and the keeper can't swing free. dormakaba's troubleshooting notes tell installers to "adjust door or strike to eliminate any back pressure" in exactly this situation. The second is power: a strike that gets too little voltage, often because of a long thin run or a tired supply, buzzes without enough force to release. The third is the strike itself, worn out after years of daily use.

We test in that order, because the cheapest fix is the most likely one. A door that opens easily when someone pushes on it while the button is held is telling us about pressure, not about a broken strike. Weatherstripping, a new lock, a latch guard or a closer adjustment can all add that pressure without anyone noticing. A strike that releases with the door held open, but not with it closed, confirms it.

A strike that buzzes loudly even when it works

Some street doors in the neighborhood buzz loudly every time they are released, and residents assume something is wrong. Often nothing is. dormakaba notes plainly that "A strike connected to an AC power supply will buzz unless a rectifier is installed." Many older buzzer systems feed the strike from an AC transformer, and the buzz is how visitors know when to push. If the buzz is new, or the strike has started to get hot or smell, that is different: dormakaba also tells installers to confirm "that the insert and power supply are the proper voltage", and a strike being fed the wrong voltage needs attention before it fails.

The door that doesn't lock at all

A street door that won't latch is the more serious fault, for the reason the Nostrand Avenue tenant described. HPD treats a defective self-closing door at a multiple dwelling's entrance as a class C immediately hazardous condition, with 14 days to correct after the notice is received. The causes are mechanical more often than electrical: a closer that has lost its adjustment and no longer pulls the door the last inch, a latch that no longer reaches the strike because the door has dropped, or a strike stuck in the released position. We check the closer's speed and latch settings, the gap at the strike side of the door, and whether the strike relocks when power is removed.

Fail-secure or fail-safe

Allegion explains the difference simply: a fail-safe device unlocks when power is lost, and a fail-secure device stays locked. With a strike, people inside can still leave by turning the lever. Most Remsen Village street doors should be fail-secure, so a power outage doesn't leave the building open. When we find a fail-safe strike on a street door, or a magnetic lock with no proper request-to-exit device, we point it out and price the correction separately. We don't make that change without the owner's written approval, because it affects how the door behaves in an emergency.

Newer Buildings

Lobby Intercom Repair in Remsen Village's New Buildings

Remsen Avenue and the streets near it are getting taller. New York YIMBY reported the affordable housing lottery for 406 Remsen Avenue, an eight-story mixed-use building with 95 residences, developed by Samnon Associates and designed by Asher Hershkowitz Architect, with amenities that include gated access, an elevator, a garage, bike storage and a senior center. PincusCo reported in May 2026 that another developer is seeking a rezoning for up to 972 units in East Flatbush, with part of the project on East 98th Street. Buildings like these don't have a buzzer. They have an entry system with layers.

Several doors, one system

A gated building may have a gate at the sidewalk, a lobby door, a garage door and a door from the garage into the building, and residents expect one system to manage all of them. When one layer fails, residents usually report it as "the intercom is down." Our first job is to find which layer actually failed: the panel, the network connection behind it, the relay that drives one particular door, or the lock hardware on that door. In a networked system, a panel that shows the directory and calls apartments but won't open one door is usually a relay or wiring fault at that door, not a panel fault.

The developer hand-off

New buildings bring a problem older ones don't: the people who installed the system are often gone by the time it needs repair. The administrator login, the list of which relay drives which door, and the warranty information may have stayed with the developer or the original installer. We ask owners and managing agents to find those three things before our first visit, because without the admin account some app-based systems can't be changed at all. If they can't be found, the diagnosis notes what's missing, and we work with the owner on recovering access through the maker's support process before we price any repair.

Residents who don't use the app

A building with a senior center and a lottery-based tenancy will have residents who don't want an app on their phone, or don't have a smartphone. When a panel calls phones instead of handsets, those residents depend on the phone number in the directory being right and on the panel's cellular or internet connection working. A good share of "intercom not working" calls in newer buildings turn out to be directory entries pointing at an old number, or a panel that has lost its connection. Those are fixed in the system settings, not with parts, and the diagnosis visit usually covers them.

Door Buzzer Repair for Remsen Village's Architecture

The name Remsen Village has been used for this part of East Flatbush since around the mid-1990s, but the buildings are much older. Developers chose the name "Rugby" for the area in the 1890s, and the houses came in a long wave from the 1920s to the 1940s, with apartment buildings on the avenues and newer construction since. These are the property types our door buzzer technician meets here, and what each means for a repair.

The Two-Family With a Doorway per Apartment

Two doorways, two buttons, two bells, often one basement and sometimes two transformers. Repairs start by finding which half of the circuit failed, and by labeling both runs so the next fault takes minutes to trace.

The Two-Family With One Shared Door

One front door, a small vestibule and two bells, often with a release button upstairs for the inner door. The shared door's strike and latch get as much use as a walk-up's and wear the same way.

The Brick Row House

Attached houses in red or yellow brick with small front yards. The button is close to the door and the run is short, so faults are usually the button, the chime or a splice in the basement, not the wire itself.

The Four-Story Walk-Up

A street door, a call panel, a cellar power supply and a handset in each apartment, with risers running up the building. Line faults, worn strikes and closers that no longer latch make up most calls.

The Garden Apartment Building

Low buildings around a courtyard, sometimes with more than one entrance. Each entrance may have its own panel and supply, and residents of one wing can lose their buzzer while the other wing keeps working.

The New Mid-Rise With a Gate and Garage

A networked lobby panel, a gate, a garage and an elevator, managed from an admin account. Faults are often in a relay, a network connection or a directory setting rather than in hardware at the door.

How the building type changes the repair

The two-family with a doorway per apartment is a matching problem. We test the two doorways against each other first, because the comparison tells us whether to look at shared parts or at one household's half. The transformer in these houses is often the original or an early replacement, sized for two plain bells, and it may have been asked to carry a video doorbell, a chime upgrade or a camera since. When both runs are labeled at the basement and at each chime, a future repair is much faster.

The two-family with one shared door behaves more like a tiny apartment building. The street door often has a strike that the upstairs household releases from a button inside, and that strike is used dozens of times a day by both families. Wear on the keeper and sag in the door account for most of the "it buzzes but doesn't open" calls in houses like this. The button upstairs is usually fine.

The brick row house is usually the simplest repair in the neighborhood. The button is on or next to the door frame, the chime is just inside, and the run to the basement is short. A dead bell in a row house is most often the button, a loose terminal at the chime, or a transformer that has simply failed with age, and many of these are fixed within the diagnosis visit.

The four-story walk-up is a riser problem. Handsets on the same line share a pair, so a pattern of failures tells us where to look: if every apartment above the second floor on one line is quiet, the fault is where that line passes the second floor, not in the handsets. The cellar supply and the street door hardware are the other two common failure points, and both are tested before we price anything.

The garden apartment building spreads its entry system out. Courtyard buildings can have several entrances, each with its own panel and sometimes its own supply. When residents say "half the building can't buzz anyone in," we first confirm which entrance they mean, then test that entrance's panel, supply and lock independently of the others.

The new mid-rise is a systems problem. Before we touch hardware, we confirm the panel is online, check its log for the failed calls or releases, and look at how each door is wired to its relay. Those buildings also have the most to lose from a careless repair, because changing the wrong setting can affect every door at once.

Some businesses on these corridors use a buzzer with a camera, so staff can see who is at a locked door before releasing it. Compliance notes for shops, offices and mixed-use buildings

  • Entrance doors in apartment buildings: the NY Attorney General's Tenants' Rights Guide says multiple dwellings built after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at all entrances, and those with eight or more apartments must also have a two-way voice intercom with a buzz-open function; older buildings must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of apartments request it.
  • Self-closing doors: HPD classifies a defective self-closing entrance door as a class C immediately hazardous violation, to be corrected within 14 days of the notice.
  • Exit from inside: locks on egress doors must still let people leave without a key or special knowledge. With a strike, the lever opens the door from inside; with a magnetic lock, building code sets conditions such as a request-to-exit release.
  • Reach for door stations and buttons: the 2010 ADA Standards set a high forward reach of 48 inches for operable parts, which matters when a panel or button is moved during a repair at a business.
  • Line-voltage work: NYC requires a licensed electrician and a permit for line-voltage electrical work. Our repairs are low voltage; if a fix needs a new circuit or outlet, that part is named separately in the quote as electrician's work.
Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Repair in Remsen Village: Questions Answered

Houses with two doorways

A contractor dug along the side path to our garage, and the bell at our rear doorway went quiet the same week. Could the digging have cut it?

It could, and the timing makes it the first thing we would test. Bell wire added to a back or side door years after a house was built often runs at shallow depth along a path, a fence line or the foot of a garage wall, sometimes with no conduit at all. A spade, a post-hole digger or a mini excavator doesn't need to cut it cleanly; nicking the jacket is enough to let water in, and the wire corrodes from there. We measure at the transformer and then at the rear button with it pressed. If the voltage disappears somewhere between the two, a tone generator tells us roughly where. If the run is damaged along the path, the quote will offer a new weather-rated cable on a protected route rather than a splice in wet ground, because a buried splice is likely to fail again.

Both doorways of our two-family ring the same chime in the front hall, so nobody can tell whose visitor is outside. Can each doorway ring only in its own apartment?

Usually, yes. In some houses the two buttons were wired to one chime for convenience, often during a renovation, and the result is that both households hear every visitor. Separating them means giving each doorway its own chime or buzzer inside the apartment it serves, and sometimes its own transformer as well. Whether that needs new wire depends on how the two runs reach the basement; if each doorway already has its own pair, the change can be as small as moving one pair to a second chime. We trace both runs on the diagnosis visit and price the separation in writing. If a full separation would mean opening finished walls, we will tell you, and offer the option of a two-tone chime instead, so each household at least knows which doorway rang.

The name slots next to both our doorbells faded years ago, and delivery drivers keep ringing the wrong apartment. Is relabeling part of a repair?

It can be, and it is one of the cheapest fixes we make. If we are already at the house for a bell or buzzer repair, replacing faded label inserts or adding a clear, weather-resistant label beside each button is done on the same visit. Where the button plate has no label slot at all, we can fit a small engraved or printed label next to it. We label by apartment or floor rather than by name unless you ask for names, because tenants change. It sounds minor, but a misdirected visitor at a two-doorway house usually means someone opening their door to a stranger looking for the other apartment, and the label fixes that.

Walk-ups and rental buildings

My father owned our walk-up for decades and has passed away. Nobody in the family knows where the buzzer's power supply is. How do you find it?

We follow the wire from the street door. In walk-ups the buzzer supply is almost always in the cellar, often near the electrical meters or on a wall close to where the riser cable drops down from the first floor, but it can also be in a closet behind the stairs or above a hallway ceiling. We trace the pair from the panel, then confirm the supply by measuring it with the release pressed. Once it is found, we label it, note its rating, and mark which breaker feeds it, so the family has a record. If the estate is still being settled, tell us who has authority to approve work; the diagnosis visit can be booked by the executor or administrator, and the written record of what we found is often useful for a sale.

Our building was sold, tenants now pay rent to a new LLC, and the buzzer has been dead since. Who should we notify, and in what order?

Start with the new owner or its managing agent in writing, using whatever contact information came with the rent instructions, and describe exactly what fails: no ring, no voice, or no release. Keep a copy. If nothing happens, file a 311 apartment maintenance complaint; 311's guide says that category covers doorbells, buzzers and intercoms that are broken or missing, and a complaint can lead to an HPD inspection. HPD's online building records show the registered owner and any open violations, which helps confirm you are writing to the right party. We can't repair the building's system on a tenant's say-so, but if the new owner contacts us, we can test the entire entry in one visit and give them a written list of what has to be fixed.

HPD's Emergency Repair Program has already done work in our building for other problems. Could the city's contractor fix the buzzer instead of the owner?

That is HPD's decision, not ours, and we can't promise how it would treat a particular buzzer. HPD told News 12 in April 2026 that it can use its Emergency Repair Program to correct hazardous conditions in a Linden Boulevard building it had placed under enforcement; under that program the city hires a contractor when an owner fails to fix a hazardous violation, and bills the owner. A broken self-closing entrance door is classed as immediately hazardous, which is why the door itself often gets attention first. For the intercom, the practical route is the same: a 311 complaint, an inspection and a violation. If the owner would rather fix it than be billed for city work, we can do the diagnosis and repair under their authorization.

Street doors and strikes

Our street door releases easily all summer, but from December on it sticks and visitors have to shove it. Is that the buzzer or the door?

Almost always the door. Wood and even steel doors and frames move with temperature and humidity, and a latch that clears the strike's keeper in June can be pressed hard against it in January. That pressure is what dormakaba's troubleshooting notes describe when they tell installers to remove back pressure from a binding strike. Cold also stiffens the oil in older door closers, so the door may not close as fully, which changes where the latch sits. We check the strike with the door held open, then closed, and adjust the keeper, the strike position or the closer so the door releases in both seasons. Replacing the strike won't help if the door is the problem, so we don't start there.

After a handyman swapped our buzzer transformer, the strike on the street door gets warm and smells faintly of burning. Should we stop using it?

Yes, stop relying on it until it has been checked, and call us. A strike that gets hot or smells is usually being fed the wrong voltage or the wrong type of power, and dormakaba's notes tell installers to confirm the correct output at the power source when a strike shows a burnt smell. Replacement transformers are often bought by size and not by rating, and a higher-voltage transformer can overheat a strike coil that was designed for less. We measure the supply, compare it with the strike's rating, and either put back a correctly rated supply or replace a coil that has already been damaged. Until then, the door's mechanical lock and the lever inside still work normally.

Our door closer slams the street door so hard that the latch seems to bounce back out of the strike. Can that leave the building unlocked?

It can. A closer set too fast hits the frame hard enough that the latch can rebound and come to rest outside the strike's keeper, leaving the door closed but not locked. Residents often don't notice, because the door looks shut. Most closers have separate adjustments for the main sweep and for the final few inches, called the latch speed, and when either is wrong the door either slams or stops short. We adjust both, test the door from fully open and from half open, and check that the latch engages every time. If the closer won't hold an adjustment, it is worn out, and its replacement is priced separately in the written quote.

Newer buildings, shops and congregations

The garage door in our building opens with a fob, but since a power cut last month it ignores the release from the intercom. Did the outage damage something?

Possibly, but often nothing is broken at all. When a building loses power, the panel, the network switch and the garage controller can come back up in the wrong order, and the link between the intercom's relay and the garage controller doesn't re-establish. Fobs may still work because they talk to the garage controller directly. We first check whether the intercom still shows the garage door in its settings and whether its relay clicks when the release is pressed. If the relay works but the garage doesn't respond, the wiring or input at the garage controller is next. A surge during the outage can damage a relay or an input board, and if it has, the replacement part and its cost are named in writing before we order it.

The buzzer for our bakery's back door hangs right by the door, but the staff work at the front counter and miss it. Can the chime be moved without running new wire through the shop?

Sometimes. If there is a clear path for a short new run along the ceiling or behind fixtures, a wired chime near the counter is the most reliable answer and usually a small job. Where a wire would have to cross a finished shop or a food prep area, a wired button at the back door can be paired with a plug-in receiver at the front, which avoids the new run, though it adds a part that depends on batteries or a wireless link. We look at both options on the visit and tell you which will hold up in a busy kitchen. If deliveries come before opening, we also check the button itself, because a rear button exposed to rain and cleaning hoses is often what fails first.

The buzzer at our church's office door rings in the office but not in the rectory next door, which it used to. Is that one system or two?

Usually one system with two outputs, and the rectory side has lost its connection. Church buildings often had a bell or chime added in a second building years after the first, sometimes on a run that crosses a yard or passes through a basement shared with another use. When the office still rings, the button and the power are working, so the fault is on the path to the rectory or at the rectory's chime. We test at the office chime, then at the point where the run leaves the building, then at the rectory. If the run crosses outside, it is the most likely failure point, and the quote will say whether it can be repaired or should be replaced on a protected route.

We rent an office over a store on Utica Avenue, and the landlord says the street door's buzzer is ours to fix. Is that usual?

It depends on the lease, which is why the first thing we ask a commercial tenant is what the lease says about the building's entrance and systems. In residential buildings the owner is responsible for the entrance door and the intercom; commercial leases can shift repairs to the tenant, and some do. If the lease does make it your responsibility, we can repair the part that serves your office, such as your station, your release button or your share of the panel, under your authorization. If the street door, strike and power supply also serve other tenants or apartments, we will tell you, and suggest the landlord be included before we work on shared parts, so nobody pays for equipment they don't control.
