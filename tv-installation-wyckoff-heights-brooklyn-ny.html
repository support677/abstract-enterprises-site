The Neighborhood

Why Wyckoff Heights Properties Need TV Installation

Ask three people where Wyckoff Heights is and you may get three answers. Wikipedia describes it as an area straddling Bushwick in Brooklyn and the southwest corner of Ridgewood in Queens, with the ZIP codes 11237 and 11385. Forgotten NY's Kevin Walsh, walking Wyckoff Avenue in 2008, doubted the name was ever much used outside the hospital that carries it. A reader writing to Urban Omnibus in 2020 called it a "pencil thin area informally known as Wyckoff Heights." For a TV installer the label matters less than the line it straddles: Forgotten NY traced the county border arriving at Wyckoff Avenue from Gates Avenue and running down the middle of the avenue to George Street, so the house on one side of the street is in Brooklyn and the house opposite is in Queens. We work on both sides. This page is written for the Brooklyn blocks and prices them on our Brooklyn list, and for a Queens-side address we tell you which list applies before you book.

What decides a TV job here is not the map but the order in which the houses went up. Wikipedia's history of the area puts the first buildings as attached wood-frame houses for three and six families, then masonry from around 1900, and finally attached one-family houses with alleys and rear garages on the last eighteen acres, developed in the 1940s. At the north end of Wyckoff Avenue, between Flushing Avenue and Starr Street, Forgotten NY found a largely industrial stretch of lofts, including a former candy factory at Willoughby Avenue, and a 1974 New York Times item described a developer turning the old Schlitz brewery "in the Wyckoff Heights section of Brooklyn" into new space. StreetEasy's record for 70 Wyckoff Avenue shows a four-story knitting mill from 1931 converted into 52 loft rentals. And in the last few years the blocks between Wyckoff and Irving Avenues near the DeKalb Avenue L have been filling with new buildings. Each of those layers leaves a different wall behind the couch.

That is why a stud finder that works in one apartment is useless in the next. A frame row from the first wave can have plaster an inch thick on wood lath over rough-sawn studs at whatever spacing the carpenter chose, often covered since then with a layer of drywall. A brick flat building can put the living-room TV on a solid party wall of soft, sandy brick with mortar that crumbles under a fingernail, with partitions inside made of gypsum block that sounds hollow but is not. A 1940s attached house is more regular, with framed walls and plaster or early drywall. A mill loft offers brick, concrete and steel, and sometimes a column in the middle of the room. A new rental gives you drywall over metal studs, a mini-split on the wall and, in some of the newest buildings, an airtight exterior wall that should not be drilled carelessly. The base price assumes the easy wall; most Wyckoff Heights jobs are not on the easy wall.

The age of the housing brings rules with it, and they matter more for a TV than most people expect. New York City's lead paint law, Local Law 1 of 2004, tells owners of buildings put up before 1960 to presume the paint is lead-based, in buildings of three or more apartments and in rented units of one- and two-family houses. Nearly everything in Wyckoff Heights apart from the new construction is older than that. Drilling four holes for a bracket disturbs very little paint, but cutting two openings for an in-wall kit, or sanding off an old bracket's scars, makes dust, and HPD's rules ask for certified contractors and safe work practices when paint of unknown lead content is disturbed. We drill with a vacuum at the bit, we lay plastic under the work, and when a household includes a child under six we say so in the plan. The electrical code is the other rule. The city's Department of Buildings says no installation or change to any electrical system can be done without a permit and a licensed master electrician, which is why our hidden-power option is a listed in-wall kit that plugs into the outlet you already have, not a new outlet cut into the wall.

Then there is the signal. In a six-family frame row, the cable and fiber lines usually come in at the cellar and climb the outside of the building or a hallway to each apartment, and after a few decades of providers and tenants the cellar holds a tangle nobody labeled. FCC broadband data for Ridgewood, as summarized by BestNeighborhood from the December 2025 collection, lists Verizon fiber and Spectrum cable as available almost everywhere, with Optimum at about one address in ten and fixed wireless home internet nearly everywhere. Which one a household uses decides what box sits where, and the box decides how far the cables have to travel to the TV. In a loft, the router may sit at the far end of a 40-foot room behind a brick wall. In one of the new buildings, the building may supply the Wi-Fi itself. We ask about all of this on the first call, because a TV that streams badly on the wall is not a finished job.

Finally, the neighbors are close. Frame rows share walls with the house next door, the brick flats stack two households on every floor, and the new buildings are full of people working from home. The city's Noise Code fact sheet sets construction hours on weekdays between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m., and we keep our drilling well inside that window. We plan the noisy part of the job, the minute or two of hammer drilling, to happen in one burst after we have warned anyone on the other side of the wall.

Same-Day Repair in Wyckoff Heights

Need Repair in Wyckoff Heights? A TV Sagging Off Lath, an Anchor Spinning in Soft Brick, or a Screen That Lost Its Signal After the Move

The urgent TV calls from buildings like these come in three shapes. The first is a screen that has dropped on one side or started to lean forward. In a frame row, that usually means the bracket was screwed into lath rather than a stud: the lath strips are only a quarter of an inch or so thick, and once the plaster around them cracks, the screws work loose a little more every time someone pulls the TV toward them. In a brick flat, the usual cause is an anchor set into a mortar joint. Old lime mortar is soft, and a sleeve anchor that felt tight on the day it went in can turn into a powdery hole after a few years of weight. The fix is the same in both cases: take the TV down, find out what the wall actually is, and fix the bracket into something that will hold.

The second kind of call is a TV that works but has lost its picture or its internet after a move, a renovation or a change of provider. In an older building, the cable that once fed the living room may have been cut in the cellar when a new line went in for another apartment, or a splitter may be feeding four rooms with a signal strong enough for one. We trace the line with a tone generator from the TV end back to wherever it goes, tell you what we find and, where a new run is the answer, quote it from what we see. The third kind is a TV that came down on its own or was taken down by a contractor, leaving a wall with holes and a wire hanging out. That one is about patching the plan rather than the wall: choosing a new spot or a new bracket, and deciding whether the old holes can be covered by the new plate.

If a screen is leaning, do not push it back toward the wall; that pries the screws further out of the plaster. If two adults can lift it safely, take the weight off, unplug it and lay it face up on something soft. Then call (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. If the same TV is going back up properly in the same visit, it is priced as a new mount from $179.99 plus the wall add-on, with the take-down added only if we are the ones who take it off the wall. We book within two hours of your call and give you the earliest open arrival window; we do not promise a same-day install until we see the schedule.

Building Specialists

TV Installation for Wyckoff Heights's Architecture

Six kinds of building cover almost every TV call from Wyckoff Heights. They differ in three ways that decide the plan: what the wall is made of, where the signal comes into the building, and who besides you has a say in what gets drilled.

The wood-frame row

Attached three- and six-family houses from the area's first building wave: framed walls, thick plaster on lath, studs that wander, and a cellar where every tenant's cable comes in.

The brick flat building

Masonry buildings from about 1900 onward, often with two apartments per floor, brick party walls, gypsum block partitions and plaster laid straight onto masonry in places.

The 1940s alley house

Attached one-family houses on the last land developed, with rear alleys and garages. Regular framing, plaster or early drywall, and living rooms that often face the back.

The mill or brewery loft

Former factories at the north end of Wyckoff Avenue, like the 1931 knitting mill at number 70: brick, concrete, columns, high ceilings and very few interior walls.

The mixed-use walk-up

A store at street level on Wyckoff, Knickerbocker or Myrtle Avenue with apartments above, where the TV wall may share a building with an oven, a walk-in cooler or a bar.

The new rental near the L

Buildings finished in the last few years between Wyckoff and Irving Avenues, from small walk-ups on Stockholm and Hart Streets to the nine-story pair at 1601 DeKalb Avenue.

The wood-frame row: thick plaster, wandering studs and many households

The frame rows are the oldest housing in the neighborhood, and they are also the walls that surprise people most. Behind the plaster are wood studs, but not the even, kiln-dried studs of a modern house. They can be rough-sawn, slightly different widths, and placed wherever a window, a door or a chimney made the carpenter put them. Over the studs sit lath strips, and over the lath two or three coats of plaster. A stud finder reads the lath, the nails and the dense plaster all at once and often gives up. So we find the framing the old way: a knock test along the wall at screen height, a strong magnet that catches the nail heads holding the lath to each stud, and then one small pilot hole where the bracket plate will hide it. When the pilot hole hits solid wood, we know where one stud is, and the others follow from there.

Irregular framing changes the bracket choice. A bracket with a wide wall plate can usually reach two studs even when they are 18 or 20 inches apart; a narrow full-motion arm sometimes cannot. When the studs fall in the wrong place for the screen position you want, the options are to move the screen a few inches, to choose a bracket with a wider plate, or to fix a painted board across the studs first and hang the bracket on the board. We show you the options with tape on the wall before choosing. Frame walls also mean hollow bays, so an in-wall kit is often possible, but frame rows were often fire-blocked and patched over the decades, and a bay can have a horizontal block halfway down. We check with a flexible light and a camera through the top opening before cutting the bottom one.

The households matter too. A six-family row has six sets of neighbors on the other side of walls and floors, and in many of these buildings one of the six is the owner. If you rent, ask your landlord before we come; if you are the landlord, the same plan works for every unit and gives each tenant a properly fixed bracket instead of six sets of holes made by six different people.

The brick flat building: sound brick, soft mortar and walls that only sound hollow

The brick buildings that followed the frame rows put the living room TV on one of three walls. The outside wall and the party wall with the next building are usually solid brick under the plaster, and they hold a TV very well if the anchors are set in the brick itself. The mason quoted in StreetEasy's guide to hanging things on brick, Michael Russo, says it is usually easier to drill into mortar than brick, but that in older buildings crumbling mortar may be too unstable for something heavy like a TV, and the answer then is to drill into the brick, away from its edges. That matches what we see. The light-brown brick in some of these buildings is soft and sandy, so we use anchors sized for soft masonry, keep them back from the edges of each brick, and space them so no two land in the same brick.

Inside walls are the trap. Many of the partitions in prewar masonry buildings are not framed at all but built of gypsum or clay block, then plastered. A knock sounds hollow because the blocks are hollow, but a nail bends on them and a drywall anchor finds nothing to grip. A StreetEasy Talk thread on mounting a TV in a pre-war building described exactly this kind of wall as likely "plastered gypsum block" and called trenching it for cables "a huge mess." Gypsum block can carry a TV of ordinary weight with the right anchor set into the block's solid face, but it cannot be opened for an in-wall kit without a lot of patching, so the cables go into a paintable cord cover or down to the console.

Many flat buildings in the area have an apartment at the front and one at the back on each floor, which means the TV wall may be shared with your neighbor across the hall rather than the building next door. Sound carries through masonry less than through frame walls, but a full-motion arm bumping a party wall at night carries a long way. We fit rubber bumpers to any arm that can touch the wall.

The 1940s alley house: the regular wall, and the room at the back

The attached one-family houses built on the last open land in the 1940s have the most predictable walls in Wyckoff Heights. Their framing is closer to a modern spacing, and the walls are plaster on gypsum lath or early drywall, which a scanner reads far better than wood lath. They are also the houses most likely to have the TV on the drywall-over-wood-studs base price, with no wall add-on at all if the wall turns out to be drywall rather than plaster. What makes them different is the layout. With the alley and the garage behind, the living room or den is often at the back of the house, and the cable and internet usually come in at the front or the cellar. That makes the run from the box to the TV longer than people expect, and we plan it before the visit.

The mill or brewery loft: brick, concrete, columns and no walls to open

The former factories at the north end of Wyckoff Avenue are open rooms with brick outside walls, concrete or heavy timber floors, and columns. The knitting mill at 70 Wyckoff Avenue, built in 1931 and leased as 52 loft-style rentals from 2017, is the kind of building we mean. Lofts give you plenty of room for a big screen and very little hollow wall to hide cables in. The brick takes the $65 add-on and holds well. Poured concrete is not a separate line on the list we publish here, so when a loft wall or column turns out to be concrete, we quote it from your photos before you book. Columns can sometimes carry a TV, which we cover in the questions below. Because the walls cannot be opened, cables in a loft travel on the surface: in a paintable cord cover, in a surface raceway painted to match, or up and across the ceiling in a neat run, the way the building's own wiring often already does.

The mixed-use walk-up: a store downstairs and a TV upstairs

On Wyckoff, Knickerbocker and Myrtle Avenues, many buildings have a store at street level and apartments above. For the apartment, that changes three things. The building's cellar is often the store's storage, so tracing a cable may mean a visit when the store is open. The floor under you may be a ceiling full of the store's equipment, so we do not drill down through floors. And the hours are not just yours to choose: a store below is open during the day, and the people above may work nights. We set the drilling time with you and, where it matters, with the store.

The new rental near the L: metal studs, mini-splits and walls not to pierce

The newest buildings between Wyckoff and Irving Avenues are built very differently from everything around them. Interior walls are usually drywall over light metal studs, which do not hold a lag screw the way wood does, so we use fixings made for steel studs or spread the load across a board. Many apartments are heated and cooled by a mini-split head mounted high on a wall, with refrigerant lines and a drain line running inside the wall below it, and the wall facing the sofa is often the one with the head on it. And some new buildings, like the Passive House senior building at 223 Linden Street a few blocks south, are built around an airtight exterior wall. We cover all three in the questions below. Management offices in these buildings may also ask for a certificate of insurance before the visit, which we send ahead.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks in a Wyckoff Heights Apartment Before the Bracket Comes Out of the Box

These notes describe how our technicians work in buildings like the ones in Wyckoff Heights. They are our practice, written down, not a story about any one address.

Where does the signal come from? Before looking at the TV wall, the technician finds the internet box and the cable jack and follows the line as far as it is visible: along a baseboard, out through a window frame, down to a hallway. In a frame row the line often runs on the outside of the building, and in a brick flat it may come up a hallway in a bundle with the neighbors' lines. If the TV is going to stream, the technician checks the speed at the TV's spot with a phone before anything else. A screen hung on a wall where the Wi-Fi drops to almost nothing is a problem better solved before the holes are drilled.

What is the wall? A knock along the wall at screen height, a scanner, and for wood lath a magnet to find the nail lines. Then one pilot hole where the bracket plate will cover it. The dust from that hole tells most of the story: white powder then wood is plaster on lath; white powder then grey grit is plaster on gypsum block; white powder then red or orange grit is brick; a sudden drop into nothing after the plaster is a hollow bay or a gap behind furring.

Is anything already in the wall? Old buildings carry old surprises: a gas pipe capped behind the plaster where a heater used to be, a radiator riser beside the wall, an abandoned cable buried under a layer of paint. The technician looks for radiator pipes coming up through the floor, for patched squares in the plaster, and for outlets that sit oddly, and asks you what you know about past work in the room.

Who else needs to know? If there is a child under six in the home, or the paint looks like many layers of old paint, the technician sets up plastic under the work and runs the drill with a vacuum at the bit. If the wall is shared, the technician knocks on the neighbor's door, or asks you to, before the hammer drill starts.

Tape, then sit down. Painter's tape marks the outline of the screen. You sit where you watch, and the technician checks height, window glare and the swing of any door. Moving tape costs nothing; moving a bracket means new holes.

Leave it labeled. Before leaving, the technician labels the cables at the TV end and, where we traced a line, at the other end too. Behind a wall-mounted TV the labels save the next person from unplugging everything to find one cable.

Signal and Wiring

From the Cellar Line to the Screen: Getting TV and Internet to a Wyckoff Heights Wall

Most TV installation pages stop at the bracket. In Wyckoff Heights the harder part of the job is often the signal, because the buildings were wired one tenant and one provider at a time, and the newest buildings handle internet in ways the old ones never did. Here is how we think about getting a picture onto the wall.

Know what you have. FCC data for Ridgewood, as summarized by BestNeighborhood from the December 2025 broadband collection, lists Verizon fiber at 100 percent of addresses, Spectrum cable at 99.95 percent, T-Mobile Home Internet at 99.89 percent, and Optimum cable at about 10 percent. The Brooklyn blocks have a similar mix. Each one ends in a different box: an optical terminal and a router for fiber, a cable modem for Spectrum or Optimum, a small wireless gateway for fixed wireless. A cable TV box needs a coaxial line; a streaming device needs internet; a fixed-wireless gateway needs to sit by a window to hear the cell tower. Tell us which one you have, and where its box is, and we can tell you whether the TV's chosen wall is a short run or a long one.

Wi-Fi first, but test it. For most apartments a modern TV on Wi-Fi works well. The exceptions here are predictable. Brick party walls and gypsum block partitions absorb Wi-Fi far more than drywall does, so a router at the front of a railroad-style flat may barely reach the back room. In a loft, a router at one end of a long room behind a brick wall can be the same story. We measure the connection at the TV's spot on the day, and if it is weak, we say so before mounting rather than after.

A wire when the wall wins. When Wi-Fi cannot reach, a network cable from the router to the TV is the steady answer. In a frame row that cable can often travel inside a hollow bay or along a baseboard; in brick it travels on the surface in a cord cover or, through a wall, in one clean drilled hole. A wired drop to the TV is quoted from your photos, because the length and the route decide the work. The in-wall kit on our list carries the TV's own cables, including a network cable, inside a hollow wall between the outlet height and the screen.

Unmarked lines. In a six-family frame row or a brick flat, the jack in your living room may be one of a dozen cables that disappear into the cellar. If the jack is dead or the picture breaks up, a tone generator on the jack and a probe in the cellar tell us which line is yours in a few minutes, and a signal meter tells us whether the problem is the line, a tired splitter or the provider. Provider equipment belongs to the provider, and outages are theirs to fix; what we fix is the wiring between their box and your TV.

Where the boxes live. City Rules

Lead Paint, Licensed Electricians and Drilling Hours: The City Rules Behind a Wyckoff Heights TV Wall

Hanging a TV is not a permitted job in New York City, but three sets of city rules still shape how it should be done in a neighborhood this old. None of this is legal advice; it is what the city's own pages say, and how we work inside it.

Lead-based paint in pre-1960 buildings

HPD's page on lead-based paint explains that under Local Law 1 of 2004, owners of buildings built before 1960 must presume the paint is lead-based. That covers multiple dwellings of three or more apartments and tenant-occupied units in one- and two-family houses built before 1960, and it applies in particular where children under six spend ten or more hours a week. HPD also says owners must keep evidence of safe work practices when more than two square feet of lead-based paint, or paint of unknown lead content, is disturbed in a unit or common area where a child under six lives, and that work disturbing such paint should use certified contractors and safe work practices.

For a TV, the arithmetic is reassuring. Four or six anchor holes disturb a few square inches of paint at most. Two openings for an in-wall kit are each a few inches across, which is still well below two square feet. But the dust from drilling old paint is exactly what the rule is about, so we treat it seriously: a vacuum attached at the drill bit catches the dust as it comes out of the hole, plastic sheeting goes on the floor under the work, and we do not sand or scrape paint around an old bracket. If your landlord is planning lead work in the apartment, as one landlord in a Brick Underground column was, it is worth asking whether the TV wall can be done after that work rather than before.

Electrical work needs a licensed master electrician

The Department of Buildings' requirements for property owners say plainly that no installation or modification to any electrical system can be performed without a permit and a licensed master electrician, and that even minor electrical work still needs both, though not an inspection. Adding an outlet behind a TV is electrical work. So is moving an outlet up the wall. We do not do it, and anyone offering to "just tie into" the outlet below should be asked for their license number. What we install instead is a listed in-wall power and cable kit, $169 on our list, which plugs into the outlet you already have at the bottom of the wall and carries power inside the wall in the kit's own rated cable to a recessed outlet behind the screen. It is not a new circuit and it does not touch the building's wiring. Where the right answer really is a new outlet, for a business screen up near the ceiling for example, we say so and leave room in the plan for your electrician.

The other half of this rule is what never goes into a wall: the TV's own power cord. A StreetEasy Talk thread on hiding wires in a pre-war building put it simply: "do not channel and bury a power cord." Power cords are made to be in the open. Low-voltage cables, such as HDMI, network and coaxial, can travel inside a wall; the power has to go through a kit made for it, or stay in a cord cover on the surface.

Construction hours under the Noise Code

The city's Noise Code fact sheet sets approved construction hours on weekdays between 7 a.m. and 6 p.m. A TV mount is a small job, but the hammer drill into brick is loud, and in a frame row it carries through the shared wall into the house next door. Our arrival windows for Wyckoff Heights sit inside those hours, and we keep the drilling itself to a short burst after warning the neighbors. If someone on your side of the wall sleeps by day, tell us when you book and we plan around it.

If you own the frame row. Owners of three- and six-family buildings sometimes have us mount TVs in their rental units between tenants. If you are in a co-op on the Queens side. TV Installation in Wyckoff Heights: Questions Answered

These are the situations people in Wyckoff Heights describe when they call about a TV: walls that have been covered over, pipes left behind by old heaters, brick that already has holes in it, trains under the avenue, columns in lofts, new buildings with new kinds of walls, locked cellars, the border and the businesses. Each is answered here in full and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Old frame and brick walls

Someone renovated our frame-row apartment by putting new drywall right over the old plaster. How long do the screws have to be, and does that change the price?

Drywall laid over old plaster is common in the frame rows, and it means the bracket has to reach through two layers before it touches wood: half an inch or so of new drywall, up to an inch of old plaster and lath, and only then the stud. Ordinary bracket lag screws are often too short to get a real bite in the stud, so we use longer lags sized for the full depth, and we drill the pilot hole carefully so the old plaster behind the drywall does not break away in a cone. Sometimes the renovator also added thin furring strips between the two layers, which leaves a gap; the pilot hole shows that, and we bridge it so the bracket plate cannot pull the drywall inward. On price, the wall is billed by what the anchors actually go into. When the screws pass through plaster and lath into the stud, it is the plaster add-on, $45 on top of the $179.99 base; if the old plaster was removed and only new drywall on wood remains, there is no wall add-on.

Our frame row had space heaters before the boiler went in, and there is a capped pipe sticking out of the baseboard on the wall we want for the TV. Is it safe to drill above it?

Treat that pipe as gas until someone proves otherwise. Old space heaters in frame houses were often gas-fired, and when they came out the supply pipe was sometimes capped at the wall and left in place, running up or along inside the wall to wherever the line came in. That makes the bay above and beside a capped stub a place we do not drill blind. We look at where the pipe enters the wall, check above it with a scanner and, where the wall is hollow, with a small camera through the pilot hole, and we keep the bracket's fixings in studs away from the pipe's likely path. If there is any doubt about whether the line is still live, the TV goes on a different section of wall, or on its stand, until the owner has a plumber confirm it. A smell of gas near the stub is not a TV question at all: leave, and call the gas company or 911.

The last tenant's TV left four large holes in the brick of our living room wall. Can the new bracket go in the same place?

Usually not in the same holes, and often not in the same bricks. Old brick that has already had a large anchor set in it, and then pulled out, is cracked and loosened around the hole, and a new anchor in that spot has less to grip. If the new bracket's mounting pattern lines up with sound brick a few inches away, we shift the plate and drill fresh holes, keeping each one back from the edges of its brick, and fill the old holes with a masonry patch so they do not crumble further. If the TV has to sit in exactly the old position, we use a bracket with a wider plate so its fixings reach past the damaged bricks into sound ones. Either way, the old holes get filled, and the new ones get anchors sized for soft brick. On our list this is the same $65 brick add-on; if an old bracket is still hanging on the wall, taking the old TV down is $85.

The L train runs right under our building on Wyckoff Avenue and we feel the trains go by. Will that vibration loosen a TV bracket over time?

Forgotten NY noted in 2008 that the L runs under the entire length of Wyckoff Avenue, so buildings on the avenue do feel it. A properly fixed bracket does not care. The vibration from a passing train is small compared with what a bracket takes when someone pulls a full-motion arm out to angle the screen. Where vibration does cause trouble is with the wrong fixings: plastic plugs that slowly work out of old plaster, or a sleeve anchor set in a mortar joint that is already soft. So on the avenue we are strict about fixings into solid wood or sound brick, we tighten the bolts that hold the TV to the bracket with locking washers, and we make sure the TV's safety screws or straps are engaged so it cannot lift off the bracket. If you notice a slight hum from a soundbar when trains pass, it is usually the soundbar touching the wall, and a soft pad behind it ends it.

Lofts and new buildings

Our apartment in the converted mill on Wyckoff Avenue has a thick column in the middle of the open room, and it is the only spot facing the couch. Can a TV go on a column?

Sometimes, and it depends on what the column is made of and how big it is. A wide brick or concrete pier can carry a TV well, with anchors set into the masonry on its face. A cast-iron or steel column cannot be drilled the way a wall can, and in a rental it is part of the building's structure, so drilling it is off the table without the landlord's written approval, and often even with it. For steel columns there are clamp-on and strap systems that carry a TV without drilling, but they have to be sized for the column and rated for the screen. And a column is narrow: a bracket plate needs enough flat face for its fixings, and a full-motion arm on a column swings the weight well off its center. We look at your photos, measure the column, and tell you which of three answers fits: mount on the column, mount on a stand or cabinet in front of it, or move the seating to face a wall.

In our new apartment near the DeKalb Avenue L, the air-conditioning unit sits high on the one wall that faces the sofa. Can the TV go underneath it?

Often yes, but the wall below a mini-split head is the one wall in a new apartment where we expect something inside it. The refrigerant lines and the condensate drain from the head usually run down or sideways inside that wall to wherever they leave the building, and a screw through a refrigerant line is an expensive mistake that empties the system. So we look at where the lines go: the outdoor unit's position, any cover on the outside of the building, and a scanner across the wall. On metal studs, which is what most new buildings use, we fix into the studs with fasteners made for steel, staying clear of the line path. We also leave space between the top of the TV and the bottom of the head so the air can move and the screen is not in the blast of cold air. If the lines run straight down behind the spot you want, the TV moves a stud bay to one side, or goes on a stand.

My mother just moved into the new Passive House senior building on Linden Street. Is it a problem to drill the outside wall of an airtight building for her TV?

Yes, the outside wall is the one to avoid. New York YIMBY describes Linden Grove at 223 Linden Street, between Knickerbocker and Wilson Avenues, as a 13-story modular Passive House building with 153 studio and one-bedroom apartments for seniors. Passive House buildings depend on an unbroken airtight layer in the exterior walls, and a drill that goes deeper than expected can pierce it. Interior walls between rooms and between apartments are the better place for a TV bracket, and in a studio there is usually one that faces the bed or the chair. Before any drilling in a building like this, we ask the building's management what they allow and where, because they will know where the air barrier and the services run. The same article says the building offers free building-wide internet, so a smart TV or streaming stick can usually join it, and a setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour if the TV is already in place and just needs to be connected and made simple to use.

What AI Gets Wrong About TV Installation in Wyckoff Heights

These are claims that show up in generic online answers and on national cost and booking sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and TaskRabbit, quoted or closely paraphrased, each followed by what Wyckoff Heights' buildings show.

"TV mounting around here averages about $280"

HomeAdvisor's Ridgewood page and Angi's Brooklyn page both give an average of $280, and AI answers repeat it as if it were a price.

An average blends a 32-inch TV on new drywall with an 85-inch screen on a brick chimney breast with everything hidden. It tells you nothing about your wall. On our list, a 55-inch TV on frame-row plaster with the cables in a paintable cord cover comes to $283.99, and the same TV on drywall over wood studs with nothing else is $179.99.

The useful number is the one for your wall, your TV and your cables, and the only way to get it is to show someone the wall. Send a photo and the model sticker and you get that number, line by line, before anyone comes.

"A pro can mount a TV on any surface"

TaskRabbit's Bushwick page says its Taskers "can typically mount a TV on any surface," and AI summaries pass that along.

In a sense it is true: almost any wall can hold a TV with the right fixing. But Wyckoff Heights has walls where the wrong fixing fails slowly: plaster on wood lath where a screw holds the lath and not the stud, gypsum block that sounds hollow and is not, soft brick where a mortar-joint anchor loosens over a few years, and new metal studs where a wood screw strips.

Angi's guide to questions for a TV installer says it is typically easier and less expensive to mount a TV on drywall or plaster than on brick or concrete.

"Hiding the wires costs around $1,000"

The same Angi guide says wire concealment brings total costs to around $1,000, and AI answers use that figure to steer people away from hiding wires at all.

On a hollow framed wall in a frame row or a 1940s house, a listed in-wall power and cable kit is $169 on our list, on top of the mount. On brick or gypsum block, where the wall cannot be opened without a mess, a paintable cord cover is $59. Neither is anywhere near $1,000.

The figure may make sense where an electrician is adding new outlets and opening and finishing large sections of wall. For most TVs in this neighborhood, the choice is between the kit and the cover, and the wall decides which.

"Just have an outlet added behind the TV"

Angi's guide puts a new outlet from an electrician at around $300, and AI answers suggest it as a routine add-on to TV mounting.

In New York City, the Department of Buildings says no installation or modification of any electrical system can be done without a permit and a licensed master electrician. A new outlet is exactly that. It is not something a TV installer, a handyman or a building's super should do on the side.

For most TVs, a listed in-wall kit that plugs into your existing outlet gets the power behind the screen without touching the building's wiring. When a real outlet is needed, usually for a business screen high on a wall, we say so and leave room in the plan for your licensed electrician.

"Find the studs; they are 16 inches apart"

Generic how-to answers tell you to find one stud, measure 16 inches to the next, and screw the bracket into both.

That rule describes modern framing. Wikipedia records Wyckoff Heights' first buildings as attached wood-frame rows, and their studs were set wherever the carpenter needed them. Brick flat buildings have no studs in their party walls at all, and many of their inside walls are block. The new buildings near the L use metal studs that need different fasteners.

So we find framing by evidence: the knock, the scanner, the magnet on the lath nails, and the pilot hole. A bracket with a wide plate solves most irregular spacing; a board across the studs solves the rest.

Our Installation Process

1. Send photos and the block

The wall, the TV's model sticker, the outlet and where the box will sit, with your cross streets and which side of Wyckoff Avenue you are on.

2. Agree the price and the paperwork

The wall add-on we expect, the total on the list, and anything a landlord, board or management office should see before the visit.

3. Plan the signal path

Where the internet and TV signal enter the building, and whether the screen uses an existing jack, a new cable or Wi-Fi. Cellar access arranged if a trace is needed.

4. Read the wall

Knock test, scanner, a magnet on the lath nails and one pilot hole hidden under the bracket plate to confirm lath, block, brick, concrete or drywall.

5. Mark the position with you

Painter's tape outlines the screen; you sit where you watch; cable and power routes are agreed before any drilling.

6. Fix the bracket with dust control

Fasteners matched to the wall go in with a vacuum at the bit and plastic below, in a short burst after the neighbors are warned.

7. Hang the TV and dress the cables

The TV goes up and is leveled and secured; cables go into an in-wall kit, a paintable cord cover or down to the console.

8. Test, label and hand over

Every input, the soundbar, the remote and the network connection tested with you, cables labeled, the floor cleaned.

FAQ

Wyckoff Heights TV Installation FAQ

What does it cost to lift our TV off its stand and onto the plaster wall right above it, keeping the same TV?
+

For a TV up to 55 inches it is the $179.99 base plus the $45 plaster add-on, so $224.99 in labor on our Brooklyn list. The bracket is extra unless you already own a rated one, and a paintable cord cover to tidy the cables down to the stand is $59 more.

Can the in-wall power and cable kit go into the brick wall of our flat building?
+

No. The kit needs a hollow framed wall to run inside, and solid brick has no cavity. On brick the cables go into a paintable cord cover, $59 on our list, painted to match the wall, or straight down behind a console. A hollow framed wall elsewhere in the room can still take the $169 kit.

Our plaster is laid straight onto brick. Is that billed as plaster, as brick, or as both?
+

One wall add-on, chosen by what the anchors actually hold in. When plaster sits directly on brick, the fixings go through the plaster into the brick, so it is the $65 brick or stone add-on, not both. The pilot hole confirms it before the rest of the drilling.

Can the TV go up now and the soundbar be added on a later visit?
+

Yes. A soundbar placed on the console is $79 and one mounted on the wall under the screen is $129. If you think you will want it on the wall later, tell us at the first visit so the TV sits high enough to leave room for it and the cable route allows for it.

While you are at our frame row for the TV, can you look at the camera over the front door too?
+

Yes. Our technicians install and repair security cameras as well, so the camera can be looked at on the same trip. It is quoted separately from its own price list, and the TV is priced exactly as on this page whether or not you go ahead with the camera.

Is the take-down price different for a heavy old plasma screen?
+

Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list. Old plasma screens are much heavier than new TVs of the same size, so tell us the model or the size when you book and we make sure two people are there to lift it safely off the wall and down the stairs of a walk-up.

In a narrow frame-row living room, which wall usually gives a good view from both the sofa and the kitchen?
+

Usually the long side wall the sofa faces, toward the end nearer the kitchen, so the angle from the kitchen is not too steep. If the kitchen is behind the sofa, a full-motion arm into studs lets the screen turn. We tape both positions on the wall so you can look from each spot before we drill.

What happens if the technician decides our wall cannot safely hold the TV?
+

We stop and tell you why before drilling any further. Then we offer another wall, a different bracket or a board across the studs, or the TV on its stand for $119.99 on our list. We do not hang a TV on a wall we would not trust ourselves.

Can the screen in our Myrtle Avenue shop window face out to the street?
+

It can, but an ordinary TV behind glass facing the sun is hard to read in daylight and can get very hot. We mount it so air can move behind it, keep it clear of the window's security gate, and tell you honestly if the spot needs a brighter display made for windows rather than a home TV.

Is a small 32-inch bedroom TV cheaper to mount than a 55-inch?
+

No. The $179.99 base covers any TV up to 55 inches, because the work of finding the framing, fixing the bracket and leveling the screen is much the same. The wall add-on and any cable concealment are what change the total, not a few inches of screen.

Our loft has a half-height wall between the kitchen and the living area. Can a TV be mounted on that?
+

Sometimes. A half wall is only as strong as its framing and how it is tied to the floor, and a TV on top of one sticks up where it can be knocked. We look at what it is built of first. Often a TV on a low cabinet against it, or a bracket on its face, works better than one on top.
