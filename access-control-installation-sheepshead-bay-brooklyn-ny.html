The Neighborhood

Why Sheepshead Bay Properties Need Access Control

Sheepshead Bay is a neighborhood of shared front doors. Between Ocean Avenue, Voorhies Avenue and Emmons Avenue the blocks are lined with six- and seven-story elevator buildings from around 1960, most of them co-ops or rentals, and between them sit walk-ups, two-family houses and, closer to the water, newer condos and one thirty-story tower. DataUSA’s 2024 figures for Community District 15, which covers Sheepshead Bay and eastern Gravesend, show about 152,500 residents, a median age of 42 and a homeownership rate of about 45 percent. In practice that means many households live behind a door they do not own outright and cannot rekey on their own: a co-op lobby, a landlord’s street door, a condo’s garage entrance. Whoever controls those doors controls how safe everybody behind them is.

The elevator buildings are where most of our Sheepshead Bay work starts, and they rarely have only one entrance. A typical 1960s building has a street door into a vestibule, a lobby door behind it, a door to the parking lot or the side yard, a cellar door used by the super and the porters, a compactor or refuse-room exit to the alley, a laundry room, a bike room, and a roof door that must never lock from the inside. CityRealty lists 3221 Emmons Avenue as a seven-story elevator co-op built in 1960 with a live-in super, and 1625 Emmons Avenue as a 148-apartment co-op from 1962. Buildings like these were wired for a buzzer and a brass key. Access control replaces the key on the doors that matter with credentials that can be cancelled the day someone moves, loses a fob or stops working for the building.

The newer buildings bring different doors. Avalon Brooklyn Bay at 1501 Voorhies Avenue, which CityRealty records as a 30-story building from 2017 with rentals on the lower floors and condos above, has an attended lobby with a revolving door, an on-site garage and amenity floors with a pool, a lounge and a fitness center. The Vue at 1809 Emmons Avenue is a seven-story condo from 2017 with on-site parking and a bike room. Here the question is not whether to have electronic entry but how the lobby, the garage, the amenity spaces and the residential floors should talk to each other, and who in a mixed building gets to decide.

Then there are the streets with shops. Sheepshead Bay Road is zoned for retail and lined with low-rise, family-run stores, many with apartments above. Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue have the same mix, and Emmons Avenue has the restaurants that face the piers. Theft is the steady problem rather than the dramatic one. The 61st Precinct, which covers Sheepshead Bay, Gravesend and Manhattan Beach, reported 541 grand larcenies in 2026 through November 8, up 7.3 percent on the same point in 2025, and 134 burglaries, up 3.1 percent. Those numbers are far below the 2,544 burglaries the precinct recorded in 1990, but they are not falling this year, and a door that stays locked and keeps a record does more for a small shop than another sign in the window.

Repairs First: When a Sheepshead Bay Door System Has Already Stopped Working

Not every call is a new system. If the fob reader in your lobby has gone dark, the strike on the parking-lot door buzzes but the door will not move, the street door releases and never relocks, or a keypad on a shop’s back door on Avenue U accepts every code but its own, ring (347) 934-8335 and describe what the door is doing, or text a short video of it opening and closing. A visit is booked within two hours of the call. In older Sheepshead Bay buildings the cause is usually the door before the electronics: a closer that leaks and no longer pulls the door shut, a keeper that has shifted in a worn steel frame, a power supply on a cellar shelf that got wet, or a controller that lost its list after a long outage. We work on systems other companies installed as well as our own, and we say plainly when a part is not worth repairing.

Access Control for Sheepshead Bay’s Architecture

The building tells us most of what the doors will need before anyone opens a catalog. A 1960s elevator co-op has steel frames, a long cable path from the cellar and a board to answer to. A walk-up has a narrow street door and a landlord who may live elsewhere. A new tower has a garage, a revolving door and several owners of different floors.

Six- and seven-story elevator buildings

The co-ops and rentals of about 1960 along Ocean Avenue, Voorhies Avenue and Emmons Avenue. Hollow steel frames take strikes well; the work is in the cable from the cellar, the number of side doors and getting a board to agree on one list.

Rent-stabilized walk-ups

Three- to five-story rentals on the side streets. Street doors are often original, closers are tired and the owner is off site, so the system has to run without a super on hand and follow the rules for stabilized tenants.

Waterfront condos and the tower

Buildings from the 2000s and 2010s on Emmons Avenue and Voorhies Avenue with garages, package rooms and amenity floors. Most already have some electronic entry; the work is extending it, replacing it or making it answer to the right owners.

Two-family and semi-detached houses

Brick and frame houses on the lettered avenues south of Avenue U, often with a shared front door, a side door to a basement unit and a driveway. Each household gets its own codes without the house becoming a building system.

Bungalow courts near Plumb Beach

Small houses on pedestrian-only lanes, built in the 1920s for summer use and many rebuilt after Sandy. Doors and gates are close together and cable is short, but the hardware has to suit salt air and water.

Stores with apartments above

Low-rise buildings on Sheepshead Bay Road, Avenue U and Nostrand Avenue, where a store’s back door and the residential street door share a wall and sometimes a hallway. The two are kept on separate lists.

What the materials mean for the install

Most Sheepshead Bay elevator buildings were built with hollow steel door frames set in brick and block. A fail-secure strike fits those frames without cutting the brick, and cable can usually be fished from the cellar up inside the frame or along the lobby ceiling. Lobby walls are often stone or tile, so we route cable from above or below instead of chasing it into the finish, and we mount readers on the frame or on a small plate rather than drilling a marble wall. Where a building has an aluminum-and-glass storefront-style lobby door, we use a strike or lock made for narrow stiles, not a heavier part forced into a profile it was not made for.

Walk-ups and houses are a mix of wood and steel doors in wood or steel frames. A wood frame needs a strike screwed into the framing behind the jamb, not just the trim, or the first hard pull will tear it out. In the bungalow courts and on the blocks nearest the water, strikes, keypads and fasteners should be rated for salt air, and anything electronic sits high and inside. Bigger, newer buildings usually have a low-voltage room already; there the job is to work inside what the developer built and leave clean records for the board that takes over.

Side Doors Before Front Doors: Parking Lots, Cellars and Compactor Exits in 1960s Elevator Buildings

Ask a Sheepshead Bay board which door to put on fobs and the answer is always the front door. Then watch the building for an evening. In many of the elevator buildings between Ocean Avenue and Emmons Avenue, residents who drive come and go through the door from the rear parking lot, porters move garbage through a refuse-room exit to the alley, the super’s staff use the cellar door, and couriers try whichever door is propped. The front door is the one with the directory panel and the brass plate. It is often not the one that lets strangers in.

So our survey of a Sheepshead Bay elevator building starts with a list of every opening, not a price for the lobby. For each one we write down how it opens today, who holds a key, whether it closes and latches by itself, and whether it is an exit that must open from inside at all times. On a typical six-story building of about 1960 that list runs to eight or ten doors: street door, vestibule or lobby door, parking-lot door, cellar door, compactor or refuse exit, laundry, bike or storage room, the super’s workshop, a package room if one was added later, and the roof door. Some of those deserve a reader. Others only need a good closer, a contact that reports when they are held open and a lock that stays locked.

The order matters because budgets are real. A board that fobs the lobby and leaves the parking-lot door on the original key has spent money on the door nobody uses after dark. Our usual advice is a reader on the street door and on whichever side door carries the most traffic, contacts and alarms on the doors that should almost never open, and the rest of the side doors added to the same controller in a second phase. We price both phases at the start, from the Brooklyn list, so the board knows the full cost before it votes on the first.

A door that does not latch makes the reader pointless

Electronic locks fail in a very ordinary way: the door stops closing. When the New York City Comptroller’s staff visited 262 NYCHA developments in 2022, they found 36.8 percent of residential entrance doors open and 40.1 percent of entrance locks broken, and reported “numerous instances where doors were propped open with bricks, rocks, or other objects.” Those are public housing buildings, not the private co-ops this page is mostly about, and nobody should read the figures as a description of the Sheepshead Bay Houses or the Nostrand Houses in particular. But the lesson carries to any building. A strike only holds a door that has swung shut and caught. So before any reader goes on, we adjust or replace the closer, check the hinges, line up the keeper with the latch and set the door to close fully against the stop, in summer and in winter. Then we put a door-position contact on it, so a door wedged open for a delivery raises an alert instead of staying open all night.

From Brass Keys to Fobs in a Sheepshead Bay Rental Building

Many of the buildings on the side streets off Ocean Avenue and Nostrand Avenue are rentals, and a good number are rent-stabilized. Changing their street doors from metal keys to fobs is common, but it is not a decision an owner makes alone, and doing it carelessly has led to complaints, rulings and, in one Queens case, a settlement with the state Attorney General. We do not give legal advice, and an owner should run the plan past a lawyer or the managing agent. What we can do is build a system that meets the conditions owners have been held to, and hand over the paperwork that goes with it.

The clearest picture of those conditions comes from a 2016 DHCR ruling, docket DR430034RT, summarized by the landlord-tenant journal LVT. DHCR allowed the landlord to replace metal keys with a fob or card system with no change in the legal rent, but only with conditions attached. Among them: metal keys still had to work for emergency access; the system could record entries but not departures; a camera had to cover the entry; management had to help tenants during office hours and give them a number to reach the super after hours; observers who cannot use electronic devices on the Sabbath had to be accommodated; the system needed a 48-hour battery backup; occupants, children included, got fobs free, plus up to four free fobs for guests or employees; replacements could cost no more than $25; and the tenant’s name, address and photo could not appear on the fob.

That ruling was about one building, and other cases may come out differently, but it is a sensible design brief. In practice it means a strike that stays locked when power fails but a key cylinder that still opens it, a controller and battery sized to keep the door working for two days, a camera on the entry, a published phone number for lockouts, and a rollout plan that hands every lawful occupant a credential without asking for more personal data than the city allows.

The city’s tenant data rules

Since Local Law 63 of 2021, owners of multiple dwellings that use keyless entry must give tenants a written data retention and privacy policy, collect only the minimum data needed, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless it is anonymized, never sell it, and never use it to track tenants’ whereabouts outside the building, to harass them or to support an eviction, according to HPD’s page on the Tenant Data Privacy Law. Biometric identifiers are covered too. We set up the system so it can meet those limits out of the box: short retention on entry records, no tracking of who visits whom, administrator access limited to the people the owner names, and an export of the settings the owner can attach to the policy tenants receive.

What the Multiple Dwelling Law expects at the entrance

Multiple Dwelling Law section 50-a requires self-closing, self-locking entrance doors in class A multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968, kept locked except when an attendant is on duty, and an intercom with voice communication and a door release at the main entrance of buildings with eight or more apartments. Older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants ask in writing. The roof door must be self-closing but not self-locking, and must open from inside without a key. A fob reader adds to those requirements; it does not replace them. Visitors still need to reach apartments, so we keep or replace the intercom along with the reader, and the roof door never goes on a lock that could trap someone on the stair.

Weekend Nights by the Piers: Doors on Emmons Avenue

Emmons Avenue is two places. On a weekday morning it is a waterfront street of co-ops, condos and restaurants facing the fishing boats. On a summer Saturday night it is a crowd. NYC Parks records ten Sheepshead Bay Piers along Emmons Avenue between Ocean Avenue and East 26th Street, built by the city in 1936 and run by Parks since 1994. In 2017 Bklyner reported about 20 party vessels docked there and more than 3,000 people gathering on the docks, streets and parking areas between cruises, with noise past 3 a.m. Peter Katsichtis of Yiasou, a restaurant on the avenue, told the paper: “The area used to be a fishing port, it’s too small to handle this many people every weekend.”

That rhythm changes what a building door on Emmons Avenue should do. A street door that is fine left unlocked into a vestibule at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday is a shelter for strangers at 2 a.m. on a Sunday. A restaurant back door that staff use every few minutes during service needs to relock the moment the last cook leaves, not when someone remembers. A parking area behind a co-op that fills with cars that do not belong to residents is a place where a held-open side door goes unnoticed. None of this needs exotic hardware. It needs schedules that follow the street’s week, and alerts that go to someone who will act on them.

For residential buildings along the avenue we usually set the outer door to lock to fobs and codes only after a set hour on weekend nights, keep the intercom working at all hours so guests can still call up, and send a held-open alert for the parking-lot and side doors to the super’s phone. For restaurants we set the back door to accept staff codes only during their shift, add a contact that tells the owner if the door is still open after closing, and put a camera over it so every alert comes with a picture. The weekday schedule stays relaxed; the weekend schedule tightens on its own.

Salt air and water along the bay

Doors that face the bay live in salt air every day, and the blocks nearest the water flooded in Sandy. Brownstoner reported that the Special Sheepshead Bay District, which runs along Emmons Avenue, “was particularly devastated by Hurricane Sandy.” For these buildings we choose stainless or coated strikes and fasteners, sealed readers, and cable runs that do not sit in a low point where water collects. The controller, the battery and the power supply go above the cellar floor, in a dry locked room on the ground floor if the building has one, so a wet cellar takes out a pump or two and not the front door.

Towers, Garages and Amenity Floors on Voorhies Avenue and Emmons Avenue

The newest buildings in Sheepshead Bay already have electronic entry of some kind, so the calls we get from them are about gaps and changes rather than first installs. A garage gate that opens for any tag the developer ever issued. A pool deck whose fob list nobody has pruned since the first summer. A package room that still opens with a code everybody in the building knows. A board that has just taken over from a sponsor and finds the system’s login belongs to someone who no longer works for the developer.

Mixed buildings add a question of ownership. CityRealty describes Avalon Brooklyn Bay at 1501 Voorhies Avenue as 30 stories, with rentals on the lower 11 floors and condos on the upper 19, an attended lobby with a revolving door and an on-site garage; leasing materials list a 19th-floor lounge, an outdoor pool, a fitness center, a children’s play area and a dog run. In a building like that the lobby, the garage and the amenities serve both the rental owner’s tenants and the condo owners, but the two sides have different managers, different budgets and different rules. Before we change anything we find out who owns which doors and which parts of the system, who may add or remove a resident, and who sees the records. We work for whoever is responsible for the doors we touch, and we do not change shared settings without the other side’s sign-off.

The technical work in these buildings is usually extending the existing platform rather than replacing it. Many developers install a cloud-managed system with readers at the lobby, garage and amenity doors and elevator floor limits. If it is a platform we support, we add doors, readers or cameras to it; if it is not, or if its subscription has become the problem, we survey it and write down what could stay and what a replacement would cost, door by door, so the board can compare before it commits.

Sheepshead Bay’s businesses are mostly small and mostly family-run, spread along a handful of streets. Brooklyn Paper described Sheepshead Bay Road as lined with low-rise, mom-and-pop shops under pressure from rising rents, quoting one resident: “People are going out of business if they don’t own their building.” For a shop like that, access control has to be modest, cheap to run and simple enough for the owner to manage from a phone.

Sheepshead Bay Road

Stores, restaurants and services from the station down toward Emmons Avenue, in retail-zoned low-rise buildings, many with apartments above. Back doors open onto narrow yards and alleys, and the store and the apartments upstairs often share a wall.

Emmons Avenue

Restaurants and businesses facing the piers, busiest on summer weekends. Kitchen doors that cooks use all night, storage rooms across the street from the boats, and offices above the dining rooms.

Avenue U

A long commercial avenue with groceries, pharmacies, medical and dental offices and services. Ground-floor suites often share a building entrance with apartments or other offices.

Nostrand Avenue

Stores and offices along the eastern side of the neighborhood near the Sheepshead Bay Houses and the Nostrand Houses, with side doors onto residential streets and rear lots.

Houses of worship and community rooms

Congregations and community centers where a hall is open to the public at set times while offices, coat rooms and storage behind it should not be. News 12 reported a man walking into a Voorhies Avenue mosque during prayer in 2025 and leaving with a wallet from a jacket.

Offices and clinics

Medical, dental, legal and accounting offices on Avenue U, Ocean Avenue and Kings Highway, where the waiting room is open and the records room, the medication cabinet and the staff door are not.

The hour before opening

Store burglaries in the neighborhood tend to happen when nobody is there to see. News 12 reported that My House Burgers and Shake on Sheepshead Bay Road was broken into around 6 a.m. on January 22, 2025, and that about $10,000, a MacBook Pro and an iPhone were taken; the report did not say how the burglar got in. In December 2025 two people entered a Sheepshead Bay jewelry store through a third-floor window at about 4:30 a.m., according to News 12. A shift manager at the burger shop put it simply: “It can happen to any store any business.”

An access system will not stop someone who comes through a window, and we do not pretend it will. What it does is make the quiet hours visible. With a contact on every outside door, the back door, the stockroom and the door from upstairs, the owner learns within seconds that a door opened at 5:40 a.m. when the first shift starts at 7. With a camera over the back door, that alert comes with a picture. With personal codes for each employee, the owner can see who opened up, who closed, and whether anyone came back after closing. And with the stockroom and office on their own readers, someone who gets into the sales floor still faces another locked door before the cash and the stock.

Rules that apply to Sheepshead Bay business doors

  • Exits always open. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door on an exit path must have a sensor on the egress side that releases it, must unlock on loss of power and when the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and must have a push-to-exit button 40 to 48 inches high within five feet of the door that cuts power to the lock for at least 30 seconds. A magnetic lock must be released by hardware on the door that works with one hand. A store, restaurant or hall exit always opens from inside without a key, code or card.
  • Listed equipment. Controllers and locks on exit doors should be listed to UL 294, the access control standard the code refers to, and installed to the maker’s instructions.
  • Fire alarm tie-in. Where a building has a fire alarm system, electrically locked egress doors are released by it. We coordinate with the building’s fire alarm contractor and do not alter the fire alarm ourselves.
  • Apartments above stores. Where a store and apartments share a building, the residential entrance follows the Multiple Dwelling Law and the city’s tenant data rules; the store’s doors do not. We keep the two on separate lists and, where possible, separate controllers.
  • Leased space. A tenant business usually needs the landlord’s written permission before changing locks or doors. We ask for it before work starts, and we leave the landlord a key path if the lease requires one.
  • Power. Lock power supplies are low-voltage, but they need an outlet or circuit. Where a new circuit is needed, a licensed electrician provides it; we do not do electrical work.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Sheepshead Bay: Questions Answered

Elevator co-ops and rentals between Ocean Avenue and Emmons Avenue

Most of our residents come in through the door from the rear parking lot, not the front entrance. Should the first reader go on the door people actually use?

Usually yes, alongside the front door rather than instead of it. The front door has to stay secure because it carries the intercom and the visitors, but if three quarters of the traffic after dark comes through the lot, the lot door is where a propped door or a copied key does the most harm. In a first phase we often put readers on both and a contact and alarm on every other outside door, so nothing is left on an uncounted key.

The lot door also tends to be the one in the worst shape: a lighter door, a tired closer and a frame that has taken years of shopping carts. We fix that first, then add the strike and the reader, and we add a camera view of the lot side so a held-open alert can be checked from a phone.

Our porters wedge the refuse-room door to the alley open on collection nights. Does a door used like that belong on the fob system?

It belongs on the system, but probably not with a reader. A door that is opened from inside by staff a few times a week needs to close and lock by itself, report when it is left open and tell the super if it is still open after a set time. That is a closer, a strike or lock that stays locked from outside, a door contact and a held-open alert. A reader on the outside is only worth adding if staff regularly come back in through it.

The habit of wedging it is the real problem. We set the alert to wait long enough for a normal collection run and then message the super, and we can add a hold-open that releases on its own after a set time, so the porters have a legitimate way to keep it open for ten minutes instead of a brick.

The lobby door has to pull shut hard enough to latch, but several shareholders use walkers. How do we set it so it locks every time without fighting them?

The answer is the closer, not the lock. A good hydraulic closer can be set to open with little force, to close slowly through most of its swing and then speed up for the last few inches so the latch catches. That latching speed is what makes the strike hold; the rest of the swing can be gentle. On heavier doors we also look at a low-energy power operator with a push plate, which opens the door for anyone who needs it and still closes and locks behind them.

Emmons Avenue, the piers and late nights

People leaving the late cruises sit in our building’s outer vestibule on summer weekends. Should the outer door lock too, or only the inner one?

If the vestibule is being used as a shelter, the outer door should lock at night. Many Emmons Avenue buildings leave the street door unlocked so visitors can reach the intercom inside, and lock only the inner door. That works on a quiet street. On weekend nights near the piers it gives strangers a warm, lit room with the residents’ mailboxes in it.

The fix is to move the visitor call point outside, onto a panel or station beside the street door, and put the outer door on a schedule: open to everyone during the day if the board wants, locked to fobs and codes after a set hour on Friday and Saturday nights. Visitors still call up from the panel, residents still tap in, and the inner door stays locked as before.

We run a charter boat from the Emmons Avenue piers and keep gear in a rented storefront across the street. Can crew get in before dawn on sailing days only?

Yes. Each crew member gets a personal code or fob with a schedule built around your trips, for example 3:30 a.m. to 9 a.m. on the days you sail, and nothing on other days unless you add it. A crew member who leaves at the end of the season simply has the code expire, and if someone needs access for one morning you can add a one-time code from your phone.

Because the storefront is leased, we ask for the landlord’s written permission first and leave a mechanical key path the landlord can use. Near the water we use a sealed keypad and a coated strike, and we add a contact so you get a message if the door is opened when no trip is scheduled.

Our Emmons Avenue restaurant closes after the last boats come in. Can the system confirm every door is shut and locked before the closing manager walks away?

It can, if each door has a contact and the lock reports its state. At closing the manager’s phone or a small panel by the exit shows each door as closed or open, and the system sends the owner an alert if any door is still open a set time after the last staff code was used. The back door relocks on its own as soon as it closes, so “forgot to lock it” stops being possible.

Sheepshead Bay Road and Avenue U storefronts

After the 6 a.m. burglary on Sheepshead Bay Road, can our back door warn me if it opens before anyone is scheduled to work?

Yes. Each staff code carries a schedule, and the door has a contact. If the door opens with no valid code, or opens with a code outside its hours, the system sends an alert to your phone at once, and a camera over the door can attach a picture. You can choose whether an out-of-hours code is refused outright or allowed and reported.

It is worth being clear about the limit. An alert tells you something is happening; it does not stop a determined burglar who forces a door or a window. Paired with an alarm that calls a monitoring station, it gets someone there sooner. On its own it gives you an exact time and a picture, which is what the police ask for first.

We take on extra staff for the summer and let most of them go in September. Is a code or a fob easier to retire when a seasonal worker leaves?

A code, in most small shops. A code costs nothing to issue, can be set to expire on a date when the person is hired, and cannot be lost in a way that matters once it is cancelled. A fob has to be collected or cancelled, and a fob that goes home with a former employee is useless only if someone remembers to delete it. Codes do have one weakness: they can be shared. Personal codes, with each person told that the log shows whose code opened the door, keep sharing rare.

The floors above our shop are empty storage reached by an inside stair. After the jewelry store break-in through an upper window, should the stair door into the shop be on the system?

Yes, as a door that should almost never open. Upper floors that nobody occupies are where a break-in can start unseen, and the inside stair is how it reaches the shop. A solid door at the bottom of the stair, locked from the stair side, with a contact that alerts the owner whenever it opens, turns the empty floors into a space that has to be broken out of rather than a free way in. If staff use the storage, they get a code on that door with their own hours.

The windows upstairs need their own answer, usually from an alarm company or a window guard, and we say so on the quote. The door system covers the door; we do not sell it as protection for the windows.

Towers and newer condos

Our lobby entrance is a revolving door with a swing door beside it. Which one gets the reader?

Almost always the swing door. A revolving door can be motorized and locked at night, but putting a reader on it is a specialist job with the door’s own manufacturer, and many buildings simply lock the revolving door after hours and route residents through the swing door, which also serves wheelchairs, strollers and deliveries. We put the reader and the intercom on the swing door and a contact on the revolving door’s night lock, so the desk or the super knows if it was left unlocked.

If the lobby is attended part of the day, the swing door can stay on free entry while the desk is staffed and switch to fobs only when it is not, on a schedule or from a switch at the desk.

Our building has rental floors under condo floors, managed by different companies. Can each side keep its own list of residents on one lobby reader?

Yes. One controller can hold separate groups with separate administrators: the rental manager adds and removes tenants, the condo’s managing agent adds and removes owners and their household, and neither can see or change the other’s list. Shared doors, the lobby, the garage and the amenities, accept both groups; private doors and elevator floors accept only their own.

What has to be agreed in writing is who owns the controller and the shared readers, who pays for repairs and the subscription if there is one, and whose privacy policy covers which residents. Rental tenants fall under the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law; we set the system up so both sides can meet it.

The pool closes after Labor Day. Can the pool gate stop accepting residents’ fobs for the winter by itself and start again in June?

Yes. The pool door gets a calendar: residents’ fobs work during posted hours between opening day and closing day and are refused the rest of the year, while the building’s staff and the pool contractor keep access for maintenance. Nobody has to remember to switch it off, and nobody has to collect pool fobs, because the pool permission sits on each resident’s regular fob.

The same approach works for a roof deck with seasonal furniture or a courtyard grill area. Each rule is written into the handover notes, so a new board knows the dates are set and where to change them.

Houses and two-families south of Avenue U

Our detached garage sits at the back of the lot, across a shared driveway from the house. Can its side door go on the house system without digging up the driveway?

Usually. A battery-powered lock or reader on the garage door can join the house system over a wireless link, so the family uses the same codes at both doors and the owner sees both in one app. Where the garage has power, a small outdoor-rated wireless bridge can carry a wired reader’s signal across without a trench. Digging is only needed if the garage needs a hard-wired lock and there is no power out there.

Because the driveway is shared, the garage door’s camera, if one is added, is aimed at your own door and not at the neighbor’s side of the drive. We check the wireless signal on the first visit before quoting either option.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Sheepshead Bay

These are generalizations we see repeated in quick online answers, consumer articles and national cost guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Sheepshead Bay door actually needs. We do not attribute a specific claim to any of those sources unless we quote it.

“A fob system is a lobby upgrade.”

Quick answers describe access control as a reader on the front door. In a Sheepshead Bay elevator building the front door is one of eight or ten, and often not the busiest after dark. The parking-lot door, the cellar, the refuse exit and the laundry are where copied keys and propped doors live.

A plan that covers only the lobby leaves the building about as open as it was, with a new expense on top. The useful plan lists every door, decides which get readers and which get contacts and closers, and phases the work so the busiest side door is done with the lobby.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

That is the price of a lock in a box. Industry cost guides put a wired door far higher; Swiftlane’s guide, for example, gives “$1,500 to $5,000 per door installed for standard access points,” including reader, lock, controller and labor. Our Brooklyn list starts a single controlled door at $1,500 to $2,500 with ten fobs.

The cost in an older Sheepshead Bay building is mostly labor: fishing cable from the cellar through a 1960s building, fixing a closer that no longer latches, working around a stone lobby. Those costs do not show up in a national average.

“Switching tenants to fobs is the landlord’s decision.”

In a rent-stabilized building, it is the landlord’s decision with conditions. DHCR has allowed owners to replace keys with fobs, but in the 2016 ruling summarized by LVT it required, among other things, working metal keys for emergencies, free fobs for every occupant, a cap on replacement fees, a 48-hour battery and accommodation for Sabbath observers.

On top of that, the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law limits what an owner may collect and how long it may be kept. Quick answers written for the whole country do not mention either.

“Fail-safe locks are the safe choice.”

The words sound reassuring. A fail-safe lock unlocks when power is lost, which is right for some interior doors and for exit paths that must release. On a Sheepshead Bay street door it means the building stands open through every outage, including the long ones that come with coastal storms.

Street doors and most outside doors get fail-secure strikes, which stay locked without power and still open from inside with the handle. The code’s egress rules are met by the free-exit side, not by unlocking the outside.

“Put the controller in the basement, out of the way.”

That is the default in installation guides written for most of the country, and the worst place for it in a neighborhood where many cellars took water in Sandy. A controller on a cellar shelf can go dark the night it is needed most.

We put controllers and batteries above the cellar floor, in a locked closet on the ground floor or in the lobby ceiling space where the building allows, with only cable and contacts below.

“Any fob system is more secure than keys.”

A fob system is only as secure as its credentials. Older 125 kHz fobs carry no encryption, and copies can be made with cheap tools sold online. A building that moved to fobs years ago may have replaced copied keys with copied fobs.

We install readers that use encrypted credentials and can be set to refuse the old kind. If a building has a large stock of old fobs, we can run both for a short changeover and then cut the old ones off on a set date.

“Access control stops burglaries.”

It does not, on its own, and saying so would be dishonest. The 61st Precinct recorded 134 burglaries in 2026 through November 8. Some came through windows, like the jewelry store entered on its third floor in December 2025.

What access control does is narrow who can open the doors, keep a record of when they opened, and raise an alert when a door is forced or held open. Combined with cameras and an alarm, that shortens the time between a break-in and a response.

Does a waterfront building on Emmons Avenue pay more for access control than a house near Avenue X?
+

How many fobs come with a single controlled door from your Brooklyn list?
+

Ten programmed fobs are included at the bottom of the single-door range. A building or business that needs more has them listed on the written quote, and later additions are enrolled on the same controller, so new fobs work on every door the person is allowed to use.

Can our high-security mechanical cylinder stay on the street door after the strike goes in?
+

Usually, yes. A strike works with the existing latch and leaves the lock’s cylinder in place, so the restricted key the board already controls becomes the emergency override. If the cylinder is worn or the key has been copied widely, we recommend replacing it with a new restricted keyway held by only a few people.

Is there a pending New York bill that would change how building fobs and smart entry work?
+

Yes. Senate bill S955, with Assembly companion A156, would require a fob, card or code option alongside any app, free keys, backup power or another way in during outages, non-electronic entry on request for religious reasons, and a three-year pause on new biometric systems. It passed the Senate in May 2025 and was in committee as of June 2026. It is not law.

Does the Special Sheepshead Bay District say anything about readers or keypads on a facade?
+

Not that we have found. City Planning describes the district, created in 1973 along Emmons Avenue, as rules for building height, bulk and waterfront character. Small door hardware is not a zoning matter in what we have read, but a building that is also changing its entrance or storefront should check with its architect or expediter.

Can each fob entry at our lobby be matched to a picture from the lobby camera?
+

Often, yes. When the access system and the camera recorder can be linked, each entry or alert can carry a snapshot or a bookmark in the recording. When they cannot, the timestamps still line up for anyone who needs to check. Either way, the privacy policy for a rental building should say how long both records are kept.

How far ahead should an Emmons Avenue co-op book work it wants finished before the summer?
+

Start the survey in winter. A board usually needs a meeting to approve the plan, the managing agent needs the insurance paperwork, and special-order hardware can take weeks to arrive. Equipment is ordered only after the deposit, and the installation is scheduled once it is in, so starting early is what puts the doors in place before the busy months.

If a resident leaves the fob at home, can they get in with a phone instead?
+

If the building chooses to offer phone keys, yes. Many systems let one person hold a fob, a code and a phone key together. We still recommend a fob or code for everyone, because a phone with a dead battery or no signal should not be the only way into the building.

Can firefighters still get through a lobby door that is on a fob system?
+

Yes. Exit doors open from inside without a credential, electrically locked egress doors release when the fire alarm activates where the building has one, and the street door keeps a mechanical key path that the super and the board hold. We do not change a building’s fire alarm; where one exists, its contractor makes the release connection.

Will the strike on our lobby door be loud enough to bother the apartment beside the lobby at night?
+

Strikes make a short click or hum when they release, and the slam of a heavy door closing is usually louder. Where a bedroom shares the lobby wall, we choose a quieter strike, set the closer to finish gently while still latching, and mount the hardware on the frame rather than on the shared wall.

Can the laundry room’s payment card machines run on the same fobs as the doors?
+

Usually not. Laundry payment systems belong to the laundry operator and use their own cards or apps. What we can do is put the laundry room door on the fob system with its posted hours, so only residents reach the machines, and leave the payment side to the operator’s contract.

Do you need the managing agent’s approval before the first visit or only before installation?
+

Only before installation. Anyone responsible for the building can book the survey and receive the written plan and price. Before work begins we need written approval from whoever controls the doors, the board or managing agent for a co-op or condo, the owner for a rental, or the landlord for a leased storefront.
