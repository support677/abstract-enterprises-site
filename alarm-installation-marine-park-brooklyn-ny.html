The Neighborhood

Why Marine Park Properties Need Alarm Installation

Marine Park is easy to describe and easy to underestimate. Wikipedia’s article on the neighborhood places it between Flatlands and Mill Basin to the east and Gerritsen Beach, Midwood and Sheepshead Bay to the south and west, and says it is mostly bounded by Gerritsen Avenue, Flatbush Avenue, Avenue U and Kings Highway. Brick Underground’s July 2022 neighborhood guide, written by Nancy A. Ruhling, describes the housing in one sentence that matters more to an alarm installer than any other: most homes are “one- and two-family detached and semi-detached residences that date from the 1910s to the 1940s.” The Douglas Elliman neighborhood guide puts most of them in the 1920s and 1930s. Either way, these are houses with four sides, or three sides and a shared wall, with yards, side paths, driveways and back doors. That is different from the attached brownstone row of north Brooklyn, where the front door is the only way in from the street.

The same Brick Underground guide notes that there are no rental apartment buildings in the neighborhood; renters live in two-family houses or on the upper floors of low-rise mixed-use buildings. Co-op buildings sit along the southern and western edges, and a few small low-rise condominium buildings stand near the middle. Single-family frame houses and duplexes are typically around 1,100 square feet; the larger brick houses run from 1,200 to 2,200; two-family houses from 1,300 to 2,000. The listings the guide describes include finished basements, backyards, a covered front porch with a large back porch, a carport, a detached garage, a private driveway for four cars and a shared driveway. Every one of those features is an opening, a hiding place or a structure an alarm has to account for.

Then there is who lives here and how they move. Marine Park has no subway station; Wikipedia lists the nearest as Kings Highway on the Brighton Line in Midwood, and Brick Underground says a typical bus ride to a subway takes about ten minutes. Wikipedia lists more than a dozen bus routes, counting the Select Bus versions separately, from the B2 and B3 to the BM4 express and the Q35, and the Belt Parkway is reached from the Flatbush Avenue exit. The Elliman guide says a car is close to indispensable. Brick Underground adds that many residents work for the city as police officers, firefighters and teachers. Put those facts together and you get households whose houses are empty in long, predictable blocks: early tours that start before dawn, school days, the commute by bus and car. A house that is empty at predictable hours is a house whose doorbell can be used to check whether anyone is home.

That is exactly what the local record shows. In March 2010 the Brooklyn Paper reported two men posing as water meter readers who knocked at a house on East 36th Street near Quentin Road at 2:40 in the afternoon, “claiming that they were there to check the meter.” When they grew impatient, one forced open a window and crawled inside, not knowing the 62-year-old homeowner was there, then let his partner in; they left with a safe holding important papers. In March 2012 the same paper described a woman calling Marine Park seniors as a National Grid operator to book boiler and furnace inspections, after which two men arrived in a van marked with the name of an agency that does not exist, removed and replaced a small part of the furnace and charged for a repair; an 89-year-old woman paid $1,094, at least four homes were targeted, and one man was arrested inside a Marine Park home. In June 2016 the paper’s 63rd Precinct report described a man who rang a woman’s doorbell repeatedly on Troy Avenue near Avenue L, at the Flatlands side of the precinct, then went into the back yard and put his head through her kitchen window before she screamed and scared him off.

Not every incident starts with a knock, but most of the ones we found happened in daylight and at the sides or back of a house. In August 2010 a fifteen-year-old used a gardening tool to pry open a back window of a house on Kimball Street between Avenue P and Quentin Road at noon; a witness saw him go in and called 911, and officers from the precinct’s anti-crime squad caught him leaving. In August 2019 someone walked into the back yard of a house on East 28th Street near Quentin Road at 2 p.m., cut the lock on the shed and left with power tools and a bike. News 12 reported four people entering a house at East 34th Street and Avenue N at about 4:30 in the afternoon on a February 23 and leaving with two smart TVs; the item gives no year. And in October 2018 three people broke into a house on Madison Place at 5:30 in the morning and attacked a retired police lieutenant and his wife, 72 and 71, before driving off in the couple’s car; two people were arrested within a day, and News 12 reported the third arrest the following month.

Two pieces of police advice given at Marine Park Civic Association meetings frame the rest of this page. In September 2008, at a meeting at P.S. 207 on Fillmore Avenue, a 63rd Precinct sergeant called lighting “the first and foremost thing to prevent crime from happening” and recommended motion-sensor lights at the front and back of the house. In April 2009, the precinct told the association’s members not to open the door to an unexpected utility or delivery worker but to call the company or 911 first: “Don’t open the door.” An alarm cannot make that decision for you. It can make it much easier to keep: by letting you see and speak to whoever is at the door without unlocking it, by keeping the back and sides of the house armed while you deal with the front, and by telling family members on their phones when an older parent’s door opens in the middle of a weekday.

The numbers deserve proportion. Wikipedia reports that the 63rd Precinct, which covers Marine Park, Bergen Beach, Mill Basin and part of Flatlands, recorded 119 burglaries and 537 grand larcenies in 2022, and that crime in all categories fell 82.7 percent between 1990 and 2022. In 2009 the precinct told the civic association that only 46 of the 367 felonies reported that year up to April 21, about one in eight, happened in the sector that includes Marine Park, and that most local grand larcenies were car break-ins. Marine Park is not a high-crime neighborhood. It is a neighborhood where the incidents that do happen tend to share a shape, and an alarm designed around that shape costs no more than one designed around nothing in particular.

Most Marine Park owners who ask about an alarm also ask about a camera over the back yard or the side path. Both are handled on one visit; our Marine Park security camera installation page covers the camera side, and most detached-house owners here pair the alarm with a doorbell camera and one rear camera.

What We Install

Alarm Installation Services in Marine Park

Nine services cover nearly every Marine Park call we get. Each is designed around how a detached or semi-detached house, a two-family or a small shop is actually entered, and all of them are priced from the same Brooklyn list.

Doorstep-first home alarms

Every design starts at the door visitors actually use. A video doorbell or door station that works in the same app as the alarm, a front-door contact with a short entry delay, and a stay mode that keeps every other opening armed while you are at the front of the house dealing with someone you did not expect.

Back and side protection for detached houses

Contacts and glass-break detection for the openings neighbors cannot see: the windows down the driveway side, the kitchen window over the back yard, the back porch door and the basement windows at ground level. The front of a Marine Park house is watched by the whole block; the side and back are watched by nobody.

Cellar and boiler-room zones

A separate zone for the stairs between the basement and the main floor, so a worker you let into the boiler room can be there without the rest of the house going unprotected, and so you know at once if anyone comes up the stairs who should not.

Shed, garage and carport sensors

Wireless contacts and outdoor detectors for the detached garage, the shed and the carport, set to send a phone alert or sound a chime rather than wake the street, because the ladder and tools kept there are exactly what someone would use on the house.

Shift-friendly arming

Arming that suits households where one person leaves for a 6 a.m. tour and the others are still asleep: the perimeter armed from the doorway or the app, interior motion left off, and the system’s history showing who armed and disarmed and when.

Family alerts for older owners

For parents living alone a few blocks away, a setup that sends a notification to adult children when the front or basement door opens during the day, plus a simple pendant or keypad button for help, set up to suit someone who does not want a smartphone.

Flatbush Avenue and Avenue U shop alarms

Commercial alarms for the stores, restaurants, salons and offices on the neighborhood’s two shopping streets: back-door and cellar contacts, glass-break behind the windows, a panic button at the counter and opening and closing reports to the owner’s phone.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Marine Park’s Architecture

Marine Park’s houses were built over roughly three decades, by many builders, for families who wanted a yard and a driveway within the city. They share a few habits that matter to an alarm: the living floor is usually a few steps above the yard, there is nearly always a basement, and the house has more doors than its owners use. Below are the building types we meet most often, as Brick Underground and the Elliman guide describe them, with what each one means for the layout.

Detached frame houses

Brick Underground puts single-family frame houses at about 1,100 square feet. They are often two stories over a basement, with siding over wood framing, a front porch and a back door to the yard. On a house this size the whole system can be wireless. What makes it a Marine Park design is the order: the porch door and the side door off the driveway first, then the kitchen and back-porch openings, then the basement windows at ground level, and only then the upstairs. Siding takes surface-mount contacts and outdoor devices well, as long as fixings are sealed against water running down behind them.

Larger brick houses

The bigger brick houses, 1,200 to 2,200 square feet in the guide’s figures, often have a finished basement with its own entrance, a back porch or deck, and wider window openings with storm windows outside the main sash. Contacts go on the inner sash, the one that actually locks; a storm window alone is not a barrier. Brick walls are fine for wireless signals over the distances in these houses, but we test every sensor’s signal from its final position before fixing it.

Semi-detached houses

Half the house shares a party wall with the neighbor and the other half has a side yard or driveway. That gives three exposed sides instead of four, and one of them, the driveway side, is usually hidden from the street by the neighbor’s car or fence. The windows along that side are often the least watched openings on the block. On a shared driveway we keep every device on your own wall and aim outdoor detectors along your house, not across the drive the neighbor uses all day.

Two-family houses

The guide puts two-family houses at 1,300 to 2,000 square feet and notes that renters in Marine Park live in exactly these. Usually the owner has the ground floor and basement and the tenant the upper floor, reached by a separate street door and stair. We set the system up in two areas on one panel: each household arms its own doors with its own code, and shared spaces such as the basement or the back door can belong to one area or require both.

Co-op buildings at the edges

Co-op buildings stand along the southern and western borders. An apartment alarm there is a small, quiet system: a contact on the unit door, glass-break or contacts on windows that open onto a terrace or a low roof, one motion detector and the app. Nothing goes on common walls or hallway doors without the board’s consent, and the system is chosen so it can leave with the owner.

The Doorstep

The Knock Comes First: Designing a Marine Park Alarm From the Front Door In

Most alarm designs treat the front door as the beginning of a countdown: it opens, the keypad beeps, someone types a code. That is the right idea for coming home. It is the wrong idea for the moments when Marine Park’s documented trouble actually began, which were moments when somebody was at the door and somebody else in the house was deciding what to do. The East 36th Street homeowner in 2010 was inside, slow to answer, while one of the two “meter readers” went to a window. The Troy Avenue resident in 2016 heard her bell ring again and again before a man appeared at her kitchen window. In both cases the front of the house was occupied by a conversation, or the absence of one, and the danger was somewhere else.

So we design the doorstep first, and we design it in three layers. The first layer is seeing and speaking without unlocking anything. A video doorbell or a small door station at the front door, and at the side door if that is the one callers use, lets you see who is there on a phone, a tablet or an indoor screen and talk to them through the door. A utility worker with a genuine appointment can hold an identification card up to the camera; Con Edison told a Community Board 13 meeting in 2009 that its employees carry a photo ID and that customers in doubt should call the company before letting anyone in. Nobody has to open the door to have that conversation, and nobody has to stand behind glass to have it.

The second layer is a doorstep mode for the alarm itself. When the household is at home, the system can sit in a stay or perimeter mode in which every door and window is armed but the motion detectors inside are off, so the family can move around freely. We set that up so that answering the front door does not disarm anything else: the front door has its own short delay or can be bypassed for one opening, while the side door, the back door, the kitchen windows and the basement remain armed. If someone at the front is the distraction and someone else tries the back, the back reports. The keypad by the front door shows the same thing in plain words, so a parent answering the bell can glance at it and see that the rest of the house is still on.

Visitors Below

Utility Visitors, the Boiler Room and the Basement Stair

The 2012 furnace scam is worth reading closely, because it shows how a visitor with a plausible reason can end up in the most private part of a house. A woman calling as a National Grid operator told older homeowners that a servicing record was overdue or that neighbors had reported problems, and booked an inspection. Two men arrived in a van, went down to the boiler or furnace, took out a small part, put it back and charged more than a thousand dollars. The Brooklyn Paper’s report does not say that anything else was taken, but it is easy to see what the visit offered: time alone in the basement and a route up the stairs while the homeowner waited.

Real visits to the basement are routine in a Marine Park house: the boiler is serviced, the meter is read, a plumber fixes a leak, a contractor finishes the space. An alarm should make those visits safe rather than awkward. We give the basement stair its own zone, using a contact on the door at the top of the stair where there is one, or a motion detector covering the top of the stair where there is not. The household can arm that zone on its own. A worker is let in through the basement entrance or walked down; the basement stair zone is armed; the homeowner goes back upstairs. If the visitor comes up the stairs, the keypad chimes and the phone shows it at once, without a siren, so the homeowner is never surprised in her own kitchen.

The same zone protects against the opposite case: someone coming in through a basement window or a rear basement door while the family is upstairs. Basement windows at ground level are among the easiest openings on these houses to work at unseen, and in finished basements they are often hidden behind furniture or blinds. Each one that opens gets a contact; fixed ones get glass-break coverage. The panel itself and its battery go upstairs on the main floor, not next to the boiler, so a visitor in the basement is never alone with the brain of the system.

Daylight

Daylight at the Side and Back of a Detached House

The front of a Marine Park house is public. The block sees it, the neighbors across the street see it, and the porch light shows it at night. The sides and back are a different place. On a detached house the side path between your wall and the neighbor’s may be a few feet wide and screened by a fence and a gate. On a semi-detached house the driveway side is often blocked from view by parked cars. The back yard is watched only by the backs of other houses, and in the middle of a weekday those houses are often empty too. The Kimball Street burglary in August 2010 happened at noon at a back window; the East 28th Street shed was opened at 2 p.m. in August 2019; the East 34th Street house was entered at about 4:30 in the afternoon.

So the survey of a Marine Park house walks the outside before it walks the inside, and it notes, opening by opening, who could see someone standing there. Windows along the side path, the kitchen window over the yard, the back-porch door, the basement windows behind the deck and any door under a rear extension get marked as the openings that matter most. Each gets a contact if it opens. Where a window has a large pane and a person could step through broken glass, a glass-break detector inside covers it. And where a back window sits behind shrubs or a fence at head height, outdoor motion lighting does the job the 2008 precinct advice described, lighting the person before they reach the glass.

Daylight changes the arming, not just the layout. A house that is empty from 7 in the morning until 6 at night needs to be fully armed during those hours, every day, including the days someone leaves in a hurry. The system can remind the last person out by phone if nobody armed it, show who armed and disarmed in its history, and, for households that want it, arm a perimeter schedule automatically on weekdays. We do not set anything automatic without walking the household through it, because an alarm that arms itself while a grandparent is napping upstairs is an alarm that gets switched off for good.

Outbuildings

Sheds, Detached Garages and Carports Behind Marine Park Houses

The structures behind the house deserve a short section of their own. Brick Underground’s Marine Park listings mention a detached garage, a carport, a backyard with parking and a deck, and nearly every yard here has a shed. These outbuildings hold the things a burglar would otherwise have to bring: ladders, pry bars, garden tools, sometimes bolt cutters. The teenager on Kimball Street in 2010 used a gardening tool on the back window. Whoever cut the lock on the East 28th Street shed in 2019 left with power tools and a bike.

Shift Schedules

Early Tours, Day Sleepers and Empty Afternoons

Brick Underground describes Marine Park as a neighborhood where many residents work for the city: police officers, firefighters, teachers. Those jobs come with schedules that shape an alarm more than any floor plan. A tour that starts at 6 or 7 in the morning means one adult leaving in the dark while the rest of the house sleeps. A night tour means someone sleeping in the afternoon while the children come home from P.S. 207 or P.S. 222. A firefighter’s long shifts mean a spouse alone in the house for a day or more at a stretch.

For the early leaver, the useful feature is arming the perimeter on the way out without arming the interior motion detectors: the person leaving sets stay mode at the keypad or in the app, the door they leave through has an exit delay, and everyone still asleep can walk to the kitchen without setting anything off. For the afternoon sleeper, the bedroom can be left out of the motion coverage while the front hall and the basement stair stay in it, so that the children’s arrival at the front door is announced by a chime instead of a siren. For the parent alone for a day, the doorstep layer described above matters most, together with a keypad by the bed and a simple way to call for help.

Older Owners

Parents Living Alone and Family on the Phone

Brick Underground describes Marine Park as a family neighborhood with a strong civic association where “everyone maintains their property.” The 2010 water-meter case involved a homeowner of 62, the 2012 furnace scam targeted seniors including an 89-year-old, and the 2010 police report from Avenue M near Ryder Street described a 77-year-old resident who could not tell whether anything had been taken after her home was ransacked. Where adult children live a few blocks away or farther off, the worry tends to be about the hours when a parent is alone and somebody rings the bell, and a parent decides alone whether to open the door.

The alarm can carry some of that worry. A parent’s system can send notifications to two or three family phones when the front door or basement door opens between, say, 9 and 5 on weekdays, without bothering anyone in the evening when visits are expected. The doorbell camera’s clip can be seen by a son at work as easily as by his mother in her kitchen. A help pendant or a large keypad button can reach the family or a monitoring center, and the call list can put family first and the central station second. We set all of this up in person, with the parent present, and leave a one-page card by the keypad in large type that says what each button does.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Marine Park

Brick Underground and the Elliman guide agree that Flatbush Avenue is Marine Park’s commercial corridor, with Kings Plaza Shopping Center just across the border for big-box shopping. Avenue U carries a second line of stores. Most businesses here are small: restaurants, pizzerias, taverns, salons, delis, grocery stores, pharmacies and offices, often with an apartment upstairs and a cellar below. Their alarm needs are different from a house’s, and the local record points to the back door and the camera recorder rather than the front window.

Flatbush Avenue

The neighborhood’s main shopping street, with restaurants, diners, groceries and services. In July 2010 a burglar broke through the back door of a crafts store on Flatbush Avenue between Avenues T and U and took $550; the manager told the Brooklyn Paper the store was installing new gates and cameras. In October 2017 another Flatbush Avenue store near Quentin Road was entered overnight through a forced back door, and the burglar removed a disc from the surveillance system and pried open the register. Back doors onto lots and alleys get heavy-duty contacts and shock sensors here, and the alarm reports off-site before anyone reaches the recorder.

Avenue U

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Marine Park: Questions Answered

These are the questions Marine Park owners ask that come out of the neighborhood’s own record: callers at the door, visitors in the basement, daylight at the back of a detached house, early tours and parents living alone. Price, scheduling and coverage questions are in the FAQ further down.

At the front door

In March 2010 two men posing as water meter readers knocked on a door on East 36th Street near Quentin Road, and when the owner was slow to answer, one forced a window and climbed in. While I am at the front door talking to a stranger, can every other door and window stay armed?

Yes, and this is the single most useful setting for a Marine Park house. When you are home, the system sits in a stay or perimeter mode: every door and window contact is armed and the motion detectors inside are off. We program the front door, and only the front door, so that opening it while you are at home either starts a short delay or is quietly allowed for one opening. The side door, the back door, the kitchen windows and the basement stay armed the whole time. If anyone touches them while you are busy at the front, the siren sounds and your phone shows which opening it was.

The keypad by the front door says so in plain words, for example “Home: perimeter armed, front door open”, so whoever answers the bell can see at a glance that the rest of the house is protected. Combined with a video doorbell, you may never need to open the door at all: the conversation happens through the doorbell, and a genuine worker can hold an ID card up to its camera while you call the company to check, as the precinct advised the civic association in 2009.

A 63rd Precinct report from 2016 describes a man on Troy Avenue who rang a woman’s doorbell over and over, then walked into the back yard and put his head through her kitchen window. Can an unanswered ring make the back of the house react faster?

It can. When the doorbell is part of the alarm app, an unanswered ring is an event the system knows about. We can set a short window, usually a few minutes, after an unanswered ring during which any back or side opening reports instantly, with no entry delay, and switches on the rear lights if they are connected. If you are at work, your phone also shows the ring and the clip, so you know someone came to the door and nobody answered.

Outside that window the house behaves normally, so a family member coming in the back door later in the day still gets the usual delay to disarm. The point is to treat the knock-and-walk-round pattern, which is how someone checks whether a house is empty, as the start of something rather than as nothing.

Daylight at the side and back

Our detached house has three windows along the driveway side that nobody on the street can see because of the neighbor’s fence. Which openings on a house like ours are hidden from everyone?

On most Marine Park detached houses the hidden openings are the windows along the side path or driveway, the kitchen window over the back yard, the back-porch door, the basement windows behind the deck or shrubs, and any door under a rear extension. The survey literally walks the outside of the house and notes, at each opening, whether anyone in another house or on the street could see a person standing there. Those openings go to the top of the list.

Each side window that opens gets a contact; large panes get glass-break coverage from inside. If the side path has a gate, a contact on the gate set to chime rather than sound the siren tells you when someone walks down the side. Motion lighting along the path, as the precinct recommended in 2008, makes a person visible before they reach the glass.

In August 2019 someone walked into a yard on East 28th Street near Quentin Road at 2 p.m., cut the lock on the shed and took power tools and a bike. Our shed holds the ladder and the tools. Should the shed report before the house does?

Yes, in the sense that the shed is the earlier warning. A wireless contact on the shed door, with a repeater if the shed is far from the house, sends a phone alert and a chime inside the house the moment the door opens. The full siren is usually not triggered by the shed alone, because family members open sheds at odd hours. But an alert while someone is still in the yard gives you time to turn on the yard lights, check the camera if you have one, and call the precinct.

Whatever the shed holds, the ladder deserves a lock or a chain. A ladder left in an unlocked shed is the way up to the second-floor windows that otherwise do not need sensors.

One of us leaves for a 6 a.m. tour while the rest of the house is still asleep. Can the person leaving arm the outside doors and windows without arming the motion detectors inside?

Yes. The person leaving sets stay mode at the keypad by the door or in the app, and the door they leave through gets an exit delay long enough to close it and walk to the car. Every door and window is then armed, and the motion detectors stay off so the people asleep can walk to the kitchen. When the rest of the household gets up, they switch to away mode on their way out, or the system reminds the last person out if nobody does.

If the early leaver also wants to know the house is fine during the day, the app shows the arming state and the history: who armed, who disarmed and when. It takes a few seconds to check on a break.

Shops and the record of a break-in

In 2017 a burglar forced the back door of a Flatbush Avenue store near Quentin Road and removed the disc from its surveillance system. If the recorder itself is taken, what record of the break-in is left?

With a monitored or app-reporting alarm, quite a lot. The alarm reports the moment the back door is forced, by cellular signal, to the owner’s phone and to a central station if there is one. Each event, which door, which motion detector, at what minute, is stored off the premises. Even if the recorder leaves the building, you know when the burglar came in, which way he went and how long he stayed, and police have a timeline.

We also put the recorder somewhere less obvious than a shelf under the counter, in a locked box with its own contact or within the motion coverage, so touching it is itself an alarm event. Cameras that can upload clips when the alarm trips add pictures to the timeline. But the first line of evidence is the alarm’s own report, and that cannot be carried out the back door.

A Flatbush Avenue crafts store that was broken into through its back door in 2010 responded by adding new gates and cameras. After a break-in, what should a small store put in first: gates, cameras or an alarm?

Fix whatever was broken first, which usually means the back door and its frame, and then the alarm, then cameras. A gate slows someone down but tells nobody that they are trying. A camera records them but does not call anyone. An alarm with a contact and a shock sensor on the back door, a motion detector inside and a cellular communicator reports the attempt while it is happening, which is when reporting matters.

Cameras are best added together with the alarm, so that a trip on the back door brings up the back-door view on the owner’s phone at once. On our Brooklyn list a standard small-business alarm is $1,500 to $3,000, and adding cameras to it is $1,000 to $2,500; we will price the door repair separately or tell you who does it.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Marine Park

Search engines now answer alarm questions with a generated summary before any website appears. We could not open Google’s AI Overview, its question boxes or its related searches from where this page was built, so nothing below is quoted from them. Instead we list seven claims that general advice about alarm installation often makes, from home-improvement guides and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr to manufacturer marketing, and set each one against what Marine Park’s own record shows.

“Burglars strike at night, so arm your alarm when you go to bed”

Night-time arming matters, and the 2018 Madison Place break-in at 5:30 in the morning is the case nobody here forgets. But most of the Marine Park incidents we found in local reporting happened in daylight: the Kimball Street back window at noon, the fake meter readers on East 36th Street at 2:40 in the afternoon, the East 28th Street shed at 2 p.m., the East 34th Street house at about 4:30.

In a neighborhood of commuters and city workers, the hours when houses are empty are the working day, and that is when a house is checked with a knock and entered at the back.

So an alarm here has to be fully armed every weekday, from the moment the last person leaves, and the system should remind people who forget. Night mode is the second setting, not the only one.

“Put the first sensor on the front door, because that is where most break-ins start”

The front door is where Marine Park’s documented incidents often began, but not where entry happened. The fake meter readers came in through a window. The Troy Avenue man rang the front bell and went to the kitchen window. The Kimball Street boy pried a back window.

On a detached or semi-detached house, the front door faces the street and the neighbors. The windows along the driveway and over the back yard face nobody.

The front door needs a contact, and a doorbell camera, and the right delay. But the first hour of a Marine Park survey is spent on the sides and back.

“A video doorbell is a security system”

A doorbell camera shows you who is at the door and records them. It does not know whether the back door has just been forced while you were watching the front, and it does not call anyone when a window breaks.

In the 2010 water-meter case, the homeowner was home and the visitors were at the door; the entry was through a window elsewhere. A doorbell would have shown the two men. Only an armed contact on that window would have reported the second one.

We treat the doorbell as the first layer of a doorstep design, tied to an alarm whose other openings stay armed while the door is being answered.

“A basic alarm costs a few hundred dollars plus a monthly fee”

Cost sites quote national averages, and DIY kits do sell for a few hundred dollars. On our Brooklyn list, which is AESS’s base price for the whole company, a basic installed residential system starts at $600 and a wireless system typically runs $600 to $1,200.

Complete protection for a whole Marine Park house, with glass-break detection, smoke and CO reporting, sirens and the app, starts at $2,500. A panel replacement that keeps good wiring from an old system is $800 to $1,500.

Monitoring is optional: from $19.99 a month through our partners, month to month, and many households run the system from their phones without it.

“Utility impostors are a scam problem, not an alarm problem”

Partly true: no alarm stops a homeowner from opening the door. But the 2009 police warning reported by the Brooklyn Paper described exactly how the scam becomes a burglary: one person keeps the resident busy with a meter or a bill while an accomplice ransacks the house.

That second person needs an open door or window somewhere else. An alarm in stay mode with the front door separate is the defense against precisely that move.

Add a basement-stair zone for genuine visits, family notifications for parents living alone, and a doorbell that lets a worker’s ID be checked without opening the door, and the alarm becomes the most practical tool against the scam.

“Wireless sensors are unreliable in brick houses”

Brick and plaster do weaken radio signals, and a sensor at the far end of a long building can struggle. But Marine Park’s houses are mostly 1,100 to 2,200 square feet, according to Brick Underground, and over those distances current wireless sensors work well when each one is tested from its final position.

The outbuildings are the real test: a shed or detached garage at the back of a deep lot may need a repeater.

We test every sensor’s signal before fixing it and write the reading on the zone list, so nobody discovers a weak signal after a real alarm.

“You need a city permit before installing a home alarm in New York”

We found no New York City rule requiring a homeowner or a small business to obtain a permit before installing a burglar alarm, and this page does not claim one.

What does apply is the Administrative Code’s fifteen-minute limit on how long a building’s alarm may sound, and the state requirement that the business doing the installation hold a Department of State license.

Ask any installer for their license number. Ours is #12000287431, and it is on every quote and invoice.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ring the Bell, Then Walk Down the Driveway

The first thing I do at a Marine Park house is not open my toolbox. I go back down the front steps, stand on the sidewalk and ring the bell as if I were a stranger, and I ask whoever is inside to do exactly what they normally do. Some people open the door straight away. Some come to the window. Some shout through the door. Then I walk down the driveway or the side path, slowly, and count the windows I can reach before anyone on the street or in the next house could see me, and I try the gate to the yard. I look at the shed and ask what is in it. Only then do we go inside and talk about sensors.

That five-minute walk tells me more about a Marine Park house than the floor plan. It shows which door callers really use, whether the people at home open the door to anyone, how hidden the side and back are, and what someone could borrow from the yard. Most of the design follows from it: where the doorbell camera goes, which openings stay armed when the door is answered, whether the shed gets a contact and how the basement visit should work. It is method, not a story about any one customer, and I do it on every survey in the neighborhood. — Anwar Timothy, NYS license #12000287431

  • Brick Underground, Nancy A. Ruhling, Buy Curious: Marine Park (July 13, 2022) — one- and two-family detached and semi-detached houses, 1910s–1940s; house sizes

  • Brooklyn Paper, Thomas Tracy, “A water meter menace” (March 31, 2010) — March 24, 2:40 p.m., East 36th Street near Quentin Road: two men posing as water meter readers, forced window, 62-year-old homeowner inside, safe taken; other 63rd Precinct items including Kings Plaza car break-ins
