The Neighborhood

Why Beverley Square Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Beverley Square is really two small neighborhoods divided by a railroad cut, and both are built mostly of freestanding wood houses more than a hundred years old, the kind of property where a camera plan has to work around porches, driveways, garages and, on some blocks, a landmarks permit. Wikipedia describes the boundary plainly: the open cut that carries the Q train between Marlborough Road, which is East 15th Street, and East 16th Street separates Beverley Square West from Beverley Square East. The East side runs from the cut to East 19th Street; the West side runs from the cut to Argyle Road or Stratford Road, depending on whom you ask. Beverley Road forms the northern edge and Cortelyou Road the southern one. The railroad came first. New York YIMBY notes that the Brooklyn, Flatbush and Coney Island Railroad began running along the eastern edge of what became Beverley Square West in 1878 and helped set off development. The Brooklyn Eagle says developer Thomas B. Ackerson began Beverley Square East between 1898 and 1901 with houses on East 19th Street, and Brooklyn Paper records that the southern half of the West side, farmland once called the Catherine Lot or the Voorhees farm, stayed empty until Ackerson bought it in 1901. In 1902 he had the numbered streets renamed Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough roads, following the naming used in Prospect Park South. By 1906, according to the City Planning Commission's designation report, 114 of 119 houses in the area later proposed for landmarking had been built.

The houses are what make a camera job here different. The Brooklyn Eagle describes both halves of the neighborhood as full of stand-alone wooden houses more than a century old, with driveways and garages, two luxuries that are hard to find in much of Brooklyn. BK Reader and the Commission describe the styles as mostly Queen Anne, Colonial Revival and Shingle, and the Victorian Flatbush preservation group says Beverley Square West was built with the intention that no two houses be exactly alike. Ackerson priced the East side from $10,000 and the West side at $6,500, according to BK Reader, and he lived in the West at 304 Marlborough Road, a house he built in 1903. The Eagle also points to the Honeymoon Cottage at 305 Rugby Road, said to have been built for a member of the Guggenheim family. For a camera installer, all of this means walls of clapboard and shingle over old framing rather than brick, porches with columns and railings, long side driveways, detached garages at the back of the lot, cellars below and attics above, and almost no two houses where the same camera layout fits. On the edges, the stock changes: city property records show six-story pre-war and postwar apartment buildings on Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road, including a 131-apartment building at 1710 Cortelyou Road from 1960 and a 96-apartment building at 1601 Beverley Road from 1940, and the Museum of the City of New York collection shows the Hilrose Court apartment building at 1713 Beverley Road at East 18th Street.

The incidents the local press has reported point at specific parts of these properties. CBS New York reported that at least six homes on Westminster Road were broken into in the middle of the afternoon within three months, and that residents said a private security team patrolled the neighborhood daily without seeming to help. Patch, in coverage from around 2012, described six homes broken into in a short span, all during the day, and noted that the police offer a free crime prevention survey to homeowners. On Cortelyou Road, CBS New York reported a burglar caught by surveillance cameras breaking into the Picket Fence restaurant and taking $2,500 from two cash registers. Gothamist, around 2014, reported a run of robberies near the corner of Cortelyou and Argyle roads, quoting a worker who called that corner notoriously dangerous and wondered why cameras were not more present, and describing a robber who fled toward Argyle and Beverley in a getaway car where no cameras could follow him. CBS New York also showed a thief on a skateboard who spotted a package on a Ditmas Park porch and stopped for it, and the NYPD's advice in that story was to file a report, especially when a thief is caught on video. These are older stories, and we do not present them as a current crime map. They are useful because they show where the useful footage has to come from: the porch, the side and back doors in daylight, the register, and the street corner.

The newer change is legal. The Commission's November 2025 vote put 118 houses on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough roads under Landmarks control, while CBS New York reported that Westminster and Stratford roads were left out and Beverley Square East was not designated. The Beverley Square West association says the Commission designated part of the neighborhood but not all of it, despite months of community advocacy for a wider district. An LPC permit is now required for exterior work on district houses, and the LPC's own business guidance says that holds even if the work does not need a Buildings Department permit and even if it touches only a part of the property that cannot be seen from the street. Security cameras appear by name in the LPC's category for health, safety and utility equipment, and the LPC's rules on cameras favor installations that are not visible unless staff decide that hiding them is not feasible. So on one side of a block line, a porch camera is a decision between the owner and the installer; on the other side, it is a decision the owner, the installer and Landmarks staff make together. Every Beverley Square quote we write starts by finding out which side you are on.

Beverley Square facts that decide where a camera goes

  • A district with edges: 118 houses built from 1894 to 1910 on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough roads are in the Beverley Square West Historic District; Westminster Road, Stratford Road and all of Beverley Square East are not.
  • Permits even out of sight: LPC guidance says exterior work on a district property needs an LPC permit even where no Buildings Department permit is needed and even where the work cannot be seen from the street.
  • Daylight break-ins: CBS New York reported at least six Westminster Road homes broken into in the afternoon over three months, and Patch described six daytime break-ins in an earlier run.
  • Freestanding wood houses: the Brooklyn Eagle describes century-old stand-alone wooden houses with driveways and garages, which means many walls of wood and long cable runs to the back of the lot.
  • Bigger buildings on the edges: city records show apartment buildings such as 1710 Cortelyou Road (131 apartments, 1960) and 1601 Beverley Road (96 apartments, 1940) along the two roads that frame the houses.
  • A precinct trend, not a guarantee: a Brooklyn real-estate team's summary of 70th Precinct figures shows its seven major crimes falling from 1,124 to 853, down 24.1 percent; we use that as context, not as a reason to skip the back door.

What Beverley Square owners tend to add next. Once the porch, driveway and back door are covered, owners of two- and three-unit houses often want each household to get its own door camera, and owners planning a cellar or attic apartment ask whether the new entrance can be added later. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a second entrance is likely, a spare cable coiled in the cellar near that door, so the walls of an old house only have to be opened once.

The District Line

Inside the Line or Outside It: How the 2025 Landmark Designation Changes a Beverley Square Camera Job

Until late 2025, a camera on a Beverley Square porch was a matter for the owner, any co-owners or tenants, and the neighbors whose yards the camera might see. That changed for part of the neighborhood on November 25, 2025, when the Landmarks Preservation Commission voted to designate the Beverley Square West Historic District, designation number LP-2692. The Commission's proposal records that the district was calendared in June 2025 and heard in October 2025; Brownstoner reported 35 speakers in favor and three against at the hearing. The final district, as 6sqft and CBS New York describe it, takes in 118 houses built between 1894 and 1910 on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough roads between Beverley Road and Cortelyou Road. The sources do not agree on every number. Some count 118 houses and some 119, and the Commission's materials and press coverage name slightly different sets of architects. We note those differences instead of choosing one, and before any job we check the specific lot on the Commission's map rather than relying on a street name.

The more practical point is what was left out. CBS New York reported that the Commission excluded Westminster Road and Stratford Road, and Beverley Square East, the houses on East 16th through East 19th streets east of the cut, was not designated. The Beverley Square West association, which advocated for a wider district, says on its website that only part of its neighborhood was designated. City records also show at least one house inside the district, 359 Rugby Road, classified by Landmarks staff as noncontributing because it has been heavily altered. That leaves an owner on Rugby Road, an owner on Westminster Road and an owner on East 17th Street with three different starting points for the same job, even though their houses were built in the same decade by the same developer.

What the Landmarks rules actually say about cameras

The LPC lists security cameras, along with light fixtures and similar items, under its category of health, safety and utility equipment. Its rules for that category, in Section 2-20 as amended in 2018, are built around one idea: the installation should not be visible, unless Landmarks staff decide that making the equipment invisible is not feasible. Chapter 11 of the LPC Permit Guidebook says staff can approve security cameras without a public hearing when the installation meets a set of listed criteria. The copy of that chapter we reviewed for this page was cut off before the full list, so we do not repeat criteria we have not read in full; when a job comes up, we read the current guidebook with the owner and design to it. What the rules make clear is the direction of travel. A camera tucked under an eave, on a rear wall, inside a porch ceiling or in a shadowed corner, finished in a color that disappears against the house, is closer to what the Commission wants than a large bullet camera on a bracket beside the front door.

What happens when cameras go up first

Brooklyn has examples of owners who did it the other way around. In 2015 the Historic Districts Council testified on a Fort Greene application to legalize a lamp post and security cameras that had been installed without Landmarks permits. The Council's suggestion was black cameras placed strategically in the cornice or other spots where they would not detract from the building. That suggestion is a good design guide even for houses that are not landmarked, and the case is a reminder that a district owner who skips the permit can end up applying for one anyway, with the equipment already on the wall. We do not install exterior cameras on a district house before the permit is in hand. Indoor cameras, which do not touch the exterior, are a different matter and can usually go in while the application is under review.

How we design a district house's camera plan

On a house inside the district, we walk the exterior twice: once as an installer, looking for where each camera needs to be to see a face at the steps, the side door or the garage, and once as a reviewer, looking for where each camera would be least visible from the street. Usually the two meet in a handful of spots: the ceiling of the porch near the front wall rather than on the porch's front edge, the underside of an eave rather than the face of a gable, the inside corner where a porch meets the house, and the rear and side walls rather than the front. We choose compact domes and small turrets in colors that disappear against the trim, avoid fixing anything into carved brackets, spindles, shingle patterns or decorative boards, and run cable inside the porch structure or through the cellar and attic, never stapled across the siding. Then we prepare what the owner's application needs: the make and model of each camera with its dimensions and color, photos of each proposed location, and a marked-up sketch showing the position and the cable route.

Outside the district, by choice

On Westminster Road, Stratford Road and the East side, nobody requires the camera to be discreet. Many owners there ask for the same approach anyway, because the houses are of the same age and style and the neighbors care how the block looks. The difference is that the owner decides, and that a camera can sometimes go in the most effective spot even if it is a little more visible. If an owner wants a visible camera at the front as a warning and hidden ones at the side and back, that is a reasonable plan on an undesignated house. If the association's push to extend the district ever succeeds, a system already built to the discreet standard is in a better position than one with brackets on every corner.

Porches, Driveways and Back Doors

The Porch, the Long Driveway and the Door Nobody Sees: Building a Beverley Square House Plan Around Daytime Risk

The break-in stories from these blocks share one detail that shapes the whole camera plan: they happened in daylight. The CBS New York report on Westminster Road described homes broken into in the middle of the afternoon, and Patch described a run of break-ins that all happened during the day. A freestanding house with a driveway and a garage is busy at the front in the morning and evening and quiet in the middle of a weekday, when the adults are at work and the street is mostly empty. Its side door is screened by the driveway and the neighbor's house, its back door faces a yard and a garage, and its porch, deep and shaded, gives anyone who reaches it a place to stand out of the sun and out of sight. A camera plan here therefore cannot be built only for night. Every camera that matters must record clearly in bright afternoon light and in deep porch shade, and the cameras on the side and back must record continuously, not only when something trips a motion setting.

The second detail is the package. CBS New York's footage of a skateboarding thief stopping for a box on a Ditmas Park porch shows how short the window is: a person sees the box from the sidewalk, walks up, takes it and leaves. On houses where the porch sits back from the sidewalk up a flight of steps, a doorbell at the door sees the person only in the last few feet, and often only from the side once they turn away. The useful clip shows the person on the steps, coming toward the camera, before they reach the box, and again as they leave with it.

The porch and the front steps

The best porch view on most of these houses comes from the porch ceiling, near the front wall and to one side of the door, looking out and down toward the top of the steps. From there the camera sees faces as people climb toward it, sees the porch floor where boxes are left, and stays in shadow and out of easy reach. On a district house it is also one of the least visible positions from the sidewalk. A video doorbell at the door adds the close view of whoever presses the button and lets you answer from your phone. We set both to record continuously in daylight, draw the alert zone around the steps and the door rather than the sidewalk, and choose a camera that copes with the contrast between a bright street and a dark porch, which is the most common reason porch footage fails on these houses.

The driveway and the garage

Long side driveways leading to garages at the back of the lot are one of the neighborhood's distinguishing features, and they give a camera two jobs. A camera near the front corner of the house, looking along the driveway toward the street, sees the face of anyone walking in from the sidewalk. A camera on or near the garage, looking back toward the house, sees who reaches the garage door and the side door and which way they leave. Getting a cable to a detached garage is often the longest run on the job; depending on the property we carry it underground in conduit, overhead where a proper span exists, or along a fence line in protected conduit, and if the garage has its own power, we look at whether a small switch there makes sense. Each option is a separate line on the quote, because the labor differs so much from house to house.

The side door and the back door

The side door, often under a small porch or hood partway down the driveway, is the entrance this neighborhood's houses hide best, and that is exactly why it needs a camera. We place it so the person at the door is facing the lens, typically on the driveway-side wall a little beyond the door, rather than directly above it, where it would see only the top of a head. The back door gets a camera on the rear wall looking down the yard toward the door and any gate, framed to stop at your own fence line. On houses where the back door opens onto a rear porch or deck, the camera goes under its roof where possible, out of rain and out of reach.

Clapboard, shingle and century-old framing

Masonry houses forgive a lot; a hundred-year-old wood house does not. Wood shingles split if a screw goes in at the wrong angle, clapboard cracks if a mount is pulled tight against an uneven course, and the trim boards around porches and eaves are often thin, decorative and original. So we never fix a camera into a shingle, a decorative board or a bracket. Each mount sits on a mounting block shaped to the siding or trim profile, with its screws passing through into the sheathing and framing behind, so the camera's weight is carried by the structure and the siding is not squeezed. Every hole through an outside wall is drilled slightly downward toward the outside, with a drip loop in the cable and an exterior-grade sealant around a grommet, so water runs away from the opening rather than into the wall. Paint is matched to the trim on district houses and on any house where the owner wants the camera to disappear. If a camera ever comes down, the mounting block comes with it and the repair is a few small holes in a block of wood, not in the original siding.

Cellars, attics and the porch in between

Most of these houses give us two good cable routes and one awkward one. The cellar, where it is unfinished, lets cable travel along the joists and come up at the side and back walls with almost nothing visible. The attic, where it is reachable, lets cable drop to eave positions on any side of the house. The porch is the awkward part, because it is a separate structure stuck to the front of the house: its roof is usually a shallow space with little or no access, and its columns may be solid, hollow or boxed around a post. On district houses, where the porch ceiling is often the least visible camera position, the route matters as much as the camera. Depending on the house, we fish cable through the porch roof framing from the house wall, run it up inside a hollow column from the cellar, or bring it through the front wall right at the porch ceiling line, where the porch roof hides the opening. Which one works is decided on the walk-through, and it is written on the quote before anything is ordered.

Houses with more than one household

CBS New York's coverage of the designation mentioned single-family houses on Cortelyou Road being converted, and 6sqft reported that the district's guidelines address accessory units in basements, cellars and attics, while ruling out new backyard homes. Whatever the arrangement, a house with more than one household needs a camera plan that respects each one. The shared views, such as the porch steps, the driveway and the garage, can sit on one recorder with each household given a viewing account limited to those cameras. Each household's own door camera goes on their account only. The owner's account can see the shared cameras and, if the lease allows, the owner's own entrances, but not the inside of anyone's apartment. We set these limits before we leave and write them down, so a change of tenant becomes a matter of removing one account, not reworking the system. If a cellar or attic unit is planned but not built, a spare cable left at the likely entrance saves opening the walls twice.

The apartment buildings on Beverley and Cortelyou roads

The larger buildings that frame the houses are a different kind of job. When we matched city violation records to these blocks for an earlier study, nearly all of the intercom violations and the self-closing door violations on lots in this area were written against buildings on the two edge roads, not against the houses. A camera system does not cure a broken intercom or a door that will not close, and we do not suggest it does, but in a building with those problems the most useful cameras are usually the ones that show the front door, the vestibule, the mailboxes, the cellar door, the laundry room and the stair doors, so that the owner and the super can see how people are actually getting in and what is happening to the doors. In buildings like these, cable travels through cellar corridors and up existing risers or pipe chases, every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor is sealed with rated firestop, and the recorder sits in a locked room on a battery unit. The owner or board decides in writing who may view live, who may export, and how long footage is kept.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Beverley Square House, Sidewalk to Garage and Cellar to Attic

This describes how we work on these houses, not a story about one job. We start on the sidewalk, because on a district house the first question is what the camera will look like from there, and on any house the second question is what a person approaching from there would see. From the sidewalk we look up the steps and into the porch, noting where the porch ceiling meets the front wall and how deep the shade is in the afternoon. We climb the steps and stand at the door, then at the top of the steps, and look for where a camera sees a face coming up rather than the top of a head. We walk down the driveway, stopping at the side door to see how screened it is from the street, and on to the garage, looking back toward the house. We go around to the back door and out to the rear fence, and look back, which shows where a rear camera sees the whole yard and where it must stop short of the neighbors' yards and windows. Inside, we go to the cellar to find the clearest route along the joists, any old wire we will need to trace, and where the internet line comes in. Then we look for a way into the porch structure, from the cellar up a column, through the porch roof framing, or at the ceiling line of the front wall. Finally we go up to the attic, if there is safe access, to check the eave routes, and we choose a closet or a corner on a living floor where the recorder can stay hidden. By the end we know how many cameras the house needs, where each one goes, how each cable gets there and, on a district house, what the Landmarks application will show. That is what the written quote is built from.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Beverley Square: Questions Answered

Porches and landmarked houses

Our house on Westminster Road has a double porch, one level above the other. Does the main camera belong on the upper porch or the lower one?

Usually the lower one, with the upper porch used for a second, wider view only if the house needs it. Brownstoner's coverage of the landmarks hearing noted double porches on Westminster Road, and on houses like these the lower porch is where people actually arrive: the steps, the front door and the floor where couriers leave boxes. A camera in the lower porch ceiling, near the front wall and off to one side of the door, sees faces on the steps at close range and stays shaded and hard to reach. A camera on the upper porch sees more of the yard and the sidewalk, but from that height people become the tops of heads, and a camera there is easy to see from the street. On Westminster Road no Landmarks permit is needed, because the road was left out of the district, but most owners still prefer the quieter position. If you want the upper porch covered as well, for example because it is reachable from the lower porch roof, we add a small camera under the upper porch roof looking along the upper floor rather than out at the street.

The Landmarks rules favor cameras that cannot be seen. How small can a porch camera be and still show a face on our steps?

Smaller than most people expect, provided it is close to the steps and aimed well. What makes a face readable is how much of the frame the face fills, not how big the camera body is. A compact dome or a small turret a few feet from the top step, with a lens chosen for that distance, gives a clearer face than a large bullet camera across the yard. On a district house on Rugby, Marlborough or Argyle Road, that is good news: the position the LPC is likely to accept, high in the porch ceiling beside the door and painted to match, is also one of the best positions for faces. The limits come from light rather than size. A deep porch facing a bright street in the afternoon is one of the hardest scenes a camera can face, so we choose a camera with strong wide-dynamic-range handling and check the picture on site at the time of day you care about before the final mount goes in.

The daytime burglars on these blocks had the house to themselves. Where in a big old house should the recorder hide so they cannot carry it off?

Somewhere ordinary-looking and out of the way, not in the cellar next to the internet modem where most people put it and most burglars would look. A recorder is a small box, and a large house has many places to put a small box: a high shelf in a linen closet, inside a cabinet in an upstairs room, behind a door in the attic stair. The cable from the cameras comes up through the walls to that spot, and the internet connection reaches it either by cable or by moving the modem nearby. We put the recorder on a battery unit so a burglar who flips a breaker does not stop the recording, and we set up remote access so the clips can be viewed and copied from your phone even while the recorder is still in the house. For the clips that matter most, we show you how to save them to your phone the same day, and where it suits the house we set up a cloud copy of selected alert clips, named on the quote with whether it carries a subscription. A recorder you own carries no monthly fee from us.

Corners, edges and the undesignated blocks

Our house is on a corner lot where Argyle Road meets Cortelyou Road, beside the shops and the library. How many of our cameras should face the commercial side?

Usually one, aimed at your own side yard and side entrance, with the rest of the plan treating the house like any other. A corner lot on the shopping street sees far more foot traffic past its side than an interior lot, and Gothamist's reports of robberies near the Cortelyou and Argyle corner show it can be a busy, sometimes rough spot. But a house camera trying to watch the whole corner will spend all day recording strangers and alerting you for every shopper. A camera on the Cortelyou Road side, framed tightly on your side door, side path and the stretch of fence along your lot, records anyone who comes onto your property from that side. Alerts on that camera are drawn around the door and the path, not the sidewalk. Because a corner house inside the district may also face Landmarks review on both street faces, we check the lot first and keep the side camera as discreet as the front.

Landmarks left Beverley Square East out of the district. What still limits where a camera on an East 18th Street house can point?

Your neighbors' privacy, your own tenants' privacy and the practical limits of what a camera can usefully see. New York Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, and according to Gavel Insight the remedies include money damages and a court order forcing the camera to be moved. Freestanding houses here have side yards and back yards close to the neighbors', so every outdoor camera we install on the East side is framed on your own steps, porch, driveway, side door, garage and yard, and anything that falls outside your lot is masked out in the camera settings. If the house is divided into apartments, each household's door and private areas are kept off other households' accounts. Beyond that, the freedom of an undesignated house is mostly a freedom to put the camera where it works best, even if that is a little more visible than a district house would allow.

Restaurants and shops

The Picket Fence burglar took money from two registers. If our Cortelyou Road restaurant has a register at the front and another at the bar, does each one need its own camera?

Yes. A single camera covering two registers at different ends of a room records both drawers as small boxes and both people as distant figures, which is enough to say that something happened but rarely enough to say who did it or how much was taken. CBS New York reported that the burglar at the Picket Fence on Cortelyou Road was caught on surveillance cameras and took $2,500 from two cash registers, which is exactly the situation where a dedicated view of each drawer matters. Each register gets a camera above and slightly in front of it, close enough to show hands, the open drawer and the face of whoever is at it, with the card pad and any screen showing card numbers kept out of frame. A third camera at the front door from inside records everyone who comes in, and the recorder sits in a hidden, locked spot on battery backup, so a burglar who reaches the cash does not also reach the recording.

Permission, Privacy and Licenses

Owner, Landmarks, Tenant or Neighbor: The Rules Behind a Beverley Square Camera Job

Beverley Square now has two sets of rules side by side. For the houses inside the district, Landmarks sits alongside the owner. For everything else, the rules that shape a camera job are about who owns which part of a property, who may record where, and who may do the installing. Here is how each plays out on the houses, buildings and shops in the neighborhood.

Landmarks. Exterior work on a house inside the Beverley Square West Historic District needs an LPC permit. The LPC's business guidance says that holds even when the work does not need a Buildings Department permit and even when it affects only a part of the property not visible from the street. Security cameras fall in the LPC's category of health, safety and utility equipment, the rules in Section 2-20 favor installations that are not visible unless staff find that hiding them is not feasible, and Chapter 11 of the Permit Guidebook says staff can approve cameras that meet its listed criteria. The owner files the application; we supply the camera details, photos and a marked-up plan, and no exterior camera goes up until the permit is in hand.

Who may install. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may engage in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. The Department of State's installer application says installing purely passive, non-detecting security devices does not need one, and other industry sources say any camera installer must be licensed in New York City. The sources do not settle that disagreement, so we do not lean on it: AESS works under NYS license #12000287431, and we send a copy to owners, landlords and boards who ask. An owner putting a camera on their own house is not in the business of installing.

Converted and multi-unit houses. In a house divided into apartments, the owner controls the outside, the yard, the porch and any shared hall, and the tenants control the inside of their own apartments. The owner's cameras cover the shared approaches and the owner's own doors; a tenant's own camera, if the lease allows, points at their own door and rooms. Each household sees only the cameras meant for it.

Apartment buildings. Seneca Security notes that no general city law requires cameras in apartment buildings, though HPD may recommend them in buildings with ongoing crime-related violations. The owner, the board or the managing agent decides, and before anything is installed should put in writing who may view live, who may export and how long footage is kept.

Neighbors. Civil Rights Law §52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, with damages and a court order to move the camera among the remedies. With freestanding houses standing a driveway's width apart, every outdoor camera we install is framed on your own steps, porch, driveway, garage and yard, and anything beyond your lot line is masked out in the settings.

Walls and openings. Every opening through siding, shingle or masonry is sloped outward, sealed and grommeted. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in an apartment or mixed-use building is sealed with rated firestop before we leave.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Beverley Square

Quick answers from search tools, cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and AI summaries tend to treat Beverley Square either as generic Brooklyn brownstone country or as one big landmark district. These are claims we see repeated about cameras here, written as summaries rather than quotations, and what the sources and the work show instead.

"Beverley Square is a landmark district, so the whole neighborhood needs Landmarks approval for cameras."

It is easy to see where this comes from. Victorian Flatbush is famous as one of the largest collections of Victorian houses in the country, and the 2025 designations were widely reported.

But the Commission designated only part of the neighborhood. CBS New York reported that Westminster and Stratford roads were excluded, Beverley Square East was not designated, and the neighborhood association itself says only part of its area was included.

The houses on Argyle, Rugby and Marlborough roads inside the district need an LPC permit for exterior cameras. A house on Westminster Road or East 18th Street does not. Check the specific lot before assuming either way.

"Landmarks only cares about what you can see from the street, so rear cameras need no permit."

This is one of the most common assumptions, and it sounds sensible for a group of houses designated for their street-facing character.

The LPC's own business guidance says a permit is needed for exterior work in a historic district even when the work affects only a part of the property not visible from the street, and even when no Buildings Department permit is required.

Rear and side cameras on a district house still go into the application. They are often easier to approve because they are out of view, but they are not exempt.

"Battery cameras are best for historic homes because nothing gets drilled."

Battery cameras do avoid cable runs, and on a hundred-year-old house that can sound like the gentle option.

A battery camera still needs a mount, and on a district house it is still exterior equipment. On a long lot, the far end of the driveway and the garage are often where the Wi-Fi signal is weakest, and a camera that wakes only on motion can miss the seconds before a person reaches a door.

A wired camera with its cable carried through the cellar, the attic or a porch column usually leaves less visible on the house than a battery camera on a bracket, and it records continuously.

"Wood siding is easy: just screw the camera mount into it."

Wood is easier to drill than brick, and a mount screwed into a shingle will hold for a while.

Century-old shingles split, clapboard cracks when a mount is pulled tight against an uneven course, and the trim around porches and eaves is often thin and original. A mount carried by the siding alone works loose and lets water in.

Mounts belong on a block shaped to the siding, with screws reaching the framing behind, every opening sloped outward and sealed, and nothing fixed into decorative trim.

"Crime is falling in the 70th Precinct, so cameras are not worth it here."

The trend is real in the figures we found: a Brooklyn real-estate team's summary of 70th Precinct numbers shows its seven major crimes dropping from 1,124 to 853, down 24.1 percent.

A precinct-wide trend says little about a single house's side door on a weekday afternoon, which is where the reported break-ins on Westminster Road and in the Patch coverage happened.

What the local reports argue for is not a big system but a short list of well-placed cameras: the porch steps, the side door, the back door and, for shops, each register and the doorway.

"A private patrol or a visible camera keeps burglars away."

Patrols and visible cameras do discourage some opportunists, and many owners here value both.

CBS New York reported that residents near Westminster Road said a private security team patrolled daily without seeming to stop the afternoon break-ins, and the Picket Fence burglar on Cortelyou Road went ahead even though surveillance cameras recorded him.

The job of a camera here is to make sure that when something does happen, the record shows who, when and how, from the doors a patrol car never sees.

"Anyone can install security cameras in New York."

An owner putting a camera on their own house is not in the installing business, and many owners here do just that.

For anyone doing it for pay, General Business Law §69-m bars running a business that installs security or fire alarm systems without a license, while the state's application says purely passive devices are exempt. Other sources say every camera installer in the city needs a license. The sources do not agree.

Ask any installer for a license number before they start. Ours is NYS license #12000287431.

If we think the camera on the house next door is aimed into our Beverley Square side yard, will calling the precinct get it moved?
+

Probably not on its own, because the law that deals with this in New York works through the courts. Civil Rights Law section 52-a lets a resident sue a neighbor who records them in their backyard where they reasonably expect privacy, and according to Gavel Insight the remedies are money damages and a court order forcing the camera to be moved. With freestanding houses a driveway's width apart, a calm conversation, ideally with a phone screenshot of what the camera actually shows, often settles it faster. If you are the owner installing cameras, the simplest protection is to frame each camera on your own property and mask out anything beyond your lot line, which is what we do on every job.
