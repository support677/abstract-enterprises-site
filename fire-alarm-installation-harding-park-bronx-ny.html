Harding Park is one of the most unusual places in New York City to plan fire detection. It sits at the southwestern tip of the Clason Point peninsula, where the Bronx River meets the East River, and it began as a summer colony of tents and small bungalows on beachfront land that Thomas Higgs leased out lot by lot. After the Second World War, families who could not find housing stayed through the winter, and the cottages slowly became year-round homes. City land records today list about 232 lots carrying a Harding Park address, 179 of them one-family houses, with year-built dates running from 1899 to 2023. The City Planning study of the neighborhood describes one- and two-story light-frame, combustible bungalows with ad hoc additions, set on narrow lots and very close together. Many have been widened over the years, and some have been joined to the house next door. That history is exactly what a fire alarm has to deal with: rooms added in different decades and at different floor levels, wiring extended a piece at a time, sleeping spaces in rooms nobody drew as bedrooms, and a neighbor's wall a few feet from your window. The Neighborhood

Why Harding Park Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Fire risk in a neighborhood is shaped first by what its houses are made of and how far apart they stand, and Harding Park is a very particular answer to both questions. The Department of City Planning's 2016 Resilient Neighborhoods report on Harding Park describes the homes as one- and two-story light-frame bungalows, combustible construction, many with ad hoc additions. A Queens College study of the colony's early years records that the developer laid out lots from about 25 by 40 feet to 30 by 60 feet, with bungalows and tents roughly twenty feet apart, which produced the narrow, alley-like streets residents still walk today. North of Gildersleeve Avenue, the DCP report says, lots are especially narrow and the houses sit very close together along even narrower private lanes. Wood-frame houses that close to each other give a fire in one home very little room before it becomes a problem for the next, and that makes early warning inside each house worth more here than on a block of brick row houses with masonry walls between them.

The second shaping force is how the houses grew. City Lore's account of the neighborhood describes bungalows that appear fused together into larger houses, and Scouting NY met a neighbor who had expanded her house to twice the standard bungalow width. The Fordham Ram's history piece notes that after the war some bungalows were renovated to almost double their original size. None of that was done to a single plan. Rooms were added when a family needed them, often by the family itself, and the result is a house where the original summer cottage may sit in the middle, wrapped by later rooms at slightly different floor heights, with ceilings that change from one doorway to the next. A smoke alarm that was enough for a one-room cottage in 1930 is nowhere near enough for that house, and in our experience the rooms that get missed are exactly the later ones: the back bedroom, the closed-in porch, the second-floor room reached by a steep stair.

The fourth is water. Harding Park sits between the Bronx River, the East River and Pugsley's Creek, and the DCP map shows FEMA V, A and Coastal A flood zones across the neighborhood. City land records flag 63 Harding Park lots inside the 2015 preliminary flood maps, the DCP report counts about 80 homes inside the 1 percent annual chance floodplain, and Wikipedia's summary notes that streets flood after heavy rain. Requests to 311 from Harding Park addresses include reports of excessive water in a basement as recently as 2026. Water and fire alarm equipment do not mix. Panels, power supplies, batteries and cable splices all need to sit where the water does not reach, and that decision has to be made on the first visit, not after the first storm.

Six Harding Park facts that set the plan before an alarm is chosen

  • It is a neighborhood of small houses, not towers. City land records list about 232 lots with a Harding Park address: 179 one-family houses, 28 two-family lots with about 56 homes, four small three-family buildings with 12 homes, one six-family walk-up built in 1945, and about 20 vacant lots. Other sources count 226 single-family homes (DCP, 2016) or 236 homes (Reader's Digest, 2019). Either way, nearly every job here is a house job.
  • The construction burns. DCP describes the homes as light-frame, combustible bungalows, one and two stories tall, many with additions built without a plan. Year-built dates in the land records run from 1899 to 2023.
  • The houses are close. Early lots were about 25 by 40 to 30 by 60 feet with houses about twenty feet apart, and north of Gildersleeve Avenue DCP says the houses sit very close together along very narrow lanes. South of Gildersleeve, most streets are private and 9 to 13 feet wide.
  • Fire has reshaped the neighborhood before. The Fordham Ram's history piece says the arson fires of the 1970s damaged or destroyed a significant portion of the residential buildings in Harding Park. The association's history records that its old firehouse was demolished in the summer of 2002, and the community center went up in its place.
  • A lot of the ground is on the flood maps. Sixty-three lots carry the 2015 preliminary flood map flag, DCP counts about 80 homes in the 1 percent floodplain, and the association's land is largely unsewered apart from its own private sanitary network, which DCP links to regular ponding in streets, yards and homes.
  • Families are big and stay for generations. Puerto Rican families arrived from the 1960s, the neighborhood was known as "Little Puerto Rico" by the early 1990s, and notes from the 2022 NYC Districting Commission hearings describe its generational households. More people under one roof means more sleeping spaces to protect, often in rooms added for exactly that reason.

Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Harding Park's Architecture

Reader's Digest's 2019 Nicest Places entry quoted the observation that no home here looks like another, and from a fire alarm installer's point of view that is close to literally true. Still, the houses fall into six broad kinds, and each kind has its own habits: where the old wiring runs, where the forgotten rooms are, and where the alarms tend to be missing.

The original one-story bungalow

A small light-frame cottage, often one bedroom or two, low ceilings, a small kitchen, and a crawl space or shallow cellar under part of it.

The bungalow that kept growing

The original cottage wrapped by later rooms: a back bedroom, a larger kitchen, a closed porch, sometimes a second story, all at slightly different levels.

Two cottages made into one house

Fused bungalows where two original houses were joined, with two sets of wiring, two old electrical services and a connecting room in between.

The two-family house

About 28 lots hold two-family houses, usually an owner's floor and a rental floor, sharing a stair, a cellar or a heating system.

The small three- and six-family building

Four three-family buildings and one six-family walk-up from 1945, where shared halls and stairs bring in landlord duties.

The newer or rebuilt house

Houses rebuilt or put up in recent decades, the newest with a 2023 build date in city records, designed with modern wiring from the start.

The original one-story bungalow: a little house where everything is close to the kitchen

An unaltered or lightly altered bungalow is the simplest Harding Park job, but it has its own trap. In a house this small, the bedroom, the living room and the kitchen are rarely more than a few steps apart, so an alarm placed by habit in the middle of the house ends up close to the stove and goes off whenever someone cooks. The usual response is for the household to take it down or pull the battery, which leaves the house with nothing. We plan the other way round. The alarm that protects the sleeping area goes as close to the bedroom door as the layout allows and as far from the cooking as possible, and if the only sensible spot is near the kitchen, we choose a device type the manufacturer rates for locations near cooking appliances, or a heat alarm in the kitchen itself linked to a smoke alarm further away. Low ceilings are common in the original cottages, sometimes with a slope where the roof comes down, and we follow the manufacturer's spacing from walls and peaks rather than mounting in the first flat spot. Carbon monoxide gets its own alarm wherever there is a gas stove, a gas water heater or a fuel-burning heater, close enough to the bedroom to wake the sleeper.

The bungalow that kept growing: every addition is a new room for smoke to hide in

This is the most common house in Harding Park, and it is where most of our planning effort goes. Each addition changes how smoke moves through the house. A step down into a back room, a doorway with a header lower than the ceiling on either side, a second floor reached by a narrow stair, a closed-in porch with its own small ceiling: each of those can stop smoke from reaching an alarm in the original part of the house quickly. So we treat every room added since the cottage was built as its own space and ask the same questions of each. Does anyone sleep in it? Is it heated by something that burns fuel or by a plug-in heater? Is it separated from the rest of the house by a door that is usually closed? Where does the smoke from a fire in this room go first? Then we place alarms so that a fire in any room is sensed in that room or the space right outside it, and link every alarm so the warning is heard everywhere. Wiring in these houses has usually been extended in pieces, so on the survey we find out which circuit each part of the house runs on before choosing between hardwired alarms with battery backup and a wireless linked set. In many grown bungalows the honest answer is a mix: hardwired in the original house where a clean circuit exists, and wireless-linked units in additions where running a new cable would mean opening finished walls.

Two cottages made into one house: two sets of wiring under one roof

When two bungalows were joined into one home, the house often still carries two histories: two old electrical services, sometimes two meters, two sets of alarms bought at different times, and a connecting room added to tie them together. The fire alarm needs to treat the result as one house, because the family lives in it as one house. A fire on the far side must wake the person sleeping on the near side. The practical problem is that alarms from different manufacturers, or from different generations of the same brand, usually will not talk to each other, and hardwired interconnect wires on two different electrical services should not simply be joined. We map both halves, decide which alarms can stay and which must go, and then put in one linked set across the whole house, using a single compatible family of alarms and, where the halves are on separate services, wireless interconnect between them rather than a wire. If the joining room was built as a passage and now has someone sleeping in it, it gets its own alarm too. We also note the doors between the halves, because a house that was once two houses often has a door that gets closed at night, and the alarm on the far side of it has to be loud enough to carry through.

The two-family house: two households, one fire

Harding Park has about 28 two-family lots, and in most of them an owner lives on one floor and rents the other, or relatives share the house. The two apartments usually share more than a roof. There may be one heating system in a cellar or crawl space, a shared stair, a laundry corner, and a single electrical service feeding both. A fire on either floor is a threat to both. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings two stories or fewer when each dwelling unit is separated by 1-hour fire partitions and has its own direct exit, which describes many small two-family houses, so most of these jobs are not building systems with a panel. That does not make them simple. We put working, linked alarms inside each apartment, a CO alarm near any shared fuel-burning equipment, and detection in the shared stair or cellar, and we think hard about linking. Many owners want the two apartments linked so a fire downstairs wakes the family upstairs, and that can be the right choice, but it also means a burned dinner on one floor sounds on the other. We explain the trade-off and let the owner choose, and we write down who looks after which alarms, who changes what, and who gets called when something chirps.

The small three- and six-family building: where landlord duties start to matter

A handful of Harding Park lots hold more than two homes: city records list four small three-family buildings with twelve homes between them and one six-family walk-up built in 1945. These are the buildings where shared halls, stairs and cellars serve several unrelated households, and where the owner's obligations under the city's housing and fire rules are more involved than in a house. Whether a particular building needs a fire alarm system with a panel depends on its occupancy, height, layout and how its apartments are separated, which is a code question we answer building by building rather than by rule of thumb. What we can say generally is that in a building like this, the stair is the shared way out, so detection in the halls and stair and at the top of it matters, and alarms inside each apartment remain essential whatever the building has. If an existing building system is there, we treat it as such: identical-part repairs stay repairs, while moving or replacing the control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY, as ASM Integrators' summary of the rules explains. Where a filing is needed, we work alongside a registered design professional; we do not pretend to be one.

The newer or rebuilt house: designed in from the first drawing

Some Harding Park houses have been rebuilt or newly built in recent decades, and city records show a build date as late as 2023. Rachel Frankel Architecture's account of rebuilding one bungalow describes choosing an approach that avoided an Alteration Type 1 filing, because that filing would have meant giving up the grandfathered existing conditions. That is one architect's account, not an official rule, but it shows how carefully owners here weigh the scope of any work. For a house that is being rebuilt or substantially renovated under drawings, the fire alarm should be planned with the design, not added at the end: alarm locations on the drawings, the interconnect wire run while walls are open, power to each hardwired alarm from a clearly identified circuit, and any equipment kept above the design flood elevation the architect is working to. If you are already working with an architect and a contractor, we coordinate with them on locations and install, label and test the alarms; we do not change the drawings ourselves.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How a Technician Reads a Harding Park House Before Mounting Anything

This is our routine for houses like the ones on this page. It is how we work, not an account of any particular address.

Start at the lane, not the door. The technician looks at the house from outside first: where the original roofline ends and the additions begin, how close the neighbors' walls are, where the windows of each sleeping room face, and where a second way out of each room might be. The roofline usually tells you the order the house was built in faster than the owner can.

Walk the house in the order it was built. Inside, we start in the oldest room and walk outward into each addition, noting every step up or down, every low header, every door that is usually shut, and every room someone sleeps in, whatever it was built as.

Find the power. At the electrical panel, we look for which breakers feed which part of the house, whether there is more than one service, and whether existing hardwired alarms share a circuit with something that gets switched off. If the panel labels are missing, we trace them.

Look at the fuel and the heat. Boiler, water heater, gas stove, dryer, space heaters, and anywhere e-bikes or scooters are charged. Firehouse.com reported in 2026 that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent that year, so charging spots go on the sheet.

Ask about the water last. How high it came, which rooms it reached, and how long it stood. That answer decides where nothing goes.

Compliance notes for businesses near Harding Park

  • Whether a system is required. Section 907 of the NYC Building Code sets out which occupancies and conditions require a fire alarm system. The answer depends on occupancy, size and what else is in the building, not on the type of business alone.
  • Plans come first. The FDNY's Fire Code Chapter 9 requires design and installation documents for fire alarm systems to be submitted to the Fire Department for review and approval before installation.
  • What a filing contains. The NYC Business portal lists a Certificate of Occupancy or approved Schedule A, floor plans, a riser diagram, an operating sequence matrix, approval by a New York State registered engineer or architect, and a fire alarm plan involving a licensed Master Electrician and an FDNY S97 certificate holder.
  • Repair or redesign. Replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no design change is a repair. Moving or replacing a fire alarm control panel needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Small residential buildings. The 2022 Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system in buildings of two stories or fewer where each dwelling unit is separated by 1-hour fire partitions and has a direct exit, which is relevant to the mixed buildings of two stories on these streets.
  • Kitchens. The 2022 Fire Code requires sprinklers in many occupancies and within 30 feet of commercial cooking equipment. Sprinklers and a fire alarm are separate decisions.
  • Outages in occupied buildings. FC 901.7.5.2 sets thresholds for systems out of service 8 or more hours in 24, or 30 or more hours in a month, which govern how we stage any upgrade in a building that stays open.
  • No landmark layer. We found no landmark designation for Harding Park or these blocks, so the Landmarks Preservation Commission is not part of the process here, unlike in historic districts elsewhere in the city.
Wood, Distance and Time

Twenty Feet Between Wooden Houses: Why Minutes Matter More Here

Early warning in the room where fires start. In a small wooden house, the useful question is not just whether there is an alarm on each floor but whether a fire in the most likely rooms is sensed in those rooms or immediately outside them. The kitchen, a room with a space heater, a room where batteries are charged, a room with an old extension cord running to an addition: those get detection close by, with heat alarms where smoke alarms would false-alarm. A restoration company's marketing page for the neighborhood lists aging electrical wiring, overloaded outlets and older heating equipment as common fire causes here. That is a company's sales copy, not a fire department statistic, but it matches what our survey sheets are built to catch.

Linking is the single biggest improvement. In a house where an addition sits behind a closed door and down a step, a fire in that room may not be heard by someone asleep at the other end of the house until it is well advanced. Interconnected alarms remove that gap: when one sounds, they all sound. In most of the Harding Park houses we see, the alarms that exist are not linked, either because they were bought one at a time or because the additions got battery-only units. Linking them, whether by a hardwired interconnect or a wireless set, is often the most valuable thing we do in the whole visit.

The neighborhood has seen what fire can do. The Fordham Ram's history piece on Harding Park says the arson fires of the 1970s damaged or destroyed a significant portion of the residential buildings here. More recently, Norwood News reported on a two-alarm residential fire at 344 White Plains Road, which it placed in the Harding Park section near Clason Point, in which three firefighters were injured and five residents were displaced; the article's date is unclear and the cause was under investigation, so we draw no conclusions from it about alarms. We mention both only because residents remember them, and because they show what any wooden neighborhood already knows.

The firehouse is gone; the engine company is on Story Avenue. The association's own history records that the old firehouse on association land was demolished in the summer of 2002 and the community center built in its place. The FDNY companies listed for the area, Engine 96 and Ladder 54, operate from 1689 Story Avenue. A fire department can only respond after somebody calls. In a house where everyone is asleep, the alarm is what starts that call, and for a house that stands empty part of the year, a monitored alarm is what makes the call for you.

Water and Grandfathered Walls

Flood Lots and Grandfathered Houses: Keeping the Job Small, Dry and Inside the House

Two things make Harding Park homeowners cautious about any work on their houses, and both affect how we plan a fire alarm. One is water: much of the neighborhood is on the flood maps, and many lots pond after heavy rain. The other is paperwork: many houses here were built or extended long before current rules, and owners do not want a small project to drag the whole house into a large filing. This section explains how we handle each. We are installers, not architects, engineers or lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. It is how we keep a Harding Park fire alarm job small, dry and inside the house.

Nothing important at the low point. The DCP report on Harding Park notes that the association's land is largely unsewered apart from its own private sanitary network, which it links to regular ponding in streets, yards and homes, and City Planning's study of the neighborhood was written to help owners retrofit single-family homes and bungalows for flood risk, including by moving critical systems up out of the water's way. For a fire alarm, that translates into a short list. No control panel, power supply, link module or battery at floor level in a crawl space or cellar. No splice boxes where water stands. Cable routed high on walls or through ceilings rather than along the floor. Alarms in a cellar or crawl space only where they serve a purpose, such as a heat or CO alarm near a boiler, mounted on the ceiling where water is least likely to reach and linked to the alarms upstairs so they are heard.

After water comes in, test before you trust. When a crawl space or cellar has flooded, anything electrical that was wet should be checked before it is relied on. For a household fire alarm, that means testing every alarm, checking any hardwired connection that was in the wet area, and replacing anything that stood in water. Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Harding Park: Questions Answered

These are the situations Harding Park homeowners, landlords and nearby business owners raise with us: houses that grew in pieces, families spread across rooms built for other purposes, houses close enough to share a warning, damp air off the water, and shops just outside the neighborhood. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Houses that grew one room at a time

Our bungalow was a summer cottage that has been added onto at least twice, and the rooms sit at three different floor levels. How do the alarms reach all of it?

By treating each level and each addition as its own space and then linking every alarm together. A step down into a back room or a step up into a porch often comes with a header or a change in ceiling height, and smoke from a fire in that room can pool there before it reaches the original part of the house. So each addition with a sleeping space gets an alarm inside the room or just outside its door, each level gets at least one alarm on the path people would use to get out, and any room with a fuel-burning heater or appliance gets CO detection close by. Then the whole set is interconnected so that a fire in the farthest addition sounds at every alarm. In houses like this, the old wiring rarely reaches every addition cleanly, so we often use hardwired alarms with battery backup in the original house and wireless-linked alarms of the same family in the additions, which join one network without new cable through finished walls. At the end we test from every bedroom with the door shut.

Part of our house used to be the bungalow next door before the two were joined. For alarm purposes, is it one house or two?

If one household lives in it, treat it as one house, because a fire on either side has to wake everyone. Joined bungalows often carry two of everything: two old electrical services, sometimes two meters, two collections of alarms bought at different times, and a connecting room that started as a hallway. We map both halves, check every alarm's date and brand, and replace the set with one compatible family of alarms linked across the whole house. If each half still runs on its own electrical service, we do not tie hardwired interconnect wires between the two services; we use wireless interconnect between the halves instead, which keeps the electrical systems separate while the alarms act as one. If the two halves are still lived in as two separate homes, we plan it like a two-family house and discuss with you whether the two sides should be linked, because a burned pot on one side would then sound on the other. Either way, the connecting room gets its own alarm if anyone sleeps or charges anything in it.

We have heard that work on grandfathered houses here can trigger an Alteration filing. Could putting in new smoke and CO alarms do that?

Not the way we scope it. An architect who has rebuilt a house in Harding Park describes avoiding an Alteration Type 1 filing because it would have meant giving up the grandfathered conditions of the existing building, and that concern is real for construction projects. Installing household smoke and carbon monoxide alarms is not construction. It does not change the layout, the use, the number of homes, the exits or the structure. We mount alarms on existing ceilings and walls, power them from existing circuits or use battery-powered linked units, and make no structural openings. The written scope says exactly that, so you have it on paper. Where we would stop and ask questions is if the job were part of something larger: adding a dwelling unit, converting a space to a different use, or a renovation under drawings. Then the architect or engineer decides what needs to be filed, and our alarm work fits inside their plan. We will not tell you a larger project needs no filing; that is not our call to make.

Our hardwired alarms are on a circuit someone extended years ago, and the panel labels are long gone. Which circuit should new hardwired alarms run on?

One we can identify, that stays on, and that is not shared with something likely to trip or be switched off. In houses that were extended piece by piece, it is common to find hardwired alarms spliced onto whatever circuit was nearest when the addition went in, sometimes the same circuit as a bathroom heater, a window air conditioner or a kitchen appliance. That causes two problems. A tripped breaker can silently cut power to the alarms, leaving them on battery backup, and nobody knows which breaker to reset. We trace the circuits with a tone generator and a tester, label the panel for the alarm circuit, and confirm that every hardwired alarm in the set is powered from a known circuit. If the existing wiring is in poor condition or the circuit is shared in a way that is not sensible, we tell you, and either use battery-powered linked alarms in that part of the house or recommend a licensed electrician to deal with the circuit first. All hardwired alarms we install have battery backup, so a storm outage does not leave the house unprotected.

We closed in our front porch years ago and now one of the kids sleeps out there. Does it need an alarm of its own?

Yes. Any room someone sleeps in should be protected as a sleeping space, whatever it was built as. A closed-in porch is one of the most common sleeping rooms we find in Harding Park, and it is often the most exposed: lighter construction than the rest of the house, sometimes heated by a plug-in heater or charged-up devices, often separated from the house by what used to be the front door. A fire inside the porch needs to be sensed there, and a fire elsewhere in the house needs to be heard there. So the porch gets a smoke alarm of its own, linked to the rest of the house, and if anything that burns fuel is used in or near it, a CO alarm too. We also look at the way out. A porch that was enclosed with fixed glass or with windows that do not open far has fewer ways out than people assume, and if the only exit is back through the house, we say so plainly. Space heaters should not be in that room overnight; if they are, they should be kept well clear of bedding and curtains and plugged directly into the wall.

Families, neighbors and the air off the water

My sister lives in the bungalow a few feet from ours. Can our two houses' alarms be linked so each family hears a fire in the other house?

It is technically possible in some cases and we will talk it through, but we usually recommend a different approach. Wireless interconnected alarms have a limited range and are meant to work within a single dwelling, and linking two separate houses means a burned dinner or a test in one house sets off the alarms in the other, which tends to lead to someone switching things off. It also blurs who looks after what. What we usually suggest instead is that each house gets its own properly linked set, and that the families add a simple, reliable way to alert each other: monitoring through our partners for one or both houses, which calls the account holders and emergency services, or smart alarms that send a notification to a phone, set up so each family receives the other's alerts. Close neighbors in Harding Park already watch out for each other; the goal is to back that up with something that works at three in the morning. If you still want the houses linked, we check the product's range and the walls between, test it thoroughly, and put the arrangement in writing.

Our grandmother's room can only be reached by walking through the kitchen. Besides an alarm, what helps her get out?

A plan for a second way out, and an alarm she will hear early enough to use it. Rooms reached only through the kitchen are common in bungalows that were extended at the back, and the kitchen is where many house fires begin. If a fire starts there at night, her only route out leads straight into it. So we look at three things. First, detection: a smoke alarm in or just outside her room, a heat alarm in the kitchen that will not false-alarm from cooking, and both linked to the rest of the house so everyone is woken together. Second, the second exit: whether her window opens wide enough and low enough for her to get through it, or whether someone in the family can reach her from outside; if the window is painted shut or blocked, that is worth fixing. Third, the plan: who wakes her, who helps her, and where everyone meets outside, practiced once with the alarm sounding. If she has difficulty hearing, tell us, because that changes the alarm we choose for her room.

On summer nights, smoke from the neighbors' grills drifts in through our windows and sets off the hall alarm. How do we stop that without losing protection?

Move or change the alarm that is catching it, and never take it down. When houses stand a few feet apart, smoke from a backyard grill or a fire pit can come straight through an open window into a hallway, and a sensitive smoke alarm placed near that window will do exactly its job. The fix starts with where the alarm sits. We look at which window the smoke enters through, how the air moves through the house when windows are open, and whether the hall alarm can be moved a few feet farther from that window while still protecting the bedrooms it serves. If the alarm is old, it may also have become more prone to nuisance alarms as it aged, and replacing it with a current photoelectric model, with a hush button that silences it briefly, often solves the problem. What we will not do is replace a smoke alarm protecting sleeping rooms with a heat alarm just to avoid nuisance trips, because heat alarms are slower for the smoky fires that are dangerous at night. Keeping windows nearest the alarm closed when the neighbors are grilling helps too.

Our house never really dries out in summer, being so close to the water. Does that humidity cause false alarms, and what can be done?

It can, especially with older alarms and in rooms where steam or damp air collects. Very humid air, condensation and steam can trigger some smoke alarms, and an alarm that has lived for years in a damp room near the water can also have dust and moisture inside it that make it more sensitive. Harding Park is surrounded on three sides by water, and many houses have rooms, such as a bathroom without an exhaust fan or a damp back addition, where the air stays heavy. We start by checking the age and condition of every alarm, because a worn alarm is a common culprit. Then we look at placement: no smoke alarm right outside a bathroom door where shower steam comes out, none in a damp laundry corner, and none in the path of a humidifier. Where an alarm must protect a space that is often damp, we pick models rated for those conditions and follow the manufacturer's guidance on locations to avoid. If the house has a persistent damp problem, the alarms are not the only thing that suffers, and we will say so.

The homeowners association owns the land under our house. Do we need its permission before putting a fire alarm inside?

For an alarm entirely inside your home, we found nothing that says you do. The Harding Park Homeowners Association owns and manages the common ground and the private lanes, and its history describes it as a cooperative that residents joined when the land was bought in the early 1980s. We looked for published association rules on alarms or exterior equipment and found none, so we cannot tell you what the association's own documents say. What we can say is that a household fire alarm is interior work: alarms on your ceilings and walls, cable inside your house, nothing on the lane or the outside of the building. If you are unsure, ask at the association's office or at a board meeting, which the association lists as every second Wednesday at 7 p.m. at the Community Center. Where something would go outside, such as a monitoring antenna, or onto shared ground, we would ask you to check with the association first, and we design to keep everything inside so the question rarely comes up.

Our tire shop on Soundview Avenue stacks hundreds of tires indoors overnight. What kind of detection makes sense for that stock?

Detection chosen for the conditions in each part of the shop, with the storage area treated seriously. A tire fire is hard to put out and produces dense, heavy smoke, so a fire in a stack of tires after closing is something you want known about early and reported to someone, not discovered in the morning. How can I tell whether the alarms already in my house are linked to each other?
+

Press and hold the test button on one alarm while someone stands near another in a different room. If the second alarm sounds too, they are linked. If only the one you pressed sounds, they are not. Our house is all electric with no gas at all. Do we still need carbon monoxide alarms?
+

A house with no fuel-burning appliances, no attached garage and no fireplace has far fewer sources of carbon monoxide, but check carefully before deciding. A neighbor's generator close to your windows, a portable heater someone brings in, or a car running near the house can all bring CO indoors. Because houses here stand so close together, we usually recommend combination smoke and CO alarms near the sleeping rooms anyway.

How do I quiet an alarm that is sounding for burnt toast without switching it off?
+

Most current alarms have a hush or silence button that quiets them for several minutes and then returns them to normal automatically. Press it, open a window and clear the air. Never pull the battery or take the alarm down, because that is how houses end up unprotected. If one alarm goes off from cooking more than occasionally, it is in the wrong place and should be moved or changed.
