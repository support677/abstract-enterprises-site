The Neighborhood

Why Coney Island Properties Need TV Installation

Most people picture Coney Island as the boardwalk, the rides and the beach. The people who call us about a TV mostly live a few blocks inland, in towers. The neighborhood's housing was rebuilt in the decades after the war around large apartment campuses, and those campuses still shape the work. Trump Village was completed in 1963 and 1964 on the old Culver Depot site, designed by Morris Lapidus and built by Fred Trump for about $70 million. 6sqft, citing Crain's, counted seven 23-story towers and about 3,700 apartments. Five of the buildings are co-ops; the other two were Mitchell-Lama rentals until 2007, and Brownstoner reported in 2015 that those two, at 2940 and 3000 Ocean Parkway, had been gut renovated and renamed Shorecrest Towers, with 880 apartments, oak floors and marble bathrooms. Nearby, the Luna Park Housing Corporation is a Mitchell-Lama co-op of about 1,600 apartments and more than 5,000 residents. Add the NYCHA developments, from Coney Island Houses to the Sites 4/5 buildings, and a large share of the households in the neighborhood live above the fifth floor in a building with a board, a management office or a housing authority between them and the wall.

That matters for a TV in three ways. The first is the wall itself. Towers of that era were built with heavy structure, and the wall you want for the screen may be poured concrete, concrete block behind plaster, or a hollow partition that sounds solid until you drill it. A stud finder that works in a frame house is close to useless on a concrete wall, and a drill that hits reinforcing steel has to stop. The second is the building. A co-op office or managing agent usually wants to know who is coming, when, and with what insurance, and many buildings set working hours for any drilling. The third is the trip up. A 75-inch TV in its box is a two-person carry that has to fit in a passenger elevator, sometimes after a wait in a busy lobby, and that is part of the plan, not a surprise on the day.

The newest buildings are a different job again. 1515 Surf Avenue, built by LCOR on the former parking lot of Gargiulo's restaurant, opened in September 2024 with 463 apartments in two towers. 6sqft described it as entirely electric, heated and cooled by the largest active geothermal system in the city's history, with oversized windows facing the ocean and the amusement park, in-unit washers and dryers, and smart-home technology in the apartments. Rybak Development's Tilyou Towers, the first housing parcel of the city's Coney Island West plan, is planned as 505 apartments in two 28-story towers on an 11-story podium, with construction that could start in 2027. New buildings like these tend to have drywall on metal studs rather than wood, and big glass on the ocean side. Metal studs hold a screw poorly, so the anchor has to change; and a wall of windows facing the water decides where a screen can go without the afternoon light washing it out.

Then there is the water. Coney Island sits on a low peninsula, and Hurricane Sandy, on October 29, 2012, showed how far the ocean can come in. Engineering News-Record reported two months later that a beach shop about 200 yards from the Atlantic had taken about four feet of water, and that a gas-fitting crew was finding homes with "nine feet of water in them." The owner of that shop, Haim Haddad, put it plainly: "all my electrical equipment is swimming in the ocean." Brooklyn Paper's ten-year look back counted at least 38 ground-floor apartments destroyed across Coney Island's three public housing complexes. The lesson for a TV is simple and cheap to follow. On a ground floor, a basement den or a house in a low block, we keep the cable box, the router, the console and the power strip on a shelf or behind the screen rather than on the floor, and we plan the soundbar and the cords so that nothing that plugs in lives at the lowest point of the room. It costs nothing extra to plan it this way, and it is the difference between a soaked carpet and a lost setup.

The last thing that shapes a Coney Island TV job is the season. Much of the commercial strip, from the boardwalk to Surf Avenue, works on a summer calendar, and the screens in those businesses have to be up and tested before the first warm weekend, then sit through a closed winter. In June 2026 the neighborhood held a World Cup watch party with the Brooklyn Cyclones at Maimonides Park, screening USA against Australia, which is a good picture of how sport and crowds run through the summer here. Bars and restaurants want screens that customers can see from the counter and the tables; homes want the game on in the living room when the stadium is not an option. For us that means the spring is busy with business screens and the rest of the year with homes, and we plan the visit around both.

Same-Day Repair in Coney Island

Need Repair in Coney Island? A Bracket Pulling Out of Plaster, a Screen Knocked Crooked, or a Setup Left Dead After a Storm

The urgent TV calls from the peninsula tend to fall into three groups. The first is a bracket that is coming away from the wall, usually in an older walk-up on or near Mermaid Avenue where someone hung a heavy screen on plastic plugs pushed into old plaster. The plaster holds for a while, then cracks around the plugs, and the top of the TV starts to lean forward. The second is a screen that was knocked, often by a child or a dog, and now hangs crooked on a full-motion arm that has lost its tension. The third is a setup that stopped working after a coastal storm or a power cut: the TV comes on but the cable box, the soundbar or the streaming stick does not, and nobody is sure which piece failed.

If a screen is leaning, do not try to push it back flat. Unplug it, clear the floor below, and if it is safe to do so with two people, lift it off the bracket and lay it face up on a blanket. Then send us a photo of the bracket and the wall behind it at (347) 934-8335. Taking down an old TV is $85 on our Brooklyn list, and we price re-mounting it on the correct anchors from the same list once we see the wall. For a setup that went dead after a storm or an outage, a setup-only visit is $195 for the first hour and then billed in 30-minute blocks, and it covers finding which device failed, resetting the TV and its inputs and getting the streaming apps back. If anything got wet, it stays unplugged until it has been looked at. We do not do electrical work, so if an outlet or a circuit is the problem, we will tell you that and you will need a licensed electrician for that part.

The renovated rental tower

Former Mitchell-Lama rentals such as Shorecrest Towers at 2940 and 3000 Ocean Parkway, gut renovated with new kitchens, oak floors and marble baths. New finishes over old walls.

The public housing development

NYCHA buildings including Coney Island Houses and the Sites 4/5 buildings, rebuilt after Sandy with new electrical systems and flood-protected equipment. Lease rules come first.

The Mermaid Avenue walk-up

Older three- and four-story buildings with apartments above shops, plaster walls, narrow stairs and fronts that face a busy avenue.

The Sea Gate house

About 850 houses behind the gate at the western tip of the peninsula: wood framing, fireplaces, porches and finished basements, many of them rented out by floor.

The new Surf Avenue tower

1515 Surf Avenue now and Tilyou Towers to come: metal studs, big ocean-side glass, smart-home systems and a management office with its own contractor rules.

The 1960s co-op tower: concrete, a board and the elevator

In a tower of this age the walls fall into two groups. Some are structure, either poured concrete or block, often finished with plaster or a skim coat so they look like any other wall. Others are partitions between rooms, and in older towers those can be block too, while later renovations may have added drywall partitions. Before we bring anything up, we ask for a photo of the wall and a description of the knock: a hard, flat sound that hurts the knuckles usually means masonry. On site, the scanner is used for steel rather than studs. In a concrete wall the danger is not a missing stud; it is hitting reinforcing bar or a conduit buried in the pour, so we scan the whole bracket area, mark anything metal, and position the bracket so its holes land in clear concrete. The anchors are sleeve or wedge anchors sized to the TV and the bracket, set in holes that are blown out clean so the anchor can bite.

The building is the other half. A co-op office usually wants the contractor's name, the date and the certificate of insurance before anyone goes upstairs, and many buildings limit drilling to set weekday hours. Brick Underground notes that alteration agreements vary from building to building and typically bar tampering with structure and building-wide systems; a TV bracket is far smaller than that, but a board can still have its own rules about drilling into structural walls. We send the insurance paperwork ahead, book the visit inside the building's work hours, and if your building wants the job approved in writing first, we give you a short description of what we will drill and where so you can pass it to the office. On the day, the box goes up in the passenger elevator on a blanket, and big screens get two technicians so nobody is wrestling a 75-inch panel through a lobby alone.

The renovated rental tower: new finishes, old bones

When an older tower is gut renovated, the kitchens, floors and bathrooms change, but the building's structure stays. That can leave a room where one wall is the original masonry and the wall beside it is a new drywall partition, or where drywall has been glued or furred out in front of the old wall. Brownstoner's 2015 report on Shorecrest Towers described new oak floors, marble bathrooms and lacquer kitchens throughout 880 apartments; nothing in a description like that tells you what is behind the paint on the TV wall. So in these buildings we treat every wall as unknown until we have scanned it and drilled one small pilot hole where the screen will cover it. The pilot hole tells us in seconds whether the bit is going through gypsum and then air, through gypsum and then masonry, or straight into concrete, and we choose the anchor from that.

Renters in these buildings also have a lease to think about. Brick Underground quoted a property manager saying that "a nail or screw-sized hole from a picture frame or shelving system would fall within wear and tear," and an attorney warning that "careless drilling of the wall or drilling to hold heavy hangings" can be treated as more than wear and tear and charged to the tenant. A TV bracket is a heavy hanging. The practical answer is to ask the management office in writing before the job, keep the reply, and if they say no, use a stand. We can do either, and we will tell you honestly which one your lease makes sensible.

The public housing development: start with the lease

NYCHA rebuilt much of its Coney Island stock after Sandy. Its own releases describe the Coney Island Houses project as a $90 million recovery and resiliency effort for over 1,100 residents in five buildings, and the Sites 4/5 project as a $41 million job for more than 1,000 residents, both with new electrical systems and flood-protected equipment. Inside the apartments, the walls belong to the housing authority, and what a resident may fix to them is set by the lease and the house rules, not by us. If your lease or your development office allows a wall mount, we will do it on the correct anchors. If it does not, or you are not sure, the stand setup for $119.99 puts a large TV on a stable stand with the cords tidy and no holes at all, and that is often the better call.

The other thing we see in larger public housing buildings is the trip. If the elevator is out on the day, a big TV may not be able to reach a high floor safely, and we would rather move the appointment than carry a 65-inch panel up twenty flights. We ask about the elevator when we confirm the booking, and we tell you straight if the size of the screen and the stairs do not mix.

The Mermaid Avenue walk-up: plaster, stairs and street noise

The older buildings along and around Mermaid Avenue are mostly three- and four-story walk-ups with shops or offices on the ground floor and apartments above. Inside, the walls are usually plaster, and plaster in an old Brooklyn building can sit on wood lath, metal lath or directly on brick. Each holds a screw differently. On wood lath, we find the studs and lag into them; on brick, the bracket goes into the masonry behind the plaster; on metal lath, we use fixings that reach through it to something solid. What we never do is rely on plastic plugs in plaster alone, which is the most common reason for the leaning-bracket calls described above.

These apartments also tend to have the TV wall on the same side as the windows or on a party wall shared with the next building. A party wall of solid brick is a good wall for a bracket and a poor one for hiding cables, because nothing can be fished through it. For those, a paintable cord cover at $59 is the clean answer. And because many of these buildings face a busy avenue, we talk about where the soundbar goes: pointing it into the room and away from the window makes the dialogue clearer without turning it up.

The Sea Gate house: framing, fireplaces and the floor below

Sea Gate is the part of Coney Island where a TV job looks most like one in a suburb. The houses are mostly wood framed, which is the easiest wall to mount on and the easiest to hide cables in, and many have fireplaces, porches and finished basements. A screen above a fireplace adds $130 on our list, because it means working higher, checking the heat that rises from the firebox and usually running the cables down the chimney breast rather than through an empty stud bay. If the chimney breast is brick, the +$65 brick line applies as well. On a framed wall, the $169 in-wall kit gives the TV its own power inlet behind the screen and a clean path for the cables to the cabinet below.

Many Sea Gate houses are rented out by floor. Wikipedia puts the share of rental housing in the enclave at about 54 percent, which means an owner downstairs, a tenant upstairs and two sets of opinions about holes in the wall. In a house that took water in 2012, the basement or ground floor may also have been rebuilt, so the walls there can be newer than the rest of the house. We ask about both. And because Sea Gate is a gated community with its own public safety department, we ask how your gate handles visitors so the technician's name is with the officers before he arrives.

The new Surf Avenue tower: metal studs, big glass and building systems

Buildings like 1515 Surf Avenue are built quickly and to a high standard, and their walls are almost always drywall on metal studs. A metal stud is thin steel; a wood screw strips in it, and a bracket hung that way will eventually sag. The answer is a toggle-style anchor rated for the load, spread across the bracket so the weight is shared. Because these buildings have smart-home technology and their own network equipment, we also look for anything in the wall before we drill: a data outlet, a thermostat cable, a smart switch. If a building's equipment needs to move, that is the management office's call, not ours.

The other feature of these towers is glass. 6sqft described oversized windows with ocean and amusement park views, which is the reason people move in. It also means the brightest part of the room may be directly opposite the only long wall. Before any bracket goes up we tape the outline of the screen on the wall and look at it with the blinds open at the time of day you watch most. Sometimes the answer is a slight tilt; sometimes it is the shorter wall at right angles to the windows. Either way, the decision is made with tape, not with holes.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Mounting a TV High Up in a Concrete Tower Without Making an Enemy of the Neighbors

These notes describe how our technicians handle a TV in a concrete or block wall in a tall building, which is the most common kind of job on the peninsula. They are the steps, not a story about a particular apartment.

Ask about hours before the visit. Concrete drilling is loud, and the sound travels through a tower's structure to apartments above and below. Most buildings with a board or a management office set the hours when drilling is allowed, so we book inside them and tell the office who is coming. If a neighbor works nights, the household usually knows, and we can put the drilling at the start of the visit so it is over in one short burst.

Scan for steel, not studs. The scanner goes over the whole area the bracket will cover, and every hit is marked in pencil. If a planned hole lands on metal, we shift the bracket rather than force the bit. Some brackets have more mounting slots than the TV needs, which gives room to move.

Protect the room and catch the dust. Concrete dust is fine and travels. A blanket goes on the floor, the sofa is covered, and the drill is fitted with a dust catcher or a helper holds a vacuum at the hole. Every hole is blown out before the anchor goes in, because an anchor in a dusty hole holds a fraction of its rating.

Anchors sized to the load. The TV's weight comes from its model number, and the bracket's weight is added to it. A full-motion arm puts far more leverage on the top anchors than a flat bracket does, so arms get more and larger anchors. Each anchor is tightened to the maker's figure, not by feel.

Cords on the surface, done neatly. On a concrete wall there is no hollow bay to fish a cable through, so the power cord and the HDMI run in a paintable cover from the screen to the cabinet. It goes on straight, gets painted to the wall color if you want, and looks deliberate rather than patched.

Leave nothing on the floor that plugs in. On low floors especially, the cable box and the power strip go on a shelf or behind the screen. Higher up, the same habit keeps cords away from a vacuum and the children.

Walls, Water and Air

Concrete, Salt Air and the Flood Line: What a Coney Island TV Has to Survive

The base price on our Brooklyn list, $179.99, covers a TV up to 55 inches on drywall over wood studs. That wall exists in Coney Island, mostly in Sea Gate houses and newer partitions, but it is not the wall most households here have. This section goes wall by wall, then covers the two things that make a peninsula job different from one in the middle of the borough: water from below and salt in the air.

Poured concrete. The structural walls of a 1960s tower, and sometimes the wall between two apartments, are solid concrete. They are the strongest wall a TV can go on and the hardest to drill. We use a hammer drill with masonry bits, scan for steel first, and set sleeve or wedge anchors in clean holes. Concrete is priced from our Brooklyn list once we see your photos, and nothing about it can be fished, so the cords run in a cover.

Concrete block behind plaster. Block walls are hollow inside the cells and solid at the webs, which means an anchor has to land in the right place to hold. A pilot hole tells us whether we have hit a web or a void, and we use anchors made for hollow masonry where we need to. These walls can look exactly like plaster on lath from the room side.

Solid brick or stone (+$65). Party walls in the older walk-ups, chimney breasts in Sea Gate houses and some exterior walls are brick. A brick wall holds a bracket well if the anchors go into the brick and not into the mortar joints between them, so we mark the joints before drilling.

Plaster (+$45). Plaster walls in prewar walk-ups and older houses crack if they are drilled carelessly. We score the surface, use sharp bits, and reach through the plaster to whatever is behind it. Plaster on brick gets the brick treatment underneath.

Drywall on metal studs. Common in the new Surf Avenue buildings and in renovated apartments. Metal studs need toggle-style anchors rated for the weight. This is priced from the Brooklyn list when we see your photos.

Water from below: planning around the flood line

Sandy put several feet of water into ground floors and basements across the peninsula in 2012. Engineering News-Record's report from December of that year described flooding that came both from ocean water flowing under the boardwalk and from sewer overflows, and a restaurant building where the ground floor took about four feet of water while the second-floor dining room had no significant damage. That last detail is the useful one: a few feet of height made all the difference. The NYC Department of Buildings' own guidance on flood-resistant construction treats new equipment placed below the design flood elevation as an increase in noncompliance in affected buildings, which is the city's way of saying the same thing.

A TV setup is cheap to protect. The screen itself is on the wall, so the question is everything that plugs into it. On a ground floor, in a basement den or in a low house, we put the cable box, router, console and power strip on a shelf, a wall-mounted bracket or the back of the TV, not on the floor or the bottom shelf of a cabinet. We route cords down from the screen to that shelf, not along the baseboard. We leave the outlet where it is, because moving or adding an outlet is electrical work and we do not do electrical work; if you want an outlet higher on the wall, that is a job for a licensed electrician, and we can plan the mount around where they put it. And we show you which single plug to pull when a coastal flood warning comes, so the whole setup can be switched off in seconds.

Salt in the air: what changes near the boardwalk

Air off the ocean carries salt, and salt is hard on bare steel. Inside a closed apartment it makes little difference. On an enclosed porch in Sea Gate, in a boardwalk business with a roll-up front, or in a restaurant that props its doors open all summer, it does. Screws and brackets with bare steel can show rust within a season or two, and the inputs on the back of a TV can corrode if they are exposed to the air every day.

For those spaces we choose powder-coated or stainless brackets and hardware where they are available, keep the screen out of direct spray and rain, and point the inputs down so moisture does not sit in them. A regular indoor TV is not built for a space that is open to the weather, so if a screen will live somewhere that is effectively outdoors, we tell you that before you buy it rather than after. We do not promise a TV will survive a space it was not made for; we tell you which spaces are a fair bet and which are not.

Hiding the wires without breaking the electrical code

The request that follows "mount it" is nearly always "and hide the wires." The shortcut that many people try, cutting a hole behind the TV and another above the cabinet and pushing the TV's power cord through the wall between them, is against the National Electrical Code. Gizmodo's summary of section 400.8 quotes the rule plainly: flexible cords are not to be used "where concealed by walls, floors, or ceilings." A cord inside a wall can overheat, get crushed or chewed, and nobody can see it.

On a framed wall, the code-compliant answer is the $169 in-wall power and cable kit: a recessed inlet behind the screen and a matching plate low on the wall, joined by rated in-wall cable, so the TV plugs into its own inlet and the HDMI and other cables share the same path. It needs a hollow stud bay, which Sea Gate houses and newer partitions have and concrete tower walls do not. On concrete, block and brick, the honest answer is a paintable cord cover for $59, run straight from the screen to the cabinet. It is visible, but it is neat, safe and legal, and painted to match it nearly disappears.
