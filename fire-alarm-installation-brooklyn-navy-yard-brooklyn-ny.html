The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Spaces Need Fire Alarms Planned With the Landlord

Brooklyn Navy Yard spaces need commercial fire alarm work planned with the landlord from the first sketch, because every building inside the fence belongs to the same public owner and every tenant system has to fit into that owner's buildings. The Yard sits on Wallabout Bay between Navy Street, Flushing Avenue and Kent Avenue, with the East River on its northern edge and Vinegar Hill, Fort Greene, Clinton Hill and Williamsburg around it. The federal government opened a naval shipyard here in 1801, built the USS Monitor, the USS Arizona and the USS Missouri, and employed about 70,000 people at the height of the Second World War. The Navy closed it in 1966 and the city bought it for redevelopment.

Today the Brooklyn Navy Yard Development Corporation (BNYDC) runs the campus as real estate developer and property manager for the city. Its first Impact Report, published in January 2026 for fiscal year 2025, counts more than 550 businesses, more than 13,000 jobs and 17 industries on a campus of about 300 acres, with more than 2.5 billion dollars of annual economic impact. "We are building the future of urban industry right here in Brooklyn," said Lindsay Greene, the corporation's president and CEO. A former BNYDC president, David Ehrenberg, put the ownership more bluntly to Untapped New York in 2018: "We're publicly owned, taxpayers own this place." City Council Member Lincoln Restler said much the same in September 2026, when he and Council Member Crystal Hudson raised neighbors' complaints about all-night events: "The Navy Yard is essentially a city agency."

For fire alarm work, public ownership has a practical meaning. BNYDC's 2026 Tenant Manual says that "plans of the proposed work must be submitted to and approved by BNYDC" before work starts, sends tenants to Article 12 of their lease and to a separate set of Tenant Alterations Guidelines, and treats unapproved work, or work whose permits were never closed, as a default under the lease. It requires that "any and all electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician." It also asks every tenant to name a Fire Safety & Security contact in the BNYDC ID Portal, whose stated role is "Local Fire Alarm contact for FDNY response/emergency contact." In other words, the landlord expects to know who answers for the alarm in your space.

The mix of uses is what makes the design interesting. One building may hold a woodshop, a coffee roaster, a robotics startup, a garment maker and an architecture office on different floors, while across the road a film studio campus, a grocery store and a ship repair yard run on very different hours. So what is a commercial fire alarm system in a Navy Yard building? It is the control panel, the smoke, heat and flame-sensitive detection, the manual pull stations, the horns, strobes and speakers, the modules that shut down air handlers, release doors and recall elevators, and the communicator that reports to an FDNY-approved central station, designed for that building and accepted by the FDNY. How does a commercial fire alarm system work? Each initiating device sits on a supervised circuit that the panel watches for alarms and for faults; when a detector or a sprinkler waterflow switch reports, the panel sounds the notification appliances, runs its programmed outputs and sends the signal out. When does a building need a fire alarm system? That depends on its occupancy and size under section 907 of the NYC Building Code. A factory floor, for example, needs one under the code's thresholds once it is two or more stories with 100 or more occupants, or 25 or more occupants above or below the level of exit discharge, and assembly, educational and many mercantile uses have their own triggers.

Repair

Need Fire Alarm Repair in the Brooklyn Navy Yard? Find Out Whose Circuit It Is First

The first question on any Navy Yard repair call is ownership: is the trouble on the building's system, which the landlord's people look after, or on devices your fit-out added? A tenant who opens the base-building panel without the landlord's knowledge can make things worse, and a landlord's technician may not know what a tenant's contractor installed three years ago. We book the visit within 2 hours of your request and start with the panel's event history and the drawings for your space. When the cause is one failed device or a loose termination, the system is often fixed in 1–2 hours; a fault that runs back into a shared riser takes coordination with the building.

Emergency fire alarm repair and emergency fire alarm service matter most on a working production floor. A bakery line, a brewery, a sound stage on a shoot day or a shop with a machine running cannot ignore a panel in alarm or in trouble. Urgent fire alarm repair begins with reading what the panel says and walking the circuit it names. Our emergency fire alarm technician will tell you on the first call whether the space needs a fire watch while the system is impaired, and in the Yard building management hears about it too.

Same day fire alarm repair is realistic for the faults we see most: fire alarm battery replacement after a battery trouble, fire alarm device fault repair on a detector clogged by sawdust or flour, fire alarm open circuit repair after a ceiling or a wall was opened, and fire alarm communication failure repair when the line to the central station drops. Fire alarm ground fault repair and fire alarm intermittent trouble take longer in old buildings, where water and decades of alterations hide inside conduit. We arrange 24 hour fire alarm repair coordination, after hours fire alarm service for night-shift tenants, and commercial fire alarm emergency repair when a system is fully down; a fire alarm panel failure repair also raises whether the panel can still be supported.

Why is my fire alarm panel showing trouble? A trouble signal means the panel has found a fault in itself or its wiring, not a fire: a low battery, a missing or dirty device, a broken circuit, or a lost connection to the central station. A fire alarm panel trouble light, a fire alarm system trouble condition or a fire alarm panel beeping repair call usually ends in one of a few fixes, among them fire alarm battery trouble repair, fire alarm trouble signal repair, fire alarm wiring repair, fire alarm circuit troubleshooting, fire alarm short circuit troubleshooting, or fire alarm SLC troubleshooting and fire alarm signaling line circuit repair on an addressable loop. A fire alarm supervisory trouble points to a sprinkler valve, a kitchen hood system or another monitored function rather than the wiring. What causes a fire alarm ground fault? Moisture in a junction box near a loading dock door, a screw driven through a cable during a partition change, or a damaged conductor touching a grounded box; fire alarm ground fault troubleshooting isolates circuits one at a time until the fault moves. A fire alarm system not communicating with the central station gets fire alarm communication failure emergency repair first, because the FDNY will not be called automatically until it is fixed. Emergency fire alarm troubleshooting, fire alarm system down repair, fire alarm system malfunction repair, fire alarm system emergency service and fire alarm trouble emergency service all start the same way here: read the panel, check the drawings, find out who last worked on that circuit. Commercial fire alarm repair, commercial fire alarm troubleshooting, fire alarm control panel repair and fire alarm control panel troubleshooting may also need the manufacturer's programming software and the building's passwords, which is one more reason to involve the landlord early. Fire alarm false alarm troubleshooting, smoke detector circuit troubleshooting, fire alarm notification circuit repair and FACP repair follow the same rule, and as a fire alarm repair contractor we end fire alarm fault diagnosis and fire alarm system diagnostics with a written note of what was found and fixed.

Building Specialists

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for the Navy Yard's Building Types

The Yard has buildings from three centuries, and each type takes cable, devices and notification differently.

Wartime concrete warehouses: Building 77

Building 77 at 141 Flushing Avenue was built in 1942 as a Navy storage building, roughly a million square feet with concrete standing in for windows on its first eleven floors. About three million pounds of concrete were removed and replaced with roughly 50,000 square feet of windows before it reopened in November 2017, when it was 85 percent leased. Its ground floor is a public food hall and manufacturing hub, and its upper floors are divided among makers, designers and offices. Concrete this heavy makes core drilling slow and noisy, so routes follow the risers and existing sleeves where they can, and every new penetration through a rated floor or wall is firestopped before we leave.

Historic shop buildings: Buildings 128 and 127

Building 128 began as a machine shop for naval equipment in 1899 and reopened in 2016 as an 84,000-square-foot home for Newlab, with a widened mezzanine, a steel catwalk and the original trusses and gantries kept in view. Building 127 dates from 1904; the Navy built boats there, and its renovation added about 95,000 square feet of manufacturing space, with a scenery and furniture maker for Broadway shows on the ground floor. High trussed roofs like these are where beam smoke detector installation, projected beam smoke detector installation or an air sampling smoke detection system earn their place, because spot detectors on a roof forty feet up are hard to reach and slow to respond.

New waterfront offices: Dock 72 and Building 303

Dock 72 is 675,000 square feet over 16 stories, standing between two dry docks with water on three sides. Its mechanical systems sit on the second floor, 19 feet above the floodplain, and its floors are only 90 feet wide. Building 303, finished in 2020 beside the Sands Street gate, offers office floors on its upper levels. In buildings like these the base-building system is modern and addressable, often with voice, so a tenant's job is mostly adding speakers, strobes and smoke detection for a new layout and having the base-building panel reprogrammed by the company of record.

Studio campuses

Steiner Studios counts 30 soundstages with column-free spans and grid heights from 26 to 45 feet, plus office, wardrobe and shop space around them. Our work there is the support space and nearby shops, where scenic carpentry, paint and stored materials sit near people working long hours.

Retail and light industry at Admiral's Row

The former Admiral's Row site on Flushing Avenue now holds a 74,000-square-foot Wegmans, opened in October 2019 with a mezzanine café seating nearly a hundred, and light-industrial and office space above and beside it. Shopping center fire alarm installation in a building like this means many separate tenant spaces under one roof, each with its own hours and its own kitchen, freezer or stockroom.

Single-story industrial buildings

Some of the Yard's older industrial buildings are wide one- or two-story sheds. One leasing flyer for Building 12 lists 20-foot ceilings, a mezzanine storage level, three gantry cranes and six drive-in gates. Industrial building fire alarm installation in a space like that has to keep detection clear of the crane path, protect cable from forklifts and bumpers, and choose heat detection near the drive-in doors where trucks idle.

Compliance

Two Sign-Offs: Landlord Plan Approval and the FDNY

A Navy Yard fire alarm job needs two approvals: BNYDC, as landlord, decides whether your work is acceptable in its building, and the FDNY decides whether the design and installation meet the code. Knowing the order saves weeks.

What the landlord's manual actually says

BNYDC's Tenant Manual does not set out fire alarm rules of its own. What it does say is general and firm: tenants submit plans and get them approved before most work; small non-structural changes such as painting may count as "Non-Material Alterations," but property management should confirm that before work starts; licensees may make no alterations at all without approval; electrical work goes to a licensed electrician; permits must be obtained and closed; and contractors' insurance certificates are tracked by the landlord. The detailed construction rules are in the Tenant Alterations Guidelines, which the manual says your leasing agent or property manager can provide. We have not seen a published copy, so we read the version your property manager gives you rather than guess at it.

Who looks after what

For utilities, the manual draws a clear line: BNYDC is generally responsible up to the point of entry into your space, and the tenant maintains the equipment inside it. The manual is silent on fire alarm systems, so the lease decides. In a multi-tenant building the panel, risers and common-area devices are usually the landlord's, and your suite's devices join that system; a tenant with a whole building may own and maintain everything. Either way, the proposal says in writing who will own each device after acceptance and who will pay for its upkeep.

When a fit-out needs FDNY plans

The Department of Buildings' Buildings Bulletin 2024-001, issued February 9, 2024, sorts work on existing fire alarm systems into repair, alteration and replacement. Repairs that match the existing layout and do not change how the system works need no new FDNY plans but must follow the Electrical Code and be done by a licensed electrician. New plans have to be filed with the FDNY for floor alterations, whole or partial; for moving or replacing a control panel; for panel technology upgrades; for enlargements; and for a change of occupancy in a space, which needs a new fire alarm system for that space. That last item is the one that catches Yard tenants: turning storage into production, or a shop into an event room, is a change of use for fire alarm purposes.

Who is allowed to do the work

The FDNY says fire alarm plans are prepared and filed by a New York State registered design professional, a Professional Engineer or Registered Architect, and that the installer must be a licensed electrician or a fire alarm vendor holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate. Any company that installs, inspects, tests or services fire alarm system components needs that certificate, and at least one of its principals must hold an S-97 Certificate of Fitness, which requires NICET Level II or higher, or a New York City master or special electrician license. Technicians can hold the S-98. The company application fee is 105 dollars, company certification lasts one year and the S-97 and S-98 last three.

The FDNY steps

The design professional files the TM-1 application, certified plans and the building's Certificate of Occupancy or Schedule A on FDNY Business. The FDNY says initial plan review can take up to eight weeks, that an objection should be answered within six months to avoid a new filing fee, and that plan submission to final approval averages about 20 to 24 weeks. Installation starts only after the plans are approved. Before the inspection is requested, the system must be 100 percent installed and free of alarm, supervisory and trouble signals, with permanent power and a live central station connection. The FDNY Fire Alarm Inspection Unit works out of 9 MetroTech Center in Downtown Brooklyn, a short trip from the Sands Street gate.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Tenant Who Signed Before Asking About the Panel

The call we get most often from inside the Yard sounds like this: a company has signed a lease on a raw floor, the architect has a layout, the builder wants to frame next month, and nobody has asked what the building's fire alarm can take. The first visit is not about devices. We go to the panel room with building management's permission, photograph the panel and its labels, read how many addresses are in use on each loop, find out whether the panel has room for more notification power, and ask who the building's fire alarm company of record is.

Then we walk the tenant's floor with the architect's plan in hand. A shop that will weld, a kitchen that will bake, a lab with a battery bench or a room that will hold a crowd each changes what detection works and whether the space is changing use. Those answers go to the design professional before the layout is final,. That order, landlord first, then sealed drawings, then the FDNY, is what keeps a move-in date from slipping by months. In the Yard, the experienced fire alarm contractor is the one who asks about the panel before the walls go up.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in the Brooklyn Navy Yard: Questions Answered

Cost and paperwork

BNYDC returned our fit-out plans asking how the fire alarm will work. Do we need sealed fire alarm drawings before the landlord will look again?

Usually not full sealed drawings, but the landlord will want to see that someone has looked at the building's system and planned for it. A device layout, a short narrative of how the new devices tie into the existing panel and who the company of record is, and a note on whether the work will need an FDNY filing, is normally what answers that kind of comment. The sealed set comes once the layout is settled, because it is the version the FDNY will examine.

Why does the proposal for our Building 77 suite include programming on a panel that belongs to the building?

Because new detectors, strobes and modules in your suite do nothing until the building's panel knows they exist. Each addressable device has to be entered into the panel's program, labeled, given its outputs and tested, and in a building with many tenants that work is usually done by, or with, the company that maintains the base system. The proposal shows it as a separate line so you can see what it covers.

Trust

A contractor offered to skip the landlord submission because our change is "only a few devices." What is the risk?

The landlord's manual treats unapproved work as a default under the lease, and a handful of devices added to a shared panel without the building knowing can leave its program, its battery calculations and its records wrong for every tenant on that system. Small changes still go through property management first; if they confirm it is minor, you have that in writing.

Our builder promises the fire alarm will be finished with the rest of the fit-out in eight weeks. Is that believable?

The installation itself may well fit inside eight weeks. The approvals usually do not: the FDNY puts plan submission to final approval at about 20 to 24 weeks on average, and the landlord's review comes before that. If your work needs an FDNY filing, ask the builder which date he means: devices on the ceiling, or a system the FDNY has accepted.

Who may do the work

Our project manager wants our own crew to pull the fire alarm cable and hire a certified company only for the connections. Will that pass?

The FDNY looks to a licensed electrician or a certified fire alarm company to install the system, and BNYDC requires a licensed electrician for any electrical work in its buildings. Cable pulled by an unlicensed crew can mean the company that signs the filing will not accept responsibility for it, and will want to test or replace it. Plan the cable pull as part of the licensed installation from the start.

Can the architect who designed our fit-out also seal the fire alarm drawings, or do we need a separate engineer?

The FDNY accepts fire alarm plans from a New York State registered design professional, which includes a Registered Architect as well as a Professional Engineer. Either way, we share our survey of the existing system with whoever seals the drawings.

Technical

The welding bay sits right next to our design office. How do you keep the torch from setting off the office detectors?

By keeping the two rooms separate in the design. Smoke detection in the office, heat detection suited to the welding bay, a door that closes between them, and exhaust that pulls fumes away from the office side all help. Hot work itself follows its own permit and fire watch rules.

Our hardware lab tests battery packs to failure on purpose. Does the fire alarm need to know about the test chamber?

Yes. A room where batteries are pushed until they vent or burn is a different hazard from an office, and the design professional should hear about it before the layout is drawn. Detection near the chamber, how the room's exhaust behaves in alarm, and whether the test equipment has its own suppression all change the sequence of operations.

Our space is two floors joined by an open stair cut through the slab. Does that opening change the fire alarm?

It can. An opening between floors lets smoke from one level reach the other, and the code treats floor openings carefully, so the design professional will look at detection around the opening and at how the two floors are notified together.

Our scenic shop builds pieces that reach the ceiling. What happens to the detectors when a tall piece blocks them?

A detector boxed in by a tall flat or a stacked crate cannot do its job. In shops like that we look at beam or aspirating detection that sees across the room rather than spot detectors that tall work can surround, and we mark keep-clear zones on the floor plan the shop actually uses.

Landlord and tenant

Building management asked us to name a Fire Safety & Security contact in the ID Portal. What does that person actually do?

The BNYDC Tenant Manual describes that role as the "Local Fire Alarm contact for FDNY response/emergency contact." In practice it should be someone who knows where your devices are, who your fire alarm company is, and who can get to the space or answer the phone at night.

The landlord's property manager and our architect disagree about whether our change is a "Non-Material Alteration." Who decides?

The landlord does, under your lease. The manual gives painting interior walls as an example of a non-material change and says to confirm with property management before starting. Anything that moves walls, adds rooms or touches fire alarm devices is unlikely to qualify, and an email from property management settles it either way.

Complaints

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About Fire Alarms in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

Search summaries and chat assistants give quick answers about fire alarm installation, and many of them are written for a generic American building with one owner. Here are the claims we hear repeated most often from tenants inside the Yard, and what actually happens on this campus.

"A tenant can hire any licensed electrician to add fire alarm devices to its space."

Part of this is true: BNYDC's manual does require that all electrical work in its buildings be done by a licensed electrician, and the NYC Department of Buildings bulletin on existing systems says repair work goes to a licensed electrician too. But a licensed electrician alone is not the whole answer for fire alarm work in New York City.

The FDNY requires any company that installs, inspects, tests or services a fire alarm system to hold an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, with at least one principal holding an S-97 Certificate of Fitness. On top of that, the device you add has to be programmed into a panel the landlord controls, by whoever the landlord trusts with that panel.

So the better question is "who is the building's fire alarm company of record, and how will my installer and that company split the work?" The answer goes into the plan you send the landlord.

"The FDNY is the only approval you need."

For a building you own outright, that is close to right. Inside the Navy Yard, the landlord's approval comes first. The Tenant Manual says plans of proposed work must be submitted to and approved by BNYDC, and treats unapproved work as a default under the lease.

That review is not a formality. The landlord looks at your work against its whole building, including shared risers and a panel other tenants depend on; the FDNY looks at code compliance. Each can reject what the other approved.

Plan both from the start. A layout the landlord has already seen, then sealed drawings, then the FDNY filing, is a shorter path than an FDNY approval the landlord learns about afterwards.

"Fire alarm installation takes a few days to a couple of weeks."

The physical installation in a tenant suite can be that fast. The project almost never is. The FDNY estimates an average of about 20 to 24 weeks from plan submission to final approval, with initial review alone taking up to eight weeks.

In the Yard you add landlord review before that, and logistics after it: BNYDC ID cards for the crew, deliveries timed to dock and freight elevator hours, and freight service that shuts down overnight on weekdays and does not run on weekends without an advance request.

How long does fire alarm installation take for a Navy Yard tenant, then? Count from the day your architect has a layout to the day the FDNY accepts the system, and set your move-in around that, not around the week the electricians are on site.

"Changing what you do in a space doesn't affect the fire alarm."

This one causes the most trouble in an industrial park. Under the Department of Buildings' 2024 bulletin, a change of occupancy in a space requires a new fire alarm system for that space, filed with the FDNY. In the Yard, uses change constantly: storage becomes production, a shop becomes a showroom, a floor becomes an event room.

The code's fire alarm triggers differ by occupancy. A factory use has thresholds based on stories and occupants; an assembly use is triggered by occupant load; educational use is covered regardless of size. Moving from one to another can turn a space that needed little into one that needs a full system.

Before you sign a lease for a use different from the last tenant's, ask the design professional to look at the occupancy question.

"Smoke detectors belong in every room."

In an office that is close to right. On a Yard production floor it is a recipe for nuisance alarms. Welding, grinding, sanding, ovens, steam and 3D printers all produce something a smoke detector can mistake for a fire.

The answer is the right detector for each room: heat detection in kitchens and welding bays, beam or aspirating detection under tall roofs, smoke detection where people work at desks, and doors and exhaust that keep each room's air in that room.

Every false alarm in a shared building disrupts every tenant on the system and brings fire apparatus through the gates.

"Fire alarm installation is priced per device."

Online cost guides often give a figure per device or per square foot. In the Yard that number leaves out most of the work: the survey of the building's system, landlord coordination, sealed drawings, FDNY filing and objections, programming on a panel someone else owns, and the acceptance test with the trades the FDNY expects on site.

Two suites with the same number of devices can differ widely in effort if one sits on a loop with spare capacity and the other needs a new power supply and a reprogrammed panel. A suite whose use is changing may need a whole new system for the space.

That is why we quote each Navy Yard job in writing after walking it, with each stage on its own line.

"Once it's installed, the landlord takes care of everything."

Some of it, perhaps. BNYDC's manual says the landlord is generally responsible for utilities up to the point of entry into your space, and the tenant maintains equipment inside it. It does not address fire alarms specifically, so the lease decides who maintains the devices in your suite.

The landlord also asks every tenant to name a Fire Safety & Security contact in its ID Portal as the local fire alarm contact for FDNY response. That person needs to know what is in your space and whom to call.

Yes, with planning. Work on a shared panel or riser is scheduled with building management, neighbors are told before any test sounds the alarm, and existing devices stay in service until their replacements are working, so no tenant is left without protection overnight.

Do your technicians need BNYDC ID cards to work inside the Yard?
+

Every tenant employee needs a BNYDC ID card, and the Tenant Manual says consultants on long-term projects should be given temporary ID cards by BNYDC Security. We arrange our crew's access through you and building management before the first day on site.

Which detectors hold up near steam, smoke and dust in Navy Yard production spaces?
+

Heat detectors, fixed temperature or rate of rise, near ovens, steam kettles and welding; beam or aspirating detection under tall roofs and in dusty shops; and smoke detectors kept to offices, corridors and stairs where the air is clean. Doors and exhaust that keep each room's air in that room do as much as the detector choice.

What happens on the building's system when a detector in one tenant's space goes into alarm?
+

In a typical multi-tenant Yard building, your devices report to the building's panel, which sounds the alarm as its program says, runs fan, door and elevator functions and sends the signal to the central station. That is why your new devices must be programmed into that panel and tested with it.

Can a Navy Yard event space use the fire alarm speakers for its own announcements?
+

Generally not for everyday announcements. A fire alarm voice system has to stay reserved for emergency messages and is supervised for that purpose. Events need their own sound system, with the design making sure the fire alarm can be heard over it or silences it when the alarm operates.

When our lease ends, do the fire alarm devices we paid for leave with us?
+

Usually not. Devices tied into the building's system typically stay as part of the building, and removing them can leave the system in trouble or out of compliance. The lease's alteration and surrender terms decide; our proposal states who will own each device after acceptance so there is no surprise at move-out.

Will the company that signs our FDNY filing be the one installing the devices?
+

It should be. The FDNY looks to the certified fire alarm company and the licensed electrician on the filing for the installation. Our proposals name both in writing before work begins, and the same names appear on the acceptance test paperwork.

Can you work after the freight elevators shut down for the night?
+

Yes, with notice. BNYDC's manual says freight cars in buildings with dedicated freight elevators shut down overnight on weekdays and do not run on weekends, and after-hours freight is requested by email at least two business days ahead. We stage materials during service hours and request after-hours freight only when the work needs it.
