The Neighborhood

Why Brooklyn Navy Yard Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

The Brooklyn Navy Yard sits on Wallabout Bay, bounded by Navy Street to the west, Flushing Avenue to the south, Kent Avenue to the east and the East River to the north. President John Adams authorized it among the country's first naval shipyards in 1801, and for 165 years it built and repaired warships, including the USS Arizona and the USS Missouri. The Yard's own history puts its Second World War workforce at about 70,000 people. The Navy decommissioned it in 1966 and the City bought it. Today it is run by BNYDC, a not-for-profit that acts as real estate developer and property manager on the City's behalf, and the organization's 2026 figures cite more than 550 businesses and about 13,000 people working inside the gates. That structure matters for a fire alarm in a way it would not on an ordinary Brooklyn block. There is one landlord for dozens of buildings, a written Tenant Manual that governs alterations, electrical work, hallways and roofs, and a property-management team for each of three zones. A tenant who wants new detection in its suite is never the only party with a stake in the panel.

Six facts that change how a fire alarm gets planned in the Brooklyn Navy Yard

  • One landlord, written rules. BNYDC's Tenant Manual says alterations generally need plans submitted to and approved by BNYDC, that electrical work must be performed by a licensed electrician, and that unapproved construction or unclosed permits can put a tenant in default.
  • The building usually owns the panel. In multi-tenant buildings, tenant devices typically join a base-building system. The Building 77 leasing flyer describes the building as fully sprinklered by a new combined sprinkler and standpipe system and goes on to mention a new addressable system; the text we could read was cut off, so we do not assume what it covers.
  • Every tenant names a fire alarm contact. The Tenant Manual lists a Fire Safety and Security role that serves as the Local Fire Alarm contact for FDNY response. That person needs to know what was installed in the space and where.
  • Three generations of buildings. 19th-century brick, 1917 and 1942 reinforced concrete, and 2017 to 2020 new construction all sit inside one fence. Cable paths, drilling and device mounting change completely from one to the next.
  • The ground floor has flooded. The Yard's resilience page says Hurricane Sandy put four to six feet of water across the campus in 2012 and caused about $100 million in damage, and that the December 1992 nor'easter and Hurricane Ida in 2021 also flooded it. Panels, power supplies and batteries need a mounting height chosen with that in mind.
  • Events now fill old warehouses. The Tenant Manual requires 45 days' notice for events of 75 or more people and 14 days for smaller ones, and the 2026 council letter shows how large some gatherings have become. Notification has to reach crowds, not just staff.

Where this page applies. It covers tenant spaces inside the Yard's gates, from Building 92 on Flushing Avenue to Dock 72 on the water and the industrial buildings near the Cumberland Street and Clymer Street entrances, plus the private houses and small buildings just outside the wall in Vinegar Hill and Wallabout. If your building is clearly in a neighboring area, our pages for Fort Greene, Clinton Hill and DUMBO describe the buildings there, and our Brooklyn fire alarm installation page covers the whole borough. The new office building: open floors that turn into rooms

Dock 72 and Building 303 were built recently, and their fire alarm designs were drawn for open, flexible floors. Dock 72's lobby is described by Metropolis as 30 feet wide and 600 feet long, carried on twenty V-shaped steel beams, and the building has entrances on both the land side and the water side. Upper floors in buildings like these are delivered open so tenants can lay them out as they wish. The moment a tenant adds full-height partitions for private offices, phone rooms, a lab or a server closet, the devices that were spaced for an open floor may no longer reach into the new rooms, and a horn that carried clearly across an open floor may be muffled behind a closed door.

The industrial shop building: high ceilings, process heat and cranes

Industrial buildings at the Yard are marketed for real production. The Building 12 flyer lists 20-foot ceilings, a mezzanine storage level, three gantry cranes, six drive-in gates and workshop spaces. In rooms like that, three things push device choice away from an ordinary ceiling smoke detector. Height lets smoke cool and spread before it reaches the ceiling, so a small fire low in the room may take longer to register. Processes like welding, cutting and grinding release fumes and particles that a smoke detector reads as fire. And cranes, mezzanines and drive-in doors limit where devices can sit and where a technician can safely reach them for testing.

The Yard is home to Steiner Studios, which describes itself with about 780,000 square feet and thirty soundstages, including a 27,000-square-foot stage it calls the largest on the East Coast; NYCCIP's account of its newest stages gives a clear height of 58 feet. In spaces like these, the fire alarm questions are about scale and people. Can a crowd hear and see the alarm over amplified music? Do strobes reach every corner of a huge room? What happens to the detectors when a production fills a stage with haze?

The houses beyond the wall: plaster, frame and landmark fronts

Just outside the Yard, Vinegar Hill holds a few blocks of Federal and Greek Revival houses mixed with industrial buildings, with Belgian block still on Hudson Avenue and Plymouth, Water and Front Streets. These are homes, and they need household protection: smoke alarms outside sleeping areas and on each level, carbon monoxide alarms where there is fuel-burning equipment, and linking between floors so a fire on one level wakes people on another. In plaster walls and old framing, we choose between hardwired interconnected alarms fished through existing cavities and wireless interconnected alarms that link by radio, based on how much opening up the owner will accept. Interior work does not normally involve the Landmarks Preservation Commission, but any exterior device or conduit on a house in either district does, so we avoid exterior work entirely unless there is no alternative.

Field Notes

Field Notes: A Yard Survey Starts at the Landlord's Panel, Not Your Ceiling

Before the visit. The technician confirms how he is coming in. The Tenant Manual says single-day visitors get an emailed QR pass, while recurring contractors on longer projects should get temporary ID cards from BNYDC Security, so the choice depends on whether the job is a survey or a multi-day installation. He also asks for the freight elevator arrangement, since the Manual says freight cars are off from 8 p.m. to 5 a.m. on weekdays and all weekend unless booked.

First stop, the building's fire alarm information. He asks the Property Manager or building staff where the panel or annunciator is, which company services it, and how the building wants testing announced. He asks whether the tenant's devices report to the building panel and whether the vendor must do any programming. Nothing in the suite gets touched until those answers are written down.

Then the suite. He walks every room with the tenant's partition plan in hand, counting devices, checking which rooms are new, measuring ceiling heights, and noting anything that will fool a detector or block a horn: a fryer, a welding table, a haze machine, a paint booth, a kiln, a charging shelf, a loud compressor.

Last, the paperwork. He compares what he found with the lease clause and the building's answers, and only then tells the tenant which category the job is in and who else has to sign off.

Compliance notes for Brooklyn Navy Yard tenants and buildings

  • BNYDC approval first. The Tenant Manual says plans for alterations are generally submitted to and approved by BNYDC before work, that electrical work must be done by a licensed electrician, and that permits must be obtained and closed. We work to whatever the tenant's lease or license and BNYDC's guidelines require, and the Property Manager is the person who confirms what applies.
  • FDNY review of design. The FDNY's Chapter 9 says fire alarm design and installation documents must be submitted to the department for review and approval before installation. The NYC Business portal adds that an FDNY fire alarm plan needs a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 holder, proof of occupancy, floor plans, a riser diagram and an operating sequence matrix, and that the system must be 100 percent complete and pre-tested before the FDNY inspects.
  • Repair or change. Trade summaries of the rules say that replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no design change counts as a repair, while moving or replacing a control panel, or changing the design, needs new plans filed with the FDNY.
  • Tall buildings. SPARC's summary of the DOB bulletin on fire alarm work in existing buildings says one-way voice communication is required in buildings over 125 feet, measured from the lowest level of fire department vehicle access to the highest occupied floor. Whether a sixteen-story Yard building crosses that line is answered by its measured height and its approved design, not by a story count.
  • Places of assembly. The FDNY's Fire Code guide says places of assembly need a Fire Department permit and a DOB certificate of operation, and the Tenant Manual adds its own notice periods for events: 14 days for up to 74 attendees, 45 days for 75 or more.
  • Hallways and roofs. The Tenant Manual prohibits storage in common hallways as a fire code matter and prohibits roof use except in emergencies unless a lease grants the roof. Any device or cable on a roof needs the landlord's agreement first.
  • Who may install. New York State's Department of State licenses anyone who installs or services security or fire alarm systems. Our license number is on every quote.

Whose Panel Is It? Sorting a Navy Yard Fire Alarm Request Before It Becomes a Lease Problem

Most fire alarm mistakes at the Yard are not about equipment. They are about ownership and order. A tenant hires someone to add detectors, the detectors go in, and weeks later the Property Manager finds devices on the ceiling that are not on the building's drawings, not on the panel's programming and not covered by any approval. The lease says unapproved construction can be a default. The building's service company refuses to take responsibility for devices it did not install. And the FDNY's records no longer match the building. This section explains how we prevent that, by sorting every request into one of four categories before we quote. We are not lawyers or code consultants, and this is not legal advice. It is how we read the public rules and the Yard's own Tenant Manual.

Category one: a like-for-like repair. A detector, horn, strobe or base is damaged or failed, and it is replaced with an identical part in the same place. The rules treat this as a repair, not a design change. Even so, in a Yard building the device usually belongs to the building's system, so the building's fire safety contact and service company are told before the work, the monitoring station is not left to dispatch on a test, and the building's records are updated afterward. This is the quickest category and often a single visit.

Category two: a supplementary device that is not part of the required system. Sometimes a tenant wants extra local warning that is not part of the building's fire alarm at all, such as a carbon monoxide alarm near a gas-fired kiln, or a standalone heat alarm in a storage room. These can be simple installations, but at the Yard they still go on the plan the tenant submits, because the Tenant Manual's approval requirement does not stop at the building's system, and because a second, unrelated alarm sound in a shared building can confuse people. We label these devices clearly as tenant equipment so nobody mistakes them for part of the building system.

Category three: an addition or change to the building's system. New walls, new rooms, a change in use or a new process can all mean the building's approved design no longer fits the space. Adding devices to the building panel, moving them or changing what the system does when it alarms is a design change. That calls for BNYDC's approval and, where the rules require it, a plan filed with the FDNY by a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 holder, with a riser diagram and an operating sequence matrix. In this category we do not pretend our low-voltage license replaces those roles. We survey, we propose device locations, we install and pre-test to the approved plan, and we coordinate with the building's vendor where the vendor must do the panel programming.

Category four: a building-level project. A full panel replacement, a building conversion, or a change that affects the whole building's sequence of operations is BNYDC's project, not a tenant's. If a tenant's request turns out to require that, we say so plainly and stop, because no tenant fit-out should be quietly carrying a building-wide redesign.

Why the sequence matters. A fire alarm does more than make noise. In a modern building, its operating sequence can recall elevators, shut down air handlers, release doors and signal a monitoring station. The operating sequence matrix the FDNY filing asks for exists because those actions have to happen in the right order. A tenant who adds a device without understanding what the building does when that device alarms might, for example, put a heat detector in a spot where it will trigger the same building-wide response as a smoke detector in a stair. That is why we ask the building what each type of device does in its sequence before we choose one.

What we cannot tell you. We found no published BNYDC rule that speaks specifically to fire alarm work in tenant spaces beyond the general rules on alterations, electrical work, hallways and roofs, and the Tenant Alteration Guidelines the Manual mentions are not public. Your Property Manager is the person who can tell you exactly what your lease and those guidelines require. We will build our plan around their answer, not around a guess.

What the Yard's Fires Teach

Fuel Tanks, Glove Cartons and Crowded Warehouses: Turning the Yard's Fire Record Into a Detection Plan

The fires in the Yard's record are not engineering studies, and none of the accounts we read describe how a fire alarm system performed. We do not draw conclusions the sources do not support. Read together, though, they point to conditions that any detection plan on this campus should answer.

Work in progress is when fire starts. The Constellation fire began during construction, when a forklift pierced a fuel tank. A working industrial campus is a construction site somewhere almost every day: a fit-out on one floor, a shop rebuilding a line on another. That is when detectors get covered, ceilings get opened and wiring gets cut. Our plan for any installation includes what happens to protection while the work is going on, who agrees to any device being covered, and who checks every device is back in service at the end of each day.

Stored goods can burn on their own. The warehouse fire that injured 67 firefighters began when cartons of powder-free latex gloves combusted spontaneously, according to the account we read. Large quantities of stored material in a big room are a particular challenge: a fire can start deep in a pile, grow before smoke reaches the ceiling, and spread quickly once it does. Detection over stored goods has to be laid out for the room's actual height and for the way the goods are stacked, and storage arrangements that change after the detection is designed should trigger a review.

Crowds change the requirement. The August 2026 letter from Council Members Restler and Hudson, as reported by the Brooklyn Eagle, concerned all-night events in the Yard's dance warehouses, and the Yard responded by changing exit procedures. When people who do not know a building fill it at night with loud music, the alarm's job is to reach every one of them quickly, by sound and by light. Spaces being converted to event use need their notification reviewed as part of the conversion, not after the first event.

Water reaches the ground floor. Sandy put four to six feet of water across the Yard, and BK Reader reported that Building 41A took 48 inches of brackish water, damaging electrical motors, wiring and control systems. The Local reported in December 2012 that Kings County Distillery, then in the Paymaster Building, lost grain and some electronics when about five feet of water came in. Salt water and fire alarm electronics do not mix. On ground floors and in low-lying buildings, we mount panels, power supplies and batteries as high as the design and the building allow, and after any flood we test every device in the wet area rather than assuming it survived.

Batteries are the fastest-growing hazard. The FDNY's 2026 figure on battery fires, reported by Firehouse.com, applies citywide, and the Yard's tenants include robotics, clean energy and transportation businesses that keep many batteries on hand. Every charging shelf and battery store we find on a survey goes on the plan, with a recommendation about detection nearby, and we remind tenants that a detector warns of a fire; it does not make a charging area safer.

Drills look like real events. Notify NYC warned residents in October 2026 about a simulated explosion with a 20-foot fireball near Washington and Flushing Avenues by the Navy Yard, as reported by Yahoo. Exercises like that are a reminder that the people around a building react to what they see and hear. The same is true inside: a tenant's own fire alarm test should be announced to neighbors and the building, so nobody evacuates or calls 911 for a test.

Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Navy Yard: Questions Answered

These questions come from the situations tenants and neighbors of the Yard actually face: a landlord's system over your head, processes that fool detectors, approvals in a set order, old buildings and water. Each one is answered fully here and does not appear again in the FAQ further down.

The landlord's system and your space

Our suite sits inside a building whose fire alarm belongs to the landlord. Can we put in detectors of our own, or does everything have to report to the building's panel?

It depends on what the devices are for, and the Property Manager has to be part of the answer. Devices that form part of the protection the building is required to have, the detectors, horns and strobes covering your rooms, are normally part of the building's system, connected to its panel and maintained by its service company under a design the FDNY reviewed. A tenant cannot replace those with a separate set of its own, and adding to them is a change to the building's design. What a tenant can sometimes add is supplementary equipment that sits outside the required system, such as a carbon monoxide alarm near a gas appliance or a local heat alarm in a storage room, clearly labeled as tenant equipment. Even those belong on the plan submitted to BNYDC, because the Tenant Manual generally requires approval for alterations and because an unfamiliar alarm sound in a shared building can mislead people about what is happening. Our first step is to ask the building which system serves your floor, who services it, and whether its vendor must do any panel work. Then we tell you what we can install directly and what must go through the building.

We are building private offices and a lab inside an open floor. Why would new walls change the fire alarm we already have?

Because the devices over an open floor were spaced for a room without walls. Smoke detectors and heat detectors each cover a limited area of ceiling, and a full-height partition stops smoke from reaching a detector on the other side. Horns and strobes were placed so people anywhere on the open floor could hear and see them; a closed office or a lab with a heavy door may be too quiet or too dark for the existing devices to reach. So the new rooms may need detection of their own, and some will need notification. In new buildings like Dock 72 or Building 303, where floors are delivered open for tenants to lay out, this is the most common reason for fire alarm work during a fit-out. The answer is to bring the fire alarm into the partition design early. Send us the partition plan, and we will mark which rooms need devices and which existing devices move, so the Property Manager and any filing engineer see the whole picture before walls go up. Planning devices into a fit-out costs less than reopening finished ceilings afterward.

The previous tenant left a small panel and a few detectors in our space that nobody can explain. Should we keep them running, remove them or test them?

Identify them first, and do not disconnect anything until you know what it is. Older Yard buildings have hosted many tenants since 1966, and equipment left behind can be anything from a live sub-panel connected to the building's system, to a separate monitored system the last tenant paid for, to cable and devices that were abandoned and are now dead. Cutting a live building circuit can put a trouble signal on the building panel and leave other spaces unprotected. Leaving dead devices on the ceiling can make occupants think a room is protected when it is not. We trace each cable with a tone generator and probe, check what each device is connected to, ask the building's service company whether the equipment appears on their records, and give you and the Property Manager a short written list: live building devices, live tenant equipment, and abandoned equipment. Removing anything that is part of the building system is a change for the building to approve. Removing abandoned equipment is usually straightforward once everyone agrees it is abandoned.

Processes that fool or outrun detectors

Our shop welds, cuts and grinds metal under a twenty-foot ceiling. What detection will not set off the building every afternoon?

A smoke detector over a welding bench reads fumes and fine particles as fire, so a shop like yours usually needs detection matched to the work. Heat detectors respond to temperature rather than particles and are the common choice in spaces where airborne particles are normal, though they react later to a slow, smoky fire, so they are not the answer everywhere in the building. In a high, open room, beam detectors that project a light beam across the space, or air-sampling detection that draws air through piping to a detector, can be better choices, provided cranes, mezzanines and moving loads do not block them and a technician can reach them for testing. Height matters on its own, because in a tall room smoke from a small fire can cool and spread before it reaches the ceiling. The right device for your shop is chosen with the building's approved design in mind, and if it differs from what is installed now, it is a change that needs the building's approval and possibly a filing. Tell us exactly which processes run where, and we will propose devices for each zone of the shop.

We fry, bake and steam in a ground-floor food unit. How should detection work alongside the hood and its suppression system?

The cooking line itself is protected by the hood's fire suppression system, which is its own specialized equipment designed and serviced by suppression specialists. The building's fire alarm is usually told when that suppression system discharges, so the building responds. Our part is the detection and notification around the kitchen. Smoke detectors close to fryers, ovens, steam kettles or a dishwasher will false-alarm, so the area near the line usually gets heat detection, while smoke detection stays where it protects exits and other rooms without being fooled by cooking. Food units that wash down their floors and walls nightly add moisture, so devices near those areas need to be rated for the conditions and mounted where hoses will not reach them. In a building like Building 77, where Market @ 77 runs along the ground floor, the kitchen's interface with the building's system belongs to the building's design. We make sure the suppression interface, the heat detectors and the notification work together, and we test the whole chain in front of the tenant and the building's representative so everyone sees what happens when the hood fires.

Our warehouse space holds tall stacks of cartons and packaging. What did the glove-carton fire at the Yard teach about detecting fire in stored goods?

That stored material can start a fire with no one nearby and grow out of sight. The account of that warehouse fire says thousands of powder-free latex gloves combusted on their own, and 67 firefighters were injured fighting it. Detection in a storage room has to be laid out for what the room actually holds and how high it is stacked. A fire deep inside a stack may produce smoke that rises slowly, and racks or pallets close to the ceiling can block devices or leave gaps between them. So we survey storage at its fullest, not on a quiet day, measure clearances from the top of the stored goods to the ceiling, and recommend device types and spacing for that layout. Housekeeping matters as much as devices. Storage that grows into aisles, up toward sprinklers or into common hallways, which the Tenant Manual forbids, changes what the detection was designed for. If your stock levels change with the season, tell us the busiest level, and plan to have the layout reviewed whenever racking changes.

We shoot with haze and smoke effects on our stage. How do we use them without triggering the building's alarm or switching off protection?

Atmospheric effects can set off smoke detectors, and the wrong response is to cover or disconnect detectors on your own. Any time devices in a building system are taken out of service, the building has to know, and its procedures apply. Those procedures belong to the building owner and the FDNY, and they may include notifying the monitoring company, assigning someone to watch the affected area and restoring every device the moment the effect ends. The best long-term answer is to plan for effects in the detection design itself. In a stage or studio where haze is part of normal work, detection suited to the space, for example beam or air-sampling detection set up with the building's engineer, can sometimes protect the room without reacting to every effect. That is a design choice for the building and, where it changes the system, a filed change. In the meantime, schedule effect days with the building's fire safety contact in advance, keep detection in support areas live, and have one person on your crew responsible for confirming protection is back to normal before the stage closes.

Our team charges e-bikes, power tools and robot batteries inside the unit. Where should detection be, and what can it actually do?

Charging areas should be inside a detected part of your space, close enough that a fire there is noticed early, and the alarm from that detection should reach people anywhere in the unit. Lithium-ion battery fires can grow quickly and produce heavy smoke, and Firehouse.com reported that the FDNY said battery-caused fires were up about 70 percent in 2026. On a survey we mark every place batteries are charged or stored, check which devices cover those spots, and recommend additions where they are not covered. We also look for practical problems that detection cannot solve: charging next to the only exit, charging on combustible shelving, chargers left plugged in overnight in an empty unit, or batteries stored in a common hallway, which the Tenant Manual prohibits. A detector warns; it does not prevent. Rules on how a business may store and charge batteries are separate from the alarm, and your company should confirm them with the FDNY and your landlord. What we can do is make sure that if a battery fails, the people in the unit and the building find out quickly.

Approvals, renovations and events

Who files the FDNY plan for a fit-out inside a Yard building, and which part of the work is yours?

When a fit-out changes the building's fire alarm design, the NYC Business portal says the FDNY fire alarm plan needs a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 holder, with proof of occupancy, floor plans, a riser diagram and an operating sequence matrix. In practice, the tenant's architect or engineer usually prepares the drawings, the licensed filer submits them, and BNYDC reviews the tenant's plans under the lease before work starts. Our part comes before and after the filing. Before it, we survey the space, identify existing devices, and propose device locations and types suited to the rooms and processes. After approval, we install pathway, cable and devices to the approved plan, coordinate with the building's fire alarm vendor where it must program the panel, label everything, and pre-test every device so the system is complete before the FDNY's inspection. We hold a New York State low-voltage license, and we are clear that it does not replace the filing roles. If a request is still at the idea stage, we can tell you whether it is likely to need a filing at all, which helps a tenant decide who to bring in first.

Our contractor wants to cover the detectors while the crew sands and paints the suite. What has to stay working, and who agrees to it?

Covering smoke detectors during dusty work is an accepted practice under DOB policy, which allows smoke detectors to be covered or removed during renovation, but the same policy generally expects manual pull stations and sprinkler flow alarms to stay operational and calls for a designated impairment coordinator. In a Yard building, the devices belong to the building's system, so the decision to cover them is not the painter's alone. The building's fire safety contact and its service company need to know which devices will be covered, for how long, and who will uncover them. Our practice is to agree a written list before work begins: the devices that may be covered, the hours, the person responsible for uncovering them each evening, and the person who confirms with the building that the system is back to normal. At the end of each phase, we check every covered device is uncovered and test a sample. At the end of the job, we test every device in the space. A detector left bagged after a renovation is one of the most common ways a room ends up with no protection at all.

We are leasing a top floor with roof space and plan to host events up there. What changes for the fire alarm?

Possibly a great deal, and it should be settled before the first event is booked. The Brooklyn Eagle described Building 77's sixteenth floor, with about 8,000 square feet inside and 16,500 square feet of roof, as suited to a restaurant or event use. Turning office space into event space can change the occupancy, and the FDNY's Fire Code guide says places of assembly need a Fire Department permit and a DOB certificate of operation. Notification has to reach a crowd over amplified sound, on a terrace and in every room guests can use, and the building's sequence may need to cut amplified music when the alarm sounds so the alarm can be heard. The Tenant Manual adds its own requirements: 45 days' notice for events of 75 or more people, and roof use only where the lease grants it. These are decisions for the building, your architect and your filing engineer. Our role is to survey the existing devices on the floor, measure how audible they are with the space set up as it will be used, and install and test the notification changes once they are approved.

Old buildings, water and houses nearby

Our building dates from before the Navy left in 1966. Could drilling for fire alarm cable disturb asbestos, and how do you handle that?

It could, which is why we ask before we drill. A mesothelioma-awareness site says asbestos was used as insulation in walls, boilers and around pipes at the Navy Yard. It is a secondary source, and plenty of older buildings have since been surveyed or abated, but it is enough reason to treat any pre-1966 building with care. Before drilling or pulling cable near old pipe insulation, ceiling materials or wall finishes, we ask the Property Manager what hazardous-material surveys exist for the building and the area we will be working in, and we route cable to avoid suspect materials wherever we can. If we meet material that looks like it could contain asbestos and there is no survey saying otherwise, we stop work in that spot and tell the tenant and the building. Testing and any abatement are for licensed specialists, not for an alarm crew. That pause can affect the schedule, so we would rather raise the question at the survey than on the day a drill is already against the wall.

Water came into our ground-floor space in recent storms. How high should a panel, power supply or battery go, and what happens after the next flood?

As high as the design and the building allow, and never on the floor. The Yard's resilience page says Sandy put four to six feet of water across the campus in 2012, and that Hurricane Ida flooded it again in 2021; Brooklyn Paper also reported business flooding at the Yard in a 2023 storm. The Yard's own resilience planning talks about elevating equipment and protecting electrical systems. For fire alarm equipment in a tenant space, that means mounting any panel, power supply, battery cabinet or module that we install well above the floor, avoiding cable routes that dip low along walls, and asking the building where its own equipment for your floor sits. Devices on the ceiling are usually safe from water, but their wiring may not be. After any flood that reaches your space, the protection should be treated as suspect until tested: salt water corrodes connections, and a system can look normal while a circuit is failing. We can test every device and circuit in the affected area and give you a written result for the building and your insurer.

We own an early-19th-century house in Vinegar Hill. How do linked smoke and CO alarms go into old plaster and framing without wrecking the house?

By choosing the method to suit the walls rather than forcing one method on every house. Vinegar Hill's Federal and Greek Revival houses often have plaster on lath, irregular framing and floors that have been rebuilt more than once. Where there are clear cavities between floors, such as a stack of closets or an old chase, hardwired interconnected alarms can be fished through with small openings that are patched afterward. Where opening plaster would damage historic finishes the owner wants to keep, wireless interconnected alarms that link by radio give the same result, with a smoke alarm sounding on every level when any one detects smoke. Carbon monoxide alarms go where fuel-burning equipment like a boiler, a furnace or a gas range calls for them. All of this is interior work. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated Vinegar Hill a historic district in January 1997, so anything on the outside of the house, from a conduit to a sounder, needs the Commission's attention, and we avoid exterior work on these houses unless the owner specifically needs it. Our Brooklyn list for a basic residential alarm system starts at $600, and wireless systems run from $600 to $1,200.

What AI Gets Wrong About Fire Alarm Installation in Brooklyn Navy Yard

We could not capture Google's AI Overview for this topic in our build environment, so we do not quote one. The claims below are paraphrased from the kind of general answers and national cost guides that come up for fire alarm questions, each followed by what the buildings and rules at the Yard actually show.

"A business can install its own fire alarm in its own space"

General answers usually picture a single business in a single building, owning the walls and the panel. In that world, the business hires an installer and decides what goes in.

At the Yard, BNYDC manages the buildings on the City's behalf, and its Tenant Manual says alterations generally need plans approved by BNYDC first. In a multi-tenant building, the detectors over a suite are usually part of a building system with an FDNY-reviewed design.

"Adding a few detectors never needs paperwork"

Many answers treat additions as minor and suggest that only new buildings or full systems involve the FDNY.

The distinction in the rules is design, not size. Replacing a device with an identical part in the same place is a repair. Changing the design, including adding devices to a building system or moving a control panel, can require plans filed with the FDNY, and the FDNY's Chapter 9 says design documents are reviewed before installation.

New offices and labs built inside an open Yard floor are exactly the kind of change that can move a job from repair to filing. We identify which it is before quoting.

"Smoke detectors are the right device everywhere"

Consumer guidance focuses on homes, where smoke detection outside bedrooms is the core of good protection, and some answers carry that advice into every setting.

The Yard's tenants weld, grind, fry, bake, brew, haze stages and finish fabric. In those rooms a smoke detector either alarms constantly or gets covered, and in a 20-foot shop or a 58-foot stage, ceiling devices may see smoke late.

Heat, beam and air-sampling detection exist for these spaces. The right choice comes from what happens in the room and what the building's design allows.

"Fire alarm installation costs a standard national range"

National cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr publish averages across many buildings and cities, and AI summaries tend to repeat them.

Those averages cannot account for coordinating with a landlord's fire alarm vendor, drilling 1940s concrete, after-hours freight elevator time that BNYDC charges for, or a filing engineer's fees.

"Any licensed alarm company can handle the whole job"

Generic answers often imply that one installer covers design, filing, installation and inspection.

The NYC Business portal says an FDNY fire alarm plan needs a licensed Master Electrician and an S97 holder. The Tenant Manual adds that electrical work at the Yard must be done by a licensed electrician, and the building's vendor may need to program its own panel.

We hold a New York State low-voltage license and say plainly where it stops. On a filed change, we work alongside the filer and the building's vendor rather than blur who is responsible.

"The whole Navy Yard is a landmark, so nothing can be changed"

Some summaries merge the Yard's various historic designations into one statement that the whole campus is frozen.

The Yard is a National Register historic district, listed in 2014, and a few individual structures such as Dry Dock 1 are city landmarks. Most tenant fire alarm work happens inside, in buildings that have been rebuilt and refitted many times. We found no published Landmarks rule specific to fire alarm work in the Yard.

Exterior work is a different matter, and the City's Design Commission has reviewed exterior projects at the Yard. Outside the wall, Vinegar Hill and Wallabout are city historic districts, so exterior devices on those houses need the Landmarks Preservation Commission's attention.

"Wireless fire alarms solve thick walls"

Answers about older buildings often recommend wireless devices as the universal way around hard construction.

In a building fire alarm system, whether wireless devices are acceptable is a design question for the building, and the lower floors of Building 77 were built with walls about 25 inches thick and no windows, which no one should assume a radio signal will cross reliably.

Wireless interconnected alarms are a good fit for many of the historic houses just outside the gates. Inside the Yard, the design decides, and we test any wireless link in place before trusting it.
