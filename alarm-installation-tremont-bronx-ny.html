The Neighborhood

Why Tremont Properties Need Alarm Systems

Wikipedia draws Tremont as a compact piece of the West Bronx, about three-tenths of a square mile, bounded by East 181st Street on the north, Third Avenue on the east, the Cross-Bronx Expressway on the south and the Grand Concourse on the west, with East Tremont Avenue running through the middle. It is part of Community Board 6, mostly in ZIP code 10457, and patrolled by the 48th Precinct with the 46th covering part of the area. The land is high and hilly, and stair streets climb between blocks at different levels. The building stock is overwhelmingly five- and six-story apartment houses, which Wikipedia counts at more than 82,000 people per square mile. That density is the starting point for every alarm we design here: when something goes wrong in one apartment, it rarely stays one apartment’s problem.

The fire record makes the point. On January 9, 2022, a space heater set fire to a duplex at Twin Parks North West, a 19-story building at 333 East 181st Street on Tremont’s northern edge. Investigators found that the apartment’s self-closing door and a 15th-floor stairwell door had both failed, and the smoke that went through them killed seventeen people. The building’s alarm had sounded straight away, but residents told reporters that false alarms were so routine that many did not believe it. Two years later, on January 16, 2024, News 12 reported a fire in a six-story Tremont apartment building that began in a third-floor apartment and spread into the hallway and up to the fourth floor. In 2018 CBS New York reported a fire on the first floor of a five-story building at 2090 Grand Concourse. None of these was a burglary, and all of them are alarm problems.

Burglary is here too, and it looks like the burglary of any dense walk-up neighborhood. The 48th Precinct reported 252 burglaries in 2022, alongside 467 grand larcenies and 447 robberies, according to the NYPD figures quoted by Wikipedia. In December 2016 CBS New York reported that police were hunting a man who had climbed a fire escape into the same apartment on Valentine Avenue three times in two months, and into a nearby apartment on the Grand Concourse as well: six entries in all, with purses, wallets, credit cards and electronics taken. A burglar who comes back to the same window is the case an alarm is best at stopping, because the second visit is the one you can prepare for.

Then there is heat. New York City requires landlords to supply heat from October 1 to May 31: at least 68°F between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m. whenever it is below 55°F outside, and at least 62°F overnight, whatever the weather. When a radiator falls behind, people plug in space heaters; the investigation of the Twin Parks fire cited a housing survey estimating that about 27 percent of households in neighboring Fordham used supplemental heat. An alarm cannot fix a boiler. It can tell you that a room has gone cold, that smoke or carbon monoxide is present while you sleep, and that your own apartment door is standing open, and it can do all of that without crying wolf, which is the part most systems get wrong.

The ground itself shapes the work. A nineteenth-century postmaster made up the name from Mount Eden, Mount Hope and Fairmount (Wikipedia calls them three neighborhoods, Forgotten New York three hills), and the neighborhood still climbs and drops from block to block. The Grand Concourse runs along a ridge on the west side; East Tremont Avenue bends around the rock outcrops of Echo Park instead of going through them; Valentine Avenue starts its run north to Fordham at the same rocks; and the land falls away eastward toward Webster Avenue, the Metro-North tracks beside Park Avenue and, at the far edge, Third Avenue, where Bronx Borough Hall once stood on its own rocky hill above the street. For an alarm installer, a hill means two things. Rear windows on a slope can sit closer to the ground, or to a neighboring roof, than the floor number suggests. And a building set into rock tends to have a deep cellar and heavy masonry walls, which we account for when testing wireless signal from a ground-floor apartment or a store’s stock room to its panel.

Tremont in six facts that shape an alarm

  • Dense walk-up stock. Wikipedia describes Tremont as dominated by five- and six-story apartment houses on hilly ground, with a population of 24,739 in about 0.3 square miles.
  • Heat season is eight months long. October 1 through May 31 under HPD rules, with 68°F by day and 62°F at night as the minimums.
  • A fire that taught the city about doors. At Twin Parks North West in 2022, two doors that did not close let smoke reach every floor (Wikipedia, citing the New York Times).
  • Burglaries in the precinct. 252 in the 48th Precinct in 2022; crime in all categories fell 60.9 percent between 1990 and 2022.
  • A landmark row of houses. The Morris Avenue Historic District, designated July 15, 1986: two-family row houses and tenements by architect John Hauser, built in five campaigns between 1906 and 1910, most with curved three-story bow fronts.
  • Rent burden. NYC Health’s 2018 profile found 60 percent of Tremont and Belmont residents rent-burdened and 31 percent living in poverty, which is why every quote we write lists every device and every dollar.
Our Approach

An Alarm Tremont Will Still Believe: One Sound for Every Meaning

The most dangerous alarm is the one people have learned to ignore. In a six-story walk-up, a siren reaches every apartment on the line, and after a few false alarms neighbors stop opening their doors to look. Inside a household the same thing happens with beeps: if the keypad chirps for a low battery, for the front door, for the window and for a fire, children and older relatives stop sorting one beep from another. So the rule we work to in Tremont is simple to say and takes care to deliver: every sound the system can make means exactly one thing, and nothing makes a sound unless it means it.

That rule changes three practical things. First, the fire side and the burglary side never share a tone. Monitored smoke detectors use the three-pulse fire pattern, carbon monoxide detectors use the four-pulse pattern, the burglary siren is a steady tone, and the door chime is a single soft note at the keypad that never escalates into a siren on its own. Second, every device is placed so that heat season cannot trip it: no motion detector looking across a radiator or a space heater, no smoke detector a few feet from a stove or a steaming bathroom door. Third, we test the system for nuisance trips before we leave, with the heat on, because a detector that misfires in January will be unplugged by February.

Layer one: the apartment door and the fire-escape window

The burglary side starts where entries are actually reported: the apartment door and any window that opens onto the fire escape. Both get contacts. The fire-escape window is an instant zone, with no entry countdown, because nobody in your household comes home that way. A motion detector covers the hallway every route has to cross, aimed away from radiators. Street-side windows that nobody can reach get glass-break detection only where the glass is large enough to climb through.

Layer two: smoke, carbon monoxide and the open door

The life-safety side is what makes a Tremont alarm different from a template. Monitored smoke detectors go outside each sleeping room and in the main hall; a carbon monoxide detector goes within fifteen feet of each bedroom door, which is also where New York City’s carbon monoxide law requires the landlord’s detector to be. And the same door contact that catches a burglar is programmed to chime when your own door has been left standing open, because an open apartment door is how smoke left the fire apartment at Twin Parks.

Layer three: the cold room

A small temperature sensor on an inside wall sends an app alert when a room drops below the HPD minimums, 68°F by day and 62°F at night, and a second alert if it falls to 55°F. The history it keeps gives you dates and times when you call your landlord or file a heat complaint with 311. It cannot make the radiators work, and we never claim it does. What it does is tell you early, before anyone in the household reaches for the oven door or a second space heater.

What we leave out on purpose

Outdoor sirens on a walk-up facade, hallway sensors on the building’s side of your door, and motion detectors in rooms with space heaters all cause more nuisance than protection in Tremont buildings. We also never link an apartment system to the building’s own fire alarm or intercom; those belong to the landlord, and tying into them makes your system less reliable, not more. Where the building itself needs work, such as a door closer that no longer pulls the door shut, we tell you in writing so you can raise it with the landlord or with 311.

Building Specialists

Alarm Systems for Tremont’s Architecture

Tremont’s buildings fall into a handful of types, and each one changes where the panel goes, how the wireless signal travels and which openings matter. These are the types we see most, described from the sources on this page and from our own installation practice.

Five- and six-story walk-ups

The bulk of the neighborhood. Brick and block walls with plaster inside, steel apartment doors in steel frames, radiators under the windows and a fire escape on the front or rear wall. The apartment door and the fire-escape windows carry most of the risk; signal is rarely a problem through plaster partitions.

Concourse elevator apartment houses

Along the Concourse at the west edge, Forgotten New York points to Art Deco and Moderne buildings of the 1920s and 1930s, and to a narrow 1914 apartment house where the Concourse, East Tremont and Monroe Avenues meet. Larger apartments, longer hallways, more windows facing light courts, and lobbies with more traffic.

Morris Avenue row houses

The landmark row between East Tremont Avenue and East 179th Street: brick two-family houses and tenements by John Hauser, 1906 to 1910, with curved bow fronts, stoops and iron areaway railings. Each house has a street door, a lower areaway door and a rear yard, and usually two households.

1970s towers on the northern edge

Twin Parks North West, built in 1972 as a state affordable-housing project, is 19 stories with 120 apartments, and Wikipedia describes the Twin Parks sites as scattered across Fordham, Tremont and East Tremont. Concrete construction, interior stairwells and self-closing doors that the whole building depends on.

Newer buildings near Webster and Third

City Planning rezoned the Tremont Avenue corridor from Webster Avenue eastward in October 2010, allowing taller mixed-use buildings with apartments over ground-floor shops. Modern windows, apartment-level smoke detection already in place, and often a smart lock or video intercom run by management.

Apartments over the avenue shops

Along East Tremont Avenue, City Planning describes single-story retail, older theaters and office buildings with shops below and upper floors that are sometimes empty. Upper apartments here often share a rear wall or a roof line with a store.

Materials and methods

On steel apartment doors we fix contacts to the inside face and frame with screws into the frame or high-bond tape, never into the door leaf, and we never touch the closer, hinges or latch. On prewar plaster we use anchors sized for plaster rather than drywall, and in rental apartments we prefer tape mounts that come off cleanly when you move. On the Morris Avenue houses everything goes inside; the bow-front windows each get their own contact on the sash that opens, because the curved wall leaves no single frame to share. In towers and newer buildings with concrete or foil-backed walls we confirm each device’s signal from its exact spot before it is fixed, and if the panel cannot hold a strong cellular signal in the closet you chose, we move the panel rather than accept a weak one.

Most Tremont households that ask us about burglary protection end up adding the fire and carbon monoxide side once they see how little it adds to the bill; most store owners on the avenue pair the alarm with camera installation in Tremont so an alarm at the back door can be checked on video before anyone drives over.

Renters, owners and the line between them

Most Tremont households rent, and most of the buildings belong to someone who is not in them every day. That makes it worth being exact about who owns what before a single screw goes in. The apartment alarm is yours: the panel, keypad, contacts, detectors and sensors you pay for stay yours, and they come with you when you move. The building’s systems are the owner’s: the street door and its intercom, the hallway lighting, the fire escape, the self-closing device on your apartment door, the building’s own smoke and carbon monoxide detectors, and any building fire alarm. We never wire into those, and we never replace a device the law requires the owner to supply.

In practice this means three things. Detectors we add sit alongside the landlord’s rather than taking their place, so nobody can later say the legally required detector was removed. Mounting on a rented door frame uses screws or high-bond tape on the inside face, never holes through the door leaf. And where something of the owner’s is broken, such as a closer, a fire-escape window lock or a street door that no longer latches, we put it in writing so you have something concrete to show the landlord or to describe to 311. A homeowner on Morris Avenue has the opposite job: everything is yours, including the duty to provide detectors for a tenant, and we design a two-household system that respects that.

Worked Examples

Five Tremont Homes, Five Alarm Plans

Every plan starts from the same few questions, but the answers differ sharply from one building to the next. These five examples are schematic, drawn to show how the reasoning plays out rather than to describe anyone’s actual home, and each one is priced from the Bronx list.

A. A fourth-floor studio in a walk-up off Valentine Avenue

One room, a short hall, a bathroom and a kitchen alcove, with the main window on the fire escape. The radiator under that window is weak, and a space heater runs on the coldest nights. The plan: a contact on the door, an instant contact on the fire-escape window, one motion detector in the hall aimed toward the door and away from the room, a smoke detector at the hall ceiling, a CO detector beside it and a temperature sensor on the inside wall of the room. Six devices, panel and keypad, inside the basic range. The space heater gets no detector pointed at it, and the temperature sensor tells the tenant when the room has given up before the heater goes on.

B. A two-bedroom on the Concourse with children

A long inner hall, two bedrooms at the far end, windows on a light court and one on the fire escape. Here the distance from the door to the bedrooms is the design problem: a siren by the door may not wake a child at the far end, and a fire in the kitchen near the door can cut the hall in two. The plan puts the sounder near the bedrooms, smoke detectors outside each bedroom and in the hall, a CO detector between the bedroom doors, an instant contact on the fire-escape window, and the door contact with its open-door chime. The court windows get nothing, because nobody can reach them, and we say so in the quote.

C. A Morris Avenue two-family with an owner below and a tenant above

Three levels: the areaway level, the owner’s floor and the tenant’s floor, with a street door, an areaway door under the stoop and a rear door to the yard. The landmark facade means nothing goes outside. The plan splits the house into two areas on one panel, gives each household its own keypad and codes, puts contacts on all three doors and the reachable rear windows, a motion detector on each floor, smoke and CO on every level and a heat detector near the boiler. It is priced on the complete home protection line, from $2,500, and the tenant’s fire and CO alerts reach both households.

D. A rear apartment over a store on East Tremont Avenue

The bedroom and living room windows look out over the flat roof of the store below, which runs into the roofs of the stores next door. Those rear windows are reachable from the roofs even though they are on the second floor, so each gets a contact and the largest gets a glass-break detector. The rest of the plan is standard for an apartment: door, hall motion, smoke, CO and temperature. The store’s own alarm is separate, and if the store owner agrees, a smoke detector in the store’s back room can be added to the store’s system to give earlier warning to the floor above.

E. A store on the avenue with empty floors above

A glass front behind a roll-down gate, a sales floor, a stock room and an office at the back, and a building above with nobody in it. The plan protects the front glass with glass-break detection and the door with a contact, the rear door with a contact, the stock room and the ceiling line with motion detection, and the door to the upper stair with its own contact. Smoke detection in the stock room reports to the station. Staff get individual codes and the owner gets opening and closing reports. It falls inside the small business range of $1,500 to $3,000.

Tremont’s eastern edge. South of East Tremont Avenue, City Planning’s maps show the manufacturing zoning that holds auto and warehouse uses: overhead doors, yards and roof hatches rather than glass storefronts.

Park Avenue beside Metro-North

A neighborhood street running alongside the Harlem Line tracks, with the Tremont station on the line. Buildings here have one wall facing the open rail right-of-way, and trains pass through the day and night.

Medical offices, day cares, salons and small offices in the bottom floors of apartment buildings, sharing a street door, a cellar and sometimes a hallway with the residents above.

Rules a Tremont business alarm has to respect

  • Fifteen minutes of siren, then silence. New York City’s Administrative Code limits an audible burglar alarm on a building to fifteen minutes; we program every commercial siren to stop well inside that, which matters doubly when apartments sit over the store.
  • A licensed installer. Alarm installation in New York State requires a license from the Department of State; ours is #12000287431, and we show it on request before any work starts.
  • Fire alarms are a separate system. Smoke detectors on a burglary panel give early warning, but where a business needs a code-required fire alarm system, that is designed and filed as its own job through our Tremont fire alarm installation service.
  • Exit doors stay exit doors. A contact on a rear exit reports when it opens; it never locks it. Anything that would delay egress needs the fire code’s approval and is not part of a standard burglar alarm.
  • Accessible controls. Where staff or customers with disabilities use a keypad, we mount it at a reachable height and pair it with a key fob as an alternative to typing a code.
  • The landlord’s building, your alarm. In a mixed-use building the street door, cellar and fire alarm usually belong to the owner. Your system covers your space, and we document anything that needs the owner’s consent before we drill.

A store’s alarm is also the upstairs tenants’ early warning

Most stores along East Tremont Avenue and Webster Avenue have apartments or empty floors above them, and a fire that starts in a closed store at night is first a danger to the people sleeping overhead. A burglary panel with a monitored smoke detector in the stock room and the office gives the earliest warning a store owner can offer them: the central station hears about it within seconds and requests the fire department, whether or not anyone is passing on the street. It costs the store a detector or two and nothing extra in monitoring.

The burglary side of a store under apartments has its own wrinkle. The siren that would be fine on a stand-alone building is heard through the floor by every family above, so we set it to sound long enough to drive off an intruder and stop well inside the city’s fifteen-minute limit, and we make sure every staff member knows how to silence it and report a mistake. Where the floors above are empty, as City Planning found in some of the corridor’s older theater and office buildings, we add protection on the ceiling line and at the door to the upper stair, because the quiet floors above are a way in as well as a neighbor.

Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Tremont: Questions Answered

These are the questions Tremont residents and business owners bring to us that are specific to how people live here: in dense buildings, through a long heat season, with fire escapes on most of the walls. The answers describe what we install and how we program it.

When people stop believing alarms

At Twin Parks in January 2022, the building alarm went off right away, but many residents assumed it was another false alarm. How do you make sure our household and our neighbors still react when our alarm sounds?

By making the system boringly reliable before it ever has to be urgent. We place every detector where ordinary life cannot trip it: smoke detectors out of the line of the stove and the bathroom door, motion detectors aimed away from radiators, space heaters and curtains, door contacts aligned so a warped frame in January does not read as open. Then we run the heat, cook something and open the bathroom door during the handover to prove it. We give each event its own sound, so a fire never sounds like a door and a low battery never sounds like a fire, and we set the burglary siren to sound inside the apartment rather than on the building’s facade, where neighbors would hear it for every mistake. Finally we keep the call list short and real: you first, then the person you named, then the monitoring station, so a genuine alarm reaches someone who will act. A system that has never cried wolf is the one people move for.

Twin Parks’ smoke spread because the burning apartment’s door stayed open after the family escaped. Can our alarm tell us, or tell someone else, when our apartment door has been left standing open?

Yes, and it works whether or not the alarm is armed. The contact on your apartment door already knows when the door is open; we program it so that after a minute the keypad gives a soft chime, after three minutes your phone gets a message that names the door and the time, and a second person on the account can be told as well. Nothing sounds the siren just because a door is open, so it never becomes another noise people ignore. If a smoke detector goes off while the door is open, the keypad shows both facts at once. What a contact cannot do is shut the door. In New York City the self-closing device on an apartment door in a building of more than three units is the landlord’s to provide and keep working, and if your door no longer swings shut and latches on its own, the event log gives you a record of how often it has been found standing open to show the landlord or 311.

Heat season, October 1 to May 31

HPD says our apartment should be at least 68°F during the day and 62°F at night all winter. Can a sensor on our alarm keep a record of how cold the apartment actually got?

Yes. A temperature sensor on the alarm reports its readings to the app, and on the platforms we install the app keeps a history with dates and times that you can scroll back through and screenshot. We set two alerts: one when the room drops below the HPD minimum for that time of day, and a sharper one at 55°F. We mount the sensor on an inside wall, chest high, away from radiators, windows, the stove and direct sun, so the reading reflects the room rather than a draft or a hot pipe. Two honest limits: an alarm sensor is not a calibrated instrument, and an HPD inspector takes his or her own readings, so treat the history as your record for calls to the landlord and to 311 rather than as proof in itself. Hot water, which must be at least 120°F all year, is not something an alarm sensor measures.

The radiator in one bedroom barely works, so we run a space heater there on the worst nights. What should the alarm add for a room like that?

Three things. A monitored smoke detector just outside that bedroom door, so a fire in the room is reported to the central station even if everyone is asleep or out. A temperature sensor in the room itself, so you know when the radiator has given up before you decide to plug anything in. And no motion detector in that room, because the warm air moving off a heater can look like a person to a passive infrared sensor. We also look at how the heater is plugged in while we are there: a heater on an extension cord or sharing an outlet with a microwave is the kind of thing worth fixing that day, and we will say so. The fire risk itself is a matter for the heater, the cord and the outlet; what the alarm adds is the early warning and the call.

When the boiler fails, some people in our building heat the kitchen by leaving the gas oven on with the door open. What would a carbon monoxide detector on the alarm do that a plug-in one does not?

First, please do not heat with a gas oven; an oven is not built to heat a room, it can fill the kitchen with carbon monoxide, and an open oven door beside towels and curtains is a fire risk. Where it happens anyway, a panel-connected CO detector does two things a plug-in unit cannot. It reports the alarm to the monitoring station, which requests the fire department even if everyone in the apartment is too drowsy to act, and it sounds the four-pulse CO pattern through the whole system rather than from one small box in the hall. It also tells your phone if you are out and somebody else at home is not answering. City law already requires your landlord to provide a CO detector within fifteen feet of each bedroom door; ours goes alongside it, not instead of it, and never replaces the landlord’s obligation.

Fire escapes and the second visit

In 2016 police said one man went into the same Valentine Avenue apartment three times in two months by its fire escape. If someone has already come in once, what should change before they try again?

Everything about the way in they used. First, the exact window: a contact on the sash, set as an instant zone with no countdown, and a glass-break detector in that room in case the glass is broken rather than the window opened. Second, the route: a motion detector placed so that anyone crossing from that room toward the rest of the apartment is seen within a step or two. Third, what they now know: new alarm codes, a new monitoring passcode, and a check on whether any keys, mail keys or cards were taken that would let them use the door next time. If the first entry was recent, we can usually fit the window and the motion detector on the first visit and add the rest the same week. The reason this matters is in that 2016 report: the burglar returned to the same place, which means the second entry is the one you can see coming.

Our store is on the ground floor of an old building on East Tremont Avenue whose upper floors have stood empty for years. Does the empty space above us change the alarm?

It changes where we expect an intruder to come from. With nobody upstairs, someone can get into the vacant floors from the roof or a rear fire escape, work undisturbed, and come down through a ceiling, a stairwell door or a shared shaft into your store. So in addition to the front glass and the rear door, we put contacts on any door between your space and the upstairs stair, motion detection that covers the ceiling line at the back of the store rather than only the floor, and a shock or glass-break sensor on any skylight or rear window that sits under the empty floors. If you can get the owner’s permission, a contact on the building’s roof door or upper stair door is worth adding, reporting to your panel. City Planning noted exactly this pattern on the corridor, theaters and office buildings with active stores below and vacant floors above.

Smoke from somebody else’s apartment

In January 2024 a fire in a six-story Tremont building started in a third-floor apartment and spread into the hallway and up to the fourth floor. If the fire is in a neighbor’s apartment, where does our smoke detector need to be to warn us while the hallway is still passable?

Inside your apartment, in the entry hall a few feet from your front door, on the ceiling and away from the corner where the wall meets it. Smoke from a hallway fire reaches you first through the gap around and under the apartment door, and a detector there catches it well before the one outside the bedrooms would. On our system that detector reports to the central station, which requests the fire department, and it sounds the three-pulse fire pattern through every sounder in the apartment, so a sleeping household hears it at the far end of the apartment as well as by the door. What you do next depends on the building and on what you see, and the fire department’s advice is the guide: after the Twin Parks fire, the FDNY commissioner said that in fireproof high-rises it is often safer to stay in the apartment than to go into a smoke-filled stairwell. Whatever you decide, keep your apartment door closed, and the open-door chime will remind you if it is not.

Wired or wireless behind Tremont plaster

Most Tremont apartments get wireless systems, and not only because most households rent. Keeping it believable: a heat-season routine

A system stays believable only if it stays quiet when nothing is wrong. That takes about ten minutes a month from October to May. At the start of the season, test every detector and replace any battery that chirped last winter; the cold is what finishes off a weak battery. In November, check that the apartment door still swings shut and latches on its own and that no motion detector has a new space heater in its view. Through the coldest months, glance at the temperature history after cold nights and keep a screenshot of anything below the minimum. In spring, dust the smoke detectors with a soft brush and check that window contacts still line up after the sashes have been opened for the first warm days.

Use the panel’s test mode for any of this, so nothing is dispatched, and call us if a device keeps causing trouble. A detector that misfires twice is moved or replaced, because a third false alarm is the one that teaches a household to stop listening.

“Renters can’t really have a monitored alarm.”

An alarm detects; a camera shows. In Tremont stores we often pair the two, so that when the back door opens at night the owner can see the camera before deciding whether to send someone.

The hardware is national; the installation is local. A Tremont walk-up from October to May has radiators knocking under every window, space heaters in some bedrooms, steel doors in steel frames and fire escapes outside. Each of those changes where a detector can sit without misfiring.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Surveying a Walk-Up With the Heat On

This is how we survey an apartment in heat season, written as a method rather than a story about any one customer. The first thing we do in a Tremont apartment between October and May is listen. Radiators knock, hiss and tick as the steam comes up; a space heater clicks on in a bedroom; the kitchen window fogs when a pot boils. Each of those is a place where a careless detector would misfire, so we note them on a sketch of the apartment before we look at a single window.

Next we walk the apartment as smoke would. Where is the nearest bedroom door to the kitchen? Where would smoke from the hallway first reach? Does the apartment door swing shut and latch when we let go of it? If it does not, that goes in writing at the top of the quote, because no alarm we install can do the closer’s job and the household should know it before anything else.

Only then do we walk it as a burglar would, starting at the fire escape. We step out to the landing where it is safe to do so, look at which windows the landing reaches, and check how the sashes lock. We look down at the yard or the store roof below the rear windows. Then we decide what is reachable and what is not, and we write both lists down.

The last step is the one people remember: before we leave, everyone in the household hears every sound the system can make, once, with the reason for it. It takes ten minutes and it is the most important ten minutes of the job.

Cold-room alerts, open-door chimes and trouble messages such as low batteries come to your phones only, because none of them needs a dispatcher. Burglary alarms go to your phone first and then to the station, which works through your call list before requesting police. You can change who receives each kind of alert at any time in the app.
