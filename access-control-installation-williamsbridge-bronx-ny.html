The Neighborhood

Why Williamsbridge Properties Need Access Control

Williamsbridge sits in the north-central Bronx, and where exactly depends on whom you ask. Wikipedia runs it from East 222nd Street in the north to Adee Avenue in the south, between the Bronx River and Boston Road, and names White Plains Road as its main street. CityNeighborhoods.NYC keeps the same north and east edges but stops at East Gun Hill Road in the south and the Bronx River Parkway in the west. Both put it in Bronx Community Board 12, and Wikipedia lists ZIP codes 10466, 10467 and 10469 and says the 47th Precinct, on Laconia Avenue, patrols it. Rather than argue about lines, this page names streets.

The name is older than the borough’s subways. Wikipedia, citing the Parks Department, traces it to John Williams, an eighteenth-century farmer whose land lay on the east bank of the Bronx River near today’s Gun Hill Road and White Plains Road and who is credited with building the first bridge across the river there. Williamsbridge incorporated as a village on November 23, 1888. The railroad came first: the Williams Bridge station on what is now Metro-North’s Harlem Line opened in 1842, and the White Plains Road elevated line reached East 219th Street in 1917. The Williamsbridge Oval park, which carries the name, is in fact in Norwood, across the river to the west; Wikipedia says so, and so does CityNeighborhoods.

What the neighborhood looks like from the sidewalk is mostly houses. Wikipedia describes Williamsbridge as multi-family homes and moderate-size multi-unit buildings, with most residences privately owned, owner-occupied two- and three-story homes on low, flat ground. CityNeighborhoods adds brick rowhouses, two-family homes and small apartment buildings, and calls White Plains Road the commercial spine, with Gun Hill Road and Boston Road as the other shopping streets. Those corridors are where this page spends most of its time, because that is where houses of worship and stores share walls and roofs.

Some of those houses of worship are landmarks in everything but name. The Church of the Immaculate Conception, whose rectory address is 754 East Gun Hill Road, was founded as a parish in 1902 and 1903; Wikipedia records that its first church burned on December 23, 1923, and that the present twin-towered brick church was built in 1925 across Gun Hill Road from the old one. CityNeighborhoods lists St. Frances of Rome, founded in 1927, and Grace Congregational Church among the neighborhood’s churches. Many more congregations are small and meet in rented stores, and those are the doors we are asked about most.

AESS schematic from the boundary descriptions in Wikipedia and CityNeighborhoods.NYC; not a survey. Where the two disagree, this page names streets rather than choosing a line.

The March 2026 fire showed how close those small congregations sit to everyone else. CBS New York placed it at a strip of stores at 1234 East Gun Hill Road, reported that 11 stores were affected, including a deli, a cleaners, a discount store, a printing business, a church, a salon and a driving school, and said the flames spread through the cockloft, the space between the roof and the ceiling. CBS counted 74 FDNY units and more than 230 first responders, and said the Department of Buildings issued a vacate order for the entire structure. ABC7 reported the fire under control just before 2:30 a.m., two firefighters with minor injuries, and owners waiting to learn whether they could rebuild. It also reported that investigators were focusing on a church in the middle of the block as the place the fire was believed to have started, while the cause remained under investigation. We do not know the cause, and nothing on this page suggests one.

Where that row belongs is itself a boundary question. CBS, ABC7 and the Daily News called the location Williamsbridge; Norwood News called it the Laconia section and logged the second, third and fourth alarms at 12:08, 12:16 and 12:24 a.m. By Wikipedia’s map it sits near the eastern edge. We use it here because the stations covering it called it Williamsbridge, and because the same kind of row lines the corridors at the heart of the neighborhood: a run of stores under one roof, each with a different tenant, a different set of hours and a different idea of who should have a key.

There is a second thread, quieter than a fire. In 2016 the Bronx Times profiled the Gun Hill Road Civic Group, founded in 2014 after a string of car break-ins in residents’ driveways, covering East Gun Hill Road between Bronx Park East and I-95, working with the 47th and 49th Precincts, and meeting in space donated by a ministry on East Gun Hill Road. That is a common pattern in the neighborhood and the reason for a whole section below: a congregation’s room that is also the neighborhood’s meeting room, opened on a weeknight for people who are not members and do not hold keys.

Finally, the corridors are busy. In its August 2014 study of East Gun Hill Road between Jerome Avenue and White Plains Road, the city’s Department of Transportation counted 3,114 pedestrians in the morning peak hour at White Plains Road, the highest single-hour count in the study. A door on a sidewalk like that is never unwatched for long in daylight, and is very much alone at two in the morning. Both conditions matter to how it is locked.

Put those threads together and Williamsbridge asks something particular of a door system. The door may belong to a congregation that rents its space, lends it out, and opens it to large crowds on a schedule. It sits in a row where a neighbor’s emergency can become its own. And it is usually opened by volunteers rather than staff, many of whom were handed a key years ago. Every section below follows from that.

Church Door Jammed or Lock Dead Before the Service in Williamsbridge? Call, Don’t Chain It

Most of our same-day calls from congregations come on the day of a service. The electric strike on the side entrance stopped releasing. The keypad on the office went dark because its battery was never changed. The panic bar on the rear exit sticks so badly that somebody has started latching the door with a slide bolt. A storefront’s glass door closer leaks, so the door no longer pulls shut behind the last person out. Ring (646) 490-0629, tell us which door and when the service starts, and we give you an arrival window on the call.

One request while you wait: never chain, padlock or bolt an exit door to solve a lock problem while people are inside. If the outside of a door will not lock, post an usher at it; if the inside will not open, use another exit and keep that door clear of the crowd. We would rather arrive to a door held by a person than to a door that cannot be opened from inside.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Williamsbridge’s Architecture

Wikipedia’s summary of Williamsbridge, mostly two- and three-story owner-occupied homes and moderate-size apartment buildings, is accurate for the side streets. The avenues add stores, mixed-use buildings and churches, and each of those has its own door problem.

Stores in a row under one roof

The type at the center of this page. Several storefronts share a roof and a void above their ceilings, each behind its own glass door and roll-down gate, with rear exits onto a yard or alley and sometimes a shared cellar. The March 2026 fire showed that the void connects them. We keep each tenant’s controller, power and cable inside that tenant’s own space.

Storefronts used for worship

A store that becomes a sanctuary changes what its doors must do. The front door is now the main exit for a crowd, the back room becomes an office or a choir room, and the cellar becomes storage for chairs and sound equipment. Assembly rules, not store rules, decide which locks belong on the exits.

Purpose-built churches and parish buildings

Masonry churches such as Immaculate Conception on Gun Hill Road, often with a hall or school on the same lot. Churches of this kind usually have heavy main doors plus side, sacristy and hall doors. We leave the great doors mechanical and work on the side, hall and office doors that people actually use every day.

Mixed-use buildings

Apartments over stores, with a residential street door squeezed between two shopfronts. The apartments need a video station and fobs; the stores below need to stay on separate systems, so a tenant upstairs never depends on a shop’s power or lease.

Two- and three-story brick houses

The houses Wikipedia says make up most of the neighborhood, often with an owner upstairs and a tenant downstairs, a side or basement door and a driveway. Keypad deadbolts per household and a video doorbell with a release do most of the work.

Small apartment buildings

Low-rise and moderate-size buildings with a street door, a vestibule, a cellar and a roof door. A video station, a fob reader on the vestibule and a contact on the cellar door cover the common risks.

Building types from Wikipedia and CityNeighborhoods.NYC; hardware choices are AESS practice.

Why the roof matters to a door system

A cockloft is, in the words Norwood News used in its fire report, an uninhabited and often inaccessible void between the top-floor ceiling and the roof. In a row of stores it can run above several tenants at once. For an installer the temptation is obvious: it is the easiest place to pull a cable from one store to the next, or from a controller to a reader at the far end of the building. We do not do it. Each tenant’s controller, lock power supply and battery live inside that tenant’s own space, and their cables stay there.

Where a wall or ceiling must be crossed, the building code says how the hole is closed. Section 714 of the New York City Building Code governs penetrations into or through fire walls, fire barriers, smoke barrier walls and fire partitions; in practice that means a listed firestop system, installed as tested, at every opening we make in a rated assembly. We photograph each one before the ceiling goes back and include the photographs in the record we hand over. We cannot say whether any particular wall in a particular Williamsbridge row is rated; the landlord’s architect can. Our rule is to treat every party wall as if it were.

There is a second reason to keep each tenant’s system in its own space. When a store is damaged, a shared controller turns one tenant’s loss into everyone’s. A separate system per tenant can be shut off, removed and rebuilt without touching the neighbors’ doors.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Williamsbridge: Questions Answered

These are the questions congregations, landlords and shop owners on the Williamsbridge corridors actually put to us, grouped by what they are really about. Each is answered where it is asked, in full.

A congregation’s door: who opens it and when

Our ministry rents a store on Gun Hill Road. Who has to agree before an electric lock and a reader go on a door we don’t own?

The landlord, in writing, before any drilling. An electric strike means cutting the door frame; electrified panic trim means replacing hardware on the door; a reader means a hole in the wall or the frame for its cable. All of that is a change to the landlord’s property, and most commercial leases say alterations need consent. We give the congregation a one-page scope to send: photographs of each door, the hardware we propose, where the cable runs, where the power supply sits, and what we would do at move-out to return the door to its original condition.

The congregation’s own board decides everything else: who gets credentials, which doors, which hours. And the space’s certificate, if it has a place of assembly certificate, may set conditions on the exit doors that neither the landlord nor the board can waive. That is a question for the architect or expediter who handled the certificate, and we supply hardware data sheets to them on request.

One thing we ask early is who pays the electric bill for the space. A lock power supply draws very little, but it has to be on a circuit the congregation controls, not on a landlord’s meter that might be switched off between tenants.

AESS practice; lease terms vary and govern.

Over the years we gave out front-door keys to ushers, musicians and the cleaning volunteer. How do we take them all back without a fight?

By making the keys stop working rather than asking for them. The sequence we follow is simple: count every key and code by role, install the readers and enroll one credential per person, and then change the lock cylinders one last time. From that day, the only working keys are the two or three the board keeps for the fire service, the landlord and emergencies. Nobody has to be asked to hand anything in, and nobody is singled out.

The census matters more than the hardware. Before we order anything, we sit with the pastor or a trustee and list every key, every alarm code and every person who opens a door, by role rather than by name. The usual surprise is the number: congregations that think they have eight keys out often find fifteen once former members, the landlord, the cleaner and a long-ago contractor are counted.

After the change, a lost fob is deleted in seconds and replaced for the price of a fob, instead of a locksmith visit for every cylinder in the building.

AESS worksheet format; we fill it in with the board at the first visit and keep only roles, not names, in our copy.

AESS sequence for congregations moving from keys to credentials.

Our storefront’s front door is also its main exit. How can the street side be open only for services while the inside always pushes open?

Yes, and without making the exit any less free. We fit electrified trim to the outside of the existing panic bar, or to a lever set where the code allows one. A schedule in the controller unlocks the outside trim for the service window, say from half an hour before the start, and locks it again at a set time afterward. The inside bar pushes open at every moment, scheduled or not. If the service runs long, the head usher extends the unlock from a phone; if it ends early, the usher locks it from the same app.

For the end of the night, the important feature is not the lock but the alert. A door contact tells two officers if the front door is still standing open twenty minutes after the schedule locked it, which is the usual way a storefront ends up unlocked overnight: the last person out props it for a moment to carry chairs and forgets.

Holidays and special services go into the schedule ahead of time. A trustee with administrator rights adds them once, at the start of the season, and checks the calendar again in the month before.

Example only. The exit side of every door stays free at all times, whatever the schedule says about the outside.

Between members and visitors, more than 75 people fill our space on Sundays. Which locks are we no longer allowed to use on the exits?

Once a room or space in an assembly occupancy holds an occupant load of 75 or more, Section 1010.1.10 of the NYC Building Code says the doors serving it may have no latch or lock other than panic hardware or fire exit hardware. That rules out a deadbolt, a slide bolt, a hasp, a keyed lever that has to be turned, and a magnetic lock that is not released by the panic bar itself. The 75 is the code’s figure, and the occupant load generally comes from the space’s design and certificate, not from a head count on a good Sunday.

Two neighboring rules matter too. Section 1010.1.9.7 does not allow delayed-egress locking systems in Group A occupancies at all. And the exception that lets stores and offices key-lock a main entrance while it is open for business, in Section 1010.1.9.3, names Groups B, M and S; it does not list assembly. So the front door of a storefront sanctuary is planned as an exit first and an entrance second.

The Department of Buildings also requires a Place of Assembly Certificate of Operation where 75 or more members of the public gather indoors for religious purposes, followed by an annual permit from the Fire Department. If your space has one, door hardware changes may need to be coordinated with it. We are not code consultants; we install hardware that fits the code as the architect applies it, and we will not install anything that locks an exit against the people inside.

Quoted or summarized from the NYC Building Code as published by American Legal Publishing and from the Department of Buildings; this is not legal advice, and the building's architect or expediter decides how the code applies to a given space.

Equipment has gone missing after events. Is a delayed-exit lock on the back door an option for us?

Not in a worship space. Delayed-egress locks, the kind that hold a door for fifteen seconds and sound an alarm, are expressly not permitted in Group A occupancies by Section 1010.1.9.7. What we fit instead is a door contact and a local alarm on the rear exit, so that the door sounds when it is opened outside an event, plus a camera that sees who walks out. The exit stays instant; the record does the work a delay would have done.

The other answer is to move what walks away. Sound equipment, instruments and the laptop that runs the screens can live in a storage room on its own reader, open only to the people who set up and pack away. A room that only four people can open, with a log, protects equipment better than any exit alarm.

For the back door itself, the most common real problem is a door that does not latch, propped or simply failing to close, so that anyone can walk in from the yard. We fix the closer and the strike first.

AESS practice for small assembly spaces; the architect of record confirms each door against the code and the certificate.

AESS schematic of a common arrangement; exact hardware depends on the door and the manufacturer's listing.

Where should the offering be counted, and how do we keep that room to two or three people?

In one room with one door, on its own credential group of two or three people, with a fail-secure strike and a lever that always opens from inside. Fail-secure means the door stays locked when the power fails, which is what you want for cash and records; the inside lever means nobody can be trapped. The reader logs every entry, so the board can see who opened the room and when.

We often add a camera that sees the door, not the table, so the record shows who went in without recording the counting itself. Most boards prefer that, and it avoids filming members.

If the room has a window onto a yard or alley, a contact on the window and the alarm on the same system finish the job. A Bronx church burglary reported by News 12 in June 2026, at a church on Sedgwick Avenue in Kingsbridge Heights, involved entry through a window just before 11:10 p.m. on May 24; it is not a Williamsbridge case, but it is the kind of entry a door reader alone does not cover.

Example layout drawn by AESS to show door roles; real storefronts differ, and the architect's plan governs exits.

When the civic association meets in our fellowship room, how do we keep the sanctuary and the office closed to them while the hall is open?

By treating the inside of the building as rooms with their own doors, not as one space behind one front door. On a meeting night the side entrance and the fellowship room are unlocked to the group, through a credential issued for that booking or by the trustee who opens up; the sanctuary doors, the office and the storage room stay on their own groups and stay locked. The group’s path from the street to the hall and the restrooms is the only route open to it, and the exits from that path always open from inside.

The Bronx Times profile of the Gun Hill Road Civic Group described exactly this kind of arrangement: a neighborhood group meeting in space donated by a ministry on East Gun Hill Road. Space lent for the good of the neighborhood is one of the things congregations here do best, and it should not cost the congregation control of its own building.

If a group meets every week, it gets a recurring credential with an end date at the close of its program year, reviewed and renewed by the board rather than left running forever.

Example only; each congregation's board sets its own groups. Names never appear in this table, only roles.

On pantry mornings a line forms on the sidewalk before we open. How do volunteers get in without the line coming in early?

By giving the volunteers a different door, or a different time on the same door. The usual arrangement is that the front door stays locked to the outside until the advertised opening time, while pantry volunteers come in through the side entrance with a credential that works only for the hour before and the length of the shift. At opening, the coordinator unlocks the front from a phone, or the schedule does it. Nobody has to open the front door a crack to let one volunteer squeeze past the line.

On a busy corridor this matters more than it sounds. DOT’s 2014 count found more than three thousand people an hour crossing at White Plains Road and Gun Hill Road in the morning peak; a queue on a sidewalk like that is a crowd, and a door opened early for one person tends to stay open.

Inside, the pantry room or storage area keeps its own reader, open to the coordinator and two or three regulars, so that stock is not reachable by everyone who holds a volunteer credential for the day.

Two congregations take turns in the same storefront. Can each keep its own members, schedule and records apart from the other?

Yes, and it is worth doing properly, because shared spaces are where keys multiply fastest. One controller runs the doors, but it is divided into two sites, each with its own administrators, its own members, its own schedules and its own logs. Congregation A cannot see who belongs to congregation B or when they came in, and the reverse. The landlord, if it holds a credential, sees neither list.

The part both share is the part the code controls: the exits. Those are the same doors with the same panic hardware for everyone, and a schedule for one congregation can never lock the inside of an exit against the other. Where the schedules meet, on a Sunday afternoon, say, we leave a gap between them, so one group’s locking time never falls during the other’s arrival.

Storage is the usual source of friction. We suggest each congregation keep its equipment and papers behind its own reader or in its own keyed cabinet, rather than sharing a back room, and we write the arrangement into the handover record so that a change of leadership on either side does not undo it.

Example arrangement; the lease and the agreement between the congregations govern.

Stores that share a roof

Ten tenants share the space above our ceiling. Is it acceptable for the reader cable to run across that shared void to the far end of our store?

Within your own store, above your own ceiling, a cable may run where the landlord allows and the code permits. Across the void into a neighbor’s space, no. The drawing above shows the difference. We keep every cable inside the tenant’s own space, and if a run must pass through a wall or a ceiling that separates tenants, the hole is closed with a listed firestop system under Building Code Section 714 and photographed before it is covered.

The reason is not only fire. A cable that passes through a neighbor’s space can be cut by the neighbor’s contractor, damaged in the neighbor’s leak, or left behind when the neighbor renovates, and nobody will know whose it was. A cable that stays in your store is your cable, recorded on your drawing.

If the only route is through someone else’s space, we look for another: down to the cellar and back up, along the inside face of the shopfront, or wireless where the door hardware supports it.

Should the lock on our office and the locks on the sanctuary exits run off the same power supply?

They can share a supply box, but not a behavior, and we usually give them separate outputs so one fault cannot affect the other. The two kinds of door want opposite things. A fail-safe lock unlocks when power is lost; a fail-secure lock stays locked. On an exit door the question is settled by the code: the exit side opens freely at all times, either through mechanical panic hardware or through a release that the code describes, such as a sensor-released lock that unlocks on power loss and on fire alarm under Section 1010.1.9.8. On the outside of that door, staying locked when power fails is usually what a storefront wants.

On interior doors, choose by what is behind them. The office and the storage room should stay locked without power, because a blackout is exactly when a store is unattended; they keep a lever that opens from inside. Only the exit locks are tied to the fire alarm release, where the space has an alarm, so an alarm opens the way out without also opening the office. Separate outputs, labeled in the supply box, keep those two jobs from being confused by the next person who works on it.

The fire service will get in regardless. In the March 2026 fire, ABC7 quoted an owner describing firefighters breaking things to contain the blaze. That is as it should be. A door system in a row of stores is not designed to keep the Fire Department out; it is designed so that the repair afterward is a known list of parts.

Firefighters forced our gate and front door to reach a fire in the row. Once we are allowed back, what does the door system need before we reopen?

A forced door is expected after a fire, and it is a repair list rather than a mystery: the door and frame first, then the strike or trim, the contact, the reader and the cable behind them. Nothing starts without permission, though. While a vacate order stands, the Department of Buildings decides who may enter, and the fire marshals may still be working. Once the owner is allowed to secure the openings, we treat the empty store as a construction site: boarded openings with a padlocked hasp or a battery-powered keypad lock box for the one door that has to be used, because the old electric lock may have no power and may be damaged.

If the system was cloud-hosted, its alerts and records keep running even with the controller gone, and the board can see whether any credential was used. If it was a box in the back room, it is probably gone. Either way, we write down what was on each door from our record and photographs, which is the list your insurer will ask for.

When the rebuild starts, the time to plan the doors is before the walls close: cable rough-in for the readers and contacts, backing in the frames for strikes, and power for the lock supply where it will be needed. Reopening is then a matter of re-enrolling credentials from the saved database and testing every door and every exit.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Williamsbridge

We could not open Google’s AI Overview for these searches while writing this page, so we do not quote it. What follows are claims we hear repeated by owners and board members who looked things up first, from chat answers, national cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, and product pages, with the correction that applies on a Williamsbridge corridor.

“A magnetic lock and a keypad will make the church secure”

A magnetic lock is the simplest electric lock to fit, which is why it turns up in so many answers. On an exit door it is also the easiest to get wrong. The NYC Building Code allows magnetic locks on egress doors only when they are released in specific ways: by a sensor and a PUSH TO EXIT button wired to cut the lock’s power, with the door unlocking on power loss and fire alarm, or by door hardware with a built-in switch that releases the lock the moment it is pushed.

Where the room holds 75 or more, the exits need panic or fire exit hardware, and a magnetic lock has to release when that hardware is operated.

So the honest answer is that a magnetic lock may have a place on some interior doors, and that on the exits of a worship space we almost always use panic hardware with electrified outside trim instead. It is simpler to keep legal, and nobody depends on a sensor noticing them.

“Use a delayed-exit lock to stop theft”

Delayed-egress locks are sold for stores and warehouses, and general advice sometimes recommends them for any building that loses equipment. The NYC Building Code does not permit them in Group A occupancies, which include places of worship.

The intent behind the advice is fair: make it harder to walk out with something. In a church the same intent is met by moving valuables into a room on its own credential group, a contact and sounder on the rear exit, and a camera that sees the door.

That combination costs about the same and keeps the exit instant, which matters most on the day the room is full.

“One code for the whole congregation is easier”

It is easier for a month. Then a member leaves on bad terms, or a code is written on a bulletin board, and the only way to take it back is to change it for everyone, which means telling everyone, which means it ends up written down again.

Per-person credentials cost no more to set up, and they turn a difficult conversation into a click: the person who left simply stops opening the door.

They also make the log useful. A log that says “code 1234 opened the office at 1 a.m.” tells the board nothing; a log with a name on it tells them whom to call.

“It’s a store, so store rules apply”

General answers about commercial door locks are written for shops, and a storefront church looks like a shop from the street. Inside, it is an assembly space. The code’s permission for stores, offices and storage buildings to key-lock a main entrance while open for business names those occupancy groups, not assembly.

A congregation that took over a store should assume that its exit doors are now governed by the assembly rules, and should ask the architect or expediter who handled the change of use how the code applies to its space.

Our part is to fit hardware that suits those rules, and to refuse to fit anything that does not.

“The landlord’s alarm covers every tenant in the row”

Sometimes a building owner has a system that watches the roof, the cellar or the common areas. It rarely watches each tenant’s back door, it does not know who holds each tenant’s keys, and its alerts go to the owner, not to the congregation.

The March 2026 East Gun Hill Road fire touched 11 businesses under one roof. Each had its own doors, its own keyholders and its own losses afterward.

A tenant should assume it needs its own contacts, its own codes, its own keyholder list and its own records, kept in its own space and on its own account.

“A box in the closet is safer than the cloud”

The worry behind this one is privacy, and it is a fair worry; door logs say a lot about people. But a controller in a back room is exactly as safe as the back room. If the store burns, floods or is broken into, the records go with it.

We let the board choose: a cloud-hosted controller, or an on-site one that exports its database on a schedule to a place the board controls. Either way the board sets who can read the logs and how long they are kept.

What we discourage is the third option, the one most small systems end up with by default: a single box, no copy, and a password nobody remembers.

“Grants will pay for whatever the installer quotes”

Security grant programs do list access control among the uses they fund, and the state’s 2026 round was the largest yet. But programs decide who is eligible and what is allowable, they award before spending is permitted, and some require the organization to describe its own risks.

An installer who promises an award, or who writes the risk description for you, is overstepping. We provide an itemized quote and product data, and we wait for the award.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Counting the Keys

This is a note about how we start, not a story about a particular congregation. Before we quote a storefront church, we ask for half an hour with whoever opens the doors, and we bring a sheet with three columns: the key or code, who holds it, and what will replace it.

We go door by door. Front door: how many copies, who has one, who used to. Gate padlock. Office. Cellar. Alarm code. The landlord’s key. We write roles, never names, and we keep counting until the person across the table runs out of people to remember. The number at the bottom is almost always larger than the number they gave us on the phone.

That sheet decides the rest of the job. It tells us how many credential groups the board needs, which doors really need readers and which can keep a key, and when the final re-key should happen. It is also the page board members tend to keep after the job is finished, because for the first time they know exactly who can open their doors.
