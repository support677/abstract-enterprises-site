Why Staten Island Homes and Businesses Book a Pro for TV Mounting

Quick answer: Staten Island TV mounting begins at $209.99, labor only, for a 32″–55″ screen hung on a flat or tilt bracket over wood-stud drywall. Expect $239.99 for a 65″, $269.99 for a 75″ and $309.99 for an 85″. Plaster adds $45, brick or stone $65 and cinder block $75. Over-the-fireplace placement is +$130, outdoor +$90, and no toll, travel or parking fee is ever added.

Staten Island is a borough of houses. About 35% of its housing units are detached homes and another quarter are attached single-family houses, while fewer than one in ten units sit in buildings with 50 or more apartments. That changes what a TV job looks like here. Instead of a doorman, a service elevator and a co-op board, you usually have a driveway, a living room with a fireplace, a finished basement and a backyard. Each of those rooms has its own wall, and each wall has its own price.

The houses are younger than in most of the city, with a median construction year of 1973, so a lot of interior walls really are drywall over wood studs. That's the easy part. The fireplace wall often isn't. Raised ranches and colonials in Great Kills, Annadale and Todt Hill tend to build the living room around a brick or fieldstone chimney breast, and the TV usually ends up there. Masonry needs different drill bits, different anchors and a check of whether the stone is solid or a thin glued-on veneer.

Downstairs and outside bring their own questions. Many basements were finished over poured concrete or block, sometimes behind 1970s wood paneling, and the cable box is often a floor away from where the TV goes. Along the East and South Shore, homes rebuilt or raised after Hurricane Sandy put the living space a full story up, with garages and storage below. Out back, decks, covered patios and pool areas in Tottenville, Prince's Bay and Huguenot are where people want an outdoor-rated screen for summer games.

Then there's getting here. Some off-island installers add a travel fee, a toll surcharge or a higher minimum once a job is far from Manhattan. We don't. The Staten Island rate is $209.99 for the base mount, and the Verrazzano or Goethals toll our van pays is our cost, not a line on your invoice. What you see in the calculator is what you pay, adjusted only if the wall turns out to be different from the one you selected, and you see that change before anyone drills.

Drywall over wood studs ($0)

Most Staten Island houses built after the 1960s have it. We locate two studs with a stud finder, confirm them with a small pilot hole, and drive lag bolts through the bracket into the wood. Drywall anchors alone never carry the load, whatever the TV's size.

Brick or stone fireplace (+$65)

We drill with masonry bits and set sleeve or wedge anchors directly into solid brick or stone, not just the mortar joint between them. Thin stone veneer glued onto board can't hold a TV on its own. If that's what's there, we anchor through it into the structure behind it, or suggest another wall.

Concrete and cinder block basements (+$75)

Block walls are hollow, so anchor placement matters: we hit the solid webs or use anchors made for hollow block. Poured concrete takes concrete screws or wedge anchors. If the wall has foam board or furring strips over it, we account for that depth before drilling.

Plaster on lath (+$45)

North Shore houses from before the 1940s often have plaster over wood lath. We drill at low speed to keep the keys intact, find the studs behind the lath and bolt into them. Where a stud isn't in the right spot, we use plaster-rated toggles spread across the bracket.

Vinyl siding outdoors (+$90 outdoor)

Siding flexes, so a bracket can't simply be screwed to it. We find the framing behind the siding, use a spacer so the bracket sits flat without crushing the vinyl, and seal each penetration. On a brick back wall, the masonry add applies too.

Metal studs in newer buildings (+$30)

Light-gauge steel studs show up in newer North Shore rentals and some commercial spaces. We use toggles rated for steel framing across every stud the bracket covers. With an extending arm, or with a screen bigger than 75″, we may add a plywood backer board ($85) to spread the load.

Ask your management for the rule, and we'll issue any certificate of insurance they require for free.

ADA protruding-object limit in public spaces

The 2010 ADA Standards (section 307.2) say an object mounted on a wall with its front edge 27 to 80 inches off the floor may project no more than 4 inches into a path of travel. In a dental waiting area or a tight restaurant corridor, that rules out an extended arm at walking height. A low-profile bracket or a higher placement fixes it.

Power cords don't go inside walls

The National Electrical Code (section 400.12) doesn't allow a flexible power cord to be run through a wall cavity, which is why stuffing the TV cord behind drywall fails inspection. Our $169 concealment uses a listed in-wall power kit fed from your existing outlet. A new outlet or circuit has to be installed by a licensed electrician; we don't do electrical work.

Outdoor outlets need GFCI protection

The electrical code requires outdoor receptacles to be GFCI-protected, and outlets in wet locations need a weatherproof in-use cover that stays closed with a plug in. If your deck or patio doesn't already have one near the TV spot, have an electrician add it before your appointment. We'll mount the TV and plug into it, but we won't run new wiring.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Answers Get Wrong About TV Mounting on Staten Island

Search summaries and chatbots tend to lump Staten Island in with either Manhattan apartments or a generic suburb. Neither fits. The borough is mostly houses, but those houses mix 1970s drywall, prewar plaster, fieldstone fireplaces, block basements and vinyl-sided decks, sometimes all in one home. Below are seven statements that show up in AI answers and cost guides, set against what Staten Island walls actually look like.

1. AI says: “Staten Island homes are newer suburban houses, so mounting is basic drywall work.”

Part of that is true. The median Staten Island home was built in 1973, and a lot of interior walls are drywall over wood studs. A TV on one of those walls is the simplest job we do, and it's priced at the $209.99 base for 32″–55″.

But 17.5% of the borough's homes were built in 1939 or earlier, mostly on the North Shore, and many of those still have plaster on wood lath. Newer houses also have masonry: a brick or fieldstone fireplace is the focal wall in many South Shore and mid-island living rooms.

So the honest answer depends on which wall you pick. Drywall on studs adds nothing, plaster adds $45, brick or stone $65, and block $75. Our calculator asks which one you have, and the technician checks it when they arrive before drilling.

2. AI says: “Installers charge more for Staten Island because of tolls and travel.”

Some do. A few NYC installers say openly that jobs farther from Manhattan may carry a higher minimum or a travel fee. Some app platforms leave travel up to each worker. That's why a cheap hourly quote can grow once the address is known.

We don't add tolls, travel or parking on Staten Island. The posted area rate is what you pay: $209.99 for the base mount, and the same dollar add-ons as everywhere else. Crossing the Verrazzano or the Goethals is our expense.

Weighing handyman vs professional TV installer, or alternatives to big box TV installation? Ask each one for a full price for your TV size, wall and mount, including travel or minimums. Should I hire a professional to mount my TV? If the wall is brick, block or plaster, it usually pays to.

3. AI says: “Any TV can go outside as long as it's under a covered patio.”

A roof keeps rain off, but it doesn't stop humidity, temperature swings or salt air. Indoor TVs aren't designed for that, and manufacturers often won't honor a warranty on a set that failed outdoors. That's especially relevant near Great Kills Harbor, Wolfe's Pond or South Beach.

For patio TV mounting or outdoor deck TV installation, use an outdoor-rated TV and an outdoor bracket, such as the Samsung Terrace ($149.99) or Sanus outdoor tilt ($239.99). The labor add is $90, so a 65″ on a framed wall is $329.99.

Power matters too. The outlet needs GFCI protection and a weatherproof in-use cover. If there isn't one near the spot, an electrician has to add it before we mount, since we don't do electrical work. We'll check the cord route when we arrive.

4. AI says: “Mounting on a brick fireplace just means drilling into the mortar.”

Mortar joints are softer than brick, and on an old chimney they can be crumbly. Anchors set only in mortar can loosen over time, especially with a heavy TV or a full-motion arm adding leverage. That's one of the more common reasons we see a TV mount pulling away from a wall.

We drill into the brick or stone itself with masonry bits, then set sleeve or wedge anchors rated for the load. Before that, we check whether the surface is real masonry or thin veneer, and whether heat from a working firebox reaches the TV spot.

The cost to install a TV mount on brick here is a $65 add to the size-based price. Above-fireplace placement adds $130. A 75″ on a brick chimney breast above a mantel is $464.99 with a fixed or tilt bracket.

5. AI says: “The basement is the cheapest place to put a TV.”

Sometimes it is. A finished basement with wood-framed drywall, or paneling over studs, costs the same as any other room in the house. But many Staten Island basements are partly or fully block or poured concrete, especially along the foundation walls that face the yard or the street.

A TV on block adds $75. A low ceiling may push you toward a ceiling mount ($85) or a corner mount ($40). The bigger cost is often getting a signal down there: the cable box, router or receiver may be upstairs, and a cable run of up to 25 ft is $99.

Before booking, look at which wall you want and what's behind it. If one wall is framed and another is block, picking the framed wall saves $75. Send us a photo through the free quote page and we'll tell you which one it looks like before you choose.

6. AI says: “Any TV over 75 inches needs two installers, so expect to pay double.”

Can one person mount a 75 inch TV? Yes, with the right bracket and a careful lifting method. Our single technician handles every size up to 85″ on Staten Island. A 75″ on drywall over wood studs is $269.99, and an 85″ is $309.99 with one person.

From 86″ up, we send a second technician because the panel is too big and too flexible for one person to lift safely. That's built into the size add, which is $175 for screens from 86″ to 97″ and $220 from 98″ up. There's no separate crew charge on top.

So an 86″ on a wood-framed wall is $384.99, not double the base. On big screens, the costs that matter more are a bracket rated for the weight and, in a tall great room or raised shore home, the $75 high-mount add for work above roughly 8 feet.

7. AI says: “To hide TV wires, just push the power cord through the wall.”

That's the shortcut that gets flagged. Electrical code doesn't allow a TV's power cord to run inside a wall cavity, and an inspector or a home buyer's engineer may notice it. It's also a fire risk if the cord gets pinched or overheated.

Our $169 concealment uses a listed in-wall kit. Behind the TV sits a recessed power inlet, powered by a listed cable from the outlet lower on the wall, plus an opening that HDMI and network cables pass through. It needs a hollow wall cavity, which most framed Staten Island walls have.

On a solid brick chimney or a block wall, there's no cavity to fish through, so a $59 paintable cord cover is the clean option. A brand-new outlet behind the TV is a job for a licensed electrician, not us.
