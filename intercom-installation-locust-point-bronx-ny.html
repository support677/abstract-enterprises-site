The Neighborhood

Why Locust Point Properties Need Intercom Installation

Locust Point is a small peninsula in the southeastern Bronx, bounded by Harding Avenue on the north, the Throgs Neck Bridge on the east and Eastchester Bay, and sometimes still called Pirate Cove. Its streets, Locust Point Drive and Pennyfield Avenue among them, wind through a compact grid and end in cul-de-sacs at seawalls or small inlets. The houses on those streets are mostly what they were built to be: homes for one family, many of them still one- and two-story bungalows, with newer construction and renovations scattered through. In a neighborhood like that, nobody is forced to install an intercom. No city rule we know of requires one in a one- or two-family house, and when we looked at HPD's violation records for the five tax blocks that make up the point, there were 94 violations of every kind on record and not one about an intercom, a buzzer or a bell. The 311 records tell the same story: 1,568 requests at Locust Point street addresses, none about a doorbell or intercom. So when an owner here calls us, it is because the house itself has created the need, not because an inspector has.

What creates the need is usually a second door. Many houses on the point were enlarged over the decades, and a common result is a lower level with its own entrance. One listing for a house on Locust Point Drive, built in 1915, describes a lower level with a separate entrance holding a family room, a bedroom, a full bathroom and a laundry room. Some of those lower levels hold grown children or a parent; others are rented. The Bronx Times, writing after Sandy, described a resident whose downstairs rental apartment in Locust Point, just below the Throgs Neck Bridge, flooded. A house with two doors and two households has the same problem as a small apartment building, just on a smaller scale. A visitor arrives at one door. Somebody on the other level needs to know, needs to see who it is, and sometimes needs to open a door they are not standing next to. A single doorbell chime cannot do any of that.

The second thing that shapes an intercom on the point is water. In Sandy, the Bronx Times reported, water came over the seawall behind houses on the south side of one block, flooding the streets, basements and ground floors. One tenant described three feet of water coming in and walls and floors having to be replaced from scratch. The National Weather Service still lists Locust Point among the places where coastal flooding causes impacts, with a foot of water recorded on Chaffee Avenue along Hammond Creek in January 2016. When the city's tax-lot records are read against the 2015 preliminary flood maps, 231 of the 265 lots on the point carry the flood flag. And the president of the civic association told the Bronx Times that her mortgage company had moved her from Zone B into Zone A. An intercom has parts that do not mind getting wet and parts that do. The outdoor station is built for weather. The power supply, the indoor monitors and any network equipment are not built to sit in water. On the point, where those parts go is as important as which brand is chosen.

Same-Day Repair in Locust Point

Need Repair in Locust Point? A Doorbell That Quit After High Water, a Lower-Level Door Nobody Upstairs Can Open, or a Ring That Doesn't Reach the Back of the House

Repair calls from the point tend to come in four kinds. The first is a doorbell or intercom that stopped after water reached the lower level. Often the transformer or power supply was mounted low, near the panel or on a joist, and the water reached it or its connections. Sometimes the system comes back when things dry out and then fails again weeks later, because corrosion keeps working on the joints after the water has gone. The second is a lower-level door that has a button but no release, or a release that clicks without opening. The lower level was added or rebuilt, someone put in a doorbell for it, and nobody ever connected a strike in the frame, or the strike was connected and the frame has since moved. The third is a ring that only the front of the house hears. The chime sits in the front hall where it was installed decades ago, while the family now lives in the rooms at the back facing the bay, behind closed doors and the noise of the television. The fourth is a system left by a previous owner, with no label, no manual and no one in the house who knows what any of it does.

If water has reached anything electrical in the lower level, leave that circuit off until an electrician has looked at it, and do not keep resetting a breaker that trips. If a door that should lock is not latching, lock it with its key or a deadbolt while the intercom is down. Then call (646) 490-0629. Many single faults, such as a failed transformer, a corroded joint, a strike that has drifted or a station that has lost its programming, are found and fixed in the same visit, and we tell you on the phone what the photos let us promise and what they do not. Service calls and extended tracing are billed at our $195-an-hour service rate with a three-hour minimum, and we give you an honest estimate of the tracing time before we start. If a repair would cost a large share of a new system, or the old equipment's parts are no longer made, we put both options in front of you in writing, so you do not pay to fix something that will need replacing anyway.

Intercom Installation for Locust Point's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every intercom question we get from the point. They differ in how many doors take visitors, how many households answer, how far below the street the lowest door sits, and how much of the house has been rebuilt. The sections after the cards explain what changes in the work for each.

The original bungalow

A one- or two-story house from the 1920s and 1930s subdivision, often with its first doorbell circuit still in place and a chime in the front hall.

The bungalow with a lower-level entrance

A house enlarged downward or rebuilt with a lower level that has its own door, used by family, a tenant or both over the years.

The legal two-family

One of the 68 two-family lots on the point, including a row of 21 built together in 1975, where two households share or split the doors.

The three-family house

One of the five three-family lots, which under HPD's definitions crosses into multiple-dwelling territory and brings door rules with it.

A house at the end of a cul-de-sac with the water behind it, where the family lives on the bay side and the visitors arrive on the street side.

The civic association's clubhouse on Tierney Place and the yacht club at the end of Longstreet Avenue, where many people hold keys and nobody is always there.

The original bungalow: one door, one chime, and a family that lives at the back

The original Locust Point bungalow was built small, for one family, and often as a summer place that was later made into a year-round home. Its doorbell is usually the simplest circuit there is: a button by the front door, a pair of thin wires running to a chime in the front hall, and a small transformer near the electrical service. That circuit was laid out for a house where the front room was the living room and the kitchen was a few steps away. Many owners have since opened the back of the house toward the yard and the water, added a deck, and moved most of daily life there. The chime stayed where it was. The most common complaint we hear from bungalows is not that the doorbell is broken, but that nobody hears it from the room where the family actually is.

An intercom solves that by putting the answering point in the right room. The outdoor station goes where the old button was, or beside it, so visitors find it in the same place. The indoor station goes in the room the family uses most, often the kitchen or the back room facing the bay, and a second one can go in a bedroom or upstairs. If the old doorbell wire tests well, a two-wire system can run over it to the first indoor station, and a short new run continues to the back. If the house is all one level with an accessible crawl space or lower level, cable can run underneath and come up into the back room through the floor. In a one-family house with one door, there is often no need for a release at all, because whoever answers is going to walk to the door anyway. The point of the intercom is to know who is there before getting up, and to talk to them through the door if you would rather not open it.

The bungalow with a lower-level entrance: two doors, two levels, and water at the bottom

This is the Locust Point house we plan for most often, and it is the one where a careless plan does the most harm. A house that has grown a lower level with its own entrance has a main door at or near the street and a second door below it, often reached by a few steps down or a path along the side. Each level may belong to a different household or to the same family. Visitors for the lower level frequently end up at the main door, and deliveries for the main floor end up at the lower one. The household on each level wants to answer its own door, and often wants to be able to answer the other one too, especially when the lower level belongs to an aging parent or a grown child.

The plan gives each door its own outdoor station with a clear label, and each level its own indoor station. Each indoor station rings for its own door by default, and with most current systems either level can also be set to ring for both doors, see either camera and talk between floors. If the lower level is rented, the tenant's station rings only for the tenant's door, and its release opens only the tenant's door. If it belongs to family, the settings can be looser. What does not change is where the expensive parts go. On the point, the lower level is the part of the house most likely to take water, so the power supply, any network switch and any battery backup go upstairs, above the level the house has flooded to before. The lower-level indoor station is a plain, inexpensive model on a cable that comes down from above, so if the worst happens, that station and a short length of cable are all that need replacing.

The lower-level door itself deserves attention. Doors below grade often stick when the frame swells, and many were replaced after Sandy with whatever was available. If that door is going to get a strike so someone upstairs can open it, the latch, the frame and the closer have to work every time first. We check that on the survey, and if the door needs hardware work, the quote says so.

The legal two-family: two households, and a row built all at once

The two-family houses on the point come in more than one form. Some are older houses divided into two apartments, one over the other. City tax-lot records also show a row of 21 two-family lots on one block, all recorded as built in 1975, which means a group of houses that started identical. In either kind, the question is the same as in the bungalow with a lower-level entrance, but the separation between households is firmer. Each apartment needs its own button, its own indoor station and its own release, and neither household's station should be able to open the other's apartment door. Where the two apartments share an entrance, the shared door is the one that gets the strike, and each apartment door keeps its own key lock that the intercom never touches.

The three-family house: where the rules start to apply

There are only five three-family lots on the point, holding fifteen homes, but they are a different case. HPD treats a building with three or more homes as a multiple dwelling, and in multiple dwellings, HPD's ABCs of Housing says building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors must be self-closing, and that the owner, manager or superintendent should repair broken locks or intercoms right away. The best-known intercom requirement, which the New York State Attorney General's tenants' guide describes as a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door in buildings of eight or more apartments, does not reach a three-family house. But the door rules do, and once tenants rely on an intercom to get visitors in, keeping it working matters.

On a three-family, we plan a station with a button per apartment, labeled by apartment rather than by family name, an indoor station in each apartment, and a strike on the shared door fitted so the door still closes and latches on its own every time the release ends. If the house has a lower apartment with its own entrance as well as a shared door upstairs, both doors get stations, and the power for both sits above the flood line. We explain more about the rules further down the page.

The house at the seawall: visitors in front, life in back

The cul-de-sacs on the point end at seawalls or inlets, and the houses at the ends of those streets have the water on one or more sides. These houses are often where the gap between the door visitors use and the room the family lives in is widest. The front door faces a dead-end street. The deck, the big windows and the kitchen face the bay. On a lot recorded at 25 by 100 feet, that can mean most of the house between the person ringing and the person who should hear it.

For these houses we usually recommend video at the street-side door and the main indoor station in the room facing the water, with a phone or app connection added for the times the family is outside on the deck or down at the seawall. The outdoor station at the front is built for weather and fixed with stainless hardware, because salt air reaches the whole point, not only the houses on the water. Any station on the water side, by a deck door for example, gets the same treatment, and we look at whether storm spray reaches the wall before choosing where to mount it. Cable to the back of the house runs inside where possible, and where it has to run outside, it goes in sealed conduit tight to the house, high enough that a surge over the seawall does not reach it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Point House From the Street Door Down to the Water Line

Walk around the house before going in. The technician counts every door that faces the street, the side walk or the yard, and asks which ones visitors really use. On the point the answer often surprises the owner: the front door is for strangers, the side door is for friends, and the lower-level door is for the tenant and everyone who gets lost looking for the tenant.

Find the water line. The owner usually knows how high the water came in Sandy or in a later storm, and often there is still a mark, a replaced strip of drywall or a raised outlet that shows it. That height becomes the line nothing expensive goes below. If the owner has already raised the boiler or the panel, the intercom's power goes up with them.

Follow the doorbell wire to its source. The wire behind the old button usually leads to a transformer near the electrical service or on a joist below. The technician notes every splice and every branch along the way. A branch that ends in a wall is often the leftover of a door that was moved, and a wire that ends cut off cleanly is often where a flood repair stopped.

Test each door before the intercom. Every door that will get a strike is opened, let go and watched. A lower-level door that swells and sticks, or an upper door that bounces off its latch, gets fixed or adjusted first.

Sit where the family sits. The technician asks where the household spends the day and stands there while someone rings. If the ring cannot be heard from that room, that is where the indoor station goes.

Water, Wire and the Lower Level

Flood Lines, Century-Old Doorbell Wire and Rebuilt Lower Levels: Wiring a Locust Point House

Most of what makes an intercom last on the point happens out of sight. A house that was a 1920s bungalow, was raised or extended in the 1950s, took water in 2012 and was partly rebuilt afterward has wiring that tells all of that history, and a new system has to fit into it without inheriting its weak spots. This section explains what the survey checks and what we do about each finding, so the quote makes sense when you read it.

Setting the line nothing expensive goes below. Before we choose where anything goes, we agree with the owner on a height. Usually it is the highest point water has reached in the house, plus a margin, and if the owner has had flood-proofing work done, it is the height that work used. The power supply, any network switch, any battery backup and the main indoor monitors all go above that line. Below it go only the things that have to be there: the outdoor station at a lower-level door, a basic indoor station in a lower-level apartment, and the cable that feeds them. The idea is simple. If water comes in again, the house loses a cheap station and a short piece of cable, not the heart of the system, and the doors upstairs keep working.

Feeding the lower level from above. When we can, we run cable to a lower-level station down from the floor above rather than along the lower-level ceiling from a power supply down there. That keeps the joints and the supply out of the space most likely to flood, and it means a damaged lower-level station can be cut out and replaced without touching anything upstairs. Where cable has to pass through a foundation wall to an outside door, the hole is sleeved and sealed so it does not become a path for water.

Testing the old doorbell wire. Old bell wire was meant to carry a little current to a chime. Many two-wire intercom systems can run over a single good pair, but good has a specific meaning: the copper is continuous, the insulation does not leak between the two conductors, and the run is not so long and thin that the voltage at the end drops too far. Wire that has sat in floodwater, even once, can look fine and fail that test, because water gets under the insulation and corrodes the copper from inside. We test every run we might reuse, look at the copper wherever it is exposed, and write the result on the quote. Runs that pass can stay. Runs that fail, or that sat in water and show any sign of it, are replaced.

Remaking the joints. Even when a run tests well, the splices along it are usually the weakest part. Twisted, taped joints on a joist near the floor, or behind a button by a door exposed to salt air, loosen and corrode. We cut them out and remake them in labeled boxes with connectors meant for low-voltage work, mounted above the flood line wherever the run allows, and we note on the wiring record where each box is.

Fixing stations to the outside of a point house. Houses on the point are finished in many ways, from original shingle and siding to newer stucco, brick and composite panels added in renovations. Each needs a different anchor and a different way of sealing the cable entry. We look for solid backing behind the finish, use stainless fasteners because the air here carries salt, set the station on a mounting plate where the surface is uneven, and seal the cable hole so wind-driven rain does not get into the wall. Where an old button sits in a small hole, the new cable usually uses that hole, and the station covers it.

Planning with a renovation or an elevation. Owners on the point are still rebuilding, raising utilities and, in some cases, replacing the original bungalow with a new house. If any of that is coming, the best time to plan the intercom is before the walls close. A spare cable to each future station location, pulled while the framing is open and capped in a box, costs very little and saves cutting finished walls later. If the electrical panel is moving up a floor, the intercom's power supply should move with it, on its own labeled circuit, and any new circuit it needs is work for a licensed electrician, which we coordinate.

The Rules at the Door

One-Family, Two-Family and Three-Family Houses: What New York Rules Mean for a Locust Point Intercom

Almost every house on the point sits on the private side of the line the city draws between private houses and multiple dwellings, which means most of the rules people have heard about intercoms do not apply to them. A few do, especially for the handful of three-family houses and the shared buildings. We are installers, not lawyers, and nothing here is legal advice. This is what the public sources say, so you can ask the right questions of your attorney, your insurer or the city.

One- and two-family houses. The well-known intercom requirements are written for multiple dwellings. The Attorney General's tenants' guide describes a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door as a requirement in buildings of eight or more apartments, and for older buildings, a requirement that applies when a majority of the apartments ask for one. Nothing we found extends that to a one- or two-family house. On the point, where 167 lots are one-family and 68 are two-family, an intercom is the owner's decision. That said, once a tenant in a two-family relies on a door system to let visitors in, keeping it working is part of keeping the apartment in good order.

Three-family houses. A house with three homes is a multiple dwelling under HPD's definitions, and HPD's ABCs of Housing says that in every type of multiple dwelling, building entrance doors and apartment entrance doors must be self-closing. HPD has said it will select buildings for inspections of self-closing doors, and owners who do not keep those doors in good repair face an immediately hazardous violation. If an intercom strike goes on a shared door in a three-family, the door has to close and latch by itself after every release. We fit strikes so the latch catches normally and adjust the closer so the door does not stop short.

Mounting heights. The Department of Buildings rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet from the floor, and the speaking and listening device must be between four and five feet from the floor. On the point, where many doors sit at the top of a few steps and lower-level doors sit below grade, we measure from where the visitor actually stands. For club and community buildings open to the public, ADA reach rules for operable parts also apply, which usually puts buttons lower.

Permits. We found vendor sources that disagree on this. One says low-voltage intercom wiring generally does not need a Department of Buildings permit in New York City; another says some intercom and low-voltage work does require a licensed contractor to pull one. Neither is DOB guidance, and we have not relied on either. What we can say is that every job we do is done under a New York State license, that any new or moved electrical circuit is done by a licensed electrician, and that if a particular job needs a filing, we tell the owner before work starts.

Getting out. Any door fitted with an electric strike must still open freely from inside, without a key, a code or a button. Every system we install controls the way in only. That matters even more on a lower-level door that might be someone's way out during high water.

Flood rules and where equipment sits. The city's flood-resiliency guidance for new and substantially improved buildings in the floodplain calls for mechanical systems to sit above the design flood elevation. Whether that applies to your particular house depends on the work being done and is a question for your architect or engineer. Our own practice, whatever the rules require, is to keep an intercom's power and electronics above the level the house has flooded to.

Landmarks. We found no historic district or individual landmark covering houses on the point. If you are unsure about an address, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's map will settle it.

City-owned buildings. The civic association's clubhouse is a city-owned building on a lease. Any door hardware or station there would follow whatever the lease and the city's agencies require, and the association would confirm that before we start.

Intercom Installation in Locust Point: Questions Answered

These are the situations owners and organizers on the point describe when they call: houses where two generations live on two levels, old intercoms from another era still in the walls, rows of houses that started identical, flood repairs, packages left in view of the street, houses that sit empty part of the year, and the shared buildings everyone uses. Each one is answered here once and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

One house, two levels

Our son's family lives in the lower level of our house, with its own door. Can the stations on each level call each other, not just answer visitors?

Yes, and in a house like that it is often the feature families use most. Most current systems that support more than one indoor station let each station call the others directly, so the main floor can call down to the lower level and the lower level can call up, with or without video between them. That replaces shouting down the stairs and saves a phone call between two people in the same house. Each station can also be set to ring for its own door only, for both doors, or for both doors at certain hours. For a parent upstairs who finds the stairs hard, a common setup is that the lower level answers its own door and also hears the front door, so someone can let in a visitor for the main floor when the parent cannot get to it. If the lower level might be rented one day, we set the system up so those shared settings can be switched off and the levels separated cleanly, without new wiring.

Our bungalow came with an old room-to-room intercom: a master unit in the kitchen and speakers in several rooms. Can any of it be turned into a door system?

The units themselves almost never can. Old whole-house intercoms were built as closed systems, their parts are rarely still made, and they cannot talk to a modern door station. The wire inside the walls is a different matter. Those systems often ran multi-conductor cable from the master unit to every room and to a speaker by the door, and if that cable tests well, some of it can carry a new two-wire station to a room that would otherwise need a new run. We test each run before deciding. Where a run is reused, the old speaker box can sometimes hold the new station's mounting plate, which saves patching. Where it is not, the old speakers can stay as blank covers, or come out and the holes be patched. We also check the old master unit's power, because those systems usually had their own transformer, and a forgotten transformer left live inside a wall is something to remove, not leave.

Our house is one of a row of two-families that were all built at the same time. Will the plan that worked for the neighbors work for us?

The equipment probably will, and that is worth knowing, because a model that the neighbors already know how to use is a fair starting point. The wiring plan usually will not carry over. City tax-lot records show a row of 21 two-family lots on the point all recorded as built in 1975, and half a century of different owners is enough to make identical houses very different inside. One house may have had its lower level gutted and rebuilt after Sandy, another may have moved the upper apartment's entrance, a third may still have its original doorbell runs intact. Ask the neighbors which system they have and what they like about it, then send us photos of your own doors and transformer. If your house matches theirs closely, the survey goes quickly. If it does not, the survey is where we find out, rather than halfway through the installation.

Floods and the lower level

Our mortgage company says the house is now in Flood Zone A, and we are moving the boiler and electrical panel up out of the lower level. How should the intercom fit into that work?

The intercom's power supply should go up with the panel, on its own labeled breaker at the new location, and any network switch or battery backup should go with it. That is the main decision, and it is far easier to make while the electrician is already moving circuits than afterward. From that new location, cable runs down to whatever has to stay low, usually the station at the lower-level door and an indoor station in the lower-level rooms, and up to the main floor. Ask your electrician to leave a short sleeve or an open path from the new panel area to the lower level, and to label a breaker for the intercom even if we install it later. If the old doorbell transformer is on the lower level, it should come out or be abandoned properly, not left live in a space that floods. Tell us the electrician's schedule and we will either work alongside them or give them a written list of what to leave for us.

The lower level of our house had about three feet of water in Sandy. If that happened again, what parts of an intercom should we expect to lose?

If the system is planned the way we plan it on the point, the answer is short: the indoor station mounted in the lower-level rooms, possibly the outdoor station at the lower-level door if the water rises above it, and a few feet of cable. The power supply, the main indoor stations and any network equipment sit above the line, so the front door and the upper level keep working, and the lower level can be brought back by replacing one or two inexpensive stations. That is why we often suggest a plain audio or basic video station for a lower level rather than the largest monitor in the range. When we design the system, we write the agreed water line on the wiring record and note which parts sit below it, so after a storm the owner and the insurer both know what was at risk. If the outdoor station at the lower door can be mounted higher on the wall within the height rules, we do that too.

Packages, strangers and an empty house

Packages have been stolen around Throggs Neck. Can we use the door station to tell a courier where to leave a box so it isn't sitting in view of the street?

Yes, and it is one of the most practical things a video station does. In 2026 the 45th Precinct posted about catching a suspect after complaints that included package theft in the area. When a courier rings, the station shows them and lets you talk to them from the indoor station or, if the system has an app, from your phone wherever you are. You can ask them to leave the box behind the side gate, inside the enclosed porch, by the lower-level door or in a delivery box, instead of on the front step. Some stations also have a small preset message or a nameplate where you can post instructions, such as which door takes deliveries. If you want the courier to put the box inside a locked space, that needs a strike or a separate lock on that space, and we can plan it so the release opens only that door and not the house. The camera also records who came up the path, if you choose a model that stores clips.

We spend part of each winter away. Can the door station handle callers without telling them nobody is home?

It can, and the right setup is the one that does not announce anything. A station connected to an app or set to forward calls to an ordinary phone lets you answer the door from wherever you are, so a caller hears a voice and has no way to know whether you are in the kitchen or out of state. We usually advise against recorded away messages and against any setting that plays a fixed greeting when nobody answers, because a predictable response is information. If a neighbor or relative is keeping an eye on the house, their phone can be added as a second answering point for the months you are gone and removed when you return. Before you leave, it is worth checking that the system's internet connection is reliable and that the indoor station's power is on a circuit you are not switching off while the house is empty, because a door station that goes silent is worse than one that rings a phone. The Bronx Times reported in 2008 on neighbors' worries about a vacant house on Glennon Place that was not secure; an occupied-looking house is part of the point's own instinct.

The point's shared buildings

At events in our clubhouse, the side door is locked once people arrive. Can latecomers ring whoever is running that night's event?

Yes. The usual way is to give the side door a station that calls a phone, through an app or by forwarding to an ordinary number, and to change who gets that call for each event. Most systems let an administrator add a phone for a day or a weekend and remove it afterward, so the volunteer running a party or a meeting gets the rings that night and nobody else's phone goes off. The volunteer sees who is at the door and presses release on their phone, and the door locks again behind the guest. For larger events it helps to have the call go to two people at once, so a ring is answered even if one of them is busy. If the building already has a staffed door on some nights, the station can ring an indoor station at that spot on those nights instead. We set up the schedule with the association and leave written steps for whoever books the hall after us.

Who should a club's front door call when no staff is on duty and the members are out on the docks or the pier?

Usually a short list rather than one person. A club with a door station that rings a single officer's phone depends on that officer, and members on the water may not hear a phone or may not have a signal. A better setup calls two or three people in order or together, chosen by the club, and lets anyone on that list see the visitor and release the door. Guests who are expected can be given a temporary code instead, valid for the day of a race or an event, so they do not need anyone to answer at all. Members themselves usually get their own codes or fobs if the club wants them, which is access control rather than intercom, and the two can share the same strike and power if they are planned together. The fishing pier at the Locust Point Yacht Club draws people from beyond the point, which is one more reason the door should call a known person rather than open for anyone who presses a button.

FAQ

Locust Point Intercom Installation FAQ

What is the practical difference between a door buzzer and an intercom for a house on the point?
+

A buzzer or doorbell tells you someone is at the door and nothing more. An intercom adds a voice, and often a picture, so you can find out who it is and talk to them from another room or level before deciding whether to open. With a strike, it can also release a door from where you are standing. In a house with two levels or two doors, that difference is the whole point.

Other companies gave us a four-hour window and then didn't show. How do you schedule a visit out on the point?
+

Requests are booked within two hours, and we agree a visit time with you rather than leaving you waiting all day. We plan the drive knowing the point has a single way in and out, and if anything changes on the day, the technician calls you before the agreed time, not after it. If a survey shows the job needs parts we do not carry, we tell you the return date before we leave.

We already use one app for a camera on our deck. Will the new door station show up in the same app?
+

Only if both come from the same maker or the maker supports the other's equipment, which is not common. Most families end up with one app for the camera and one for the door. If having a single app matters to you, tell us the camera's brand and model before we choose the door station, and we will say honestly whether a matching station exists and whether it suits your doors.

After water reaches our lower level, what should we check before trusting the intercom again?
+

Leave any circuit that got wet switched off until an electrician has checked it. Then look at whether the water reached the lower-level station, its outdoor station or any cable joints. Press each button and answer from each level, and release each door while someone watches it relatch. If anything that sat in water still works, have it checked anyway, because corrosion can cause failures weeks later.

If we raise or rebuild the house in a few years, can the stations come down and go back up?
+

Usually, yes. Outdoor and indoor stations unscrew from their mounting plates and can be stored and reinstalled, and the power supply moves with the panel. The cable is what normally has to be replaced, because a rebuild changes where the walls and doors are. Keep the wiring record we leave, and give it to your architect or contractor so they leave paths for the new runs.

Can the indoor ring be set to a light or a softer tone so it doesn't set off the dog or wake the baby?
+

On most current indoor stations, yes. Many let you choose among several tones and set the volume, and some can flash a light or the screen instead of ringing. Each station is set separately, so the room by the nursery can stay quiet while the kitchen station rings normally. If this matters, mention it before we choose the model, because the simplest stations offer fewer choices.

Can you install a door station and monitors we bought online ourselves?
+

Yes, as labor-only work, provided the equipment suits your doors, your number of levels and the wire in the house. Send us the model numbers before the visit. We will tell you if anything is missing, such as the power supply or a module the kit needs, and whether the outdoor station is rated for weather near the water. Equipment you supply is covered by its maker, not by our warranty.

Can we see who is at the door on our television?
+

A few systems can send the door camera's picture to a television or a streaming device, but most are built to show it on their own indoor screens and phone apps. If seeing the door on the TV in the back room is important to you, tell us, and we will check which models support it. Often a small indoor monitor in that room does the job more reliably.

Can our children answer the door station without being able to open the door?
+

Yes. On many systems each indoor station can be set to talk and see but not release, and app accounts can be given the same limit. A station in a child's room or a playroom can let them see who is there and tell a parent, while the release stays on the stations and phones the adults use. Some models also let the release require a short code.

Can you give us a price from photos without coming to the house?
+

Photos let us tell you what the house probably needs, which options make sense and what we would want to check. The written quote follows a short survey, because on the point the things that change a price, such as old wire that sat in water, a swollen lower-level door or where the panel now sits, cannot be judged reliably from pictures alone. Looking at the photos costs nothing.

Will the person at the door be able to see us on the station's screen?
+

On almost every residential door station, no. The camera points outward, and the visitor hears your voice but does not see you. A few stations have a screen for visitors, usually used for directories or messages, not to show the household. If you prefer that a visitor cannot even tell you are watching before you speak, most systems let you view the camera without opening the audio.

Our houses are close together on narrow lots. Will the neighbors hear our conversations at the door?
+

Not if the station is set up for the spot. Outdoor stations have adjustable speaker volume, and we set it so a visitor standing at the door hears clearly without the sound carrying to the next house. We also aim the station's camera at the visitor's position rather than across a side path toward a neighbor's windows, and on models that allow it we can mask parts of the picture.
