The Neighborhood

Why Tottenville Properties Need Smart Lock Installation Planned for the End of the Line

Nothing in New York State sits farther south than Tottenville. The neighbourhood covers about two and a half square miles at the very bottom of Staten Island, with the Arthur Kill along its western and northern edges and the open water of Raritan Bay to the south. Its railway station is the southern terminus of the Staten Island Railway and the southernmost station in the state; the ride to St. George runs about fourteen miles and takes roughly forty-two minutes. Arthur Kill Road, Amboy Road and Hylan Boulevard, three of the island's longest roads, all finish here. When people say Tottenville is the end of the line, they mean it literally.

That geography is the starting point for how we plan a smart lock here. A keyless door is only as good as what happens on the day something fails: a battery that dies a week earlier than the app promised, a router that drops after a storm, a phone left on a desk in Manhattan. In much of the city, the answer to those days is a quick fix nearby. At the far end of the South Shore, the better answer is a door that never needed rescuing in the first place. Every lock we fit in Tottenville is planned with a second way in that does not depend on the internet or on a phone, and with a household that knows how to use it.

The houses themselves are the second reason. Tottenville grew up as a working waterfront town. Oystering carried it through the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries until the city's health department banned the harvest in 1916, and wooden boatbuilding lasted until steel took over around 1900; the last shipyard closed in 1930. The families who made money from oysters, ships and the ferry to Perth Amboy built houses that still line Bentley Street, Hopping Avenue, Satterlee Street and the blocks off Main Street: Greek Revival houses from around 1850, Gothic Revival cottages, Italianates set back on deep lawns, and a remarkable run of Queen Anne and later Victorian houses with wraparound porches, bays, multiple gables and fish-scale shingles. Neighbourhood histories call the area's housing stock a strong Victorian heritage unique among South Shore neighbourhoods, and a walker writing for Forgotten New York in 2026 put its painted-lady houses among the best north of Cape May.

Those houses come with doors that were never designed for an electronic lock. Many front doors sit behind a porch, sometimes with a storm or screen door in front; some are thick, panelled and hand-planed; plenty have been fitted, plugged and refitted for different locks over a century or more. A smart deadbolt can work beautifully on them, but only after someone has measured the door, looked at its edge and checked what the inside of the lock will sit against. That is door work as much as lock work, and we price it that way.

Then there is the other Tottenville. The southeast part of the neighbourhood, beyond Hylan Boulevard, stayed largely undeveloped until the 1990s, and a building boom filled many empty lots across the South Shore with newer detached, semi-detached and attached houses. Tottenville was contextually rezoned in 1995, 1996 and 1997, and in 2005 the City Planning Commission approved a further 65-block rezoning, bounded by Arthur Kill Road, the Arthur Kill, Raritan Bay and Page Avenue, that restricted part of the area to single-family homes and required large lots for any new two-family house. Its sponsor argued it would stop the teardown of large historic single-family homes. The result is a neighbourhood where a nineteenth-century house with a hand-made door can stand a few blocks from a twenty-year-old two-family with a steel entry door and an attached garage. The two need very different lock plans, and a lot of our work here is telling them apart.

The third reason is the water. Tottenville took some of the most destructive waves in the region when Superstorm Sandy came ashore in October 2012, according to New York State's housing agency; the storm destroyed a restaurant at Tottenville Beach outright. In response, the state funded Living Breakwaters, a chain of eight ecologically enhanced breakwater segments, about 2,400 feet in total, set between roughly 790 and 1,800 feet off the Tottenville shore to calm storm waves and slow erosion; construction finished in 2024. Breakwaters change what a storm does to the beach. They do not change the salt that the wind carries inland every day. A door that faces the bay or the Kill with no porch roof over it needs a lock with a finish and gasket chosen for that, and a maintenance habit to go with it.

Finally, there is the way people here live. About 70 percent of Tottenville's homes are owner-occupied — census figures put the renter-occupied share at 29.4 percent — and around a third of households have children. Many working adults commute a long way: to St. George by rail and on by ferry, or to Manhattan on the SIM2, SIM25 and SIM26 express buses. That adds up to houses that stand empty for long stretches of the day, with children, grandparents, dog walkers, contractors and deliveries all needing to come and go on their own schedule. Codes, schedules and alerts are what a smart lock does best; the trick is setting them up so they serve that rhythm without leaving a door open to it.

Tottenville is also, by the numbers, a careful place to live. The 123rd Precinct on Main Street ranked safest of the city's 69 patrol areas for per-capita crime in 2010, and the precinct recorded 42 burglaries in 2022, with crime down about 70 percent across major categories since 1990. Safe is not the same as untouched: in May 2026 police reported that someone used a crowbar to pry open the front door of a supermarket on Ellis Street late at night and got into its vestibule, though nothing was reported stolen. Residents on local forums describe the neighbourhood as having a small-town feel with family-owned businesses. Our view is that a smart lock in a neighbourhood like this is mostly about control — knowing who has a way in, and being able to take it back — and only secondly about stopping a determined break-in, which is a job for frames, strikes, glass and alarms as much as locks.

Building Specialists

Smart Lock Installation for Tottenville's Architecture

Tottenville's houses fall into a handful of families, and each family of house tends to bring the same handful of door problems. Recognising which one we are standing in front of tells us most of what the job will involve.

Mid-nineteenth-century Greek Revival and Gothic Revival houses

The oldest houses in the old village, from around 1850, often with small windows, heavy doors and frames that have moved over 170 years. Expect plugged bores, mortise pockets in the door edge and a strike that has to be rebuilt before any motorised bolt will throw cleanly.

Italianate houses on deep lawns

Set well back from the street, which puts the front door a long way from the router and from the curb. Signal planning matters as much as hardware, and a keypad by a door at the end of a long front walk needs good lighting to be usable at night.

Queen Anne and late-Victorian houses with porches

The classic Tottenville house of the 1880s to 1910s: wraparound or front porches, bays, gables and panelled doors, sometimes with glass in the upper half. The porch roof protects the lock from the worst weather, but storm and screen doors, panel mouldings and glass near the lock all have to be measured around.

Early-twentieth-century cottages and Colonial Revival houses

Houses from the 1900s to the 1920s on streets like Hopping Avenue, usually with simpler doors than the high Victorians and often a good candidate for a standard smart deadbolt once old hardware holes are dealt with.

Post-1990s detached and semi-detached houses

Steel or fibreglass entry doors, a side door, an attached garage with a door into the house, often a rear slider. The doors are standard, so the planning is mostly about which doors go keyless, which lock themselves, and how the codes work.

Townhouses and two-families

Built in rows or pairs before the downzoning of the mid-2000s pushed new construction toward detached houses. Shared walls, doors close together, and in two-families, two households whose codes must stay separate.

Methods by door type

  • Measure first. Common residential smart deadbolts are made for doors roughly 1-3/8 to 2 inches thick with a bore set back 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inches from the edge (Schlage's published preparation for its Arrive deadbolt, for example). Older Tottenville doors are often outside that range, and the moulding of a panelled door can sit exactly where the inside unit wants to be flat. We check thickness, backset, edge condition and the face of the door on both sides before anything is ordered.
  • Plug, then drill. Old bores are filled with glued hardwood plugs and allowed to set before new holes go in. Screws into putty or filler do not hold a motorised bolt for long.
  • Rebuild the strike. On frames that have moved, the strike is reset and backed with a reinforcing plate and long screws into the framing, so the bolt meets the strike squarely and the frame resists prying.
  • Retrofit where the door should not be touched. Some retrofit units mount entirely inside over an existing deadbolt and leave the outside alone. August, for example, describes its retrofit as compatible only with single-cylinder deadbolts and notes that some installations need clearance around the thumb-turn.
  • Seal for the weather it actually gets. Exterior gaskets go on every outside lock; on unsheltered doors facing the water we choose finishes made for coastal use and set expectations about cleaning.
  • Prove the way out. Before we leave, we open every lock we fitted from inside with one movement of a thumb-turn or lever, with the batteries in and with them out.
The Self-Reliant Door

A Smart Lock That Never Strands You at the End of the Line

The single idea that shapes our Tottenville work is this: a smart lock should fail in a way the household can fix on its own, on the doorstep, in a couple of minutes. Here is what that looks like in practice.

Two ways in, and both of them rehearsed

Every keyless door gets a second way in that has nothing to do with the internet. On most locks that is a key cylinder, and the key lives somewhere it can be reached without getting into the house: with a trusted neighbour, at a relative's home, in a secured lock box at the property if that suits you. On keyless models without a cylinder, it is usually an emergency power contact. Yale's support pages, for instance, describe holding a 9-volt battery against an emergency terminal at the bottom of some keyless locks to wake them long enough to enter the code. A backup that nobody has tried is not a backup, so at handover we have someone in the household use it while we watch.

Batteries on a calendar, not on an alert

Low-battery warnings are useful, but they arrive on the lock's schedule, not yours. In a household that leaves before seven and gets home after dark, the warning can sit unread on a phone for days. We suggest changing batteries on a fixed date — the first weekend of spring and of autumn is easy to remember — using the type the maker specifies, and keeping a spare set in a known drawer. Doors exposed to cold wind off the water can drain batteries faster in winter, which is another reason not to wait for the alert.

Codes that work when the internet does not

Keypad codes are stored in the lock itself, so a well-chosen smart lock keeps opening for everyone with a code even when the Wi-Fi or the maker's servers are down. What stops working in an outage is the remote part: unlocking from the office, getting alerts, changing codes from afar. We set households up so that daily life relies on the parts that keep working, and the remote features are a convenience rather than the only way in.

An admin who is not the only admin

If one phone holds the only admin rights, losing that phone can freeze the system. We set up a second administrator in every household — a spouse, an adult child, a trusted relative — and write down which accounts own which locks, so that one person's lost phone or changed email does not lock everyone else out of managing the door.

Hardware that matches the weather, so it lasts

A lock that corrodes or lets water into its keypad will fail at a bad moment. On doors that face the Kill or the bay without shelter, we choose locks with finishes and seals intended for coastal use, fit the gaskets properly, and leave simple care instructions. Lock-finish guidance from dormakaba, a major commercial lock maker, treats any exterior door within three miles of saltwater as an ocean-front application, warns that the wrong finish in that setting voids its warranty, and recommends regular wiping in salt-mist areas. Tottenville, with water on three sides, is well inside that line.

A way out that never depends on electronics

The inside of every door stays simple: a thumb-turn or lever that opens it in one movement, with or without power. New York City's building code requires egress doors to be readily openable from the egress side without a key or special knowledge or effort. On a family house, that same principle is simply common sense for a fire at night, and we apply it to every door we touch.

Hylan Boulevard runs nearly fourteen miles from Rosebank and ends at Conference House Park in Tottenville. Medical and professional offices along the South Shore stretch often need separate codes for staff, cleaners and after-hours visits, and a record of who opened which door.

Former industrial land by the Kill

The Nassau Smelting plant operated by the Arthur Kill from 1900 to 2001; in 2016 new owners bought 30 acres of the site and proposed a rezoning that met local opposition, and its future use is still undecided. Industrial and yard properties near the waterfront need gate and outbuilding access planned as carefully as front doors.

Compliance points we keep in view

  • Egress first. NYC Building Code section 1010.1.9 requires egress doors to be readily openable from the egress side without a key or special knowledge or effort, and section 1010.1.9.5 generally limits unlatching to one operation. Every shop or office lock we fit keeps a free exit from inside.
  • Fail-safe or fail-secure is a decision, not a default. An electric strike can be set to stay locked or to release when power is lost. We agree that choice with the owner, based on how the door is used and who needs to get out.
  • Landmarked buildings. Main Street has individually designated landmarks, and the Landmarks Preservation Commission requires a permit before work on a landmarked property; replacing doors falls under its Permit for Minor Work. Where a building is designated, we plan hardware that can be approved, and the owner files with the commission before we drill.
  • Apartments above shops. Buildings with three or more apartments are multiple dwellings under state law. In those, the city's tenant data privacy law limits how owners may use smart access systems on tenants' doors, and the lockout provisions of the city's Administrative Code protect occupants of 30 days or more. A shop lock never becomes the only way into anyone's home.
  • Our licence. Low-voltage work — readers, strikes, power supplies, the cable that connects them — is carried out under our New York State low-voltage licence, #12000287431.
Newer Houses

Beyond Hylan Boulevard: Planning Four Doors as One System

The houses built across southeastern Tottenville since the 1990s are a different job from the old village. Their doors are usually factory-made steel or fibreglass, pre-bored to standard sizes, and a smart deadbolt fits them in the way the box promises. The real work is deciding how four or five ways into the house should behave together.

A typical newer house has a front door that guests see, a side door by the driveway that the family actually uses, a door from the attached garage into the house, and a sliding door onto a deck or patio at the back. Each one has a different job. The front door wants a keypad that visitors and helpers can use and that looks right from the street. The side door is the daily door, so it benefits from relocking itself a short time after it closes. The garage-to-house door is often the weakest link, because the garage door opener and its remotes effectively become a key to the house. The slider usually cannot take a standard smart deadbolt at all and is better covered by a secondary lock and a sensor that reports when it is open.

Our approach is to put every lock on one app, give each person one code that works on every door they are meant to use, and set the relocking and alerts per door rather than for the whole house at once. Where the garage connects to the house, we talk about who holds garage remotes and whether the inner door should be locked whenever the house is empty; model residential codes treat the door between an attached garage and the house as a fire separation, and some require it to be self-closing, so we never fit anything that stops it closing and latching on its own.

Two-family houses from this era add a second household. The cleanest setups give each household its own entrance and its own admin, with no codes in common, and treat any shared side or basement door as a door that belongs to both, with both admins able to see its history. Where an owner lives in one unit and rents the other, the tenant's own door stays the tenant's: their codes, their app, and a key the owner holds only for emergencies, agreed in writing.

None of this needs expensive hardware. It needs an hour of planning at the walkthrough and a written sheet afterwards that says which door does what. That sheet is what lets a household change its own codes confidently years later.

Your Questions Answered

Smart Lock Installation in Tottenville: Questions Answered

These are the questions Tottenville households and shop owners bring to us that are not already answered elsewhere on our Staten Island pages. They are grouped by what they are really about.

Keeping the door working on its own

Our keypad has two small metal contacts on the bottom — what are they for, and when would we use them?

On many keyless smart locks those contacts are an emergency power terminal. If the batteries inside go completely flat and the lock will not respond, you hold a fresh 9-volt battery against the two contacts, the keypad wakes up, and you enter your code to open the door. Yale's support guidance describes exactly this for some of its keyless models: insert a 9-volt battery into the emergency terminal at the bottom of the device, then enter the code. Once inside, you replace the main batteries as normal.

Three things are worth knowing. First, not every lock has these contacts, and on some models they are hidden under a cover, so check yours now rather than on a cold night. Second, the 9-volt battery does nothing unless you also know a working code, so this is not a way for a stranger to get in. Third, a 9-volt battery kept in a car glovebox or a garage drawer for years may be flat when you need it; swap it when you change the lock's batteries.

At handover we show you where the contacts are on your lock, or tell you plainly that your model does not have them, and we make sure the household's backup plan matches the lock you actually own.

We want a retrofit unit over our existing deadbolt — how do we tell whether the deadbolt we have will take one?

Start inside the door. A retrofit replaces only the inside thumb-turn of a deadbolt, so the deadbolt must have a thumb-turn on the inside and a key cylinder on the outside: a single-cylinder deadbolt. If your deadbolt needs a key on both sides, a retrofit will not fit it, and a double-cylinder deadbolt on a home's exit door is a poor idea anyway for getting out in a fire. August, one of the main retrofit makers, says its retrofit works only with single-cylinder deadbolts.

Next, look at what is around the thumb-turn. The retrofit body is bigger than the plate it replaces, so a raised panel moulding, a door handle set close above, a glass rail or a chain guard can get in the way. Some makes publish a clearance template you can print and hold against the door. Finally, try the bolt by hand: it should slide in and out with one finger on the thumb-turn and the door closed. If you have to push or lift the door to make it lock, the retrofit motor will struggle too, and the strike needs adjusting first.

Send us a photo of the inside of the door with the thumb-turn in it, and one of the outside, and we can usually tell you from those whether a retrofit is likely to work. On an original Tottenville door you would rather not drill, a retrofit is often the best way to go keyless without touching the outside at all.

Our lock keeps reporting “jammed” even though the door seems to close fine — what usually causes that?

A smart lock reports a jam when its motor cannot drive the bolt all the way out, or all the way back, in the time it expects. The door can look perfectly closed while the bolt is catching the edge of the strike by a millimetre or two. The most common causes, in order: the strike hole is slightly too shallow or slightly out of line, so the bolt hits the back or side of it; weatherstripping is pushing the door outward so the bolt rubs as it moves; the door has seasonal movement and binds in humid weather; or the batteries are getting low and the motor has less push than it did.

A quick test: with the door open, lock and unlock it from the app. If the bolt moves freely and the jam goes away, the problem is at the strike, not in the lock. Close the door, hold it firmly shut against the stop, and try again. If it works when you hold the door, the strike or the weatherstripping needs adjusting.

The fix is usually ten minutes of chisel and file work at the strike, or a small change to the door's stop, followed by the lock's own calibration routine. We treat repeated jam alerts as a warning, not an annoyance: a lock that sometimes stops short is a lock that sometimes leaves the door unlocked while telling you it tried.

Will a lock that connects straight to Wi-Fi drain its batteries faster than one that talks to a plug-in bridge?

Generally, yes. A battery lock with Wi-Fi built in has to keep a radio ready to answer the internet, and Wi-Fi radios use more power than Bluetooth or Thread radios. Makers design around this, but on most models the trade-off is still real: built-in Wi-Fi is convenient because nothing needs plugging in near the door, while a lock that uses Bluetooth or Thread and talks to a small plug-in bridge or border router usually gets longer from a set of batteries.

The distances matter. August, for instance, advises placing its Wi-Fi bridge within 10 to 15 feet of the lock, and Thread locks need a Thread border router from the same ecosystem as your app. In a deep Victorian with the router at the back, or a newer house where the front door is a long way from the nearest outlet, that sometimes decides the matter for you.

Our rule of thumb for Tottenville houses: if there is a convenient outlet near the door and the household wants the longest battery life, a bridge-based lock is usually the better choice; if there is nowhere to plug anything in and the Wi-Fi signal at the door is strong, built-in Wi-Fi is fine, with the batteries changed on a calendar rather than on an alert.

Old doors, painted doors

On our panelled Victorian door, the inside part of the lock would sit on the raised moulding — can it be made to sit flat?

Often, yes, but how depends on how much of the inside unit overlaps the moulding. The inside half of a smart deadbolt is a flat-backed box with a mounting plate, and it needs to sit tight against a flat surface so its screws pull the outside and inside halves together squarely. On a panelled door, the bore for the lock is usually in the stile, the solid vertical part beside the panels, but on some older doors the stile is narrow and the inside unit's footprint runs onto the panel moulding.

The options, from least to most change: a lock with a slimmer inside unit that fits the flat area of the stile; moving the bore slightly, if the door allows a different backset; a thin hardwood or metal spacer shaped to sit on the stile and bring the face out flat; or, on doors where none of that works without spoiling the look, moving the smart lock to a different door the household uses daily. Carving away original moulding is the last resort, and on a landmarked building it would need the Landmarks Preservation Commission's approval anyway.

We check this at the walkthrough with the lock maker's template held against the door, and we will tell you which models fit your door before you buy anything.

Should we paint or refinish the door before the smart lock goes on, or after?

Before, if you can. Painting after the lock is fitted means either taking the lock off — and possibly recalibrating it — or painting around it and risking paint in the keypad seal, on the bolt and on the strike. A thick new coat on the door edge or in the strike pocket can also be just enough to make the bolt rub, which is how you end up with jam alerts a week after the painter leaves.

If you are planning to strip and refinish an old door, do that first, let the finish cure, and book the lock after. If the door is being painted soon but not yet, we can fit the lock and show you how to remove the outside and inside units cleanly, or come back and refit it after the paint is dry. Either way, ask the painter to keep paint off the strike, the door edge where the bolt runs and the weatherstripping.

The same applies to porch work: if a porch floor or step is being rebuilt, the door may end up sitting at a slightly different height, so lock work goes best after the carpentry.

Security, privacy and the network

Could someone sitting in a parked car pick up our lock's Bluetooth signal and use it to open the door?

The risk is real for one specific feature and very small for ordinary use. In May 2022 the security firm NCC Group published research showing a Bluetooth Low Energy relay attack: one device near the lock and another near the owner's phone or key fob pass the signal between them, so the lock thinks the owner is standing at the door. They demonstrated it against a residential smart lock's touch-to-open proximity feature, and said the fix is not a simple software patch.

That attack targets proximity unlocking — features that open the door automatically because your phone is nearby, without you pressing anything. NCC Group's advice was to disable passive unlock that does not require explicit approval, or turn off Bluetooth when it is not needed. Unlocking by entering a code, or by opening the app and pressing unlock, is a different action and not what that research defeated.

Our default setup in Tottenville leaves automatic proximity unlock switched off unless a household specifically wants it and understands the trade-off. Codes, fingerprints and deliberate app taps give you the convenience without the open-ended risk.

Should the lock and its bridge go on a separate Wi-Fi network from our laptops and phones?

It is a sensible step, and New York State's own Office of Information Technology Services recommends it: use a separate Wi-Fi network for household smart-home devices, so that if a device is ever compromised, it cannot easily reach the laptops and phones that hold personal information. Most home routers can run a guest network, or a dedicated network for smart devices, with its own name and password.

Two cautions. Many smart locks and bridges only connect on the 2.4 GHz band, so the network you set up for them must offer 2.4 GHz. And some apps need your phone and the bridge on the same network during the first setup, so it is easiest to create the separate network before installation day and let us join the lock to it while we are there.

If your internet provider manages your router and you cannot change its settings, a small separate access point for smart devices is an option. We can set that up as part of the job and write down which devices live on which network.

If we switch from Android to iPhone, or the other way, do the locks have to be set up all over again?

Usually not the locks themselves. With most current smart locks, your codes, users and settings live in the lock and in your account with the lock's maker, not on the phone. When you change phones, you install the maker's app on the new phone, sign in with the same account, and the locks appear. Keypad codes keep working throughout, because they never depended on the phone.

What may need redoing is anything tied to the phone's own platform: Apple Home or Google Home links, phone-tap or wallet keys, voice assistant routines and automations built inside one ecosystem. If you added your locks to Apple Home through Matter and move to Android, for example, you would add them to Google Home separately. And if the only admin account was created with a phone-specific sign-in that you no longer use, recovering it can be slow, which is another reason every household should have a second admin.

Before you switch, write down the email address on the lock account and confirm you can sign in on a computer. That one step avoids nearly every problem.

Who lives behind the door

Someone in our house is deaf — can the door let them know that somebody has just come in?

Yes, though usually through other devices rather than the lock itself. A smart lock records each unlock and which code or user did it; the useful part is sending that event somewhere a deaf household member will notice. The common ways: a push notification to a phone or smartwatch set to vibrate; a smart-home automation that flashes a lamp or a smart bulb when the front door unlocks; or a video doorbell paired with a visual alert indoors.

The details depend on the ecosystem. Locks that work with Apple Home, Google Home, Amazon Alexa or a Matter controller can trigger lights in the same system; the lock and the lights have to be in the same home and able to talk to the same hub or border router. Some households also want a visual signal when the door is left unlocked at night, which works the same way.

Tell us at the walkthrough who needs what kind of alert, and we will choose a lock that fits the household's existing system, set up the automations, and test them with the person who will rely on them before we leave.

Shops and offices

After a crowbar was used on a Tottenville supermarket's front door in 2026, which holds better against prying: an electric strike or a magnetic lock?

Neither holds well if the frame or the door gives way, so it is worth starting there. In the May 2026 incident on Ellis Street, police said the front door was pried open with a crowbar to reach the vestibule. A pry attack works by bending the door away from the frame until the bolt or latch slips out of its keeper. What resists it is a strong frame, a solid door, a latch or bolt that engages deeply, and a guard plate that covers the gap between door and frame.

An electric strike lives in the frame and holds the door's own latch or bolt; its holding strength depends on the strike's rating, the depth of the latch and the stiffness of the frame around it, and a latch guard helps a great deal. A magnetic lock holds the top of the door to the header with a rated holding force; it has no latch to slip, but force applied low on the door can sometimes peel the bottom corner away, and it must release on power loss and on a free-exit device, so it suits some doors better than others. On many shop doors, a mechanical deadbolt used after hours alongside the electronic lock gives the best of both.

For a Main Street or Page Avenue shop, our advice is usually a properly rated strike with a latch guard, a door-position contact tied to the alarm so a forced door is reported, and good glass protection. We look at the actual door and frame before recommending anything.

“Keyless means you can never be locked out”

At the end of the railway line, the cure is planning: a key cylinder or emergency power contact, a spare battery where it can be found, at least two people who can manage the lock, and codes that do not depend on the internet.

“Auto-unlock is the most convenient feature”

Automatic unlocking when your phone approaches is genuinely convenient, and it is often presented as a headline feature. It is also the feature most exposed to the Bluetooth relay attacks that NCC Group demonstrated in 2022.

Geofenced or proximity unlocking can also misfire in ordinary ways: a phone that thinks you have arrived when you are parked in the driveway with the car running, or a household member's phone triggering the door as they walk past the house to the station.

We leave automatic unlock off by default and set it up only for households who want it and understand the trade-offs. A code, a fingerprint or a deliberate tap is almost as fast.

“Any smart lock fits any standard door”

Smart deadbolts are designed around standard door preparation: published ranges such as 1-3/8 to 2 inches of door thickness and a 2-3/8 or 2-3/4 inch backset. Doors made to those standards in the last few decades usually take them easily.

Many Tottenville houses predate those standards. Thick hand-made doors, narrow stiles beside panels, mortise pockets and repeated re-drilling are common in the old village, and the inside unit may need a flat surface the door does not have.

“A smart lock makes your home more secure”

A smart lock adds control and information: who has a code, when the door was opened, whether it is locked now. It can also remind you to lock up and lock itself. Those are real security gains.

It does not, by itself, make a door harder to force. A pry attack like the one reported on Ellis Street in 2026 goes for the frame and the gap between door and jamb. Glass beside a lock, a weak strike or an unsecured side window can make the best lock irrelevant.

Water on Three Sides

Looking After a Smart Lock Near the Arthur Kill and Raritan Bay

Every Tottenville house is within easy reach of saltwater. For a door under a deep porch, that changes very little. For a door that faces the bay or the Kill and takes the wind straight on, it changes which lock we pick and how it should be cared for. This is the routine we leave with households whose doors are exposed.

Choosing the lock

We look for locks whose makers describe finishes suitable for coastal or harsh outdoor use, and whose outside units have a proper gasket against the door. Satin or brushed finishes tend to hide wear better than polished ones. Touchscreen keypads have fewer gaps for grit and moisture than rubber or physical buttons, though both can work well with care. Where a door is very exposed, a lock whose outside part is mostly keypad and very little moving metal is usually a better bet than an elaborate handleset.

Fitting it

The gasket supplied with the lock goes on every time; it is part of how the maker keeps water out, and dormakaba's guidance, for one, warns that a missing gasket can void a lock's warranty. We check the cable passage through the door is sealed, keep the outside unit square so its seal sits evenly, and make sure the door's own weatherstripping is not pushing the lock out of line.

Looking after it

  • Wipe it. After windy weeks or storms, wipe the outside unit with a soft damp cloth and dry it. Salt film is what slowly attacks finishes and seals.
  • Do not oil the keypad. If the key cylinder gets stiff, use a dry lubricant meant for locks in the keyway only, never oil or grease on electronics.
  • Check the batteries for corrosion. When you change batteries, look at the contacts. Any white or green crust is a sign moisture is getting in, and the seal needs attention.
  • Watch for the first fault. Intermittent keypad response, a backlight that flickers or buttons that need a harder press are early signs of water inside. Call before it fails completely.
  • After a big storm, test the backup. Try the key or the emergency power contact once the weather clears, so you know it still works when you might need it.

None of this is complicated, but it is the difference between a lock that lasts for years on an exposed Tottenville door and one that fails in its second winter. Doors that face the water and are used every day get the most attention in our walkthrough for that reason.

Long Days Away

Setting Up Codes for a House That Stands Empty Through the Commute

When the adults in a Tottenville house leave on an early train or express bus and get home after dark, the house runs on codes for most of the day. Here is how we usually help households set that up without creating gaps.

  • One person, one code. Every regular visitor — children, grandparents, a sitter, a dog walker, a cleaner — gets their own code. The history then tells you who came, and one code can be removed without touching anyone else's.
  • Schedules for helpers. A dog walker's code that works only on weekday middays, a cleaner's code only on their day. Outside those times the code simply does not open the door.
  • Short-lived codes for one-off visits. A contractor quoting for a job or a delivery that needs to go inside gets a code that expires the same day.
  • Auto-lock where it helps, not everywhere. Side and back doors that children use after school often benefit from relocking a short time after closing. A front door that adults use to carry shopping in may be better with a reminder alert than an aggressive auto-lock.
  • Alerts that matter. Notifications for a child's first arrival home, for any use of a helper's code outside its schedule and for a door left unlocked at night. Not for every single opening, or nobody reads them.
  • A review every few months. Codes accumulate. Delete the ones nobody uses, and change shared codes when someone leaves the household's circle.

We set the first version of this up with you on installation day, write it down, and show you how to change it yourself. It is your house; the plan should be yours to run.

Field Notes from the Southern Tip

How our installers approach a house at the far end of the South Shore — general working practice, not an account of any particular job or customer.

The first question we ask at a Tottenville door is not which lock you want. It is what you would do tonight if the lock stopped responding. If nobody in the household has a clear answer, that becomes the first thing we design. A key with a neighbour, a 9-volt battery in a known drawer, a second admin on a second phone: simple things, decided in advance.

Then we look at which way the door faces. A porch door on the street side of an old house is a sheltered life for a lock. A side door on a newer house facing open water, with no roof over it, is a hard one, and it gets a different lock and a different conversation about care. We also run a hand along the door edge and close the door slowly a few times to watch the bolt meet the strike, because a motor that has to fight the door will drain batteries and eventually report a jam.

Last, we stand at the door with a phone and check the signal, and we ask who comes and goes and when. In a house that is empty most of the day, the code plan is as important as the hardware. And on every door, the inside stays one simple movement to get out.

Do your technicians keep any record of our codes once the job is done?
+

No. We help each person set their own code during the visit and do not keep a list of household codes afterwards. Any temporary code we use for testing is deleted before we leave, and admin rights stay with your household’s accounts. If you want extra reassurance, change the codes yourselves after we go; we show you how.

Can one screen show us the battery level of every lock in the house?
+

With most current smart locks, yes, as long as all the locks are on the same maker’s app or the same smart-home system. The app usually lists each lock with its battery level and last activity. Mixing brands means checking more than one app, which is one reason we suggest keeping a house on one family of locks.

Our retrofit lock has no keypad — can one be added outside without drilling the door?
+

Often, yes. Several retrofit makers sell a small wireless keypad that pairs with the lock and mounts beside the door with screws or adhesive, so the door itself is not drilled. It needs to be within the keypad’s Bluetooth range and in a spot sheltered from the worst weather. We confirm your model supports one before you buy.

We have lost the only spare key for the smart lock’s cylinder — what now?
+

If the lock still opens by code, there is no emergency, but it is worth fixing soon, because the key is your backup. A locksmith or we can rekey the cylinder or fit a new one to match a key you choose, and some smart locks accept a standard replacement cylinder. Then put the spare somewhere you can reach without getting into the house.

We already have a monitored alarm — what does a smart lock add that the alarm does not?
+

The alarm tells you when something happens; the lock controls who can get in and records who did. A smart lock lets you give and take away access without keys, see which code opened the door, and lock up from a distance. Some locks can also arm or disarm compatible alarms, though we set that up carefully so a code never disarms more than it should.

Can we keep the door chain or swing-bar guard once the smart lock is on?
+

Usually, yes. A chain or swing-bar guard is a separate piece of hardware above or below the lock, and most smart deadbolts sit alongside one without trouble. The thing to check is clearance: the inside unit of a smart lock, especially a retrofit, is bulkier than a plain thumb-turn, so the guard may need moving an inch or two. Remember a guard only works while someone is home to fasten it.

Should the keypad code be changed after a big party or a house full of summer guests?
+

It is a good habit. If a shared code was given out for a gathering, or guests stayed for a week, delete that code or change it afterwards rather than leaving it active. Giving guests their own temporary code from the start makes this automatic, because it simply expires when you set it to.
