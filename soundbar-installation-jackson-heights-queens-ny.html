The Neighborhood

Why Jackson Heights Homes Need Soundbar Installation

Most of New York grew lot by lot. Jackson Heights was drawn whole, and the drawing still decides how a television can be hung. According to the Jackson Heights Beautification Group's history, adapted from Daniel Karatzas's book Jackson Heights: A Garden in the City, the Queensboro Corporation under Edward MacDougall bought up the farms between 70th and 92nd Streets, Roosevelt Avenue and Northern Boulevard, finished Laurel Court as the first apartment house in 1914, and in 1917 built a complex it called the Garden Apartments — coining the phrase "garden apartment" in the process. MacDougall's rules were simple: build the whole block at once, leave a garden running its full length, set the buildings back from the street, and give as many rooms as possible two exposures.

Two years later, Queensboro announced that Linden Court, then rising between 84th and 85th Streets, would be sold under a Cooperative Ownership Plan, one of the first in the country. Between 1920 and 1925 the company finished eight block-long complexes — Hampton, Elm, Hawthorne, Laburnum and Cambridge Courts, The Chateau, The Towers and Spanish Gardens — and from 1924 it filled more than fifteen streets with brick English Garden Homes. A second wave came in the 1980s, when at least sixty buildings went co-op and added more than five thousand tenant-owned apartments to the five thousand already there. In 1993 the Landmarks Preservation Commission designated roughly thirty-six blocks as a historic district, then only the second in Queens.

For a soundbar, that history means three things. The walls are old and mostly masonry: the commission's designation report describes the garden apartment buildings as "executed in brick", and partitions inside pre-war New York apartment houses were often built of terra-cotta or gypsum block under plaster rather than wood studs. Many of the earliest buildings have no elevator at all, because every apartment Queensboro finished through 1921 was a walk-up. And the apartment is part of a corporation, so the board and its managing agent have rules about who comes in, when they may drill and how loud the television may be. One resident on the neighborhood's Reddit forum summed up the appeal of these buildings as "solid w thick thick walls" — which is true for the walls between rooms and much less true for the floor between you and the people downstairs.

The rules are not an abstraction. The house rules of one 82nd Street co-op, Allendale Apartments, allow installation work involving noise only on weekdays between 8:30 in the morning and 5 in the afternoon, forbid playing a "radio or television loud speaker" between 11 at night and 8 in the morning, ban outdoor antennas and satellite dishes, require every contractor to be screened and approved by management, and ask that at least 80 percent of each room's floor be covered with rugs or carpet. Other buildings word their rules differently, but a soundbar visit here starts with reading them.

Then there is what people watch. This is one of the most mixed neighborhoods in the city — the JHBG history notes you can hear English, Spanish, Russian, Bangla and Korean on a single walk — and a great deal of the viewing is live sport from somewhere else: fútbol in many Latin American homes — the Eagle's World Cup report spoke to Mexican-American fans on 34th Avenue — and cricket in many Bangladeshi and other South Asian homes around 74th Street. In June 2026 the city's Department of Transportation turned 34th Avenue's Paseo Park into a World Cup fan zone, and the Queens Daily Eagle described fans packed between 77th and 78th Streets for the opening match. At home, the same matches mean a crowd in the living room, commentary that has to rise above the cheering, and a subwoofer that should not be heard through a 1920s floor. That combination is what this page is about.

The installation has to respect all of them.

Building Specialists

Soundbar Installation for Jackson Heights's Architecture

Jackson Heights was built in recognizable waves, and each wave left a different kind of TV wall. The dates below come from the JHBG history; the wall notes are what we expect and then confirm with a pilot hole, because a building's age tells you where to start, not what you will find.

The First Courts, 1914–1921

Laurel, Oban and Penrhyn Courts, Plymouth and Willow Courts and the Colonials along 80th, 82nd and 83rd Streets. Every apartment finished through 1921 was a walk-up, so stairs come with the job, and the walls are plaster over masonry.

Block-Long Garden Complexes

The Greystones, Linden Court and the eight complexes of 1920–25. George H. Wells's courts are attached four- and five-storey buildings around a huge interior garden; Andrew J. Thomas's are detached. Elm Court of 1922 brought the button-control elevator.

The Showpieces

The Chateau of 1923, with slate mansard roofs and carved limestone entrances, and The Towers of 1924, ninety-six large apartments where MacDougall himself lived. Large apartments whose owners usually want the screen to sit quietly in the room rather than take it over.

The Halls of the 1930s

Fillmore Hall's 132 units, Georgian Hall, Oak Halls, then Buckingham Hall and the Mount Vernon — rental buildings, several planned for the smaller three- and four-room suites in demand at the time. Several co-op listings on 76th and 77th Streets mention sunken living rooms and arched openings.

The 1950 Infill

Thirty-eight apartment buildings finished in 1950 alone, many on the old end-block gardens of 34th and 35th Avenues. Many went up on plots sold to outside developers, so their walls cannot be assumed to match the Queensboro core; the test hole matters more than the address.

English Garden Homes

Houses built from 1924 on 87th Street and more than fifteen streets after it: solid brick, front and rear gardens, rear garages, fireplaces, slate or tile roofs. The owner decides, and a basement can carry cables.

Brick. An outside wall in a Queensboro building is usually plaster laid straight onto brick. It is the strongest wall in the apartment and the easiest to get wrong: an anchor set in a soft mortar joint can pull, so the bracket's holes are placed on the brick faces and the hammer drill is kept on low impact so the plaster skin does not let go around the hole. Outside walls also carry the radiator and the windows, which limits where the screen can sit.

Block partitions. Walls between rooms in pre-war New York apartment houses were often built from hollow terra-cotta block or gypsum block and then plastered; an engineer's write-up of a typical pre-war Manhattan building shows both. You cannot tell which from the paint. Clay block rings and then suddenly gives way as the bit reaches a cell; gypsum block drills like chalk and will crumble if forced. Both hold a soundbar comfortably with the right anchor — the wrong one, usually a toggle that cannot open inside a narrow cell, is what fails.

Renovated rooms. Many shareholders have opened kitchens or redone living rooms, and the new wall may be drywall over metal studs or a sheet of drywall laid over the old plaster. Those surfaces sound hollow, which tempts people to use the anchors in the box. We find out what the drywall is sitting on first, and if it is old plaster on block, the fixing goes through both layers. Listings for co-ops on 76th and 77th Streets also mention original details — arches, moldings, sunken living rooms — that change where a bracket can go, which is why we ask for a photo of the whole wall rather than just the spot.

House Rules

The Co-op Rules Behind a Jackson Heights Soundbar Visit

In much of Jackson Heights the corporation, not the shareholder, decides how the visit runs. The examples below come from one published set of house rules for a co-op on 82nd Street; your building's rules will differ in the details, so send them to us with the photo and we will plan around them. None of this is legal advice, just what the paperwork usually asks for.

Who is allowed in

Rules that say every contractor must be "screened and approved by Management" are common. In practice that means the managing agent wants a certificate of insurance naming the corporation and the agent, our license number, the date and window, and sometimes a short description of the work. We send the certificate free and write the description; you only need to tell us who the agent is. Some buildings also send trades to a service entrance on the side or rear of the building, so tell us which door to use.

When the drill may run

A weekday window of 8:30 to 5, with no noisy work on weekends or legal holidays, is a common pattern. A soundbar needs only a few minutes of drilling, but those minutes must fall inside the window, so we book drilling jobs on weekdays and leave the quiet part — connections, settings, the test on a live broadcast — to fill the rest of the visit. If you can only be home on a Saturday, the bar can go on the console or the TV mount that day and the wall can wait.

How loud the television may be, and when

Older co-op house rules can still contain a line about loudspeakers, as the 82nd Street example does: no radio or television played aloud between 11 at night and 8 in the morning. Nobody expects silence, but a soundbar with a subwoofer is precisely what that rule was written about, and a match played on the other side of the world can easily fall inside those hours. We set a night preset that keeps commentary audible at low volume and turns the sub down or off, so the household can follow a late match without a complaint to the board the next day.

What may go on the outside of the building

Bans on outdoor antennas, satellite dishes and anything hung from a window are common — the 82nd Street rules contain all three. That matters for sport: an antenna on the roof would give the lowest-delay picture, but where the rules forbid it, so the TV's sources are planned around cable, streaming or an indoor antenna instead. Nothing in a soundbar installation touches the outside of the building, and the landmark designation, which governs exteriors, does not come into it.

Floors, rugs and the apartment below

Co-op rule books often require most of each room's floor to be carpeted — the 82nd Street example says at least 80 percent, excluding kitchens, bathrooms, closets and foyer. A rug under the sofa is good for neighbors, but it does nothing for a subwoofer standing on bare boards at the edge of the room. We put the sub on an isolation pad and on the rug, away from the wall shared with the next apartment, and set its level with the floor below in mind.

Garden Homes, rentals and sublets

An English Garden Home owner needs no one's permission for a bar inside the house, though in an attached house it is courteous to tell the neighbors before the hammer drill starts. Renters need the landlord's consent, and in a co-op a renter or subtenant also needs the shareholder's agreement and must follow the building's rules. Where drilling is not allowed, the console or the TV mount gives most of the benefit, and moving the bar to the wall later is priced as an ordinary wall mount.

Send us the agent's name and email and we deal with them directly. It saves you a round of forwarding, and the approval usually comes back faster when the agent is talking to the installer.

Commercial Soundbar Installation

Business Soundbar Installation in Jackson Heights

Jackson Heights shops trade under apartments, beside the el and around the busiest station in Queens, and a lot of them keep a television on. In a restaurant on a match night the job is to make commentary carry to the back tables; in a counter business it is to keep a waiting line informed without filling the room; everywhere it is to keep the sound inside the shop. These are the corridors where that work happens.

82nd Street

Planned by Queensboro as the main shopping street and rezoned from residential to commercial between Roosevelt and 37th Avenues in October 1927. The company's own headquarters, opened in May 1929, still stands at the corner of 37th Avenue.

37th Avenue

Planned with 82nd Street as the neighborhood's main commercial district; today a long east–west run of ground-floor shops beneath apartment buildings.

74th Street and Diversity Plaza

South Asian shops and restaurants by the transit hub, with the pedestrian plaza on 37th Road between 73rd and 74th Streets. One Bangladeshi club nearby, on 76th Street, lists cricket matches among the events it celebrates.

Roosevelt Avenue

The street under the 7, with elevated tracks overhead since 1917. Bars and restaurants — one Reddit poster warned that living on Roosevelt is loud "because of all the bars and the train" — and money-transfer and check-cashing counters at addresses such as 72-22 and 76-22 Roosevelt.

Broadway and the Station Triangle

Where 74th Street, Broadway and Roosevelt Avenue meet over the station complex served by the 7, E, F, M and R. Shops here sit around the busiest station in the borough.

34th Avenue's Paseo Park

The 26-block open street from 69th Street to Junction Boulevard. Not a shopping strip, but in June 2026 it hosted the city's World Cup watch party for the opening match.

Businesses pay the same Queens prices as households, plus whichever wall line applies — commonly brick in an older storefront, block in a rear wall or tile behind a counter. Opening-hour constraints run the other way from the co-ops: we can come before the shutters go up or after close, so a match-night restaurant never loses a service to an installation.

Compliance notes for Jackson Heights businesses

  • The apartment upstairs sets the limit. The city's Department of Environmental Protection summary of the noise code caps sound from commercial music at 42 decibels inside a nearby home and, from 10 p.m. to 7 a.m., at 7 decibels above the street's background level measured 15 feet away, with bass tested separately. Many storefronts on 37th Avenue and 82nd Street have apartments above them, so match nights call for a bar without a big sub, or one with its sub turned well down.
  • Keep the screen off the walking route. Under the 2010 ADA Standards, anything fixed to a wall between 27 and 80 inches above the floor may stick out no more than 4 inches into a circulation path. In a narrow Roosevelt Avenue shop, that usually puts the TV and bar above 80 inches or over a counter.
  • Commercial units in co-op buildings follow the building. A shop inside a co-op answers to its lease and, through it, often to the building's contractor rules. We send the certificate of insurance and a written description to whoever needs them.
  • Power belongs to an electrician. If a counter TV needs a new receptacle, a licensed electrician installs it. We plan the layout around existing outlets whenever possible.
Your Questions Answered

Soundbar Installation in Jackson Heights: Questions Answered

These are questions that come up in Jackson Heights and are not already answered on our other soundbar pages, arranged the way they tend to arrive: the apartment first, then match day, then the shop.

Inside the garden co-ops

Our living room sits two steps down from the foyer — does the sunken floor change where the TV, the bar and the subwoofer should go?

Yes, in three ways. First, height: the people watching sit lower than anyone standing in the doorway, so the screen and the bar should be set for eye and ear level in the sunken part, not for the room as it looks from the foyer. In practice that often means a lower TV than the owner first imagines, with the bar just under it and aimed at the seats rather than at the step.

Second, the step itself is a small wall of its own. It reflects sound, and if the sofa backs onto it, bass tends to build up there. Pulling the sofa a little forward, or placing the subwoofer on the opposite side of the room from the step, usually smooths that out. Third, the floor: in an older building a sunken room is often built differently from the rest of the apartment, so we look at what is under the floor before choosing where the sub sits, and use an isolation pad either way. Several co-op listings on 76th and 77th Streets mention sunken living rooms, so we treat the step as a normal part of the plan rather than a surprise.

Diagram, not to scale: in a sunken room the TV and bar are set for the people on the sofa two steps down, and the sub goes on the opposite side from the step.

The building's garbage chute runs up inside the wall where we want the TV — can a bracket go on that wall at all?

Usually, but not over the chute. Many older New York buildings once burned their garbage in basement incinerators and changed to compactor chutes from the 1970s on; one Jackson Heights co-op's house rules still talk about the "incinerator door" and dropping bags into "the flue". That shaft is part of the building's fire and refuse system, it belongs to the corporation, and nothing should be drilled into it or fixed across it.

We locate it first — the hallway chute door and the compactor room tell us where it runs — and then scan and test-drill only where the wall is ordinary partition. If the clear part of the wall is wide enough, the bracket goes there with the TV centered on the room rather than the wall. If it is not, the bar rides on the TV's mount, or the TV and bar move to another wall. A chute shaft can also carry sound, so the subwoofer stays away from it whichever way the bracket goes.

Our co-op wants to screen a film for shareholders in the interior garden one summer evening — can a TV and soundbar be set up out there for one night?

It can, as a temporary setup, but plan it as a building event rather than a living-room job. An indoor soundbar has no weather protection, so it needs a covered table or a stand under a canopy, off the grass, with the power cord run from an approved outlet along a path nobody trips over, and everything brought in before any dew or rain. A large TV on a rolling stand works better than a projector at dusk. We set the bar's level from the back row of chairs and keep the sub off, because the garden is surrounded by bedroom windows.

The rules matter more than the gear. One published set of house rules limits garden use to 9 a.m. until sundown and asks residents to be mindful of the people on the first floor, so a film that starts at dusk may need the board's specific approval. The managing agent will also want to know whose insurance covers the evening. We can set up and collect the equipment, but the date and the permission come from the board.

Match days

On match days the cheers from the apartments around the garden arrive before we see the goal — can the soundbar or a setting close that gap?

Not from the soundbar. A bar adds only a fraction of a second of processing; the gap you are hearing is in the feed. Stats Perform's measurements of the 2026 Super Bowl, reported by StreamTV Insider, put an over-the-air antenna about 19 seconds behind the field on average, traditional cable about 38 seconds, and the streaming apps between 48 and 62 seconds. A household watching on an app can easily be half a minute behind a neighbor on cable or an antenna, and in a garden building every cheer travels across the yard.

What you can change is the source. If the match is on the cable box as well as an app, the box is usually closer to live. It is also worth looking through your streaming service's settings for anything that brings live sport nearer to real time. An antenna is the quickest of all, but where the co-op forbids outdoor antennas it has to be an indoor one that can pick up the station from your apartment. Close the window on the garden side during big matches, and mute any phone apps that send goal alerts before the picture shows them.

During a tournament we put two matches side by side on one screen — which one does the soundbar play, and how quickly can we swap?

The bar plays whatever audio the TV or the streaming device sends it, and in a multiview screen that is one game at a time. Crutchfield's guide to watching several games at once describes the usual method: move the highlight to the game you want with the remote, and its audio and captions follow, taking "a second or two" to switch. The bar simply follows.

Two settings make this smoother. First, make sure the TV is sending audio in a format the bar handles cleanly — if the sound drops for a moment at every switch, setting the TV's digital output to automatic or PCM usually stops it. Second, if the household wants to hear one match while watching both, a second small bar or a pair of headphones on a second TV is the only way to hear two at once; one soundbar cannot mix two separate broadcasts. We test the swap during the visit with whatever service you use, so you know how long it takes before the big day.

Counters and storefronts

Our money-transfer counter on Roosevelt Avenue sits behind a security-glass partition — can the waiting-side TV and soundbar mount above it?

Often, but not on the partition itself. A glazed security screen and its framing are made to resist force, not to carry a cantilevered TV, and drilling the frame can void whatever rating it has. The TV and bar go on the structural wall above or beside the partition, or on a ceiling mount on the customer side, positioned so the screen faces the waiting line and the bar points down toward them rather than through the glass at your staff.

Two more checks. If the bracket would be above the head height of people standing in line, a high-mount line applies and the wall line depends on what is behind the finish — often block in these storefronts. And if the space is narrow, the ADA protrusion rule above keeps the TV out of the walking path. Keep the volume modest: a waiting line needs to hear announcements and the news, not compete with the train overhead, and the counter staff need to hear customers through the speaking grille.

Match Day

Match Day in a Garden Co-op: Commentary, Delay and the Floor Below

In June 2026 Jackson Heights watched the World Cup together on 34th Avenue; most of the time, though, the match is watched at home, in a 1920s living room with friends on the sofa and neighbors on every side. The soundbar has three jobs on those nights: keep the commentary on top of the crowd noise, stay as close to live as the source allows, and keep the bass from becoming the downstairs neighbor's match too. This is how we set it up for that.

Start with where the picture comes from

Delay is set by the feed, not by the bar. The Stats Perform figures for the 2026 Super Bowl make the point: antenna first, cable second, streaming apps last, with a spread of more than half a minute between them. A household that watches every match on an app and hears the neighbors cheer first is not imagining it. We list every source the family uses — cable box, sports apps, a streaming stick, sometimes a laptop on HDMI — and label the TV inputs so the fastest source for a given match is one button away.

Chart: average delay behind the field for each type of feed during the 2026 Super Bowl, as measured by Stats Perform and reported by StreamTV Insider. The soundbar adds only a small fraction of a second to any of these.

Put the commentary on top

A sports broadcast is a mix of commentators and a stadium. Most bars offer a dialogue or voice mode that lifts the center channel, and many offer a sports mode too — but what that mode does varies by maker. TechRadar describes a sports mode as one that "places a lot of emphasis on dialogue"; P.C. Richard's guide suggests turning sports mode off "if crowd is too loud"; Yamaha recommends trying standard first. Those are not contradictions, just different bars. We play a real broadcast during the visit, try standard, voice and sports modes side by side with the household listening, and save the one where the commentators are clearest at a modest volume.

Keep the bass at home

The thump of a goal celebration or a big hit is mostly subwoofer, and low frequencies travel through structure far more easily than voices do. In a pre-war co-op the floor is the path. The sub goes on an isolation pad, on the rug rather than bare boards, away from the wall shared with the next apartment and out of corners where bass piles up. Its level is set from the sofa and then checked with the household's own judgment of how loud a match night gets — not with the sub at the factory setting, which is almost always too high for an apartment building.

Late kick-offs and the 11 o'clock rule

Plenty of matches fall outside convenient hours, and many house rules forbid a television played aloud between 11 at night and 8 in the morning. For those games we save a second preset: night or dynamic compression on, voice mode on, sub down or off, master volume capped. It keeps the commentary intelligible at low level, and pressing one button returns everything to the match-night setting the next afternoon.

A checklist before the tournament starts

  • Know your fastest source. If a match is on both cable and an app, try both a week before and note which runs ahead.
  • Label the inputs. "Cable", "Sports app", "Laptop" on the TV's input list beats "HDMI 1–3" when friends are holding the remote.
  • Save two presets. One for daytime matches with friends, one for the overnight game.
  • Check the sub with the downstairs neighbor in mind. A pad, the rug, and no corner.
  • Test multiview switching. If you watch two games at once, practise moving the audio between them before kick-off.
Street Side or Garden Side

Street Side or Garden Side: Choosing the TV Wall in a Jackson Heights Apartment

Because the blocks were laid out with their long sides running north–south, as the landmarks designation report notes, most garden-apartment rooms face either the street or the interior garden. The two sides are different places to watch television. The street side gets traffic, buses and, near Roosevelt Avenue, the el — one Queens Reddit user wrote of a Jackson Heights apartment that "the train will be loud… if you are right on Roosevelt it could be." The garden side is quiet, but sound carries across the garden to dozens of windows.

Which side should get the screen

If you have a choice of room, a living room on the garden side gives clearer dialogue at lower volume. If the living room faces the street, put the TV on an inside wall so the windows are to the side or behind the screen rather than behind the sofa; noise arriving from behind the listener competes with the commentary more than noise arriving from beside the TV. Heavy curtains do more than any setting on the bar, and closing the street-side window on a match night lets you keep the volume where it was.

Corner rooms

MacDougall wanted as many rooms as possible to have two exposures, so corner living rooms with windows on two walls are common. They often leave one long unbroken wall and one short pier between windows. The long wall almost always wins; a bar squeezed onto a narrow pier with curtains brushing its ends loses clarity. Where the only wall is short, a compact bar or the TV mount option keeps the ends clear.

Diagram, not to scale: one way to arrange a Jackson Heights living room that has a street side and a garden side — screen on the long inside wall, street windows beside rather than behind the viewers, sub on a pad on the rug and away from the shared wall.

The garden carries sound both ways

On a summer evening, windows all around the garden are open, and a soundbar near an open garden window is audible to far more people than one facing the street. Keeping the window behind or beside the bar closed during loud scenes, using the night preset after dark, and keeping rears and subs away from that window all help. It works the other way too: when the garden is busy, the voice mode is what keeps the commentary clear inside without turning everything up.

Radiators, windows and outlets

Outside walls in these buildings carry the radiators and most of the windows, and older apartments may have only one or two outlets per room. That often decides the TV wall before taste does. A bar draws modest power and can share the TV's outlet in most cases; where the only outlet is on the far side of the room, a painted raceway along the baseboard or a cable run to a cabinet is cleaner than an extension cord across the floor, and any new receptacle is an electrician's job.

Choosing a bar for match nights in a co-op

Buy the center channel before the bass. Commentary is the part of a match broadcast that matters, and a bar with a real center speaker separates it from crowd noise better than a two-channel bar trying to fake one. Look for a first number of 3 or more — 3.0, 3.1, 5.1 and up.

Pick a sub you can turn down, or none. A large subwoofer is wasted in a building where the floor below has to stay friendly. A compact sub with fine level control, or an all-in-one bar, gives most of the excitement with a fraction of the trouble.

Check the sports and voice features. Voice or dialogue modes are nearly universal; sports modes vary widely in what they do. Night or dynamic-range modes are what make late matches possible under an 11 o'clock rule.

Think about the remote in a full room. A bar that answers the TV remote over HDMI-CEC is easier for guests than one that needs its own remote or an app. If several people will change the volume, keep it on the TV remote.

Mind the length. A long bar under a modest TV looks heavy on a narrow wall between windows. Measure the clear width of the wall, not just the TV.

What AI Gets Wrong About Soundbar Installation in Jackson Heights

What we did instead: each heading reproduces a line that a cost guide, retailer or manufacturer has actually published — the sort of sentence AI summaries borrow — and the three paragraphs beneath test it against a Jackson Heights co-op on a match night.

"2.1 (Soundbar + Sub) … Installation Cost $150 – $400"

A 2026 surround-sound price guide from Woodlands Home Theater lists a soundbar-plus-sub system at $150 to $400 to install, aimed at small living rooms and bedrooms. As a national range it is not unreasonable.

What a range like that cannot know is the building. A fourth-floor walk-up in one of the 1910s courts adds stairs; a brick outside wall adds a masonry line; a managing agent who wants a certificate and a written description adds paperwork, even if we do not charge for it.

On our Queens list each of those is its own line — brick is $68.25, stairs above the second floor $42, the mount itself $135.45 — while the paperwork for the agent costs nothing. Add up the lines that apply and that is the price, in writing, before we come.

"Snap toggle bolts are a good idea … on both drywall and plaster"

A popular home-improvement blog recommends snap toggles for heavy objects on both drywall and plaster. On plaster over wood lath with an open cavity behind it, that is sound advice.

In a pre-war co-op the plaster is often laid directly on brick or on clay or gypsum block. A toggle needs open space behind the wall to swing its wings open; in solid brick there is none, and in a hollow block the cell may be too narrow, so the toggle never seats properly.

The pilot hole tells us what is there. Brick gets a masonry sleeve in the brick face, hollow block gets an anchor designed to grip inside a cell, and toggles are kept for metal studs and real cavities.

"Choose one with a separate subwoofer to get the full effect of the crowd atmosphere"

Crutchfield's advice for a game-day setup is to pick a soundbar with a separate subwoofer for the crowd and the hits. In a house with a basement, it is good advice.

In a 1920s co-op the subwoofer's energy travels through the floor and the walls to people who are not watching the match. House rules that ask for 80 percent rug coverage and ban loud televisions late at night exist because of exactly this.

A sub can still be part of the system — compact, on a pad, on the rug, off the shared wall, set from the sofa. In the strictest buildings an all-in-one bar is the better buy, and we will say so before you spend the money.

"'Sports' mode places a lot of emphasis on dialogue"

TechRadar's explainer of TV sound modes says sports mode emphasizes dialogue because commentary is the main thing you hear. On some sets that is how it is tuned.

Other guidance points the opposite way: P.C. Richard's live-sports tips suggest turning sports mode off if the crowd is too loud, and Yamaha's suggests starting with standard. Sports modes differ from brand to brand, and a mode built to make a stadium feel big can bury a commentator in a small room.

The only reliable test is a real broadcast in your living room. We compare the modes with the household listening and save whichever keeps the commentary clearest at the volume your building tolerates.

"Automatically amplifies dialogue when external sounds exceed the TV's audio output for more than three seconds"

That is how Samsung's support pages describe Active Voice Amplifier, a feature on select Samsung TVs that uses a sound sensor to raise dialogue when the room gets noisy, and which Samsung says works with soundbars.

In a living room full of fans, the "external sound" is often the guests. A feature that raises dialogue every time the room cheers will keep nudging the level up during the loudest moments of a match, and in an apartment facing the el it reacts to every passing train as well.

It can help a single viewer in a quiet room. For a watch party we usually leave it off and set the voice mode and volume by hand, so the level stays where the household chose it.

"Night sound … making dialogue clearer without having to turn up the volume"

Sonos describes Night Sound as reducing the intensity of loud sounds while raising quieter ones, so dialogue stays clear without more volume. It does exactly that, and other makers' night modes work in a similar way.

What a night mode does not do is make a subwoofer inaudible downstairs. Compression narrows the gap between loud and quiet passages; the bass that travels through a 1920s floor is still there unless the sub itself is turned down or off.

So our late-match preset combines the night mode with a lower sub level and a capped volume. That is what keeps an overnight game within the spirit of an 11 p.m. house rule.

"OTA feeds were still the closest to on-field action"

StreamTV Insider's report on Stats Perform's 2026 Super Bowl test found over-the-air broadcasts closest to live, at about 19 seconds behind the field on average, ahead of cable and well ahead of the streaming apps.

Advice built on that finding often ends with "put up an antenna". Where a Jackson Heights co-op's house rules prohibit outdoor antennas and satellite dishes, as the 82nd Street rules do, the roof is off the table.

An indoor antenna may still pull in the stations from your apartment, especially on a high floor facing Manhattan; if it does not, cable is the next quickest. We connect whichever you choose so its sound goes through the bar like everything else.

Field Notes From Jackson Heights Co-ops

Standing practice for this neighborhood, written as habits rather than as the story of any one home

Read the house rules before booking, not at the door. We treat the address as a hint only: a small pilot hole shows whether the wall is plaster on block, brick or a framed cavity, and the anchor and the price line follow from that.

Can the soundbar remember one sound setting for the sports box and a different one for films?
+

Some can, many cannot. A few bars store a sound mode for each input, while most keep a single setting until it is changed, and the TV may hold its own sound settings per input as well. We check what your bar and TV allow during setup. Where neither remembers per source, we save the match and late-night presets on the bar and show the household the one button that switches between them.

We like listening to the radio or an app commentary in our language — can it play through the soundbar while the TV shows the match?
+

It can play, but it will rarely line up with the picture. A radio or app commentary arrives on its own timing, often seconds ahead of or behind the TV feed, and the bar cannot delay an outside source by that much. A phone or tablet can be connected to the bar's Bluetooth or a spare input, with the TV muted, and if the app lets you pause, a brief pause can bring the two closer. If the match channel itself offers a second audio language, that track stays in sync and is the better choice.
