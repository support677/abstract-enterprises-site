The Neighborhood

Why Foxhurst Buildings Need Fire Alarm Installation Now: From the Big House to Home Street's Permits

Foxhurst knows fire better than most of New York. The firehouse at 1213 to 1219 Intervale Avenue, across from Home Street, holds Engine 82 and Ladder 31 (RadioReference, Bronx). In the late 1960s and early 1970s that house, nicknamed the "Big House," answered more than 10,000 calls a year, roughly one every 45 minutes, and the BBC filmed it in 1972 for The Bronx Is Burning (Coffee or Die). Firefighter Dennis Smith joined Engine 82 in 1966 and wrote Report from Engine Co. 82 about it before founding Firehouse Magazine in 1976 (Wikipedia, Dennis Smith). Property records list the two-story masonry firehouse, built in 1931, inside Foxhurst (PropertyShark, 1215 Intervale Avenue).

Half a century later, the blocks around that firehouse are being built again, and building again is exactly what puts commercial fire alarm work on the schedule. A string of permit filings tells the story. 870 Home Street: six stories, 20 apartments and about 6,100 square feet of commercial space, filed March 19, 2026 (Marketproof). 880 Home Street: four stories, seven apartments over roughly 11,350 square feet of commercial space, filed February 2, 2026 (Marketproof). 840 Freeman Street: eight stories, 74 feet, 39 apartments, filed in January 2026 between Elmo Hope Way and Chisholm Street (100% Bronx, reposting New York YIMBY). 919 Freeman Street: six stories, 15 apartments and commercial space, filed June 19, 2025 (Marketproof). 1111 Fox Street: 14 stories, 145 feet, 87 apartments and commercial space, filed in June 2022 (Marketproof). Several of those filings replace two-story houses built in 1901. Every new building on that list with more than 16 apartments, or with apartments three or more floors above the street exit, is a building where the NYC Building Code calls for a fire alarm system (NYC Building Code 907.2.9).

The second thread is supportive housing on church land. In October 2023 the Governor's Office announced HELP Home Simpson, a seven-story building with 72 apartments, 44 of them for chronically homeless single adults, in the Foxhurst section; it replaced a one-story church building, and St. Peter's Deliverance Ministries came back into 8,500 square feet on the ground floor, where it runs a food pantry and community programs (Governor's Office, October 19, 2023). A few blocks away, 980 Westchester Avenue, which The Doe Fund and Bolivar Development call Tiffany Court Plaza, received its temporary certificate of occupancy in August 2022: seven stories and 151 apartments, with 31 apartments receiving supportive services, over a first floor of retail and community facility space (The Doe Fund; New York YIMBY). The permit filing for that building listed about 20,370 square feet of commercial space and 22,870 square feet of community facility space (New York YIMBY, May 2018). A proposed project at 1017 Home Street, on a site a Presbyterian church used, describes 63 senior apartments over eight stories with a ground-floor community facility (New York Housing Conference).

Put those together and Foxhurst's fire alarm work has a shape that is different from its neighbors. It is less about one old store at a time and more about whole buildings that mix uses on purpose: a congregation and a pantry under apartments, case managers and a 24-hour desk under formerly homeless tenants, stores under the elevated tracks on Westchester Avenue, and community rooms that host the public at night. Each mix changes how the system is designed, who answers its signals, and who pays to keep it working. People still remember what a fire on these blocks looks like: in November 2019 a three-alarm fire at Rogers Place and Westchester Avenue spread to two neighboring buildings and displaced 12 families. "Somebody knocked on the door and said, 'Fire, fire!'" resident Vincent Holmes told CBS New York (CBS New York, November 18, 2019). A building alarm exists so that the knock is not the only warning.

This page covers commercial and code-required fire alarm systems only: the building-wide systems in multifamily and mixed-use buildings and the systems in stores, churches, schools and community facilities. Smoke alarms inside individual houses follow separate rules and are not part of this service.

Need Fire Alarm Repair in Foxhurst? Start With What the Panel Says

If a panel in a Foxhurst apartment building, church, store or program space is showing trouble, beeping, or sounding with no fire, call (646) 490-0629 and we book the visit within 2 hours. While you wait, write down exactly what the screen says, take a photo of it, tell the central station that a technician is coming, and ask the front desk or the porter not to keep pressing reset. Every reset erases a little of the history a technician needs.

Do not disconnect the panel or its batteries, and do not cover a detector that keeps alarming. In a building where people sleep upstairs, a system that cannot report a fire is the emergency, and in New York City a required system that is out of service means notifying the FDNY and keeping the fire watch it calls for until the system is back. The goal of urgent fire alarm repair is a system that works, not a quiet lobby.

The emergency calls we expect most in Foxhurst follow the building types. New buildings in their first year throw troubles as tenants move in, contractors finish punch lists and a sprinkler valve gets bumped, so same day fire alarm repair is often a supervisory signal nobody understood. Older walk-ups and rehabbed buildings lose their monitoring when a phone line changes, which makes the visit a fire alarm communication failure emergency repair. And a store under the el whose panel went dark after a sign or gate installation is a fire alarm system down repair. People search for 24 hour fire alarm repair or after hours fire alarm service at two in the morning; the honest promise is a booking within 2 hours, a real arrival time, and buildings that cannot report a fire put first.

Each emergency fire alarm technician begins with emergency fire alarm troubleshooting at the event log, then walks to the device or circuit the log names. A fire alarm trouble emergency service call in a supportive building often ends in a detector a resident covered, and a fire alarm system emergency service call in a church often ends in a battery that has quietly aged out. Emergency fire alarm service, emergency fire alarm repair, fire alarm panel failure repair and fire alarm system malfunction repair all end the same way: the cause written on the ticket and a plain explanation of what keeps it from coming back. Commercial fire alarm emergency repair on a new building also gets copied to the developer, because warranty work and service work are not the same thing.

Building Specialists

Commercial Fire Alarm Systems for the Building Types on Foxhurst's Blocks

Commercial fire alarm systems in Foxhurst fall into a handful of building types, and each one asks different things of the design.

New mid-rise apartment buildings (six to eight stories)

The Home Street and Freeman Street filings are mostly in this range: concrete or masonry buildings of six to eight stories with 15 to 40 apartments, often with commercial space underneath. For the apartments, the NYC Building Code calls for a fire alarm system "without alarm notification appliances" plus smoke alarms in the apartments; detectors in the spaces the code lists, such as electrical and telephone equipment rooms, elevator machine rooms and elevator lobbies, report to a central station or a constantly attended location rather than ringing the whole building (NYC Building Code 907.2.9). The commercial floor below may have its own requirements. Getting that split right on the drawings is the first job of commercial fire alarm system design.

Taller buildings (the 1111 Fox Street scale)

A 14-story, 145-foot building is a different animal. Under the model building code, a building with an occupied floor more than 75 feet above the lowest level of fire department vehicle access is a high-rise, and Section 403 of the code adds life-safety requirements for those buildings (NFSA). Expect the design professional to address voice communication, a fire command location and more elevator and smoke-control interfaces than a six-story building needs. High rise fire alarm installation is planned as early as the structure.

Supportive and senior housing with staff on site

HELP Home Simpson and 980 Westchester Avenue both pair apartments with on-site services (Governor's Office; New York YIMBY), and the proposed 1017 Home Street project adds senior apartments with supportive services (New York Housing Conference). Staff are in the building, but they are not firefighters. The design questions are practical: where the annunciator sits so the desk sees it, how zones are named so a night worker knows which floor to check, and how the system handles a resident who covers a detector. Nursing home fire alarm installation is a separate category with healthcare rules; a supportive apartment building is an R-2 residential building, and the drawings say which rules apply.

Church floors under apartments

When a congregation trades its lot for a new building and returns to the ground floor, the sanctuary, the pantry and the fellowship hall become part of a larger building. A room where many people gather can be an assembly space with its own fire alarm triggers, while the pantry's storage and the kitchen bring heat detection and hood questions. The church usually wants its own area on the panel and a clear line in the agreements about who maintains what.

Older walk-ups, rehabs and HDFC co-ops

Between the new buildings stand the four- and five-story walk-ups of the early 20th century and the buildings that community groups such as Banana Kelly and People's Development Corporation took over and rebuilt after the 1970s (CityNeighborhoods.nyc). Many depend on apartment smoke alarms rather than a building system, and some have a small panel from a rehab decades ago. When those buildings change use, add a commercial tenant or renovate deeply, the fire alarm question comes back.

Stores, restaurants and the BID corridor

Ground-floor retail along Southern Boulevard and Westchester Avenue sits in old two- and three-story buildings and under new mid-rise ones. Business fire alarm installation in these spaces ranges from a few devices tied into the building's panel to a store's own system, depending on size, occupant load and what the building already has.

Commercial Fire Alarm

Business Fire Alarm Installation on Southern Boulevard, Westchester Avenue and the BID Blocks

Southern Boulevard was Foxhurst's commercial artery early in the 20th century, when it held bakeries, butcher shops, candy stores and movie houses (CityNeighborhoods.nyc). Today it and Westchester Avenue carry bodegas, delis, supermarkets, bakeries and Latin American and Caribbean restaurants (Homes.com). The Southern Boulevard Business Improvement District was created by local law to cover Southern Boulevard and Westchester Avenue between East 163rd and East 167th Streets, including Westchester Avenue between Fox and Hoe Streets; it had 134 businesses and a first-year budget set by the City Council when it was approved (New York Real Estate Journal, January 2008). Bronx Community Board 2 lists the BID among the neighborhood's business organizations (Bronx CB2).

For a store owner on those blocks, commercial fire alarm installation is usually triggered by one of four events: a new lease in a new building whose landlord's system has to cover the space, a change of use such as a retail space becoming a restaurant or a clinic, a renovation big enough to need a filing, or an FDNY inspection that writes up what is already there. Each event gets the same first question from us: what does the building's Certificate of Occupancy say, and what system already exists?

Working under the elevated tracks

The 2 and 5 run above Westchester Avenue and Southern Boulevard (CityNeighborhoods.nyc), and the 6 joins the corridor at Whitlock Avenue. For fire alarm work, the el changes logistics more than design: deliveries and lifts are planned around the columns and the sidewalk, exterior conduit is kept clear of anything the transit structure needs, and test days are scheduled so horns inside a store are not lost in train noise. Overhead utility lines matter too; at the 2019 Rogers Place fire, electrical wires and utility lines in front of the building made firefighting harder (CBS New York).

Compliance callouts for storefronts

  • Design comes first. The FDNY reviews fire alarm plans prepared and filed by a registered design professional on a TM-1 application, and work starts only after the FDNY issues a Project Authorization (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems; NYC Business, fire alarm application).
  • Who may install. The installer must be a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm company holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, and at least one principal of a certified company holds an S-97 Certificate of Fitness, which requires NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license (FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate FAQ).
  • Monitoring. Where a system must report off site, the central station connection has to be active before the inspection is scheduled (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).
  • Fees. NYC Business lists the FDNY fire alarm application fee at $420 for a new application, with $525 more if complex technical analysis is required (NYC Business, fire alarm application).

Office building fire alarm installation in converted upper floors, shopping center fire alarm installation in the larger supermarket buildings, and medical office fire alarm installation in clinics follow the same path. A local fire alarm installation company that knows the corridor saves owners the most time at the very beginning, by asking the right questions before anyone orders a device.

Field Notes

Field Notes: The Ground Floor Gets Tested Last

On a mixed building in this neighborhood, the apartments are usually ready before the ground floor is. The residential floors get their detectors, the elevator lobbies get theirs, the riser is dressed and labeled, and then the commercial or church space sits as a shell for months while its tenant raises money or signs a lease. That shell is still on the approved drawings. If the drawings show detection and a pull station in the vacant space, they have to be there and working on test day, even with bare concrete underfoot.

So we walk the ground floor first, not last. We ask the developer three questions in the first week: will the commercial space be finished before the acceptance test, who will be the tenant if anyone knows, and does the tenant's work need its own filing later. The answers decide whether we put temporary devices in the shell, whether the design professional shows the space as unfinished, and how the panel labels it so a future tenant's devices drop in without reprogramming the whole building. The church on the ground floor of a supportive building is the same problem with more people: the congregation wants to know what it owns, and the developer wants the building approved. Getting that conversation done in month two keeps it from becoming a punch-list fight in month ten.

Your Questions Answered

Foxhurst Fire Alarm Questions From Developers, Congregations, Managers and Store Owners

These are the questions we expect from the people building and running Foxhurst's buildings: developers on Home Street and Freeman Street, churches that traded land for apartments, supportive housing operators, HDFC boards and Southern Boulevard merchants.

Cost and budgeting

Our congregation is giving up its lot for apartments, the way St. Peter's Deliverance did for HELP Home Simpson. Which fire alarm costs land on the church, and which on the developer?

The building-wide system, its panel, the apartments, corridors, risers and central station connection are normally the developer's, because they are part of getting the building approved. What the church carries depends on the deal: some agreements deliver the ground floor finished, devices included, and others hand over a shell and let the congregation build out its sanctuary, pantry and offices later as a tenant fit-out. Ask for the fire alarm scope to be written into the ground-lease or condominium documents now, with the church's devices shown on the drawings as its own area. Settling who carries the commercial fire alarm installation cost for the church floor is easier before the first shovel than after the temporary certificate of occupancy.

Two developers on Home Street filed similar six-story mixed-use buildings. Why might their fire alarm budgets end up far apart?

Because "similar" on a permit filing hides the details that drive the number. One building may have 6,000 square feet of retail and the other 11,000; one may have an elevator and the other not; one may have a restaurant tenant whose hood has to report to the panel; one may be filed on a tight schedule that needs overtime to meet a temporary certificate of occupancy. The fire alarm system installation cost follows the drawings, the device count, the cable routes through concrete, the interfaces and the testing, and the honest way to compare the two is line by line once both have sealed designs.

Our HDFC co-op off Intervale Avenue has a reserve fund but no engineer. What is the first fire alarm expense we should plan for?

The design professional, not the equipment. If the board is replacing an aging panel or the building needs a system it does not have, the first real spend is a New York State licensed engineer or architect to survey the building, read its records and produce sealed drawings, because nothing can be filed or priced honestly without them. We can do the survey alongside the engineer and give the board a written fire alarm installation estimate once the scope is clear, so the reserve plan rests on a real document.

Quality and trust

Our supportive housing nonprofit has to show its funders that every life-safety contractor is qualified. Which fire alarm documents belong in that file?

Collect five things. The FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate of the company doing the work, the name of its principal holding the S-97 Certificate of Fitness, the license of the electrician of record on the filing, the sealed drawings with the FDNY's Project Authorization, and, at the end, the acceptance test report and the FDNY Letter of Approval (FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate FAQ; FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems). Add the central station contract and the first year's inspection reports. A funder or a monitor can check every one of those against a public agency, which is the point.

Our general contractor on Freeman Street wants its own electrician to install the fire alarm. What should we confirm before agreeing?

Confirm that the electrician is NYC licensed and will be the electrician of record on the FDNY filing, that whoever programs and tests the system works for a company with an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate, that the panel manufacturer's programming tools are available to them, and that someone holding an S-97 or S-98 Certificate of Fitness will be present at the acceptance test, as the FDNY requires (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems). Many electricians pull wire well. The questions are about who signs, who programs, and who stands next to the inspector.

Our block association heard that alarms from our building go straight to the firehouse on Intervale Avenue. Is that how it works?

Not directly. A monitored building fire alarm sends its signal to a central station, and the central station passes fire alarms to the FDNY's dispatchers, who send the companies assigned to that address. Being a few blocks from Engine 82 and Ladder 31 helps response time, but it does not replace the monitoring connection, and the FDNY wants that connection active before it schedules the fire alarm inspection (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).

DIY or professional

A deacon in our church is a retired electrician. Can he wire the new detectors in the fellowship hall under the apartments?

Not on a filed system unless he is working for, and named by, the licensed electrician or certified company on the FDNY filing. The fellowship hall's devices are part of the building's approved fire alarm, and the FDNY ties installation to licensed electricians and certified fire alarm companies (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems). His experience is still valuable: he can be the church's eyes during the work, help plan around services, and understand what the technicians are doing.

Our porter wants to replace the cracked cover on a lobby pull station himself. Is that his job or the fire alarm company's?

Leave it to the fire alarm company. A pull station is an initiating device on the building's system; opening it, swapping parts or remounting it can cause an alarm or a trouble condition, and the device has to be tested after any work on it. What the porter can do is report the damage, keep the area clear, and make sure nobody tapes over or blocks the station while the fire alarm pull station repair is scheduled.

Technical

A new Fox Street tower will have only two elevators. How does the fire alarm decide where the cars go when a detector trips?

The fire alarm elevator recall integration is written into the drawings. Detectors in the elevator lobbies, the machine room and the hoistway are programmed to send the cars to a designated floor, usually the main entrance level, or to an alternate floor if the fire is at the main level, and some detectors also signal a warning to firefighters. With only two cars, the elevator fire alarm interface has to be tested car by car at the acceptance test, with the elevator contractor present.

Our Southern Boulevard store's landlord installed a new roll-down gate and sign. Why did the panel start showing a ground fault the next day?

Because a ground fault means a fire alarm conductor has touched something grounded, and new metal on the storefront is the first suspect: a screw through a cable behind the sign box, a conduit crushed by the gate motor bracket, or wet wiring in a disturbed junction box. Fire alarm ground fault troubleshooting starts at the panel, which on most addressable systems narrows the problem to a circuit, and then follows that circuit toward the new work. That answers the question of what causes a fire alarm ground fault in practice: physical damage or moisture far more often than a failed panel.

Landlord and tenant

Simpson Street tenants ask why the hallway horns stayed quiet when a neighbor's apartment smoke alarm went off. Is the system broken?

Probably not. In many NYC apartment buildings the apartment smoke alarm is meant to wake the people in that apartment only, and the building system's smoke detectors report to the central station without sounding the whole building; the code for newer R-2 buildings says smoke detector activation "shall not initiate a signal to alarm notification appliances" (NYC Building Code 907.2.9). Tell residents what the building's system does and does not do, and post the building's fire safety plan where they can read it.

A food pantry runs out of the ground floor of our building. Who answers for the fire alarm devices in the pantry's rooms?

The lease or the use agreement should say so in a sentence. Usually the building owner maintains the system as a whole and pays for testing, while the pantry operator keeps devices unblocked, reports damage, and pays for changes caused by its own work, such as a new partition that cuts off a strobe. If the agreement is silent, settle it before the next inspection rather than during it.

Complaints

Neighbors complain that the new building next door on Home Street sounded its horns for a whole afternoon. Is that normal during construction?

During the weeks before an acceptance test it can be. Every horn, strobe and speaker has to be proven, which means sounding them, often floor by floor, and a pre-test usually comes before the FDNY's witnessed test. A considerate contractor tells the neighbors when the noisy days are, keeps testing to working hours, and silences the system between floors. If the horns sound at night or for days with no explanation, the developer should hear about it.

The central station keeps calling our Westchester Avenue store owner at 3 a.m. about a trouble signal, not a fire. How do we stop the calls?

Fix the trouble; the calls are doing their job. A repeating overnight trouble is often a communicator losing its connection, a battery failing its test, or an AC power interruption, each of which the panel is required to report. What AI Answers Get Wrong About Fire Alarms in Foxhurst's New and Mixed Buildings

Ask an AI assistant about fire alarm work for a new Bronx building and you get answers written for nowhere in particular. Here is where those generic answers go wrong for the buildings going up around Home Street and Westchester Avenue.

"A fire alarm can be designed and installed in a few weeks"

The installation can move quickly once it starts. The approval cannot. The FDNY says initial plan review can take up to 8 weeks and that the path from plan submission to final inspection averages about 20 to 24 weeks (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).

For a developer on Freeman Street, that means the fire alarm drawings are a critical-path item when the structure is still rising, not a finishing trade. A filing made after drywall leaves the building waiting for a review it could have had months earlier.

The fix is simple and boring: bring the design professional and the fire alarm team in during design development, file early, and keep the drawings current as the building changes so a revision does not restart the clock.

"Any licensed electrician can install a building fire alarm"

Partly true, and the missing part matters. The FDNY accepts a NYC licensed electrician or a fire alarm company holding an FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate as the installer, but the design must come from a registered Professional Engineer or Registered Architect, and a certificate of fitness holder must be present at the acceptance test (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).

A certified company also needs a principal with an S-97 Certificate of Fitness, and the S-97 itself requires NICET Level II or higher or a NYC master or special electrician license (FDNY Fire Alarm Company Certificate FAQ).

In Foxhurst's new buildings the risk is a general contractor who assumes its electrician covers everything. Fire alarm contractor licensing is a set of separate roles, and each needs a name on paper before the filing goes in.

"Every apartment building needs horns and strobes on every floor"

Not for the apartments in a typical new R-2 building. The NYC Building Code calls for a fire alarm system "without alarm notification appliances" in those buildings, with detectors in specific rooms reporting to a central station or a constantly attended location (NYC Building Code 907.2.9).

The commercial floor, a church space, or a taller building's voice system can bring notification appliances into the picture, which is why mixed buildings here look different from one another.

Getting this wrong costs in both directions: devices nobody required, or a missing requirement that fails the test. The sealed drawings settle it, building by building.

"A temporary certificate of occupancy means the fire alarm is approved"

The two run on related but separate tracks. The fire alarm is approved by the FDNY after its own acceptance test, which follows NFPA 72 as modified by the Building Code's appendix, and the FDNY issues a Letter of Approval when the system passes (FDNY Tips for Fire Alarm Systems).

For supportive housing operators who must open on a funder's schedule, that difference is real. A building can be ready for residents in every other way and still owe the FDNY a corrected item from the test.

Plan the test request with the TCO date in mind, and keep the Letter of Approval in the building's permanent records next to the Certificate of Occupancy.

"A self-monitored or app-based alarm is fine for a small commercial space"

Not when the building's fire alarm is required to report off site. Required systems report to an FDNY-approved central station, and an FDNY bulletin says a required connection that was dropped must be restored through an approved central station, with a change of central station or communicator calling for reinspection (FDNY bulletin, central office connection, January 2012).

Consumer apps are built for houses. A store under a new apartment building is part of that building's required system, and its signals have to travel the approved path.

If a store owner wants phone notifications too, they can be added on top. They never replace the central station.

"You can look up what a fire alarm costs online"

Online ranges describe somebody else's building. A fire alarm for a 14-story tower, a seven-story supportive building with a church downstairs and a single storefront under the el share almost nothing in scope.

The number depends on the sealed drawings, the device count, the interfaces, the cable routes through concrete or old masonry, the filing and the test, none of which exists until the design is done.

That is why every Foxhurst job gets a written proposal after a survey. A figure given over the phone for a building nobody has seen is a guess, and guesses are how budgets break.

"Once installed, a fire alarm takes care of itself"

NFPA 72 sets schedules for inspecting and testing every part of the system, from visual checks to device tests to battery and sensitivity testing, and the records have to be kept for the inspector.

New buildings make this easy to forget. The installer leaves, the warranty period ends, and the first annual test lands on a property manager who never saw the drawings.

The answer is a service plan that starts the day the Letter of Approval arrives, with the as-built drawings, the programming backup and the test records handed to whoever will maintain the system.

+

It depends on when the building was built or last altered and what its Certificate of Occupancy says. Many prewar walk-ups were built long before today's fire alarm rules and rely on apartment smoke alarms; a major renovation, a change of use or a new commercial tenant can bring a building-wide requirement. A design professional reads the building's records and tells you which rules apply.

When should a Home Street developer bring in the fire alarm team so the FDNY test does not hold up the opening?
+

Before the design development set is finished. The FDNY puts the path from plan submission to final inspection at roughly 20 to 24 weeks, and the plan review alone can take up to 8 weeks, so the drawings need to be filed while the building is still being framed. Waiting until the walls are closed is the most common way to lose a month.

Does a roof terrace on a new Foxhurst building need fire alarm devices?
+

Sometimes. An occupied roof can change how the design professional counts floors and exits, and a terrace with a door back into the stair or the elevator lobby may need a manual station or notification nearby. The answer comes from the sealed drawings, which is why we ask about terraces at the first survey.

Does the Bronx Halloween Parade on Southern Boulevard affect scheduled fire alarm work?
+

On parade day, yes. The parade runs along Southern Boulevard each October, and the 2026 parade started at Simpson Street and East 163rd Street on October 24. We keep deliveries, lifts and acceptance tests on that stretch off parade day and plan around street closures the week before.

Can one fire alarm panel serve both the apartments and the church space on the ground floor?
+

Often it can, with the church space shown as its own area on the panel and its own devices on the drawings. Some projects choose separate systems that report to each other instead. The design professional decides which arrangement the code allows for that building, and the owners decide who maintains what.

Our building's ground-floor commercial space is still vacant. Does the fire alarm have to cover it before a tenant signs?
+

The vacant space is part of the building, so the approved drawings show what protection it gets while it is empty, and the fire alarm has to match those drawings at the acceptance test. When a tenant later builds out the space, its changes are filed and tested as a tenant fit-out on top of the base system.

During a power outage on our block, how long does the fire alarm keep working?
+

A properly installed system switches to its batteries, and NFPA 72 sizes those batteries to run the system in standby for a set number of hours and then sound the alarm for a set number of minutes. The panel reports the power loss to the central station as a trouble signal. After the power returns, have the batteries checked, because a long outage can leave them weak.
