The Neighborhood

Why Carroll Gardens Properties Need Access Control

Carroll Gardens is a neighborhood of small buildings. Writing in Brick Underground in September 2023, Nancy A. Ruhling put the share of housing that is single-family brownstones, or brownstones converted into boutique condos or co-ops, at about 95 percent, and quoted a local broker explaining why: “It’s not a neighborhood of big buildings. The zoning doesn’t allow them.” The listings she walked through make the point. An eight-unit co-op on Luquer Street from 1910. A turn-of-the-century brownstone on President Street newly split into three condos. Eight apartments in a four-story limestone on Clinton Street from 1920. Eight more in a four-story building on Court Street from 1900. Even the new buildings are small: a seven-story condo with 17 units on President Street finished in 2018, and a four-story building with 13 units on Degraw Street that opened in 2019.

That changes what access control is for. In a high-rise the problem is volume: hundreds of residents, a desk, a package room. Here the problem is that one doorway, built for one family in the 1870s, now serves several owners who each have their own keys, their own tenants, their own cleaners and their own contractors. The street door is the only thing all of them share, and in a brownstone conversion there is rarely a super or a managing agent watching it. Keys get copied for a dog walker and never come back. A renovation crew keeps a key for months. When a unit sells, nobody is sure how many keys went with it. A fob or code system replaces that uncertainty with a list: each person has their own credential, each credential can be switched off, and the door records which one opened it.

The doorway itself is the second reason this neighborhood is different. The Carroll Gardens Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on September 25, 1973, takes in 134 rowhouses built between 1869 and 1884 in the late Italianate and neo-Grec styles, set back behind the deep, fenced front yards the neighborhood is named for. The typical entrance is a stoop rising to a pair of tall wooden doors, often with glass in their upper panels, opening into a vestibule with a second door beyond. Owners on the Brownstoner forum have described the result for years: double front doors about three feet by ten, an intercom but no door release, and a locksmith telling them “there is no system to accomodate a buzzer” on that kind of door. There is. It just takes a different lock, a way to get the wire into the door, and a plan for the leaf that does not move.

The third reason is what has actually happened here. Carroll Gardens is a quiet neighborhood by the numbers, and we do not sell systems on crime statistics. But the incidents that do get reported have a pattern that matters for doors. In January 2025, ABC7 reported that burglars cut a hole in the roof of the Smith and Ninth Street Deli and came down into the store; the owner, Saleh Hussan, described it simply: “They make the hole, they come down.” They took cash and cigarettes and ripped out the alarm cables and the video recorder on the way. A Patch police blotter from 2012 records a burglar walking into a house on Clinton Street through a basement door that was closed but not locked. Neither of those is solved by a fob reader alone, and we say so further down. Both are solved in part by doors that lock themselves, report when they are left unlocked, and keep the equipment that runs them out of reach.

Carroll Gardens facts that shape an access control plan

  • Where it is. About 40 blocks in ZIP code 11231, Brooklyn Community District 6, served by the NYPD 76th Precinct at 191 Union Street, with the F and G trains at Carroll Street and Smith–Ninth Streets (Wikipedia; NYPD).
  • The district. 134 rowhouses, built 1869–1884, designated September 25, 1973 and listed on the National Register in 1983; uniform setbacks and fenced, landscaped front yards (Wikipedia, Carroll Gardens Historic District).
  • The housing. About 95 percent single-family brownstones or brownstones converted to boutique condos and co-ops; example buildings of 3, 6, 8, 13 and 17 units (Brick Underground, September 2023).
  • The streets. Mostly three- and four-story rowhouses zoned R6, with commercial overlays along Court Street and Smith Street, and front yards that the City Map counts as part of the street on the garden blocks (Department of City Planning, 2008).
  • The Landmarks limit. One intercom panel and two light fixtures per door opening, mounted in mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metal, never through cast iron or decorative surfaces; card readers and keypads are not addressed (LPC Permit Guidebook, chapter 11).
  • The privacy law. Owners of multiple dwellings, meaning buildings with three or more households, who use fobs, apps or biometrics at the door must follow the city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law (HPD).

Most Carroll Gardens owners who ask about the street door also ask about the garden-level door under the stoop and about a camera that shows the front gate. Both are covered on this page, priced from the same Brooklyn list, and both can join the same controller later if you start with the stoop doors alone.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Carroll Gardens’ Architecture

The building decides the hardware. Carroll Gardens has fewer building types than most Brooklyn neighborhoods, but within them the doors vary a great deal: some houses still have their original paired doors and vestibule, some lost them to a renovation decades ago, and some newer buildings have aluminum storefront doors that behave nothing like a nineteenth-century door. These are the six we see most often.

Single-family district brownstones

Rowhouses of the 1869–1884 district, three and four stories over a garden level, set back behind a front yard and an iron fence. One household, but usually a nanny, a cleaner, a dog walker and a contractor who need to get in, and a family that wants to stop handing out keys.

Brownstones split into condos

The same house divided into two, three or four units, each owned separately, with a small association and no manager. The stoop doors, the vestibule and often the cellar are common, and every owner wants their own credentials and their own phone to ring.

Small pre-war co-ops

Four- and five-story buildings of six to twenty apartments, such as the 1900–1920 co-ops on Luquer, Clinton and Court Streets that turn up in listings. A street door, a cellar door, sometimes a rear yard door, and a board that changes every few years.

Two- and three-family houses

Owner on the lower floors, tenants above, one shared street door. At three households the house is a multiple dwelling under New York law, which brings the Tenant Data Privacy Law into play the moment the door goes keyless.

Newer boutique condos

Buildings of the last twenty-five years, from a six-story condo built in 2000 on Huntington Street to the 2018 and 2019 condos on President and Degraw Streets: a lobby door, a garage or service door, and an entry system the developer chose that the board now has to live with.

Mixed-use buildings on the avenues

Rowhouses and walk-ups on Court Street and Smith Street with a shop on the ground floor and apartments above, where the shop door and the residents’ door sit side by side and must never share a credential list.

What the materials mean for the install

Brownstone is a soft sandstone, and in Carroll Gardens much of it has been patched, resurfaced or replaced with a cement coating over the years, so we do not drill the face of it if we can avoid it. The Landmarks guidance points the same way: equipment is to be fixed at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metal, not through cast iron or decorative surfaces. In practice the cleanest places for an entry panel are the masonry beside the door frame, the wood of the frame itself, or the iron of the fence or gate post in front of the entrance, which the guidance also allows for an intercom. The cable then goes straight back through the wall or down into the cellar, rather than running across the front.

The doors are wood, usually thick and heavy, and often original. That is good news for strength and bad news for wiring, because a lock that sits in the door needs power in the door, and power has to cross the hinge. Glass panels in the upper half of the doors are common, and they matter for two reasons: they decide where a request-to-exit sensor can see, and they decide whether someone could break a pane and reach the inside lever. Inside, the vestibule floor is often tile or marble, the vestibule walls are plaster on brick, and the cellar ceiling under the stoop is usually open joists, which gives us a clear route for cable without touching a finished room.

Newer buildings are the opposite: aluminum storefront doors with narrow frames, a telecom room, and conduit already in the walls. There the work is about choosing a lock that fits a narrow aluminum stile, and about inheriting a system someone else picked. Every building type above can take a reader, a keypad or a phone credential. What changes is the lock, the route the wire takes, and who holds the administrator login when the work is done.

Owners of condo brownstones who put a reader on the stoop doors usually pair it with an entry panel that calls each unit’s phone, and many add a contact on the garden-level door at the same time. Both are on the same Brooklyn list below.

Ten Feet of Wood and Glass: Putting an Electric Lock on a Carroll Gardens Double Door

The paired stoop doors are the single most common reason a Carroll Gardens access control job is harder than the same job in a newer building, and the most common reason owners are told it cannot be done. A single door is simple: the latch in the door meets a strike in the frame, and we replace that strike with an electric one. The lock in the door stays mechanical, the wire stays in the frame, and nothing has to cross the hinge. A pair of doors has no frame on the side where the two leaves meet. The active leaf latches into the inactive leaf, or into a strike set in the edge of the inactive leaf, and the inactive leaf is held by bolts at its top and bottom. There is nowhere obvious to put a strike, and that is where the “no system” answer comes from.

There are three honest ways to electrify a pair, and which one suits a door depends on what the door already has. The first keeps the lock mechanical and electrifies the meeting edge: if the inactive leaf is solid enough, an electric strike can be mortised into its edge, with the wire run up inside that leaf and across its top hinge. The second, and on a heavy original door usually the best, puts the electricity in the lock itself: an electrified mortise lock replaces the existing mortise lock in the active leaf, so the outside lever is locked or free depending on the signal, and the inside lever always opens the door. The third uses an electrified exit device or a surface lock where the doors are used by many people and a mortise lock is not practical; on a residential brownstone that is rare.

Whichever lock is chosen, the wire has to reach it. On Brownstoner, an owner who had done it during a renovation warned that “You’ll need to open up the walls, probably quite a bit,” and that the doors needed a routed channel and a drilled hole. That was true of the way it was often done, and it can still be true in a house where the walls are already open. In an occupied house we try to avoid opening anything. The cable comes up from the cellar inside the wall beside the frame, which in a brownstone is usually reachable from below. It crosses into the door through a power transfer hinge, a hinge with wires running through its knuckle that replaces one of the existing hinges and looks almost the same, or through an armored door loop on the hinge side where a hinge swap is not possible. Inside the door it runs through a channel drilled from the hinge edge to the lock pocket. That channel is the one invasive step, it is drilled from the edge where nobody sees it, and it is planned so the door could take a mechanical lock again later.

The leaf that does not move

Electrifying the active leaf does nothing if the inactive leaf can be pushed open. On many old pairs the top and bottom bolts no longer reach their keepers properly: the floor has settled, the head has been painted over, or the bottom bolt was removed years ago because it scraped the sill. A determined push on the meeting edge then opens both doors together, lock and all. Before we price a double-door job we check those bolts, and where they are worn we quote their repair or replacement as part of the door work. On doors used by many people, such as the street door of a co-op, automatic flush bolts that throw themselves when the active leaf closes are worth considering, because manual bolts depend on the last person to open the pair remembering to set them.

Owners sometimes ask whether the inactive leaf can still open for furniture or a piano, as the old doors were built to do. It can. Nothing in the electrified lock stops the inactive leaf’s bolts being withdrawn by hand from inside. We simply make sure that once it is closed again, the bolts are thrown and the pair is secure.

Glass, levers and the request-to-exit

Many Carroll Gardens stoop doors have glass in their upper panels. A thief who breaks a pane and reaches the inside lever opens a mortise lock just as a resident would, so on glazed doors we look at three things: how far the lever is from the nearest pane, whether the glass is old single-pane, and whether a laminated replacement or a security film is sensible. We do not fit a lock that needs a key from the inside, because the inside of a residential street door has to open without one; a locked inside lever is a fire hazard. The request-to-exit device, the sensor or switch that tells the controller someone is leaving rather than forcing the door, is placed so it sees a person approaching from inside but cannot be triggered by a hand through the glass or a stick through a mail slot.

The lever itself matters too. An electrified mortise lock can usually take the original style of knob or lever trim, and where the house has its own historic hardware we can often keep the outside trim and change only the lock body inside the door. That way the doors look the same from the street, which is what most owners want and what the historic district expects.

One Panel per Doorway: Building the Reader Into the Entry Station

The Landmarks Preservation Commission’s Permit Guidebook allows two light fixtures and one intercom panel per door opening on a building in a historic district, placed within or next to the door opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, with a design, size and finish that is “neutral and discreet.” Its staff can approve installations that meet those criteria without a hearing, and replacing an existing intercom in kind needs only manufacturer cut sheets rather than drawings. The same chapter says nothing about card readers or keypads. That silence is not a loophole. It means a separate reader on a district house is a question for Commission staff rather than something the guidebook already blesses, and the simplest answer to it is to avoid a separate device at all.

That is why most of our district work uses a combined panel: one entry station with a camera, a call button or a short directory, and a fob reader built into the same faceplate. To a passer-by it is one intercom panel, the same size as the one it replaces or smaller. To the residents it is a doorbell that rings their phones, a reader for their fobs and a way to let in a courier without coming downstairs. Where an owner wants a keypad as well, for a dog walker or a cleaner who will not carry a fob, we choose a panel with a keypad built in, or use a phone credential instead, rather than adding a second box beside the door.

The cable route matters as much as the panel. The guidebook asks for conduit to be concealed by running it through plain masonry or the wall behind the installation, and where that is impossible allows roughly ten inches of visible conduit, painted to match. On a brownstone that almost always means drilling straight back through a mortar joint behind the panel and down into the cellar or the vestibule wall, which is how we would want to do it anyway. Exposed cable running across the stoop wall looks bad, gets kicked and lets in water.

Inside the district, outside it, and on the fence

Not every Carroll Gardens house is in the historic district. The district covers 134 rowhouses, and the Department of City Planning places its blocks of Carroll and President Streets between Smith and Hoyt; many fine brownstones a block away are outside it, and so are the avenue buildings on Court and Smith. We check the lot before the visit so the owner knows whether Landmarks has a say at all. Outside the district the panel can go wherever it works best, though we still keep it small and keep cable off the facade, because the neighbors notice and because the house will be sold one day.

Many owners would rather have the panel at the front-yard gate than at the door, so a visitor is answered before they walk thirty feet up the garden path. The guidebook allows an intercom on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, and on the garden blocks the Department of City Planning notes that the front yards are part of the mapped street, so we treat anything in the yard with care. A gate panel needs its cable to reach the house, which means a conduit under the path or a wireless bridge, and the gate itself needs its own lock if the panel is meant to open it. We price that as a separate door, because it is one.

Three to Eight Owners, One Street Door: Credentials in Boutique Condos and Co-ops

A Carroll Gardens condo association is often three neighbors who bought floors of the same house. A co-op board might be five shareholders in an eight-unit building who meet a few times a year. Neither has a managing agent on call or a super living in the cellar. That is the setting an access system has to suit, and it is why we spend as much time on the credential plan as on the hardware.

We start by writing down every kind of person who uses the door. Owners and their families. Renters, where an owner leases their unit. Regular workers: cleaners, nannies, dog walkers, home aides. Occasional workers: contractors, exterminators, the plumber. Couriers. Brokers and buyers when a unit is for sale. Each kind gets a type of credential and a rule. Owners get fobs and phone keys that never expire. A renter gets a fob whose end date matches the lease, issued by the owner who leased the unit. A cleaner gets a code that works on Tuesdays. A contractor gets a phone key that ends the day the job is signed off. Couriers get the entry panel, which calls a resident, or a short-lived code for a package room if the building has one.

Then we decide who can change what. In a three-unit condo the usual answer is that each owner can manage the people attached to their own unit, and that two people, not one, hold the administrator login for the whole system, so it survives one owner selling or moving away. In a co-op the board holds administration and the managing agent, where there is one, gets a limited account. Every change is logged, so if a fob that should have ended is still working, the log shows who issued it and when.

Cloud or local, and what it costs to run

For a building this size a cloud-managed system is usually worth it, because the people who run it are volunteers who are rarely in the building at the same time, and a cloud account lets any of them add or remove someone from a phone. National guides put cloud licensing at roughly $3.50 to $15 per door per month; on a building with one or two controlled doors that is a small figure, and we tell you the actual subscription for the system we propose before you agree to it. A local system with no subscription is also possible, managed from a computer or an app on the building’s own network, and suits a single-family house or an owner who does not want any monthly charge. Either way, the door keeps working if the internet goes down: the controller holds the credential list and decides on its own.

The privacy side is not optional once there are three or more households. New York City’s Tenant Data Privacy Law applies to owners of multiple dwellings that use smart access, and it limits what data may be collected, requires a written privacy and retention policy, and requires that authentication data be destroyed within 90 days unless it is anonymized. A condo owner who rents out a unit, or a co-op with renters among the shareholders’ apartments, needs that paperwork before the fobs go live. We set the system up to collect only what it needs, and we give the board a plain-language summary of what is stored and for how long, to adapt with its own lawyer.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Carroll Gardens

Commercial Carroll Gardens is mostly two streets, zoned with commercial overlays on top of the residential R6 that covers the rest, plus a scatter of studios, practices and small offices on the side streets and toward the Columbia Street edge. Almost every business here sits in the ground floor of a building with apartments above, which shapes every access job: the shop’s doors are the shop’s, but the street door beside it belongs to the residents, and the cellar is often shared.

Court Street

The older shopping street, with bakeries, food shops, restaurants and services in rowhouse ground floors; a 2014 guide named Caputo’s Bake Shop, open since 1904, and D’Amico Foods, there since 1948, and the Esposito pork store closed in 2023 after a century. Typical jobs: a back-door keypad for early staff, a reader on a cellar stair, and schedules that end at closing.

Smith Street

The dining street since the 1990s, with restaurants that close late, boutiques and service businesses, running south to the elevated F and G at Smith–Ninth Streets. The common jobs are a staff door for the kitchen, a manager’s office that only two people open, and a residential door upstairs kept entirely separate.

Union Street and the cross streets

Corner shops, cafés and a few offices where the avenues meet the residential blocks. Small doors, often aluminum and glass, with a roll-down gate in front at night; the reader goes where it can be reached when the gate is up and the keypad works with gloves.

Practices and studios in rowhouses

Therapists, small medical and dental offices, design studios and tutors on parlor and garden floors. The office door takes a reader or keypad on the practice’s own schedule, while the building’s street door stays with the residents.

Toward Columbia Street

The western edge, where Patch placed a yoga studio at 259 Columbia Street that a thief entered through an unlocked door at about 3 a.m. in February 2019. Studios and small businesses there want doors that relock on their own and tell the owner when they have not.

Lower Smith and Ninth Street

Delis, shops and service businesses near the Smith–Ninth Streets station and the transit lots the city notes along Smith Street, including the Smith and Ninth Street Deli that burglars entered through the roof in January 2025.

Shops in rowhouse ground floors: front, back and below

A Court Street shop usually has three doors that matter. The front door, which is open while the shop is and behind a gate when it is not. A back door to a yard or an alley, used by staff and deliveries. And a stair to the cellar, where stock, coolers or a prep room live. Access control does most of its good at the second and third. A back door on a keypad with a code for each employee means nobody has to hide a key, and a schedule means an opener’s code does not work at midnight. A reader on the cellar stair means a delivery driver who is let in the back cannot wander downstairs. Where the cellar is shared with the apartments above, the shop’s cellar door and the residents’ cellar door become separate doors on separate lists.

Restaurants on Smith Street add a fourth: the office, where the safe and the cash are counted. That door takes its own reader, open to two or three people, and its own record. The record is the point. If cash goes missing, the owner can see who opened the office and when, without having to accuse anybody.

What the deli burglary teaches about where the equipment goes

The thieves at the Smith and Ninth Street Deli did not come through a door. They cut through the roof, and once inside they tore out the alarm cables and the video recorder before leaving. A reader on the back door would not have stopped that. What an access plan can do is make sure the equipment that runs the doors is not the first thing a burglar reaches, and that interior doors still mean something once someone is inside. So we mount controllers and power supplies in a locked steel enclosure, preferably in the cellar or behind a door that is itself on the system, never on the wall beside the register. We give the office or stockroom its own electrified lock, so a burglar who has got past the roof still meets a locked door. And we connect the system to the store’s alarm, where there is one, so a forced door or a tampered enclosure reports immediately rather than when the owner arrives in the morning.

Rules that apply to Carroll Gardens entry installations

  • Egress first. Under the NYC Building Code, an electrically locked door must still open from the inside. Sensor-release systems need a sensor on the egress side, must unlock on power loss, and need a manual “push to exit” button 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door that cuts power to the lock and keeps it unlocked for at least 30 seconds; they must also release on a fire alarm or sprinkler signal and use UL 294 listed equipment (BC 1010.1.9.8). Magnetic locks have their own rules, including release by the door hardware itself (BC 1010.1.9.9).
  • Self-closing, self-locking entrances. The New York State Multiple Dwelling Law requires automatic self-closing and self-locking entrance doors on class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968, and an intercom with voice and a remote door release where a building has eight or more apartments; older buildings must install them when a majority of tenants ask in writing (MDL §50-a). Roof doors must be self-closing but not self-locking, and openable from inside without a key.
  • Tenant data. Where three or more households share a building and the owner uses fobs, apps or biometrics, the Tenant Data Privacy Law requires consent, a written privacy and retention policy, minimum data, no sale of data and no tracking outside the building, and it bars requiring smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment (HPD).
  • Keys still matter. Reporting on tenant lawsuits over keyless buildings, amNY noted that city rules still required a traditional key-based way in. We always leave a mechanical key override on every electrified street door.
  • Landmarks. Inside the historic district, one intercom panel and two light fixtures per door opening, fixed in mortar joints or plain material, with concealed cable or roughly ten inches of painted conduit at most; staff-level approval where the criteria are met (LPC Permit Guidebook, chapter 11).
  • Who may do the work. New York State licenses businesses that install, service or maintain security and fire alarm systems under General Business Law Article 6-D. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431; any 120-volt wiring a system needs is done by a licensed electrician.
Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Carroll Gardens: Questions Answered

Double doors and vestibules

Our stoop entrance is a pair of tall wooden doors with glass panels. Which leaf should the electric lock go on, and what happens to the other one?

The lock goes on the active leaf, the one people actually push open, which is almost always the leaf that already has the lockset and the knob or lever. On a heavy original door that usually means an electrified mortise lock in place of the existing one: from outside the lever is locked until a fob, code or phone releases it, and from inside it always opens. If the inactive leaf is solid and its edge is deep enough, an electric strike set into that edge is the alternative, and it leaves the active leaf’s lock mechanical.

The other leaf stays a fixed leaf, held by its top and bottom bolts. Its job is to not move, so we check those bolts before anything else; a worn or missing bolt lets the pair be pushed open no matter how good the lock is. It still opens by hand from inside when you need the full width of the doorway.

Does wiring a lock into a wooden door mean cutting a channel through it and opening the walls, as people on Brownstoner warned?

Some drilling into the door is unavoidable if the lock is in the door, but it is far less than people expect. The wire runs through a hole drilled from the hinge edge of the door to the lock pocket, out of sight, and crosses from the frame into the door through a power transfer hinge that replaces one existing hinge, or an armored loop where the hinge cannot be swapped. On the wall side we come up from the cellar inside the wall beside the frame, which in most brownstones is reachable from below without touching the plaster.

Opening walls “quite a bit” was the advice of an owner who did the work during a renovation, when the walls were open anyway. In a lived-in house we plan the route so nothing finished has to be cut. If the only possible route needs a small opening, we tell you before you agree to the job, not on the day.

If someone breaks a glass pane in our front doors, can they reach in and open the electric lock?

They can if the inside lever is within arm’s reach of a pane, because an inside lever that always opens the door is what the law and common sense require. So the answer is not to lock the inside lever; it is to make the glass hard to get through. Old single-pane glass near the lever can be replaced with laminated glass of the same look, or covered with a clear security film, and the request-to-exit sensor is aimed so that a hand through the glass does not trigger it.

We also fit a door contact and set the system to report a forced door, so a broken pane followed by the door opening without a credential sends an alert at once. On doors where the lever sits well away from any glass, none of this is needed.

Our heavy parlor doors never swing shut by themselves. Is there any point adding a reader before that is fixed?

No. A door that stays ajar is unlocked, however good its electronics, and a reader on it gives a false sense of security. The first part of any job on old doors is making them close and latch on their own: rehanging a leaf that has dropped, freeing a door that binds on swollen wood, and fitting or adjusting a closer sized for a tall, heavy door. On a pair, the closer goes on the active leaf, and the bolts on the other leaf are checked at the same time.

Once the doors close reliably, a door position contact lets the system tell you if they are ever left standing open, which is the alert most brownstone owners end up valuing most.

Keys, carriers and old buzzers

What is a postal lock, and can it be wired to our new door release?

A postal lock is a small keyed switch, set beside the door, that only the letter carrier’s key turns. On Brownstoner one reply described wiring the electric strike on a brownstone’s outer door to a “PO (Post Office) lock,” so the carrier’s key releases the door briefly and the mail gets to the boxes in the vestibule. That is a sound arrangement, and on a combined system it is simple: the postal switch is wired to the controller as another way of opening that one door, and its use appears in the log like any other.

Arranging for the carrier to have a key to the switch is a matter for the post office, not for us; we install the hardware and leave the space and wiring for it. If your mailboxes are outside the doors, you do not need one at all.

A neighbor told us to key the outer and inner doors differently. Does that still matter once there are fobs?

It matters less, but the idea behind it still holds. The advice, given on Brownstoner for a three-family with an outer pair and an inner door, was to key the doors differently so that losing a key or removing someone means changing only one lock. With fobs on the doors, losing a fob means deleting it in the app, so the keys become a backup rather than the everyday way in. We still recommend the two doors use different key cylinders, kept in very few hands, so that the backup key to the outer doors is not also the key to the inner door.

Keep one spare cylinder for each door. If a key ever goes missing, swapping a cylinder takes minutes and the fobs keep working throughout.

We already have a buzzer. Can a Wi-Fi relay wired to its door-release terminals do the job of a real access system?

It can give you remote buzz-in from a phone, and for a single-family house that may be all you want. A reply on Brownstoner described exactly this: a Wi-Fi relay connected to the existing release contacts, matched to the system’s voltage, which it put at typically 8 to 16 volts AC or DC, with the hard part being “programming the system to smartphones,” not the wiring. What a relay does not give you is individual credentials, schedules, end dates or a log of who opened the door, and on most cheap relays everyone shares one app account.

In a condo brownstone or a small co-op those missing pieces are the reason to have access control at all, so we would fit a proper controller. Where a relay is the right answer we will say so and quote it, and the same reply’s warning stands: hardwired systems should be installed by someone licensed for low-voltage work.

Isn’t it safer to have no remote release at all, so somebody has to come down and look before opening?

Some buildings decide exactly that; one Brownstoner poster said their building chose not to add a buzzer “for safety reasons” and preferred residents to open the door in person. It is a reasonable choice for a household that is always home. It does not suit a building where people work, travel, or need a cleaner or a home aide to get in, because the usual result is a key under a pot or a door left on the latch.

A video entry panel gives you the benefit of looking before opening without the walk downstairs: you see the person at the door on your phone, talk to them, and decide. Nobody gets in on a button press alone unless you choose to let them, and every release is recorded.

Condos and co-ops

How do three condo owners in one brownstone usually split the cost of a street-door system?

The street doors, the vestibule and the cellar are usually common elements, so most associations treat the system as a common expense and split it the way their declaration splits other common costs, often by percentage interest. Extras that serve one unit, such as an indoor answering screen in one apartment or a reader on one owner’s private garden door, are usually paid by that owner. The association’s own documents decide this, not us, but we price the job so the split is easy: common work on one line, each unit’s extras on its own.

The written quote is addressed to the association, and if the association or its insurer asks for a certificate of insurance naming it, we send it before the first visit.

One owner rents out their unit. Should the renter’s fob be issued by that owner or by the association?

By the owner, within limits the association sets. The cleanest setup gives each unit owner the ability to add and remove people attached to their own unit, so the owner who signs a lease issues the renter’s fob with an end date that matches it, and ends it when the renter leaves. The association’s administrators can see that credential and can disable it if they need to, but do not have to be involved in every lease.

Because a renter now lives in the building, the owner and the association should have the Tenant Data Privacy Law paperwork in place, including the renter’s consent and the written policy on what the system records.

Shops and garden-level doors

The burglars at the Smith and Ninth Street deli tore out the alarm cables and the video recorder. Where should an access controller go so it isn’t the first thing pulled out?

Somewhere a burglar has to work to reach, and somewhere that tells you when they try. For a shop that means a locked steel enclosure for the controller and power supply, in the cellar or in a back room whose own door is on the system, with a tamper switch on the enclosure. It does not mean a plastic box on the wall beside the register or above the front door, which is where these things often end up because it is convenient on install day.

The cables to the readers are run inside walls or in conduit where they pass through the shop, so they cannot be cut in one pull. If the store has an alarm, the access system reports a tampered enclosure or a forced door to it, and the video recorder gets the same treatment: locked away, and not in sight.

A Patch blotter described a burglar walking into a Clinton Street house through a basement door that was closed but not locked. Can a system tell us when our garden-level door is shut but unlocked?

Yes, if the lock reports its own state. A plain door contact only knows whether a door is open or shut. To know whether a shut door is locked, the system needs either an electric strike or lock with a monitoring switch, which reports whether the latch is held, or a deadbolt with a bolt-position sensor. With that in place, the app can show “closed, unlocked” and send an alert if the garden-level door stays that way past a time you choose.

Simpler still, an electric strike on that door makes the question disappear: the latch drops into a locked strike every time the door closes, so there is no thumb-turn to forget. That is why we usually suggest the garden-level door goes on the system even when the stoop doors are the priority.

Types of Access Control We Install in Carroll Gardens

Electrified mortise locks

Lock bodies that replace the existing mortise lock in a heavy wood door, locking or freeing the outside lever on a signal while the inside lever always opens. The usual answer for the active leaf of a pair of stoop doors, and they can often keep the house’s own outside trim.

Electric strikes

The part in the frame that releases the latch. Used on single doors, vestibule doors, garden-level doors and shop back doors, in versions for wood frames, narrow aluminum storefront frames and the edge of an inactive leaf, with a monitoring switch where you want to know the door is latched.

Power transfer hinges and door loops

The hidden link that carries the wire from the frame into a door that has its lock in it. A transfer hinge replaces one existing hinge and looks almost identical; an armored loop is used where the hinge cannot be swapped.

Combined entry panels

Video stations with a camera, one button or a short directory, and a fob reader or keypad built in, sized for the narrow masonry beside a brownstone door and finished in dark or brushed metal. Larger touchscreen panels for newer condo lobbies.

Exit devices and contacts

Request-to-exit sensors and push buttons that meet the Building Code, door position contacts that report a door held or forced open, and automatic flush bolts for inactive leaves on busy doors.

Controllers, credentials and apps

Battery-backed controllers in a locked enclosure in the cellar, encrypted fobs and cards, phone credentials, and an app that calls each unit owner when someone presses their button, with local or cloud management.

ADT, Ring, SimpliSafe and a local installer at a Carroll Gardens door

Many Carroll Gardens households already have a national alarm company’s panel, a Ring doorbell or a SimpliSafe kit. Those do their own jobs well, and for plenty of homes they are enough. What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Carroll Gardens

These are generalizations we hear repeated in quick online answers, forum advice and national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is set against what a Carroll Gardens door actually needs. We do not attribute any specific claim or figure to any one of those sources unless we name it.

“An electric lock can’t go on old double doors.”

This one comes from real experience. An owner on Brownstoner was told “there is no system to accomodate a buzzer” on a pair of glazed brownstone doors, and plenty of locksmiths still say the same, because the usual answer for a single door, an electric strike in the frame, has nowhere to go where two leaves meet.

The answer is to move the electricity. An electrified mortise lock in the active leaf, fed through a power transfer hinge, works on almost any sound wooden pair. Where the inactive leaf is deep and solid, a strike can go in its edge instead. Either way the doors look the same from the street.

What is true is that a double door costs more than a single one, needs its inactive leaf to be properly bolted, and takes more planning. That is a reason to quote carefully, not a reason to give up on the doors.

“Landmarks rules make a keypad impossible on a historic rowhouse.”

The Commission’s guidebook does not forbid card readers or keypads. It does not mention them at all. What it does say is that a door opening may carry one intercom panel and two light fixtures, placed within or next to the door or on a fence or wall in front of it, in a neutral and discreet design.

So the practical route on a district house is one combined panel that includes the reader or keypad, fixed in a mortar joint, with its cable taken straight back through the wall. Replacing an existing intercom in kind needs cut sheets but not drawings, and staff can approve installations that meet the criteria.

Where an owner wants something the guidance does not cover, such as a separate reader, the right step is to ask Commission staff before installing. We prepare the cut sheets and photos that conversation needs.

“Access control costs $1,500 to $3,500 a door, anywhere.”

That range appears in national guides; one 2026 guide gives $1,500 to $3,500 for a fully installed card or mobile system per door. It is not a bad starting point, and our own Brooklyn list for one controlled door, $1,500 to $2,500, sits inside it.

What a national range cannot see is the door. A single garden-level door with an open cellar ceiling under it is quick work. A pair of stoop doors that needs an electrified lock, a transfer hinge, a closer and new bolts on the inactive leaf is more. A combined video panel that replaces an old buzzer falls on a different line of the list altogether.

That is why we give a range on this page and a fixed figure only after the visit, in writing, with each door priced on its own line.

“A Wi-Fi smart lock is all a brownstone needs.”

For one household and one ordinary door, a good smart lock may well be enough, and we will say so. Many Carroll Gardens doorways are not that. A mortise lock in a heavy door rarely takes a retail smart lock without filling and re-boring, which is not something to do to an original door lightly.

Batteries are the other issue. National guides note that wireless locks bring annual battery replacement costs, and a lock that dies on a winter evening on a stoop is a lockout. A wired lock in the door runs from the controller and its backup battery in the cellar.

And in a condo brownstone, a smart lock usually means one app, one account and no separate credential for the renter on the third floor. The building needs a system that knows who is who.

“Buildings can go completely keyless now.”

Plenty of articles describe the keyless building as the future. In New York City it has been contested in court. amNY reported in 2019 that tenants in a Manhattan building sued after their landlord installed an app-based lock and stopped giving out mechanical keys, and noted that city rules still required a traditional key-based way in.

The city’s Tenant Data Privacy Law, which followed, bars owners of multiple dwellings from requiring smart access to enter a tenant’s own apartment. For a Carroll Gardens three-family or a small co-op, that settles it: keys stay as a backup.

We fit every electrified street door with a key override, and we keep the backup keys in very few hands so that the backup does not become the problem it replaced.

“Small buildings don’t have rules to worry about.”

A brownstone feels like a house, so owners are often surprised. Once three households live in it, it is a multiple dwelling, and a fob system on its door brings the Tenant Data Privacy Law with it: consent, a written policy, minimum data, and deletion of authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized.

The Building Code applies to the door whatever the size of the building: an electrically locked door must open from the inside, release on power loss where it uses a sensor, and use listed equipment. At eight apartments, the Multiple Dwelling Law adds the intercom with a remote release.

None of this is difficult to get right if it is planned for. It is much harder to fix after the fobs are already handed out.

“Access control stops burglaries.”

It stops some, and it changes the odds on others, but it is a door system. The Smith and Ninth Street Deli burglars came through the roof, and no reader on the back door would have changed that. A yoga studio on Columbia Street lost its waiting-room furniture to a thief who walked through a door left unlocked.

What access control does well is make doors lock every time, show you when one has not, keep keys from multiplying and put an end date on every outsider’s access. In a neighborhood where the reported break-ins often involve an unlocked door, that is a real improvement.

For roofs, skylights and rear windows, the answer is an alarm and sometimes physical hardening. We will tell you which parts of your property a door system covers and which it does not.

Our Installation Process

The same eight steps cover a single garden-level door and a co-op with three controlled doors. What changes in Carroll Gardens is the second and fifth steps: who has to approve the work, and how much the old doors need before any electronics go on them.

1. Photos of each door and the address

A photo of each door from the stoop and from inside, close-ups of the hinges and bolts on any pair, the address and who uses each door, so we arrive knowing the doors.

2. District check and who signs

We check whether the lot is in the historic district, and who has to approve: the owner, the condo association, the co-op board under its alteration rules, or the landlord for a shop.

3. Site visit

A technician checks each leaf, hinge, bolt and frame, the glass, the vestibule, the cellar for a cable route and a controller spot, and the nearest power for the supply.

4. Written door plan, cut sheets and Brooklyn price

A door-by-door plan naming the lock, the panel, how the cable crosses into the door, what each door does in a power cut and who gets which credential, with manufacturer cut sheets and a price from the Brooklyn list.

5. Doors made to close and latch first

Before any electronics, each door is adjusted to swing shut and latch on its own: rehung, freed, given a closer, and the inactive leaf’s bolts repaired so the pair holds.

6. Cable, power transfer and controller

Cable fished up from the cellar to the doorway, across the hinge through a transfer hinge or loop into the door, and the controller and battery in a locked enclosure on a cellar wall.

7. Credentials and schedules

Fobs, codes and phone keys per person, end dates for renters and contractors, schedules for cleaners, an account for each unit owner, and alerts for doors left open or unlocked.

8. Test with the power off and hand over

Every door tested on battery and with all power removed to prove the inside lever always opens it, then two administrator logins and a one-page guide for the owner or board.

Twice a year: a five-minute door check

When the clocks change, open the stoop doors and let them close by themselves: does the active leaf latch, and are the bolts on the other leaf still thrown? Do the same with the vestibule door and the garden-level door. Check the battery level in the app before the first cold week, because old wooden doors swell and stick in damp weather and shrink in the cold. Read the user list and remove anyone who no longer needs access: a renter who moved, last summer’s contractor, a cleaner who stopped coming. If a door drags, a call now is cheaper than a lockout later.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Push on the Other Leaf First

This describes how we approach a pair of stoop doors, not any particular customer or house. Before we look at the lock, the panel or the cellar, we close both leaves, set the bolts on the inactive one, and push hard on the meeting edge from outside. On an old pair that has not been looked at in years, the doors often give: the top bolt no longer reaches past paint in its keeper, the bottom bolt has been cut short, or the inactive leaf has shrunk away from its frame. If the pair moves under a push, the most expensive electrified lock in the world will not secure it, and the first line of the quote is the bolts.

Then we open the active leaf and look at its hinges, because one of them will carry the wire. We check the edge for old lock bores and filled holes, because the channel to the lock has to pass between them. We check the gap at the meeting edge, because a wide gap lets a blade reach the latch. Only then do we go down to the cellar to find the route up to the frame, and only then do we talk about which panel goes on the stone. The order matters: a sound door with an ordinary lock is better than a weak door with a clever one.

FAQ

Carroll Gardens Access Control FAQ

Is a double-door brownstone entrance priced higher than a single door on the Brooklyn list?
+

It falls on the same line, a single controlled door at $1,500 to $2,500, but usually toward the top of it, because a pair often needs an electrified lock in the door, a power transfer hinge and work on the inactive leaf’s bolts rather than a simple strike in the frame. If the doors need rehanging or a closer first, that is quoted on its own line. Sales tax is added, and the written figure is fixed after the visit.

How long does the cable that crosses from the frame into the door last before it needs replacing?
+

A power transfer hinge carries the wires inside the hinge knuckle, where they are protected and flex very little, and on a residential door it normally lasts as long as the hinge. An armored door loop is exposed to more bending and is the part we inspect first on any service visit. On heavy doors that are slammed or propped often, a loop can wear through over years, which shows up as a lock that works only when the door is held a certain way.

Can the second leaf of our double doors still be opened wide for moving furniture?
+

Yes. The electrified lock is in the active leaf, and the inactive leaf is still held only by its top and bottom bolts, which you withdraw by hand from inside as before. Once the move is done, close the inactive leaf, throw both bolts and close the active leaf against it. If you use automatic flush bolts, they release when the active leaf is opened and throw again when it closes.

Can our wooden doors come off for stripping and refinishing after they have been wired?
+

Yes. We fit a connector at the hinge end of the door cable so the door can be unplugged and lifted off its hinges without cutting anything, and we label it. Tell your refinisher the door has a lock with a cable inside and that the channel runs from the hinge edge, so they do not drill or plane that edge. When the door goes back up, plug it in and test it, or ask us to.

Can tapping a fob once at the outer doors also release the inner vestibule door?
+

Yes. With both doors on one controller, a resident’s fob can release the outer doors and then the inner door a few seconds later, so they walk straight through. Visitors are treated differently: a resident buzzing someone in from upstairs can choose to release the outer doors only, so the visitor waits in the vestibule until someone comes down or releases the inner door as well.

Can the street door be set to stay unlocked during a broker’s open house and relock afterward?
+

Yes. An administrator can schedule the street door to stay unlocked for the hours of an open house and relock automatically at the end, or give the listing broker a phone key that works only on those days. In a condo or co-op, the other owners should be told in advance, and we recommend the inner vestibule door stays on fobs so visitors reach only the unit being shown.

Can residents keep the fobs they already carry when the new panel goes in?
+

Sometimes. If the existing fobs are an encrypted type that the new panel’s reader supports, they can be enrolled and keep working. If they are the older 125 kHz kind, which can be copied with cheap equipment, we recommend replacing them; the new reader can often read both during a short changeover so nobody is locked out on the first day.

Why can an electrified lock for an old door take longer to arrive than a standard strike?
+

Old doors are not standard. An electrified mortise lock has to match the door’s thickness, the distance from the edge to the lever, the hand of the door and often a trim style, and the right combination is not always on a supplier’s shelf. We tell you before the deposit whether a part is special order, order it after the deposit, and book the installation once it is in hand rather than promising a date we do not control.

Will an electric strike work with the old brass rim lock on our inner door?
+

Often, yes. A rim lock is mounted on the inside face of the door with its latch catching a keeper on the frame, and there are electric strikes made to replace that keeper. The lock stays, the key still works, and the latch is released electrically. If the rim lock is worn or its latch is loose, it is better to replace it with a sound rim or mortise lock before electrifying it.

We throw the old surface bolt on our doors at night. Does that defeat the system?
+

It locks out every fob and code while it is thrown, which is fine if everyone is home and nobody is expected. It also means a family member coming home late cannot get in, and a door contact will not tell you whether the bolt is set. If the bolt is part of your routine, we can fit a sensor that reports it, or set the electric lock to a night mode that accepts only family credentials.

Who patches and paints after the wiring goes in?
+

We plan routes so nothing finished has to be opened: up from the cellar, through mortar joints and hinge edges, and along the cellar ceiling. Where a small opening cannot be avoided, we tell you before the job starts. We do not patch plaster or paint, so if your doors or vestibule are being refinished anyway, it is cheapest to schedule the access work first and the painter after.

Does the panel’s camera count against the Landmarks limit on cameras?
+

The guidebook sets separate limits for intercom panels and for cameras, and it does not say whether a camera built into an intercom panel counts as a camera. We treat that as a question for Commission staff and raise it in the application when a district house already has cameras on its front. In most cases the panel is the only device near the door and the question does not arise.
