The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Park South Properties Need Alarm Installation

Prospect Park South was planned before it was built. In 1899 Dean Alvord bought farmland below Prospect Park, laid the streets and utilities, marked the entrances with brick piers carrying "PPS" plaques, and had the landscape gardener John Aitkin plant the lawns and malls before he sold a single lot. He called it rus in urbe, the country in the city. The landmarked district that survives covers 47 acres, bounded by Church Avenue on the north, the open cut of the BMT Brighton Line on the east, Beverley Road on the south and, on the west, the blocks between Stratford Road and Coney Island Avenue. The city designated it a historic district on February 8, 1979, and it went onto the National Register of Historic Places on July 21, 1983. The Prospect Park South Association describes it as about two hundred homes, two blocks long from Church Avenue to Beverley Road and four blocks wide from Stratford Road to Buckingham Road, reached on the B and Q at Church Avenue or Beverley Road.

Alvord's rules are what make the alarm question here different. According to Wikipedia, every house had to be a substantial freestanding home of more than 3,500 square feet costing over $5,000. The Brooklyn Eagle adds that houses were set back 39 feet from the sidewalk, with 8 feet of planting strip between the street and the walk, and that by 1905 almost every plot was built on. Trees went in at the building line as well as the curb, partly, Wikipedia says, "to block out adjoining houses, and provide the illusion that each house was the only one on the block." The architects, John J. Petit chief among them, worked in everything from Queen Anne to a Japanese-inspired house on Buckingham Road.

The result is a house of unusual size standing alone, with porches, side and cellar doors and often a garage off a driveway, its neighbors deliberately screened from view. Brownstoner's October 2026 report on 1305 Albemarle Road gives the upper end: more than 11,000 square feet, nine bedrooms over four floors, a former top-floor ballroom, multiple porches and balconies, and, in the 1910 census, two live-in servants. A 2025 listing of 194 Rugby Road describes a nine-bedroom house of about 5,600 square feet that one family kept for three generations. Few households today have staff living in, and the gap between the size of the house and the size of the household is where most of what follows comes from.

It is also where the burglaries have come from, for a very long time. Brownstoner's 2014 history of 1306 Albemarle Road, the John S. Eakins house, says that as "a wealthy neighborhood full of large expensive houses," Prospect Park South "was a natural target for thieves," especially in summer, when most families left for their summer homes. In the summer of 1920 a gang of three burglars under the age of 20 broke into at least five houses in daylight while the owners were out of town, taking jewelry and silver and wrecking rooms; they were caught in the act at a house down the block after a caretaker and gardener at another property noticed them. In the summer of 1935 another spree hit a number of houses, and by then, the article says, the neighborhood had a private security patrol, which "didn't catch these guys for a while." In 1946 the house was burgled again, this time with the owner, a chief assistant district attorney, losing $1,500 in jewelry and cash.

The recent record is smaller but sharper. On a Sunday in February 2024, just before 3 p.m., masked men forced their way into the Wat Samakki Buddhist Temple, which NBC New York describes as "a residential house on Rugby Road in Prospect Park South that was converted into a temple"; one brandished a firearm, and the group left with cellphones and cash from a donation box. The NYPD's chief of detectives said they "kick in the back door of the temple." In May 2018, Bklyner reported, police answering a burglary call on Stratford Road shortly before 6 p.m. found the 66-year-old homeowner fatally injured in his basement and a suspect hiding in a closet. And not every threat is a person. In November 2012 a fire badly damaged the house at 80 Rugby Road; the Brooklyn Eagle reported two years later that the owners were still waiting to move back. Wikipedia records that Alvord's own mansion at 1522 Albemarle Road burned down around 1955. In a big wooden house, the alarm is also the thing most likely to notice smoke on a floor where nobody is.

None of this makes Prospect Park South unusually dangerous; the 70th Precinct, which the NYPD says is "home to Midwood, Fiske Terrace, Ditmas Park, and Prospect Park South," covers a large swath of Flatbush, and we do not quote its numbers as if they described these few blocks. What the record shows is a pattern: entries at the back, entries while the family is away, and trouble in parts of the house nobody is in. A camera at the back door and the driveway usually belongs in the same plan, which we describe on our Prospect Park South security camera installation page, and many owners add a code lock on the kitchen or side door through access control in Prospect Park South so that sitters, cleaners and contractors stop carrying keys.

Prospect Park South at a glance, from the sources we read

  • Houses: about two hundred; the association counts two hundred and six homes in Alvord's 1899 plan.
  • Size rule: freestanding houses of more than 3,500 square feet, set back 39 feet from the sidewalk behind an 8-foot planting strip (Wikipedia; Brooklyn Eagle, July 27, 2025).
  • Land: about 60 acres bought in 1899 (Wikipedia) or 40 acres from the Dutch Reformed Church and the Luther Voorhies estate (Brooklyn Eagle); we give both because the sources differ.
  • Precinct: the NYPD lists Prospect Park South under the 70th, whose station house is on Lawrence Avenue.
  • Transit: B and Q trains at Church Avenue and Beverley Road, the two stations the association tells visitors to use.
Our Approach Here

Too Big to Watch by Eye: An Alarm Built Around the Rooms Nobody Is In

In a small apartment the people inside hear almost everything. In a Prospect Park South house that stops being true somewhere around the second staircase: from a second-floor bedroom you cannot hear the kitchen door, from the kitchen you cannot hear the third floor, and nobody hears the cellar. Most home alarms assume the household covers the inside by ear and only needs the outside watched at night or when away. In a house of 5,000 or 11,000 square feet, that leaves most rooms unwatched most of the time.

So we start from the household, not the floor plan: which rooms are used daily, which floors stay shut, who travels and for how long, and who nearby could come with a key. The answers sort the house into three layers. None of this is a product; it is how we program an ordinary hybrid or wireless panel for a house this size.

Layer one: the shell

Every outside door, cellar door and bulkhead, every first-floor window and every upper window reachable from a porch roof, balcony, bay or garage gets a contact. In these houses that is a long list, on all four sides, since there is no party wall to leave out. The shell is armed at night and when the house is empty.

Layer two: the everyday rooms

The kitchen, the sitting room, the main hall and the main stair are where people move all day. Motion here is armed only when the house is empty, or at night on the ground floor while the bedroom floor stays off. Each detector is named by its room, not a number.

Layer three: the rooms at rest

This is the layer most houses here need and most alarms never get. The top floor that is used for guests twice a year, the bedrooms of children who have moved out, a library nobody sits in, the attic, the far end of the cellar: rooms that nobody in the household will enter on an ordinary day. We set them up as a separate area of the panel with its own detectors, usually a contact on the door or stair that leads into them and a motion detector inside. That area stays armed while the family is at home and moving about the rest of the house, and it is disarmed deliberately, with a code or the app, on the days someone does want to go up. If anyone else crosses into it, the household hears about it at once, from a sounder on the floor they are on and on their phones, even in the middle of a Sunday afternoon.

An intruder who gets in at the back of a large house has to cross a boundary the household never crosses. That boundary is the cheapest, most reliable witness a big house can have.

Five rules we apply to every house this size

  • Every alert names a floor and a room. "Third floor, back bedroom, motion" tells you something; "Zone 23" does not. We write the names with you during the survey.
  • A sounder on every floor people sleep or sit on. The panel's own speaker is rarely heard two floors away; a small voice sounder on each occupied floor says which door opened, and the siren is reserved for real alarms.
  • Boundary doors are contacts, not just motion. The door to the top-floor stair, the door down to the cellar and the door into a closed wing get their own contacts, so the rest area is entered through a door the system is watching.
  • Entry delay only where you actually come in. The door the family uses gets a short countdown; every other door, and the rooms at rest, report without delay.
  • Somebody local is on the list. A neighbor, a relative or a caretaker who lives within a few minutes is named as a keyholder, with a code that works and instructions on what to do, because a central station can call the police but cannot walk round the back of your house.

The back of the house is the front door for a burglar

Alvord's 39-foot setbacks put every front door in full view of the road. The back is the opposite: kitchen, service and cellar doors face yards screened by plantings, garages and neighboring houses. When robbers went into the Rugby Road temple in February 2024, police said they kicked in the back door. A contact alone reports that door only once the intruders are inside.

So back and side doors here usually get a contact, a shock sensor that reports a blow hard enough to break a lock, and a motion detector just inside, plus glass-break where there is glass. A forced door then reports as an impact, an opening and a person. The front door, watched by the whole street, rarely needs more than a contact and a doorbell camera.

The setback works the other way at the front: anyone on the walk is in plain view of the road, so the front is where a camera earns its place and the alarm can start at the porch.

Away for weeks: the caretaker, the patrol and the central station

The two oldest sprees in the district's record came while families were away for the summer. In 1920 the gang was caught after a caretaker and gardener at another property noticed them; in 1935 a private patrol did not catch the next gang for some time. A patrol drives past; a caretaker knows which house should be empty.

An alarm cannot replace a person who notices, but it can tell that person where to look. For a long absence we build an alert ladder: the household's phones, then a nearby keyholder who has agreed to go and look, then a staffed central station if you choose monitoring. Each rung hears different things; a back door at three in the morning goes to everyone, a low battery goes only to the owner.

If an alarm reports movement inside after a break-in, the keyholder watches from the street and waits for the police. The May 2018 Stratford Road case, in which police found a suspect hiding in a closet, is a reminder that someone may still be in the house.

Fire on a floor nobody is on

The Historic Districts Council describes Victorian Flatbush, of which Prospect Park South is a part, as known for the largest concentration of wooden Victorian-style homes in the country. The restorers of 1306 Albemarle Road uncovered its original clapboard and shingles, and the 1305 Albemarle Road listing advertises wood-burning fireplaces. A smoke alarm that only beeps in an empty attic protects nobody. On a panel, every smoke, heat and carbon monoxide detector reports like a door: it sounds through the house, names its floor and room on every phone on the list, and, with monitoring, goes to a central station that can call the fire department even if the house is empty. The 2012 fire at 80 Rugby Road and the loss of Alvord's own mansion around 1955 are the district's reminders of what fire does to these houses.

Monitored fire devices on a burglar panel are a household safety measure, not a code-required fire alarm system for a multiple dwelling; when a building needs the latter, our Prospect Park South fire alarm installation team handles it.

Event days on Albemarle Road

Some days the district fills up. NYC DOT's 2026 Open Streets listing closes Albemarle Road between Marlborough and Argyle Roads to cars on Friday evenings and weekends from April 15 to December 31, with the Prospect Park South Association as partner, and its Trick-or-Streets listing closes the road on Halloween evening. When everyone is at the front, the back of the house and the rooms at rest are least watched, which is why we set up an event mode.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Prospect Park South's Architecture

No two houses in the district are alike, but they share the size rule and the freestanding lot. The types below are named in the sources we read; the houses mentioned are cited for their published descriptions, not houses we have worked in.

Queen Anne and Shingle-style houses with towers

Wikipedia lists Queen Anne houses by John J. Petit at 1423 and 1501 Albemarle Road and a Shingle-style house at 115 Buckingham Road. Brownstoner describes the 1905 Eakins house at 1306 Albemarle, at the corner of Argyle Road, as a twelve-room house with a shingled turret and a wraparound porch that is one of the longest in the neighborhood. Long porches put several doors and many windows under one roof.

Colonial and Classical Revival mansions

The Gale house at 1305 Albemarle Road (Henry B. Moore, 1905) has two-story Ionic columns at the entrance, multiple porches and balconies and four floors; Wikipedia also lists Colonial Revival houses at 15 Stratford Road (1905) and 1510 Albemarle Road (1900), and a Classical Revival house at 104 Buckingham Road.

Tudor Revival and Prairie-influenced houses

The Herbert Krantz house at 183 Argyle Road (1904) is listed as Tudor Revival, and 1519 Albemarle Road (1902) as Prairie School and Neo-Jacobean, both by Petit.

The exotic houses: chalet, villa and the Japanese house

A Swiss chalet at 101 Rugby Road (John E. Nitchie, 1900), an Italian villa at 143 Buckingham Road (Walter S. Cassin, 1906) and the Frederick S. Kolle house at 131 Buckingham Road (Petit and Green, 1902–03), the Japanese-inspired house that City Neighborhoods describes as an exotic landmark of wood and terra cotta. Unusual windows mean more glass-break and motion coverage.

Houses converted to other uses

NBC New York describes the Wat Samakki Buddhist Temple as a house on Rugby Road converted into a temple; Wikipedia records a mosque at 1325 Albemarle Road from 1972; Brownstoner notes a doctor's office removed from 1306 Albemarle in 2009. Converted houses need a schedule for open hours.

Apartment buildings at the edges

Wikipedia lists The Sovereign at 55 Parade Place, a large 1927 residential building across from the Parade Ground, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported a May 2025 fire in a six-story apartment building at 1409 Albemarle Road that drew more than 60 firefighters. In buildings like these the alarm stops at the apartment door; the building's own systems are the owner's.

Old walls, old wood and Landmarks rules

We reuse cable an earlier alarm left in the walls where it tests good, run new cable through the cellar and closets, and use wireless devices with repeaters where cable would mean opening finished rooms. Everything lives indoors. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook says permits are needed for work affecting the exterior of a building in a historic district, including work in front, side and rear yards, and for interior work only when it needs a Department of Buildings permit or affects the exterior. An indoor alarm normally needs neither. Anything that goes on the outside, such as a camera or a doorbell station, follows the Commission's staff rules for small equipment, which keep it off the main facade where possible, small, attached at joints or plain material, and with little or no visible conduit.

Rules that apply to alarms here

  • Fifteen minutes, then silence. Under § 24-238 of the city's Administrative Code, an audible alarm on a building has to cut itself off within a quarter of an hour; our sirens are timed to stop sooner.
  • Who may install. Paid alarm work in New York needs a Department of State installer license; ours is printed on each quote.
  • Landmarks. An indoor alarm normally needs no Landmarks permit; exterior cameras, doorbell stations and conduit follow the Commission's small-equipment rules described above.
  • Detectors in rented units. The city's Housing Preservation and Development rules put smoke detector duties on owners of multiple dwellings and require carbon monoxide detectors in rented one- and two-family homes with fuel-burning appliances, within fifteen feet of sleeping rooms. A house with a rented apartment inside it has those duties in the apartment.
  • Street events. On Open Streets and Trick-or-Streets days, the road in front may be closed to cars; we schedule installs and service visits around those hours.
Your Questions Answered

Alarm Installation in Prospect Park South: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners of large freestanding houses ask us most, grouped by the part of the problem they belong to. Each is answered in full here, and none is repeated in the shorter FAQ further down.

A house bigger than the household

There are two of us in a nine-bedroom house, and most days we use the kitchen, one sitting room and our bedroom. Should the rooms we never go into be armed differently from the rooms we live in?

Yes, and in a house this size it is the most valuable change you can make. We would put the unused bedrooms, the top floor and any closed-off rooms into their own area of the panel, with a contact on the door or stair leading into them and a motion detector inside. That area stays armed day and night while the two of you go about the rest of the house with the everyday rooms off. If anyone crosses into it, a voice sounder on the floor you are on names the room, and your phones get the same alert, whether it is two in the afternoon or two in the morning.

When one of you wants to go up, you disarm that area alone from a keypad or the app and re-arm it on the way out, or let it re-arm itself after an hour. It costs little more than a conventional layout; what changes is the programming.

Our top floor is a former ballroom we open for parties a few times a year and otherwise leave shut. What should the alarm do with a floor that is empty almost every day and full of guests on a handful of nights?

Treat it as a rest area with a calendar. On ordinary days the top floor is armed with everything else that is closed: a contact on the door at the foot of its stair and a motion detector covering the room and any mezzanine or back stair. On the nights you entertain, you disarm that area before guests arrive, and the panel can remind you at the end of the evening if it is still off at a set hour.

On party nights we can leave the bedroom floor and the cellar armed while guests use the ballroom and the main stair, and the top floor needs its own smoke detector and sounder so an alert is heard over the music. Brownstoner describes the top floor at 1305 Albemarle as a former ballroom with a spiral stair to a mezzanine; a mezzanine like that needs its own coverage, because a detector on the floor below will not see up there.

Besides the main stair, our house has a narrow second staircase that comes up from beside the kitchen. Does a second staircase change where the motion detectors go?

It does. A motion detector on the main stair sees anyone going up that way, but a second staircase from beside the kitchen bypasses it, so someone who comes in at the back can reach the bedrooms without crossing the main hall.

We cover it with its own motion detector at the top, aimed down the flight, or a contact on a door the family keeps shut. At night anyone coming up it is announced; if it continues to the top floor, that landing joins the rest area.

Upstairs there is a balcony door that nobody has opened in years. Can it be on the alarm all the time instead of being armed and disarmed with the rest of the house?

Yes. A door that the household has decided never to use can be programmed as a zone that reports whenever it opens, whatever mode the rest of the system is in, much as panic buttons and fire devices are always live. If that door opens on a quiet weekday with the alarm otherwise off, you hear it on the floor you are on and on your phones. We do this for balcony and porch-roof doors, the old doctor's-office entrance that some houses still have, and cellar doors to the yard that have been bolted for years.

If the door ever needs to open for a repair, the zone is bypassed first, with the bypass set to expire on its own. And a door nobody opens is often one whose lock nobody has checked in years, so we look at the door itself too.

Doors the street cannot see

The robbers at the Rugby Road temple in 2024 got in by kicking in the back door. What should the back door of a big freestanding house have that a door contact alone does not give it?

When a door is kicked in, its contact reports at the moment the intruders are inside. We add a shock sensor on the frame, which reports the blow, often seconds before the door gives, and a motion detector just inside, which confirms someone came in, plus glass-break where the door has glass.

Timing matters too. If you come and go by another door, the back door should have no entry delay at all; if it is your everyday door, keep the delay short and let the shock sensor report at once. A strong strike plate and long screws into the frame are part of the same answer.

Our house sits nearly forty feet back from the sidewalk behind an open lawn. Is it worth detecting someone on the lawn or the front walk, or should the alarm start at the house?

For most houses we start the alarm at the house. Outdoor sensors on an open lawn see dogs, squirrels, leaves and Halloween crowds; they need mounts and cable in yards that Landmarks permit rules cover; and a lawn alert at 2 a.m. rarely tells you anything you can act on.

What works better is to make the porch the first line, with a camera on the walk and steps and contacts on everything under the porch roof. Where an owner wants detection outside at the back, out of public view, an outdoor sensor along the path to the kitchen door can chime and record rather than sound the siren.

Away for weeks: an old story here

In 1920 a caretaker at a neighboring house spotted the burglars. When we travel, who should play that part today, and what should they be able to see and do?

Someone who lives within a few minutes, knows the house and has agreed in advance to go and look: a neighbor, a relative, a house manager or a regular cleaner. We set that person up as a keyholder with their own code, which can be limited to the days you are away and to the door they will use, and their own app login that shows alarms, fire and water alerts and nothing else. They do not need to see your daily comings and goings when you are home.

Their job is written on a card: on an alarm, look from the street and call the police if anything is wrong, never going inside after a break-in; on a fire alert, call 911; on a trouble message, tell the owner. With monitoring, the central station calls them after you, so they hear even if your phone is off on a plane.

A house sitter will stay in one guest room while we are away for a month. Can the rest of the house stay armed around her?

Yes, and it is a better arrangement than giving a sitter the whole house. We would set up the sitter's room, the bathroom she uses, the kitchen and the route between them and the door she comes in by as the area she can arm and disarm with her own code. Everything else, including the other bedrooms, the top floor, the cellar and the rooms with valuables, stays armed for the whole month as a separate area her code cannot touch.

Her code stops working the day you return. If she needs an armed room, she calls you and you disarm that area from your phone. Because her part of the house is on the same panel, a smoke alarm in any room reaches her as well as you.

Some neighbors have talked about paying for a private patrol, as the neighborhood had in the 1930s. Would a patrol car on the roads do the job of a monitored alarm?

It does a different job. A patrol can be seen, can deter someone casing a block and can respond to a call, but it cannot see the back door of a house set behind a lawn and trees, and it cannot know that the third floor of a particular house is supposed to be empty. Brownstoner's history of 1306 Albemarle notes that the neighborhood had a private patrol in 1935 and that it "didn't catch these guys for a while" during a spree of burglaries that summer.

The two can work together: a patrol's dispatcher can sit on a house's call list as a keyholder and check the outside when an alarm comes in. We would not drop central station monitoring for a patrol, because the station is what dispatches police and the fire department at night.

Fire and the floors nobody is on

A fire badly damaged a house on Rugby Road in 2012. Should detectors on the floors nobody uses report to someone outside the house, rather than only sounding inside?

In a big house, yes. A stand-alone smoke alarm in an attic or a closed top floor is only useful if somebody hears it, and in a house where the family lives on the first two floors, that is far from certain, and impossible when the house is empty. A detector connected to the alarm panel sounds through the house, sends the alert with its floor and room to every phone on the list, and, with monitoring, reaches a central station that can send the fire department to an empty house.

We put monitored smoke detectors on every level, heat detectors in the attic and boiler room, and carbon monoxide detectors near bedrooms and the boiler; smoke alarms required in a rented unit stay in place alongside them. The Brooklyn Eagle reported that the owners of 80 Rugby Road were still waiting to move back two years after the fire. In a wooden house, minutes matter.

We light the wood-burning fireplaces all winter. Will smoke from a fireplace set off a monitored smoke detector in the same room?

It can, if the detector is in the wrong place or the chimney draws badly when the fire is first lit. We keep smoke detectors out of the immediate path of smoke that puffs back from a fireplace opening, usually by placing the one for that floor in the hall outside the room rather than above the mantel, and we use photoelectric detectors, which respond to visible smoke from a smoldering fire. In a room where a fireplace is used constantly and a detector must be there, a heat detector is the better choice for that room, with a smoke detector just outside.

If a fireplace does trip a monitored detector, the central station calls before dispatching and you cancel with your passcode. A fireplace that regularly puts smoke into the room deserves a chimney inspection, and carbon monoxide detectors stay on the bedroom floors because a blocked flue cannot be seen or smelled.

Event days on Albemarle Road

On Halloween our stretch of Albemarle Road is closed for trick-or-treating and hundreds of people come up our walk between four and eight. Can the front door chime while the rest of the house stays armed?

Yes. The city's Trick-or-Streets listing closes Albemarle Road from Buckingham Road to Stratford Road from 4 to 8 p.m. on Saturday, October 31, 2026, so the front of the house will be busy for those four hours and the household will be at the front door. We set up an event mode for exactly that: the front door and the front hall chime but do not alarm, while the side and back doors, the cellar, the bedroom floor and every room at rest stay fully armed. The doorbell camera records the porch as usual.

The same mode works for open-street weekends, garden tours and parties, on a schedule if you like. When everyone is at the front, the back of a big house is the least watched it will be all year; anyone who uses the crowd as cover meets an armed kitchen door.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Prospect Park South

We could not open Google's AI Overview or similar AI summaries from the environment this page was written in, so nothing below is quoted from one. These are claims that circulate widely in generic alarm advice and on lead-generation sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr, set against what a house in this district actually needs.

"A typical home needs a handful of door and window sensors"

Starter kits are sized for an average house, and the advice that goes with them assumes a few doors and perhaps ten windows. A Prospect Park South house was built under a rule that it exceed 3,500 square feet, and many are far larger: 1305 Albemarle Road is reported at more than 11,000 square feet over four floors. Freestanding on all four sides, with porches, balconies, cellars and sometimes two stairs, these houses routinely have three or four times the openings a kit assumes.

A kit that runs out of capacity forces choices about which windows go unprotected, and a base station in the front hall may not hear the third floor. A survey counts openings first and sizes the panel to the count.

The other mistake is to think of the count as the plan. The count tells you how many sensors; the household tells you how to arm them. In a house this size, the second question decides whether the alarm is useful on an ordinary Tuesday.

"Arm it when you leave and when you go to bed; that's all an alarm is for"

That rule works in a house the family can hear from end to end. In a large house it leaves most rooms unwatched most of the day. The district's own record includes a robbery in daylight on a Sunday afternoon, through a back door, at a converted house on Rugby Road, and a 2018 burglary on Stratford Road shortly before 6 p.m.

The better rule here is that the rooms nobody uses stay armed all the time, the back and side doors announce themselves whenever they open, and the everyday rooms follow the family's day. That is a programming choice, not extra equipment.

An alert from an armed rest area is rarely a mistake, because the only people who enter it know to disarm it first.

"Outdoor motion sensors are the best first line of defense"

On a house with a small fenced yard, perhaps. Across the 39-foot front lawns and open side yards of this district, outdoor motion detectors mostly report squirrels, leaves, dogs, delivery drivers and the crowds on Albemarle Road during open-street weekends and Halloween. Alerts that are almost always nothing get ignored.

Exterior work in the historic district also follows Landmarks Preservation Commission rules that cover front, side and rear yards. We prefer a camera that records the walk and porch, with the alarm starting at the doors and windows of the house itself.

If anything goes outside, it belongs on the hidden route to the kitchen door.

"A smoke alarm in each bedroom is enough fire protection for a house"

Battery smoke alarms in bedrooms are a legal baseline in many places, but in a big house they protect only the people who can hear them. An attic, a top floor used twice a year or a cellar boiler room can burn for a long time before a beeping alarm upstairs is noticed, and not at all if the house is empty.

Detectors that report through the alarm panel to phones and a central station are what turn an empty floor's smoke into a phone call. The district has lost houses to fire before; Wikipedia records that Alvord's own mansion burned around 1955, and the Brooklyn Eagle reported that 80 Rugby Road was badly damaged in 2012.

None of that replaces the separate rules for rented apartments.

"Alarm installation costs a few hundred dollars"

Some starter packages do, and a small apartment can fall in that range. Our own Brooklyn list starts a basic system at $600 and a wireless system for a modest home at $600 to $1,200. A complete installation for a large house starts at $2,500, and custom hardwired systems run $2,000 to $6,000.

The difference is almost entirely the count of openings and the number of keypads, sounders and detectors a house of this size needs. We would rather say that plainly than quote a starter price that does not fit a nine-bedroom house.

The figure is written at the survey, line by line.

"Landmark status means the alarm needs city approval first"

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's Permit Guidebook ties permits to work that affects a building's exterior, including yards, or interior work that needs a Department of Buildings permit. An alarm installed indoors normally needs neither.

Exterior items such as cameras and doorbell stations follow the Commission's staff rules for small equipment, which keep them small, off the primary facade where possible and with little visible conduit. We did not find a New York City rule requiring homeowners to register a burglar alarm, and we do not charge for a permit that does not exist.

The rule that does exist concerns noise: a house alarm's siren may not run on for more than a quarter of an hour, and ours are programmed accordingly.

"Anyone with the right kit can install an alarm"

Self-installing a kit in your own home is legal. Installing for paying customers is not open to anyone: New York's Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers after coursework, an exam and fingerprinting, and their employees are registered too.

For a house of this age the license is the minimum. What matters as much is experience with plaster walls, old cable, original woodwork and houses that do not fit a kit, and a written plan for which rooms do what.

Before anyone drills, a house this old deserves to see both the license and a plan on paper.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

An apartment and a four-floor house follow the same seven steps; in a big house the survey simply covers more floors.

1. Talk about the household first

A phone call about who lives in the house, which rooms are used every day and which stay shut, how often you travel and who nearby could hold a key, followed by photos of the back door, the cellar entrance and the stair to the top floor.

2. Survey every floor

A technician walks the house with you, starting at the door you use, then every room, both stairs where there are two, the attic, the whole cellar and the garage, asking at each door who opens it and when.

3. A one-page plan of layers

Before any equipment is chosen, the plan lists the shell, the everyday rooms, the rest areas, the never-used doors, the fire devices and who hears which alert while you are home and while you are away.

4. Written price the same day

The count from the survey becomes a written quote off the Brooklyn list, normally handed over at the end of the same visit.

5. Install with the house in mind

Cable runs through the cellar, closets and existing chases; wireless devices with repeaters reach the places cable cannot; sounders go on every occupied floor; nothing is glued to restored woodwork.

6. Program areas, names and the ladder

Each area, zone and sounder is named and tested room by room, the modes are set, and the alert ladder is tested with your phones and, if you wish, the keyholder on the line.

7. Hand over, then review after your first trip

Each person gets a code or the app and the keyholder gets a card; after your first long absence we go through the log together and adjust anything that alerted too much or too little.

Before every long trip: a ten-minute check

Put the system in test mode. Open the back door, the cellar door and the door to the top-floor stair one at a time and listen for each name on the floor you are on. Press the test button on one smoke detector per floor. Confirm in the app that the keyholder's code and alerts are active for the dates you are away, and that the central station has the right numbers. Then arm the rest areas and the shell, and leave by the door with the entry delay.

FAQ

Prospect Park South Alarm Installation FAQ

What does an alarm for a large freestanding Prospect Park South house cost on your Brooklyn list?
+

Most houses in the district start at the complete-home line, from $2,500, and rise with the count: a freestanding house has openings on all four sides, often two or three porch or balcony doors, a full cellar and three or four floors. A first stage covering the back and side doors, the cellar and one rest area can fit the wireless range of $600 to $1,200, and a house whose old contacts still test good can start with a panel replacement at $800 to $1,500. Fire devices, keypads and sounders are their own lines, and the total is written after one visit, plus sales tax.

Can one control panel handle a house with forty or more doors and windows, or will we outgrow it?
+

One panel can, if it is chosen for the house rather than for an average one. The hybrid and wireless panels we fit in large houses are built for many more zones, several separately armed areas and dozens of user codes than a small house needs. We count every opening, detector, keypad and sounder at the survey, choose a panel with room to spare, and add repeaters so the far floors report reliably.

Clients come to a home office on our ground floor on weekdays. Can that room and its entrance be armed separately from the rest of the house?
+

Yes. The office and its entrance become their own area of the panel, with their own codes for you and any staff, armed and disarmed on their own schedule. Clients come and go through the office door during working hours without the rest of the house being disarmed, and the door from the office into the family rooms gets a contact so the household knows if anyone wanders through. Staff codes arm and disarm the office only.

If we later send the original doors or window sashes out to be restored, what happens to the alarm contacts on them?
+

Surface contacts and their magnets come off with a couple of screws each and go back on the same marks when the door or sash returns, and we label each one with its zone so it goes back to the right opening. While the joinery is away, the opening is usually boarded or fitted with a temporary door, and a temporary wireless contact or a motion detector can cover it so the house stays protected. Recessed contacts set into a door edge are best removed by us before the restorer starts, so the holes are recorded and reused rather than filled and drilled again.

Will the voice sounder on our bedroom floor announce every door in the house, or can each floor hear only what matters to it?
+

Each sounder can be assigned to the parts of the house that matter on its floor. A common arrangement is that the ground-floor sounder announces the front door as a chime, the bedroom-floor sounder stays quiet for everyday doors but speaks for the back door, the cellar door and any rest area at night, and the top-floor sounder speaks only when someone enters that floor. Real alarms, fire and carbon monoxide sound on every floor.

How long should a new panel and its sensors last in a house we expect to keep for decades?
+

Wired contacts and cable, once installed and labeled, can serve for decades and outlive several panels. Wireless sensors last for many years, with battery changes every few years, and the app reports a weak battery well before it fails. Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors carry a replace-by date from the manufacturer, which we note in your zone list. The part most likely to need replacing early is the cellular communicator, when carriers retire older networks, as happened when 3G service was shut down.

We heat only part of the house in winter and close off the rest. Will motion detectors in cold, closed rooms cause false alarms?
+

Usually the opposite. Passive infrared motion detectors look for the warmth of a body moving against the background, and a cold, empty room gives them a clearer contrast than a warm one. The problems we watch for are different: a radiator, heater or sunny window inside the detector's view, which can produce warm air moving across it, and very cold attics, where battery life shortens and condensation can form. We aim detectors away from heat sources, use lithium batteries and sealed housings in unheated spaces, and choose dual-technology detectors for rooms where heat cycles cannot be avoided.

A painter will work in two bedrooms for a week. Can those rooms be left off the alarm that week while the rest of the house is armed every night?
+

Yes. The sensors in those two rooms can be bypassed for the week, so the painter can work and leave windows open for ventilation while every other part of the house arms as usual at night. A bypass can be set to end on a given date, so the rooms come back on their own when the job is done, and the keypad and app show the bypassed zones whenever you arm, so nothing is forgotten.
