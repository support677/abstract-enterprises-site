The Neighborhood

Why Prospect Heights Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Prospect Heights is a neighborhood of front doors that sit above the sidewalk, back gardens that sit out of sight and a historic district that decides how much of the front a camera may occupy. Wikipedia bounds it by Flatbush Avenue on the west, Atlantic Avenue on the north, Washington Avenue on the east and Eastern Parkway, starting at Grand Army Plaza, on the south. The area was mostly farm and woodland until the middle of the nineteenth century; according to the 6tocelebrate history project, the land between Sterling Place and Prospect Place was sold off in the 1880s and built up with the row houses that now make up most of the historic district, while the strip between Eastern Parkway and St. John's Place was tied up in title litigation and stayed empty until well after 1900. The Brooklyn Paper and CityLand describe Neo-Grec row houses from the 1860s and 1870s, Romanesque and Renaissance Revival houses from the 1880s and 1890s and larger mixed-use buildings along Flatbush Avenue. Brownstones and townhouses went up on the side streets and apartment buildings on the avenues, and one guide places the densest rows on Park Place, Prospect Place, St. Marks Avenue and Underhill Avenue. Wikipedia adds larger apartment buildings on Lincoln Place and St. Johns Place between Underhill and Washington. It is ZIP 11217 and 11238 and Community District 8.

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Prospect Heights Historic District on June 23, 2009. The Historic Districts Council counts about 850 buildings, mostly single-family row houses and apartment buildings, and Wikipedia gives its rough limits as Flatbush Avenue, Sterling Place, Washington Avenue and St. Marks Avenue, with one section reaching north to Pacific Street. Sources rank it differently: CityLand reported at designation that it would be Brooklyn's largest district and the city's largest since 1990, and Wikipedia now calls it the fifth largest in New York City. Either way, most houses in the neighborhood fall under it, and the LPC permit guidebook names security cameras directly, with a limit on how many may go on the street side of a building and examples of dome cameras finished to match the facade. That rule is the single biggest difference between a camera plan here and one on an unlandmarked block in another part of Brooklyn, and it is why we count the front before we design anything else. A separate, older National Register district, listed in 1983 with 305 contributing buildings from 1865 to about 1900, overlaps part of the same ground; the camera rules we follow come from the city district.

The newer side of the neighborhood is just as real. The arena at 620 Atlantic Avenue opened in 2012 at the northwest corner, and the Pacific Park project over the rail yards has, by BK Reader's count, brought about 3,000 apartments in eight new buildings, with a plan unveiled in June 2026 for seven more on six sites. Along Eastern Parkway and Plaza Street sit prewar elevator co-ops, including the 16-story, 186-unit building at 135 Eastern Parkway, built in 1926, and on Grand Army Plaza and Vanderbilt Avenue there are glass condominiums such as One Grand Army Plaza and the 278-unit building at 550 Vanderbilt. Prospect Places describes the brownstones as having deep backyards, and that matters: a rear garden here is often a long, walled space that nobody on the street can see, reached by the garden-level door under the stoop. Wikipedia puts the 2010 population at 19,849 on about 237 acres. One block can hold a single-family brownstone, a four-unit rental row house, a corner store and a co-op elevator building, each with a different owner deciding where a camera may go.

The incidents in the local press point at the same few places on a building. Patch reported a thief, caught on residents' surveillance footage, breaking into the mail bin in front of 55 Eastern Parkway at least three times and taking the whole bag of mail for six buildings near Underhill Avenue; years earlier Patch had covered repeated mail thefts at a dozen or more homes on Prospect and Park places between Underhill and Vanderbilt. amNY described a 2020 burglary pattern that included an attempt at 8:45 a.m. on January 8 at a home on Park Place near Carlton Avenue, where the burglar tried to damage an apartment door lock. In July 2026, the Brooklyn Eagle reported, a burglar who hit 11 Brooklyn businesses that summer entered a Prospect Heights bar at 80 Sixth Avenue through a basement door around 4:15 a.m., forced his way into an office and took about $4,000; police said he got into businesses primarily through basement-level doors. Hoodline reported that two suspects committed 19 commercial burglaries across 16 neighborhoods, Prospect Heights among them, between August 3 and September 10, 2026, and in October a bicycle was taken from a residential building near Underhill Avenue and Dean Street. Patch reports the 78th Precinct covers the western side of the neighborhood, from Flatbush Avenue to the western half of Vanderbilt, and the 77th covers the rest. We found no crime statistic that ranks Prospect Heights blocks, and we do not offer one.

Prospect Heights facts that decide where a camera goes

  • A camera allowance on the front: the LPC guidebook allows no more than two security cameras per 25 feet of street frontage on small residential and commercial buildings, and no more than three on large buildings, under LPC Rules Section 2-20.
  • A big district: about 850 buildings, designated in 2009, covering most of the row-house blocks between Flatbush and Washington avenues.
  • Two doors on most houses: a parlor door at the top of the stoop and a garden-level door under it, plus a cellar and a deep rear garden.
  • A split between precincts: the 78th west of the middle of Vanderbilt Avenue and the 77th to the east, according to Patch.
  • Basement doors as the way in: the summer 2026 business burglar entered primarily through basement-level doors, including at 80 Sixth Avenue.
  • An odd street grid: Patch traced lots laid out at roughly 45 degrees in 1833, and three buildings reached only by an alley and a doorway through another building.

What Prospect Heights owners tend to add next. After the parlor door and garden cameras go in, owners often ask whether the stoop camera's picture can show on an intercom screen inside, or whether the areaway gate could report when it is left open. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a video intercom is likely, a spare cable to the vestibule, so the cellar ceiling only has to be opened once.

The Facade Budget

Two Cameras per 25 Feet: Planning a Prospect Heights Front Under the Landmarks Rules

Most camera plans start by asking how many cameras the owner wants. In the Prospect Heights Historic District the better first question is how many the front of the building may carry. The LPC Permit Guidebook's chapter on health and safety work says that, for small residential and commercial buildings, no more than two security cameras may be installed for every 25 feet of street frontage, and for large buildings no more than three, and it points to LPC Rules Section 2-20. The guidebook illustrates the idea with two dome cameras finished to match the facade. The Commission's rules page explains the consequence: work that meets the rules can be approved by LPC staff, and work that does not has to go before the full Commission at a public hearing. So the facade allowance is not a ban. It is a budget, and the job is to spend it on the two views that matter most.

On a row house, those two views are almost always the parlor door at the top of the stoop and the garden-level door beneath it, or one camera that covers both from the right height. Everything else that people want to see, the rear garden, the cellar door, the side of the areaway, the hall inside the vestibule, can usually be covered from places that do not face the street at all. The guidebook passage we rely on counts cameras against street frontage; for walls that do not face a street, we ask LPC staff rather than guess. One security vendor's summary of the rules says they generally discourage installation on a primary facade unless no other option exists. That is a vendor's reading, not the Commission's wording, but it fits how we plan anyway: the front gets the fewest, smallest cameras that do the job, and the inside of the house does the rest.

The review is detailed. Presentation materials the Commission posted for a brownstone at 7 East 7th Street in December 2025 show pole-mounted cameras attached under the water table, mock-ups on the building and comparisons of camera housing sizes, and the Commission posted presentation materials in January 2026 for 628 Carlton Avenue, an 1871 building inside this district. The guidebook's application section asks for manufacturer cut sheets for security cameras, light fixtures, intercoms and mailboxes. Skipping the process has a cost: CityLand reported that homeowners in Hamilton Heights, a historic district in Manhattan, received Landmarks violations for putting up cameras to catch graffiti vandals. Our part is to choose cameras that will fit the rule, give you the cut sheets and marked-up photos the application asks for, and wait for the permit before anything goes on the front.

Spending the allowance on the right two views

With only two cameras on the street side, each has to earn its place. The one we place first almost always looks along the stoop from the side of the door opening, low enough to record a face at the top step and wide enough to show the bottom of the stoop and the gate to the areaway. If the front has room for a second, it goes where it can see the garden door under the stoop from a height that catches faces rather than hats, often in the door opening itself. Anything that would only look at the sidewalk for the sake of it, or that duplicates a view the first camera already has, is left off.

Making the inside of the house carry the plan

A camera inside the vestibule, looking out through the inner door at whoever comes through the outer one, does not count against the facade at all and sees faces at close range. A camera on the back wall of the house covers the whole garden. A camera inside the cellar watches the cellar door from the side that matters. On most Prospect Heights row houses, the finished plan has two or fewer cameras on the street side and three to five inside or at the rear, all feeding one recorder in a dry, locked spot.

The Street Calendar

Arena Nights, Open-Street Weekends, Market Saturdays and Labor Day: How the Calendar Shapes a Camera Plan

Prospect Heights has busier days than most residential neighborhoods, and they come back on a schedule. The arena at the northwest corner seats 17,732 for basketball and about 20,000 for concerts, according to Wikipedia, and has no dedicated parking lot of its own. A 2013 Brooklyn News Service report described what residents near it lived with after its opening: bass and vibration near Dean Street, crowds after concerts, and buses, black cars and limousines idling on Sixth Avenue behind the building. Vanderbilt Avenue between Atlantic Avenue and Park Place becomes an open street, closed to cars Friday through Sunday under the program NYC DOT described in June 2022, run by the Prospect Heights Neighborhood Development Council. The Grand Army Plaza Greenmarket runs every Saturday, year-round, from 8 a.m. to 4 p.m., GrowNYC says, at the northwest entrance of Prospect Park. And on Labor Day, NY1 reported for 2026, the plaza circle, Flatbush Avenue from Grand Army Plaza to Caton Avenue, Washington Avenue from Sterling Place to Lincoln Road and the Eastern Parkway main and service roads from Plaza Street East are all closed for the parade.

None of this is a reason to fear the neighborhood. It is a reason to set cameras up for it. On those days the sidewalks fill with people who have nothing to do with your house, and a camera set to alert on any motion in front of a stoop will send dozens of notifications an hour until you turn alerts off, which is the moment something that matters goes unnoticed. A camera framed too wide records a crowd in which no one can be told apart. And a drive sized for a quiet week may write over Thursday night by the time a neighbor asks, on Sunday, whether anyone saw who tried the door.

Alerts that ignore the crowd

The fix is to separate what the camera records from what it tells you about. The stoop camera records everything, all the time. The alert zone is drawn tightly around the stoop treads above the second step, the areaway gate and the garden door, so a person walking past on the sidewalk never triggers it and a person climbing toward the door does. Many cameras also offer line crossing, so the alert fires only when someone passes through the gate in the inward direction. On a building near the arena we usually add a schedule that sends stoop alerts only after the hour the street normally empties, while the garden and cellar cameras alert at any hour, because nobody should be in your garden on an event night either.

Framing that picks one person out of many

When the sidewalk is crowded, the camera that helps is the one with a narrow, close view of a doorway, not the one looking at the whole block. A face filling a good part of the frame at the top of the stoop is identifiable even with forty people walking behind it. We aim the door camera to fill its frame with the door and the top steps, set a fast enough shutter that people moving at walking pace do not blur, and keep the wide street view, if there is one, as background context only.

Storage sized for the slow-news weekend

Reports of a tried door or a missing package on a busy weekend often surface days later. We size the drive so the entrance cameras keep at least two weeks of continuous recording at the quality you need, and show you how to lock a clip so the recorder skips it when it overwrites. Before a known busy weekend, such as Labor Day, it takes two minutes to open the app and confirm every camera is recording and the clock is right; we show you how during the handover.

Brownstone row houses: the cellar does the work

Nearly every row house here has a cellar running front to back under the garden floor, and it is the reason a camera job on these houses can be tidy. Cable for the stoop, the garden door, the cellar door and the rear garden travels along the cellar joists and comes up through small openings at each camera, so nothing runs across the face of the building. At the front, where a camera has to sit on or near the facade, we mount into mortar joints or the wood of the door frame rather than into brownstone, which is a soft sandstone that spalls when it is drilled carelessly. At the rear, the wall is usually brick and far easier to work with, and a camera there at the height of the parlor floor sees the full depth of the garden. Reaching a camera on an upper floor means following a closet stack or an old chase up through the house; plaster on lath is opened only at small, patchable points. The recorder goes in a closet on the owner's floor or a dry corner of the cellar well above any spot that has taken water, on battery backup.

Romanesque and Revival houses: work around the stone

The 1880s and 1890s houses tend to have deeper, rounder entrances, rough-faced stone and carved panels. These are the details the historic district exists to protect, and they are never drilled. The recess of an arched entrance is often a good place for a small camera, because it is sheltered and sees the top of the stoop square on, and a mounting plate can be fixed into a joint inside the opening where it is barely visible from the sidewalk. Where the stonework leaves no joint to use, we fall back to a camera inside the vestibule looking out, which often gives a closer, clearer face anyway.

Prewar co-ops on the parkway: the board, the staff and the service door

The co-ops on Eastern Parkway and Plaza Street were built as elevator apartment buildings; one source describes the parkway in the mid-1920s becoming a corridor of 15-story buildings. They usually have staff in the lobby for part of the day, a service entrance on a side street or in the rear, a package or mail room, a cellar with storage, and sometimes a garage. A camera plan here is a board decision, and the board needs to settle who may view and export footage before anything is installed. The cable routes are usually good, through risers and cellar corridors to an equipment room, and the views that add most are the ones staff cannot see from the desk: the service entrance, the package room, the cellar storage doors and the elevator lobbies on the floors.

Old-grid buildings: when the facade does not face the sidewalk

Patch's history of the old grid explains that in 1833 the Hoyt and Underhill land was laid out in 25-by-100-foot lots at roughly 45 degrees, oriented to the old Flatbush Turnpike, and that some lot lines survived the 1839 grid. The result is houses such as 323 Prospect Place sitting at an angle, wedge-shaped rooms, and three buildings that can only be reached by an alley and a doorway through another building, mostly between Underhill and Washington avenues. For cameras, an angled facade means the door camera sits on the corner nearest the direction people approach from, so it sees faces rather than shoulders; a building reached through a passage means the most important view is the passage itself, which belongs to someone else.

The Brooklyn Eagle reported the July 2026 burglar entering 80 Sixth Avenue through a basement door around 4:15 a.m. and forcing his way into an office; DNAinfo reported in 2015 that a thief cut through a wall into the owner's office at a restaurant on Carlton Avenue at Park Place sometime between midnight and 9 a.m.; and the Brooklyn Paper reported a restaurant at Vanderbilt and St. Marks avenues where the front door was broken around 3:30 a.m. and an ATM holding about $13,000 was taken, in a year the article does not give. For a restaurant or bar, the cameras that matter are therefore inside the cellar door, inside the office covering the whole room including its walls, at the front door from inside, over the bar and register, and at any ATM. Kitchen cameras, if wanted, go on the pass rather than the line. The recorder goes somewhere other than the office, on battery backup, so a thief who reaches the office does not also reach the recording. Customer areas get overview cameras; restrooms are never covered.

Shops: the counter in daylight, the glass at night

Patch reported two women taking more than $6,000 in jewelry from Specific Gravity at 613 Vanderbilt Avenue, between Bergen Street and St. Marks Avenue, and police released the store's footage. Hoodline described the 2026 spree in which an object was thrown through a store window and cash and merchandise were taken. Those two incidents point at the two jobs a shop camera has. During the day, a camera at head height just inside the door records each customer's face, and a camera above and slightly in front of the counter records hands and what passes over it. At night, a camera inside the glass watches the window and the door, so it keeps recording if the glass is broken. A back corner overview and a camera on the stock room or rear door from inside complete the plan. Screens and card pads stay out of frame, and audio stays off.

Ground-floor tenants in towers and mixed-use buildings

A store at the base of a Pacific Park tower, or on the ground floor of a mixed-use building on Flatbush Avenue, shares walls, doors and sometimes a cellar with a building that has its own cameras and its own rules. The lease usually gives the landlord control of the outside of the building and the shared areas, and in the historic district the landlord's front facade also carries the Landmarks camera allowance, which the store and the building share. We plan the store's cameras inside the store and on its own doors, get written approval from the landlord for anything outside, and keep the store's recorder separate from the building's unless both sides want them joined.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Prospect Heights

  • The facade allowance is shared. In the historic district, a store's front cameras count against the same street frontage as the building's, so the owner and the tenant have to agree on who uses it.
  • Private rooms stay unrecorded. Restrooms and staff changing areas are never covered, and we check each view for mirrors that might reveal one.
  • Video, not conversations. We set business cameras to record pictures only.

Field Notes

Field Notes: How We Walk a Prospect Heights Row House, Sidewalk to Rear Lot Line

This is a description of our method on these houses, not a story about one job. We start on the sidewalk across the street, because that is where you see what the Landmarks Commission will see: how wide the frontage is, where the door opening sits, whether there is a mortar joint inside it, and how the stoop and areaway gate line up. Then we stand at the bottom of the stoop and at the top, and at the garden door below, looking for the height at which a face would fill a frame. In the vestibule we check whether a camera inside could see through the outer door's glass. In the cellar we look for the clearest run from front to back, any sign of past water, the old wires we will need to trace, and the spot where the recorder could live. Finally we go out the garden door and stand at the rear lot line looking back at the house, which shows where a rear camera would see the whole garden and which windows on the next street overlook it. By the end we know how many cameras the front can carry, where the others go and how long each cable is, and that is what the written quote is built from.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Prospect Heights: Questions Answered

Landmarks and the front of the house

Our row house has about twenty feet of frontage inside the historic district. How many cameras can the front carry under the Landmarks rules?

The LPC Permit Guidebook allows no more than two security cameras for every 25 feet of street frontage on small residential and commercial buildings, under LPC Rules Section 2-20, so a twenty-foot row house can have up to two on its street side if they meet the rest of the rule, and work that meets the rules can be approved by LPC staff. Large buildings may have up to three per 25 feet, but whether an apartment house counts as large is a question for LPC staff, not something we assume. For a corner building, ask LPC how each street frontage is counted before planning a side-street camera. In practice, most row houses here are well served by one or two small cameras at the door openings, with the garden, cellar and vestibule covered from places that do not face the street.

We saw a Landmarks presentation where cameras were tucked on a short pole under a brownstone's water table. What does that position actually see?

The water table is the projecting stone course at the base of the facade, near the top of the garden level. Presentation materials for a brownstone at 7 East 7th Street, posted by the Commission in December 2025, show pole-mounted cameras attached beneath it, along with mock-ups and housing size comparisons. A camera there sits roughly at head height for someone at the garden door and below eye level for someone climbing the stoop, so it sees faces at both entrances rather than the tops of heads, and the projecting stone above it shelters it from rain and hides much of it from the sidewalk. The trade-off is that it is within reach of the areaway, so it needs a hard housing and a secure mount.

Our street is in the 1983 National Register district, but we are not sure it is inside the 2009 city district. Which one decides the camera rules?

The camera limits we follow come from the city's Landmarks Preservation Commission, which designated the Prospect Heights Historic District in 2009 and publishes the permit guidebook. The National Register district, listed in 1983 with 305 contributing buildings from 1865 to about 1900, is a separate federal listing that overlaps part of the same area. Because the city district's boundaries are irregular, roughly Flatbush Avenue, Sterling Place, Washington Avenue and St. Marks Avenue with a section reaching Pacific Street, we check your exact address on the Commission's map before planning the front.

Row-house layout

Our row house has a parlor door at the top of the stoop and a garden door underneath it. If we can only afford one camera at the front, which entrance gets it?

Usually the one people actually use. On many houses with a garden rental, the garden door is the busy entrance and the parlor door is opened mostly for guests, and on single-family houses it is often the reverse. If both see traffic, a single camera placed low in the parlor door opening or under the water table, angled to see down the stoop and across to the garden door, can cover both, though neither as closely as a dedicated camera would. We decide this standing on the stoop with you, because the answer depends on the angle of the steps and how deep the areaway is.

Our house follows an old lot line and sits at an angle to the sidewalk. How do you aim a front camera when the facade does not face the street squarely?

We mount on the side of the door opening that people approach from, so the camera looks toward their faces as they come along the sidewalk and up the steps. On a facade turned away from the street, a camera fixed square to the wall would spend most of its frame on the sidewalk at a slant and see people side-on. Patch's history of the old grid notes these angled lots are most visible between Underhill and Washington avenues, where 1830s lot lines survived the 1839 street plan, and on those blocks the neighboring house is often angled too, so we check that the corrected view does not end up looking at the neighbor's windows instead.

Would a camera up at the rear roofline see more of our garden than one at the parlor-floor level?

It sees more ground but less detail. From the roofline of a four-story house, a camera takes in the whole garden and the neighbors' gardens too, looks steeply down on anyone in it and shows mostly the tops of heads. At the height of the parlor floor, it sees the full depth of a typical deep Prospect Heights garden, the rear fence and the garden door at a better angle for faces, and it is easier to keep out of other people's yards and windows. We usually put the main rear camera at parlor-floor level and add a high camera only when the garden has a structure or a corner the lower camera cannot reach.

Our building is reached through a passage that belongs to the building in front of ours. Can we put a camera in that passage?

Only with the other owner's written agreement, because the passage and the doorway through their building are their property. Patch found three buildings in the neighborhood reached only by an alley and a doorway through another building, and for them the passage is the one approach everyone uses, so it is the most valuable view to have. If the neighboring owner agrees, the camera goes where it sees the passage from your end; if not, the camera sits on your own door, looking back along the passage as far as your property allows. Either way, we note in the camera names and on the map how a responder actually reaches your door.

Event days and the avenues

When thousands of people walk past after an arena concert, can a stoop camera still pick out the one person who came up our steps?

Yes, if it is framed for the door rather than the street. The camera that identifies someone in a crowd is one whose frame is filled by the top of the stoop and the door, so a person standing there is large in the picture while the crowd behind is small and out of the way. A fast shutter keeps people at walking pace from blurring under streetlights. Add a detection zone drawn only around the upper steps and the areaway gate, and the camera can record the whole sidewalk while alerting you only when someone actually comes up to the house.

On open-street weekends Vanderbilt Avenue is closed to cars. Does that change what our storefront camera should be aimed at?

It changes what the outside view is good for. With the roadway full of people and seating from Friday through Sunday, a camera looking out at the street records a crowd, and car-focused views lose their purpose on those days. The views that stay useful are the ones on your own door, your counter and any equipment you keep outside, recorded from inside or from your own frontage. We often set a separate alert schedule for open-street weekends so the storefront camera does not alert on the constant foot traffic, and keep the after-close alerts on the door and cellar at full sensitivity.

Our block is inside the Labor Day closure area. What should we check on the cameras before that weekend?

Open the app and confirm every camera is showing a live picture and that the recorder's clock matches your phone. Check that the drive is recording continuously on the entrance cameras and has room for the weekend. Make sure the people who should get alerts are still on the account, and that the battery backup unit is plugged in and charged. NY1 reported that for the 2026 parade the plaza circle, Washington Avenue from Sterling Place to Lincoln Road and the Eastern Parkway main and service roads from Plaza Street East were closed, which means a technician may not be able to drive to your door that day, so these checks are worth doing on the Friday before.

Bars, restaurants and buildings

The July 2026 burglar got into Brooklyn businesses mostly through basement-level doors. Our bar's office is one flight up from the cellar. Which camera along that route matters most?

The camera inside the cellar door, recording from the dry side, matters most, because everyone who comes in that way passes it first and it can alert the owner the moment the door opens after closing. The office camera comes next, covering the whole room, including the safe or cash drawer and the walls, since a forced office door or a cut wall both end there. The stair between them is the third view. In the 80 Sixth Avenue case reported by the Brooklyn Eagle, the burglar came through a basement door around 4:15 a.m. and forced the office; a system that watched only the front door and the bar would have missed both.

The package room in our co-op fills up by afternoon. Where does the camera go so it shows who picks up which box?

Over the shelves, looking toward the door, so it records each person's face as they enter and then their hands as they take a box from a shelf. A camera only at the doorway shows who came in but not what they took; a camera only over the shelves shows boxes moving but not faces. In a larger room, a second camera at the back covers the far shelves. We keep the view inside the package room, away from the lobby desk's screens, and the board decides in writing who may review footage when a resident reports a missing delivery.

Licenses, Privacy and Permission

Who Decides, What Is Allowed and Who May Install: The Rules Behind a Prospect Heights Camera Job

A camera job in Prospect Heights runs into more rules than in most Brooklyn neighborhoods, because the Landmarks rules sit on top of the ones that apply everywhere in the state. Here is how each set plays out on the houses, buildings and shops here.

The Landmarks Commission, on the front. In the historic district, the Landmarks Preservation Commission's rules set how many security cameras may go on the street side of a building, and its permit guidebook asks for manufacturer cut sheets for cameras, light fixtures, intercoms and mailboxes. Work that meets the rules can be approved by staff; work that does not goes to a public hearing. The same chapter of the guidebook says light fixtures and intercom panels must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening, and that one intercom panel can be installed per door opening, which matters when a camera, a light and an intercom all want the same spot. We give you the equipment details and photos for the application and do not put anything on the front until the permit is in hand.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law Article 6-D requires anyone in the business of installing or servicing security systems to be licensed. Owners installing cameras on their own property are a different case, but a contractor or handyman doing it for pay is not. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431, and we send a copy to owners, boards and landlords who ask.

Where a camera may look. Civil Rights Law §52-a gives a resident a civil claim against a neighbor who records them where they reasonably expect privacy, and Penal Law §250.45 makes recording people in private places such as bathrooms or bedrooms without consent a crime. Row houses here share party walls and gardens backed by the windows of the next street, so every outdoor camera is framed on your own stoop, door, garden and fence, and anything else that falls in the frame is masked. Outerbridge Law notes that recording a tenant's conversation in a corridor or elevator without consent might break wiretapping laws, so we set building cameras to record video only.

Owners, boards and tenants. Brick Underground notes that renters need the landlord's permission to install equipment in common areas, including the hallway outside their own apartment, and an attorney answering on Justia notes that landlords and co-op boards can generally install cameras in lobbies, entryways, hallways and other shared spaces. In a row house split into apartments, the owner decides about the outside and the halls; in a co-op or condo, the board does. In the historic district, a tenant's ground-floor store shares the building's facade allowance, so the owner has the final word on the front.

Is anyone required to have cameras? One New York compliance guide notes that city law does not universally require apartment buildings to install security cameras. Sources disagree on whether a city law requires buildings with common-area cameras to notify residents: one installer cites "Local Law 50," another site cites "Local Law 3 of 2019," and we could not confirm either against an official source. Telling residents in writing where cameras are and who reviews footage is good practice regardless, and we recommend it to every board and owner.

Walls and openings. Every opening through brick or a mortar joint is sloped outward and sealed against water. Every opening through a fire-rated wall or floor in an apartment house, co-op or tower is sealed with rated firestop before we leave. Carved stone is never drilled.

Small cameras are easy to overlook from the sidewalk, and plenty go up without anyone asking.

"The arena brings crime to the whole neighborhood, so you need a camera on every wall."

The arena does change the neighborhood on event nights, and residents have described the noise, the crowds and the idling vehicles behind it.
