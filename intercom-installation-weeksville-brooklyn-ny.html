Intercom installation in Weeksville means fitting a front-door system to some of the most varied housing in central Brooklyn, inside a neighborhood whose name most maps leave off. The historic village that James Weeks began buying into in 1838 is usually traced between Fulton Street on the north, East New York Avenue on the south, Troy Avenue on the west and Ralph Avenue on the east, at the eastern edge of Crown Heights where it meets Brownsville and Bedford-Stuyvesant. Inside those lines the doors we are asked about come in very different shapes. There are late-19th-century brick rowhouses built as the street grid pushed east, a few wood-frame houses that survived from an older era, two- and three-family homes that changed hands for between about $900,000 and $2 million in recent years, and four-story walk-ups from the 1920s such as the 17-apartment co-op at 578 Ralph Avenue. There are the two low-rise buildings of Weeksville Gardens, privately managed since 2020, the 1941 Kingsborough Houses just to the east, and new affordable buildings like Weeksville Place, which sets aside apartments for residents with mobility, vision and hearing needs. A good intercom in any of them does four things: it lets every household know who is at the door, it lets them speak clearly before deciding, it releases the right door and lets it lock again, and it works for the actual people who live there, including the ones who do not carry smartphones. This page explains how we plan each building type, what New York rules require at different sizes, where public housing works differently, how the new buildings change the conversation, and how a written quote comes together.

Repair Calls in Weeksville

Need Repair in Weeksville? A Panel Nobody Can Read, a Floor That Stopped Ringing, or a Release Nobody Trusts

Repair calls in Weeksville tend to follow the age of the building. In the 1920s walk-ups, the usual story is that most apartments still ring but one or two floors went quiet years ago, and nobody remembers when. That usually means a damaged run somewhere between the cellar and the floor, often where a past renovation opened a wall, rather than a failed panel. In the two- and three-family rowhouses, we tend to find bell wiring that was extended one apartment at a time by different hands, a transformer that has been replaced at least once, and buttons whose labels no longer match the floors. In the newer buildings, the complaint is more often about the software side: residents who never received an account, an app that stopped working after a phone update, or a panel that still shows the names of people who moved out. And in every kind of building, a release that buzzes while the door stays shut usually comes down to a strike that has drifted out of line with the latch, a closer that pulls the door too hard against its stop, or a power supply that can no longer pull the strike.

We approach a repair the same way we approach a new installation: find out what the building actually has before replacing anything. Some old audio systems in Weeksville still have years of life in them once a single failed run is found and fixed; others are past saving because replacement stations are no longer made. When an owner asks whether to repair or replace, we answer with what we measured, what parts are still available, and what each choice costs, written down so a co-op board or a family can decide together. If the system is in a PACT or NYCHA building, we tell the caller plainly that the repair belongs to management and explain where to report it.

Brick rowhouses: a parlor door, a garden door and a lot of plaster

The rowhouses that went up as the grid spread east were built for one family and are now usually shared. The Eagle singles out the rows at Ralph Avenue and Prospect Place and the barrel-vaulted fronts on Bergen Street past Buffalo Avenue; most look like this inside, with plaster laid over brick party walls and lath on wood studs only in the interior partitions. The first planning question is which door visitors use. In some houses the parlor door at the top of the steps is the main entrance and the garden-level door belongs to one apartment; in others the parlor door has been sealed and everyone uses the lower door. The station goes where visitors actually arrive, and if two doors are in use, each one gets its own button arrangement so a visitor for the garden apartment is not ringing the floors above.

Wiring in these houses usually travels up from the cellar. Where a chase, an old dumbwaiter shaft or a closet column runs floor to floor, we use it. Where none exists, the cable can often follow the stair, run behind baseboard or inside a neat raceway painted to match. We avoid chasing grooves into the brick party wall, because it carries both houses' structure and neighbors' quiet. Old wiring is tested before anyone decides to replace it; a two-wire video system can sometimes run on a single good pair left from the old bell or audio system, which keeps the plaster intact.

Surviving wood-frame houses: hollow walls and wood that moves

The frame houses are few, but they are some of the most valued buildings in the neighborhood. Brownstoner's description of 1718 Dean Street, with fish-scale shingles, a bracketed cornice, a canopy and a historic double door, is typical of what survives: a narrow house, two stories, a front door that may be original, and a kitchen in a rear extension. Hollow wood walls make it easier to hide cable than in a brick row, since a wire can often be fished from the cellar up inside a stud bay to the station location without opening plaster. The harder part is the door. An old wood door and frame swell and shrink with the seasons, so a strike that fits perfectly in October may bind in August. We set the strike and keeper with that movement in mind, use a strike that tolerates some door gap, and tell the owner to call if the door starts catching once the weather turns.

Two- and three-family houses: one front door, more than one household

The two- and three-family houses are the most common intercom request in Weeksville. StreetEasy's listing for 167 Schenectady Avenue, a 1910 three-family, is a good example of the type, and the Rahme Team's Crown Heights guide puts typical sales between about $900,000 and $2 million, with renovated houses near Albany Avenue at the top. In these houses the intercom question is usually not legal but practical. With fewer than eight apartments, the city's intercom requirement does not apply, though every multiple dwelling's entrance door still has to be self-closing and self-locking. What owners want is for each household to answer its own visitors, for the release to work from each floor, and for nobody to have to walk downstairs to let in a delivery. A three-button video kit is often enough, but we check the cable route to each floor, where the power supply will live so the owner can reach it, and whether the tenants should see the owner's door camera at all.

1920s walk-ups and co-ops: the building the law has the most to say about

The four-story walk-ups that arrived with the subway era are where intercom law bites hardest. A building like 578 Ralph Avenue, which StreetEasy lists with 17 units and a 1922 build date, falls into New York's rule for buildings put up before 1968: the state Attorney General's tenant guide says such buildings must have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request it. Many already have one, often an audio system installed decades ago with a lobby panel of buttons and a handset beside each apartment door. The work in these buildings is mostly about the riser. The cable from the cellar to each floor has been patched over the years, and the first step is testing every pair to every apartment. Where most pairs pass, a two-wire video system can reuse them. Where many fail, a new riser can run in the stair hall or a closet column. Because a co-op board answers to its shareholders, we write the scope so it can be read aloud at a meeting.

New affordable buildings: networked entry and residents with specific needs

The new buildings are where Weeksville's intercom work is changing fastest. The New York Housing Conference describes Weeksville Place as an eight-story building with 45 apartments on a formerly vacant city-owned lot on Prospect Place between Ralph and Buffalo Avenues, built by the Settlement Housing Fund through HPD's Neighborhood Construction Program, with a community room, laundry, roof terrace, bike room and rear yard. Brick Underground's lottery report notes that a small share of its apartments are set aside for residents with mobility, vision and hearing needs. Brownstoner describes a 13-story senior building planned with 200 apartments on Bergen Street next to the existing Kingsborough senior building, and reports that residents' visioning sessions asked for improved safety, security and pedestrian access. Buildings like these arrive with networked panels, apartment screens or app answering, and often a staffed desk for part of the day. The intercom work is less about cable and more about making the system usable by every resident and keeping it documented as staff and managers change.

Field Notes

Field Notes: What a Technician Checks at a Weeksville Front Door Before Quoting

Find out which era the door is from. The technician looks at the door, the frame and the hardware first. An original wood door on a frame house, a replacement steel door on a brick row and an aluminum-and-glass door on a new building each take a different strike, and the frame decides more of the price than the panel does.

Ask who actually answers. How many households, how many people in each, and does anyone have trouble hearing a chime, reading a screen or getting to the door in time? A building with one older resident on the top floor may need a different station on that floor than everywhere else.

Count every door a visitor could use. Parlor door, garden door, side door from the yard, cellar door from the sidewalk, door from a shop into the hall. Each one is either controlled by the intercom or deliberately left alone, and the owner should know which.

Follow the wire to the cellar. The transformer or power supply, junction points and the run up through the building are traced and photographed, and every existing pair is tested. In a rowhouse the technician expects bell wire, an old intercom and telephone cable side by side, and labels what each one is.

Read the directory as a stranger would. Are the names current? Are apartments numbered the same way on the panel, the mailboxes and the doors? A mismatch here causes more wrong calls than any hardware fault.

Ask who owns the building and who signs. An owner on the premises, an owner who lives elsewhere, a co-op board, several relatives who inherited together, a nonprofit or a management company. The answer decides who receives the logins and the wiring record.

Switch off the power and try the door. With the intercom and lock power off, the door is opened from inside by hand and from outside with a key, and the closer is watched to make sure it latches on its own. That same test is repeated at handover.

Stations Everyone Can Use

Designing a Weeksville Intercom for Older Residents and for Apartments Set Aside for Hearing, Vision and Mobility Needs

Weeksville's newest buildings are making a point that applies to every building in the neighborhood: an intercom is only as good as the least able person who has to use it. Weeksville Place sets aside apartments for residents with mobility, vision and hearing needs. The planned senior building on Bergen Street will house 200 households of older residents. And in the walk-ups and family houses, the Furman Center's district figures suggest that one resident in eight is 65 or older and that more than a third of households are one person living alone. In practice that means many calls are answered by someone who moves slowly, hears less than they used to, or has no one else at home to help.

For a resident who is hard of hearing, the most useful features are a station with a visual call indicator, so a light or a flashing screen shows that someone is downstairs, adjustable ring volume and speech volume, and a hands-free speaker loud enough to be heard across a kitchen. For a resident with low vision, a station with large physical buttons in a fixed layout, distinct tones for "call arriving" and "door released," and a screen that is bright and large often works better than a touchscreen with menus. For a resident who uses a wheelchair or walker, the station goes at a height they can reach comfortably from a seated position, and the release time on the street door is set long enough for a visitor to get through before it relocks, without leaving it open so long that someone follows them in. At the lobby panel, the city rule in 1 RCNY 42-01 caps push buttons at six feet and sets the speaking device between four and five feet above the floor, and in buildings open to the public, accessibility reach ranges usually bring controls lower still.

None of this requires exotic equipment, but it does require asking the question before ordering. In a new building, we ask the owner or the nonprofit which apartments were set aside and what their residents need, and we offer to meet residents during setup so the station is configured for them rather than left on factory settings. In an older walk-up, we ask the owner whether any tenant has trouble with the current handset, because a tenant who cannot hear the buzzer is a tenant who gives a spare key to a neighbor or leaves the street door propped open. We also check that the release works the same way on every station, so a resident who moves to a different apartment in the building does not have to learn a new routine. And where a building mixes residents who prefer phone apps with residents who do not use them, we design for both rather than forcing one.

Two settings matter more than most people expect. The first is how long a call keeps ringing before it gives up; a resident who needs a minute to cross the apartment should not find the call has already ended. The second is what happens when nobody answers at all. In a building with a desk or a super, an unanswered call can roll to them; in a building without staff, the panel can at least tell the visitor clearly that the call was not answered, so they do not keep pressing buttons for other apartments. We agree both settings with the building and write them into the handover record.

Public Housing in Weeksville

Weeksville Gardens, Kingsborough and the Shift to Private Management: Who Can Change the Entry System

For residents of these buildings, the practical rule is that the intercom and the entrance doors belong to the management company and the housing authority. A tenant who finds the lobby intercom dead should report it to management in writing and keep a copy, and if the repair stalls, file a complaint with 311, which accepts reports of broken doorbells, buzzers and intercoms in apartments and common areas. Tenants should not hire their own installer to change equipment in the building, however tempting it is after weeks of waiting, because the equipment is not theirs and the work could create problems with the management's contracts. The Comptroller's audit, which found broken entrance locks at 40.1 percent of the NYCHA buildings it examined citywide, shows why residents raise the issue loudly. An intercom that rings is worth little if the lock it releases does not hold.

When a PACT manager or a NYCHA contractor asks us to bid on entry work, we follow their specifications, including the fail-secure strikes and fob systems their guidelines call for, and we coordinate with the management office on access to apartments. Our installers carry the license and insurance documents managers ask for, and the work is quoted in writing like any other building.

The Rules by Building Size

Rowhouses, Two-Families, Walk-Ups and New Buildings: What New York Rules Ask of a Weeksville Entrance

Weeksville includes every size of residential building, so which rules apply depends on the building, not the neighborhood. Nothing here is legal advice; it is a guide to public sources so owners, boards and tenants know what to ask an attorney or managing agent. We note where the sources disagree.

One- and two-family houses. The intercom requirements are written for multiple dwellings, which begin at three apartments. In a one- or two-family rowhouse or frame house, an intercom is the owner's choice, though once a tenant relies on it, keeping it working is part of maintaining the rental.

Three to seven apartments. Many of the three-families and small walk-ups here fall in this range. Parker Custom Security summarizes the rule as leaving buildings with fewer than eight apartments outside the intercom requirement while still requiring self-closing, self-locking entrance doors.

Eight or more apartments, built or converted after 1968. The Attorney General's tenant guide says these buildings must have a two-way voice intercom from each apartment to the front door, and tenants must be able to buzz the entrance open. That covers Weeksville Place and the other new buildings.

Eight or more apartments, built before 1968. The same guide says older buildings must also have self-locking doors and a two-way intercom if a majority of the apartments request it. The 1920s walk-ups and co-ops on Ralph Avenue and the side streets are in this group.

Audio is enough. Impera Security notes that a basic audio intercom meets the city's requirement; nothing in the rules requires video. We recommend video in many buildings because it helps residents decide, but an owner should know the difference between a legal requirement and an upgrade.

Violation class. Parker Custom Security describes a broken intercom as a Class B hazardous violation enforced on tenant complaint. Self-closing doors are a separate matter: an HPD bulletin on Local Laws 62 and 63 of 2022 says owners who fail to keep self-closing doors in good repair are liable for a Class C immediately hazardous violation, with a shorter correction window. An owner who receives a notice should follow the class and instructions printed on it.

Mounting heights. The city rule 1 RCNY 42-01 says no push button may be more than six feet above the floor and the speaking and listening device must sit between four and five feet above it. On a building with steps up to the door, that floor is where the visitor stands.

Getting out. The Housing Maintenance Code requires self-locking doors to open readily from the inside without a key. Every door we fit with an electric strike or lock opens from inside without a key, code or button.

Landmarks. Our reading of the Historic Districts Council's description of Crown Heights North III is that the district stops at Albany Avenue, west of Weeksville's historic Troy Avenue line, so most blocks here are not in a historic district. Where an address is designated, the LPC permit guidebook lists intercom panels among the safety equipment staff can approve without a public hearing. We check each address before work starts.

Local data. We could not find Weeksville-level counts of HPD intercom violations, 311 intercom complaints or DOB permits for intercom work that we could verify, so this page does not quote any.

Compliance notes for Weeksville shops, congregations and mixed buildings

  • Free exit at every door. Any door fitted with an electric strike or lock opens from inside without a key, code or button, which matters most in churches and community rooms that fill with people at set times.
  • Residential doors latch on their own. Where apartments share a building with a business, the residential door closes and locks itself after every release, whatever the shop's hours.
  • Separate systems for separate occupants. The apartments' intercom gets its own power and cable path, independent of a store's tenant changes and renovations.
  • Reachable controls. Push buttons at or below six feet and speaking devices between four and five feet under 1 RCNY 42-01; businesses open to the public also follow accessibility reach rules.
  • Life-safety devices left clear. Stations and cable never block an exit sign, a fire alarm device, a sprinkler head or a door's swing, and low-voltage cable stays apart from power wiring.
  • Written approval in leased space. With most merchants renting, a business gets the owner's written approval before any new hole in an outside wall or any mounting on the facade.
  • Landmark addresses. Work on the exterior of a designated building, such as the Hunterfly Road houses, goes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission; most commercial buildings here are not designated.

Co-ops and houses that changed hands

Our 1922 co-op walk-up on Ralph Avenue has seventeen apartments. The state rule says older buildings need an intercom when a majority of apartments ask for one, but in a co-op the residents are the owners. How does that work for us?

In a co-op, the request and the decision run through the same people, so the rule mostly matters for the co-op's rental apartments and for clarity about obligations. The Attorney General's guide describes the pre-1968 requirement as triggered by a majority request from the apartments; in a co-op where some units are rented to tenants by shareholders or held by a sponsor, those tenants are part of that count. In practice, most co-op boards in buildings like this decide on an intercom through the normal budget process, after shareholders raise it at a meeting. What we can do is give the board the facts it needs to decide: whether the existing system can be repaired, what a replacement involves, which apartments' runs failed testing, and a written scope with separate lines for the panel, stations, riser and door hardware. If the building already has an intercom, whether or not anyone ever formally requested it, keeping it working is part of the board's maintenance duties, and a co-op's attorney is the right person to confirm how the rule applies to its specific mix of shareholders and renters.

The listing for the three-family we bought on Schenectady Avenue advertised a "virtual doorman," but nobody gave us any paperwork. How do we find out what is installed and whether there is a contract we are now paying for?

Start with the hardware, then follow the money. StreetEasy lists a "Doorman Virtual" amenity for 167 Schenectady Avenue, and the term is used loosely in listings for anything from a video intercom with an app to a monitored service in which a remote attendant answers the door. We look at the panel and any equipment in the cellar, note the brand and model, and check whether it is connected to the internet. The panel's label or the app it uses usually tells us the manufacturer or service company. Then look at the bank and card statements the house is paid from, and at the closing documents, for any recurring charge. Many app-based systems require an annual or monthly subscription; if the previous owner's account lapsed, the equipment may still work for calls but not for app answering. If there is a service contract, the company can usually transfer it, cancel it or tell you what happens to the equipment. We can then tell you whether the equipment is worth keeping under your own account, can be reconfigured to work without a subscription, or should be replaced with a system you own outright.

New buildings and residents with different needs

Our new eight-story building mixes seniors, families who came from shelters and a few apartments set aside for residents with mobility, vision and hearing needs. Can one lobby system work for all of them, or do some apartments need different equipment?

One lobby system can and should serve everyone; the differences belong in the apartments. The panel downstairs needs a readable directory, a camera at face height, clear audio, buttons within the city's height limits and, ideally, spoken or tonal feedback when a call goes through, so a visitor or resident with low vision knows something happened. Upstairs, most apartments can share a standard station, while the set-aside apartments get stations chosen for the resident: a visual call light or flashing screen for someone who is deaf or hard of hearing, large fixed buttons and distinct tones for someone with low vision, and a station mounted at a seated reach height for a wheelchair user. As long as every station belongs to the same system family, the building has one management login, one directory and one set of release rules. We ask the owner which apartments were set aside before ordering, and we prefer to configure those stations with the resident present so settings like ring length, volume and release time suit the person who will use them. Weeksville Place, as described by the New York Housing Conference and Brick Underground, is the kind of building this approach is designed for.

Congregations and community doors

Our church runs a food pantry from a side door on distribution days, and a line forms on the sidewalk. Can a door station let volunteers inside know the next family is ready without the door being opened to the whole line?

Yes, if the routine is designed around the line rather than one visitor at a time. Berean Missionary Baptist Church on Bergen Street is one of the congregations Brownstoner describes running food pantries and other programs, and many congregations on the Ocean Hill–Weeksville corridors do the same; we are not describing any particular church's system. A practical arrangement puts a video station at the pantry door with a wide view of the waiting area and routes its calls to a station or tablet at the volunteers' table inside, not to the church office. A volunteer sees who is at the door, speaks to them, and releases the door for one family at a time; the closer is adjusted so the door shuts promptly behind them. On days with heavy demand, some congregations prefer to keep the door locked and bring bags out, using the station only to call the next person forward. Because the pantry door is an exit too, it opens from inside without any key or button. The hardware has to handle hundreds of cycles a day on distribution days, so we specify a strike and closer rated for heavy use.

Our cultural organization admits visitors only by reservation, in small groups. Can the door station help staff check a group against the booking before opening the door?

It can make that check easier and keep the door from being opened on reflex. The Weeksville Heritage Center, for example, says its guided tours are booked in advance and limited to fourteen people; we are not describing its entry system, only a routine many small institutions share. A video station at the entrance, with a camera wide enough to show a group rather than one face, lets a staff member at the front desk or on a phone see who has arrived, speak to the group leader, and confirm the name against the booking before releasing the door. If staff are out with another group, calls can forward to a second staff phone, and the panel can play a short message telling early arrivals when the next tour begins. For events with larger crowds, the station can be set to ring the event coordinator's phone. The door still opens freely from inside, and any separate doors to offices or collections are kept on their own locks so a visitor admitted to the lobby cannot wander further.

Shops and turnover

The store on the ground floor of our Utica Avenue building has changed tenants three times in ten years, and each one seemed to leave with a way into the apartments' hallway. How do we stop the next one from doing the same?

Separate the two occupancies so the shop never needs access to the apartments in the first place, then control what access remains. The most common leak we find is a back door from the shop into the residential hallway or stair, sometimes on the same key as the apartments' street door, sometimes released by the apartments' intercom so the shop could receive deliveries. The fix begins with the intercom: the apartments' system gets its own power supply and its own strike on the residential door, with no button or release in the shop. If the shop genuinely needs to reach the cellar for its meter or storage, that door gets its own lock and the hallway door its own, keyed differently. Where the building uses codes or fobs, each commercial tenant gets credentials that are deleted on move-out, and the owner keeps the administrator login. We then write down every door, every lock and every credential so the next lease can say exactly what the shop receives. The city's commercial study found that 83 percent of merchants on these corridors rent, so turnover is a fact of life; the entry system should assume it.

FAQ

Weeksville Intercom Installation FAQ

Our walk-up's street door is a steel door in a steel frame that was filled solid with grout. How is an electric strike fitted into a frame like that?
+

It takes more cutting but it is routine. The strike pocket is cut into the steel frame and the grout behind it is chipped out carefully to the depth the strike body needs, then the strike is secured to the frame and the cable is brought in through a small channel or a surface raceway along the frame to a junction box above the door. We choose a strike made for hollow-metal frames and size the pocket so the frame keeps its strength. On some grouted frames a surface-mounted strike or a strike with a shallower body is the better choice, and we decide that after measuring the frame during the survey.

The best spot for our panel is behind the glass of the outer door. Will the camera see visitors clearly through the glass?
+

Usually not well, and especially not at night. Glass reflects the camera's own infrared light and the lobby lights back into the lens, so faces outside often appear washed out or hidden behind glare. A panel works best with nothing between its camera and the visitor. If it must sit inside, we place it tight against the glass with a shield or turn off the infrared, add light outside the door, and test the picture after dark before finishing. In most buildings, mounting the panel on the outside wall beside the door gives a far better image.

If a resident answers the door with a phone app, what happens when their phone is on Do Not Disturb?
+

The call may arrive silently or not at all, depending on the phone and the app's settings. Many intercom apps can be allowed to break through Do Not Disturb or a focus mode, but each resident has to set that on their own phone, and phone updates sometimes reset it. During setup we show residents how to mark the intercom app as an exception, and in buildings where reliable answering matters, such as for older residents or home care visits, we recommend a wall station that rings regardless of what the phone is doing.

Some residents have older phones that cannot install the newest version of an intercom app. What happens to them?
+

Eventually the app stops working on those phones, because manufacturers drop support for older operating systems. Before choosing an app-based system, we check the oldest phone versions it supports and how long the maker usually keeps supporting them. For residents with older phones, the safer options are a wall station in the apartment, or a panel that can ring an ordinary phone number so the resident answers like a normal call and presses a key to release the door. Mixed setups let each household use what works for them.

Some new panels let a visitor scan a code and place the call from their own phone. Is that a good idea for a small Weeksville building?
+

It can be useful as an extra, not as the only way to call. Scan-to-call features help when the panel's speaker is hard to hear on a busy street or when a visitor prefers to talk on their own phone. But they depend on the visitor having a working phone with data, and on the maker's service staying online. We set them up alongside the panel's own buttons and speaker, never in place of them, so a visitor without a phone can still reach the apartment and the building still meets the two-way intercom requirement.

Who installs firmware updates on a networked lobby panel, and how often should that happen?
+

Someone named in writing should own that job, usually the building's manager or the installer under a service arrangement. Makers release updates to fix security problems and bugs several times a year, and a panel left on old software for years is more exposed. We set up the panel so updates are reviewed before they install, note the current version in the handover record, and show the owner or manager where to check. When an update is needed, it is applied at a quiet hour and every apartment is tested afterward.

Our house is owned jointly by several relatives after an inheritance. Who has to sign the work order for a new intercom?
+

We need a signature from whoever has authority to approve work on the property. When a house is held by several co-owners, that is often one relative the others have agreed should handle repairs, sometimes an estate executor, and sometimes all of them. We do not judge family arrangements, but we do ask who will receive the quote, who pays, and who holds the logins afterward, and we put those names in writing. If owners disagree, it is better to settle that before work starts than after the door has been drilled.

Can a resident look at the lobby camera from their apartment station at any time, even when nobody has rung?
+

Many video stations have a monitor button that shows the panel's camera on demand, so a resident can check the entrance before going down or after hearing noise outside. Whether every apartment should have that ability is a building decision, since it lets any resident see who comes and goes. Some buildings allow it, some limit it to the owner or super, and some switch it off. We set it the way the building decides and note the choice in the handover record.

How many apartments can a two-wire video system serve before it needs extra power supplies or distribution equipment?
+

It depends on the brand and the length of the runs, but most two-wire systems power only a limited number of stations from one supply before distribution modules or added supplies are needed. In a 17-apartment walk-up that is normal, and the extra modules usually sit in the cellar or a closet near the riser. Long runs to upper floors and old, thin wire reduce what one supply can carry. We size the equipment from the manufacturer's tables and from our own measurements of each run, and the quote shows every module so nothing is a surprise.

Will a networked lobby panel slow down the internet in our building?
+

Not noticeably if it is set up properly. A panel and its stations use very little bandwidth when idle and a modest amount during a video call. We put the entry system on its own network segment, so it does not compete with residents' streaming and residents' devices cannot reach it, and we limit any cloud video to what the building actually uses. If the building's internet service is very slow or unreliable, we discuss that first, because the intercom's app features depend on it even though calls to wired stations do not.

Can the lobby panel give spoken or raised-button feedback for a visitor or resident who cannot read its screen?
+

Many panels can. Options include physical buttons with raised markings or Braille, a keypad with a raised dot on the five key, tones that confirm a call is ringing and that the door has released, and on some models spoken prompts. We choose those features when a building tells us a resident or frequent visitor has low vision, and we mount the panel where it can be found by touch, at a steady height beside the door handle. The release tone matters most, because it tells someone the door is ready to push.

Our rowhouse's old bell plate sits in a brownstone door surround that is cracked. Will you repair the stone when the new station goes in?
+

We are intercom installers, not masons, so we do not rebuild cracked stone. What we do is avoid making it worse: we mount the new station on sound material, use anchors suited to the stone or brick, and if the surround is too damaged to hold anything, we suggest an alternative spot beside it. We tell you before drilling if the stone looks unsafe, and we can leave the old plate's holes neatly filled and ready for a mason to restore when you schedule that work.
