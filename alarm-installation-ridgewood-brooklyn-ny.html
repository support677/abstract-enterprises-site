The Neighborhood

Why Ridgewood Properties Need Alarm Installation

Wikipedia's article on the neighborhood opens plainly: Ridgewood "is a neighborhood in the New York City borough of Queens." It borders Maspeth to the north, Middle Village to the east and Glendale to the southeast, all in Queens, and Bushwick to the southwest and East Williamsburg to the west, both in Brooklyn. The same article adds that "historically, the neighborhood straddled the Queens-Brooklyn boundary." The name was first applied by the Kings County government to an area inside Brooklyn, and the Dutch town of Bushwick and the English town of Newtown argued over the line until 1769, when it was drawn through a boulder now called Arbitration Rock, still on the grounds of the Vander Ende-Onderdonk House on Flushing Avenue.

That line ran on a diagonal. Houses built across it were sometimes taxed by both counties, Wikipedia records, and one might have Brooklyn's fire protection while its neighbor relied on volunteers. In 1925 the boundary was redrawn to follow streets instead, which is why it now zig-zags: from Newtown Creek along Onderdonk Avenue, Flushing Avenue, Cypress Avenue, Grove Street, St. Nicholas Avenue, Palmetto Street, Wyckoff Avenue, Covert Street and Irving Avenue, then into the cemeteries. That change moved 2,543 people's addresses from Queens to Brooklyn and 135 the other way. The mail took longer: routed through Brooklyn, given the Brooklyn ZIP Code 11237 in 1963, Ridgewood only received 11385 on January 13, 1980, after 93 percent of ballots returned to Representative Geraldine Ferraro's office asked for it.

For a burglar alarm, none of that is trivia. An alarm is a machine for sending one message to the right people at the right address, and Ridgewood is a place where the address is easy to get subtly wrong. Its police are the 104th Precinct, whose station house is at 64-02 Catalpa Avenue and whose area also takes in Glendale, Middle Village and Maspeth; across Wyckoff Avenue, Bushwick is a different precinct in a different borough. Most Ridgewood buildings carry a Queens-style hyphenated number, but Wikipedia notes a band of streets between Metropolitan Avenue, Forest Avenue and the Brooklyn line where the avenues that run parallel to the border never adopted the hyphen system, and where the numbers on cross streets jump at the county line. We come back to what that means for a monitoring account further down the page.

The second reason Ridgewood needs its own page is the building stock. Most of the neighborhood went up in a single burst between about 1905 and 1915, after a 1905 ordinance required new buildings to be masonry, and Wikipedia says more than five thousand buildings were put up between the start of the century and the First World War. Because whole blocks were built at once by a few builders and architects, Louis Berger & Co. above all, and have changed little since, Ridgewood still reads as rows of near-identical yellow-brick buildings: the six-family Mathews Model Flats nearest the Brooklyn border, two- and four-family Ring-Gibson houses with stores, the curved two-family Stier houses, and two-family and one-family rowhouses deeper into Queens. A six-family building is not a house and not an apartment. It has a cellar nobody lives in, a stair hall everybody walks through, a roof door at the top and a yard at the back, and the person who pays for protecting those spaces is usually the owner, who may live on the first floor.

The burglaries reported from here follow that geography. QNS reported on July 7, 2026, that in a single morning on June 17 a man broke into a first-floor apartment and then a second-floor apartment near 70th Street and Central Avenue by breaking window screens, and that afternoon went through a gate at a house near 72nd Street and broke a rear basement window, taking jewelry. In September 2023, QNS described two men working the 104th Precinct by night who tried the basement cellar gate of a pharmacy on Myrtle Avenue and the cellar doors of another on Flushing Avenue. In October 2017, Bushwick Daily reported a man who got into three Ridgewood apartment buildings over a month and rode off with bicycles from two of them. And on September 5, 2026, amNewYork reported, two men were held at gunpoint and bound with duct tape after the door of an apartment near Linden Street and Cypress Avenue was kicked in. Rear windows, cellar openings, common areas and apartment doors: most of these belong to the building, not to any one tenant.

The Six-Family Building

An Alarm for the Parts of a Ridgewood Flat-House Nobody Rents

The Mathews Model Flats are the reason Ridgewood looks the way it does. Wikipedia describes them as "a type of low-rise brick apartment house built in Queens," mostly between about 1908 and 1930, put up by Gustave X. Mathews and designed by the architect Louis Allmendinger. The standard building is three stories over a basement on a lot about 27.5 feet wide and 100 feet deep, with six apartments, one on each side of a central entrance on each floor, and an open rear yard. Each apartment had five rooms and a private bath, and narrow air shafts cut into the side walls brought daylight to every room. They were "cold-water flats": each was warmed by a coal range in the kitchen and a kerosene burner in the parlor, and central heat came to many buildings only in the 1930s. The fronts are buff and amber Kreischer brick with cast-stone trim. G.X. Mathews built and sold more than 300 of these tenements in Ridgewood between 1909 and 1912, and in 1915 the city's Tenement House Department displayed them at the Panama-Pacific International Exposition in San Francisco as the "most up-to-date method of housing for the masses at a minimum of cost."

The Landmarks Preservation Commission's report on the Ridgewood North Historic District, designated on September 15, 2009, describes 96 buildings of exactly this kind between Fairview Avenue, Woodbine Street, Forest Avenue, Gates Avenue, Grandview Avenue and Linden Street: buff and amber brick, heavy bluestone stoops, iron railings at the stoops and areaways, and, on the buildings facing Fairview and Grandview avenues, storefronts on the ground floor with apartments above. Corner buildings, Wikipedia adds, often had a store with four apartments above.

Look at that plan the way a burglar does and the weak points are not in the apartments at all. They are the shared front door and its vestibule, which open to anyone a tenant buzzes in. The stair hall, which runs from the cellar to the roof and connects every apartment door. The cellar, which holds the boiler, the meters, storage, often bicycles, and sometimes its own door to the yard or a set of steel doors in the sidewalk. The roof door or hatch at the top of the stair, which opens onto a roof that continues across the next building and the next, because the row was built in one piece. And the yard, which backs onto the yards of the buildings on the next street. A tenant can put an alarm in their own apartment, and many should. But no tenant's alarm watches the cellar, the hall or the roof, and those are the spaces the owner is responsible for.

So the alarm we plan for a Ridgewood six-family is usually two things on one panel. The first is the owner's building system: contacts on the cellar door, the yard door, any sidewalk cellar doors, the roof door or hatch, and, where the owner wants it, the street door; motion or glass-break in the cellar; and a panel and battery in a dry, locked spot in the cellar or the owner's own apartment. The second, where the owner lives in the building, is the owner's own apartment, as its own partition with its own codes. Tenants who want protection can join the panel as partitions with their own codes, or keep a separate system; nobody gets a code that turns off someone else's protection.

The cellar and roof are armed around the clock because, in most buildings, nobody but the owner, the super or a contractor has any reason to open them. The street door is the opposite: it opens dozens of times a day, and arming it would mean a siren every time a tenant came home. Its job is better done by the intercom and a camera, which is why a door buzzer repair in Ridgewood is often the first fix in a building where the street door does not latch.

The cellar is the room that matters most

In a Ridgewood flat-house the cellar is where the building's money is. The boiler and water heater, the electric and gas meters for each apartment, the main water valve, tenant storage and bicycles, the owner's tools and paint, and in a corner building the store's stock. It is also, in the reports above, the part burglars go for. A cellar on the alarm, with a sensor on every door and every window that opens to the yard or the areaway, protects the owner's property and the tenants' at the same time, and it is the one part of the building no tenant's system will ever cover.

Cellars are also where alarm equipment goes to die if it is put in the wrong place. Before anything is mounted we check where water has stood, where the boiler's heat and the laundry's damp collect, and where the cellular communicator gets a usable signal under a full building of brick. The panel and its battery go high on a dry wall, or in the owner's apartment with only sensors below. We prefer contacts and glass-break down there, and use motion only where it can be aimed away from the boiler and the laundry.

If the building's tenants have storage or bicycles in the cellar, the owner can also send the cellar-door alert to a second person, a super or a family member who lives nearby, so that a door opened at three in the morning is not discovered at eight.

The Address

A Queens Address on Blocks That Run Into Brooklyn

When a monitored alarm goes off, an operator at the central station reads the account on a screen and calls the police with the address that is written there. Nobody at that moment checks a map. In most of New York that is fine. In Ridgewood the account has to survive three local quirks, and we set every Ridgewood account up with all three in mind.

The hyphen. Queens house numbers are written with a hyphen: the part before it is the nearest cross street, the part after is the house. Ridgewood's own post offices show the system: the Fresh Pond Station is at 60-80 Woodbine Street and the Ridgewood Station at 60-60 Myrtle Avenue. Drop the hyphen and 60-80 becomes 6080, a number that does not exist on Woodbine Street. Some software strips punctuation. We type the number exactly as it appears on the building's tax bill and then read the saved account back to make sure the hyphen survived.

The streets that never took the hyphen. Wikipedia notes that while buildings on streets west of Forest Avenue and the Bay Ridge Branch use Queens numbering, the avenues in the band bounded by Metropolitan Avenue, Forest Avenue and the Brooklyn line, the ones running parallel to the border, do not, and that streets crossing the line show a jump in numbering between the boroughs. The third Ridgewood post office, the Wyckoff Heights Station, is at 86 Wyckoff Avenue, with no hyphen at all. A number without a hyphen looks like a Brooklyn number. On those blocks we put the borough, the ZIP Code and the nearest cross street in the account in so many words, so the address cannot be read as the same number on the Brooklyn side.

The name. Since 1980 the postal name has been Ridgewood, NY 11385. Before that, mail for the same buildings went to Brooklyn addresses, and Untapped New York describes buildings near the line that "display two addresses to ensure accurate mail delivery." Older insurance policies, leases and alarm contracts sometimes still carry the Brooklyn form. When we take over an existing system we check what the old monitoring company had on file before we change anything.

The fix is simple and takes a few minutes at the end of every installation: we place a test signal, the central station reads back the address, cross street, borough and precinct exactly as an operator would see them, and the owner hears it. If any of it is wrong, it is corrected before we leave.

What goes on a Ridgewood monitoring account

  • The number exactly as the city writes it, hyphen included, and the street spelled out in full.
  • "Ridgewood, Queens, NY 11385", with the word Queens written out, not only the postal name.
  • The nearest cross street and the side of the street, so a car sent at night stops at the right door.
  • A description of the building, because on a row of identical flat-houses the description is often what tells an officer which door: "third building from the corner, store on the ground floor, green door."
  • Which apartment or space the alarm protects, so that a cellar alarm in a six-family is not answered by knocking on a tenant's door.
  • Two people who can get in, with keys to the street door and the cellar, and who know their own passcode for the central station.

Building Specialists

Alarm Installation for Ridgewood's Architecture

Ridgewood was built in rows by a handful of builders working from a handful of plans, so the same building types repeat block after block. That is good news for an alarm: once we have planned one Mathews flat, the building next door usually has its cellar door, its stair and its roof hatch in exactly the same place. These are the types we meet most.

Mathews Model Flats and their copies

Three stories over a raised basement, six apartments, a central entrance with a bluestone stoop, air shafts on the sides and an open rear yard. The building system lives in the cellar and the stair hall; tenants' apartments are partitions or separate systems.

Corner flat-houses with a store

A store on the ground floor and four apartments above, as Wikipedia describes the corner buildings. The store is a separate partition, usually with a separate tenant, and the cellar is shared between the store's stock and the building's boiler.

Ring-Gibson two- and four-family houses

Smaller multi-family houses, some with stores, which Wikipedia lists among Ridgewood's centrally planned blocks. Two households, one cellar and one yard: two partitions on one panel is the usual answer.

Stier houses and two-family rowhouses

Two-family rowhouses, including the curved rows Wikipedia attributes to the builder Stier, deeper into Queens toward Fresh Pond Road. Front and back doors, cellar and the rear windows over the yard.

The Stockholm Street rowhouses

Thirty-six two-story brick rowhouses with two garages and a stable, built 1907 to 1910 on what the Landmarks Commission calls the only remaining brick street in Ridgewood. Small houses, tight fronts, and landmark rules on anything outside.

Older frame houses and later single-family homes

Wikipedia notes that most houses built before the 1905 masonry rule were wood frame, and that some later houses were single-family homes with garages. These get a house-style plan, with the garage door and the side path on the alarm.

Methods by building type

Six-family flats. Cable runs along the cellar ceiling and up the stair hall only where it can follow the stringer; elsewhere we use wireless contacts. The roof door or hatch gets a heavy-duty contact rated for a door that flexes in the wind, and, because the top of the stair is a long way from a cellar panel through brick and plaster, we test its signal before the cover goes on and add a repeater on the top landing if it needs one. Cellar windows get contacts if they open and glass-break if they do not.

Corner buildings with a store. The store is wired as its own partition with its own keypad by the store's back door, its own codes and, if the store tenant wants one, its own monitoring account. The shared cellar is split by use: if the store's stock is behind a partition wall, that space belongs to the store; the boiler room and the building's storage belong to the owner. Glass-break behind the storefront and a contact on the roll-down gate protect the street side.

Rowhouses. One- and two-family rowhouses in the north and east of the neighborhood are protected much as a house is: the front door, the back door, the cellar, and any rear window that can be reached from the yard, a fence or a kitchen extension roof.

In every type, the yellow brick is the surface we avoid. We mount on the wood of door frames, on plaster inside, and in mortar joints where something has to go on masonry, never through the face of a glazed brick or through the cast-stone trim that gives these rows their character.

Landmark Districts

Alarms on Blocks the Landmarks Commission Protects

A large part of Ridgewood is landmarked twice. Ten historic districts were added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1983, and the city's Landmarks Preservation Commission has designated four districts of its own: Stockholm Street in 2000, Ridgewood North in 2009, Ridgewood South in 2010, and Central Ridgewood in 2014, the largest, with 990 buildings, mostly brick rowhouses put up between 1906 and 1915 by small builders. Two buildings are individual city landmarks as well: the Vander Ende-Onderdonk House, designated in 1995, and the Ridgewood Theater building of 1916, designated in 2010.

Inside a city district, anything fixed to the outside of a building needs the Commission's permission, and the Commission's permit guidebook has a chapter on exactly the equipment an alarm job can involve. It says equipment "cannot be on or in front of a primary facade unless no other option exists," and where it must go there, it belongs in a discreet spot, "beneath a band course, underneath a stoop, behind an areaway wall or fence." It may be fixed "at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," but "not attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces." Conduit should go through the wall behind the equipment; if that is impossible, staff may approve "a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit," painted to match. For cameras on small residential and commercial buildings, staff can approve "no more than two security cameras … for every 25 feet of street frontage," and doorbells, intercom panels and light fixtures belong "within or adjacent to a door opening."

For a burglar alarm, almost all of this is easy to respect, because nearly everything an alarm needs is inside. Contacts, keypads, the panel, motion and glass-break sensors, and the interior siren never touch the facade. What does touch it is an outdoor siren or strobe, a camera, and any cable that has to reach them. Our default in a landmarked row is an outdoor siren at the rear, better heard from the yards anyway, and cameras under the stoop or at the doorway. Where an owner wants something on the front, we tell them before the job which permit it will need, and we do not mount it until they have it.

None of this is legal advice; the Commission's staff decide every application.

Commercial Alarm Installation

Business Alarm Installation in Ridgewood

Wikipedia describes Ridgewood's commerce as low-density districts along Myrtle, Forest and Metropolitan avenues and Fresh Pond Road. Most of those shops are on the ground floor of a building with apartments above, and most of them have a cellar, a back door to a yard and a roll-down gate at the front. So a Ridgewood store alarm has to be agreed with the building's owner and set with the people upstairs in mind.

Myrtle Avenue

The Myrtle Avenue Business Improvement District covers Myrtle and its side streets from Wyckoff Avenue to Fresh Pond Road and lists more than 300 retail businesses; QNS dates the BID to 1988. The Ridgewood Savings Bank's 1929 headquarters stands at Myrtle and Forest. Shops here sit under apartments, with cellars below.

Fresh Pond Road

One of the oldest roads in the neighborhood, a former Native American trail according to Wikipedia, with its own M train stop. ABC7 reported in May 2020 that a man broke into two Ridgewood stores five hours apart, smashing a glass front door on Fresh Pond Road and taking cash from the registers.

Forest Avenue

A north–south shopping street with an M stop where it meets the el, crossing Myrtle at the bank. Wikipedia notes several Serbian businesses here.

Myrtle–Wyckoff and Wyckoff Avenue

The transit hub where the M and L meet and the Ridgewood Terminal buses turn, on the Brooklyn line itself. Shops on the Wyckoff side may be in Brooklyn; we check which side of the avenue a store is on before we set up its account.

Seneca Avenue and the side streets

The M stops at Seneca Avenue, and smaller shops, delis and offices fill the ground floors of flat-houses on the streets around it. These are the shops most likely to share a cellar with a residential building's boiler.

Metropolitan Avenue and the north side

One of Ridgewood's oldest roads, laid out as a plank road. Wikipedia places the old factories and mills in the north of the neighborhood near Newtown Creek; the buildings that remain are larger, with loading doors and roofs that need their own plan.

The most useful local example for a store owner is the September 2023 pattern that QNS reported. Just before 4:30 a.m. on August 8, 2023, a man tried the basement cellar gate of a pharmacy at 67-02 Myrtle Avenue and could not get in; about half an hour later he tried the cellar doors of a pharmacy at 64-02 Flushing Avenue and gave up. In both cases the target was the cellar, reached from the sidewalk, not the glass front. On a Ridgewood store alarm the sidewalk cellar door is one of the first things we put a contact on, reporting even while the store is open.

Rules that apply to Ridgewood alarms

  • Siren limit. New York City Administrative Code § 24-238 requires an audible burglar alarm on a building to stop automatically within fifteen minutes. In a building with apartments above a store, we set it well inside that.
  • Installer licensing. The New York Department of State licenses security and fire alarm installers. Our license number, #12000287431, appears on every quote.
  • Detectors in rented apartments. A six-family is a multiple dwelling, and the city's housing department, HPD, places smoke and carbon monoxide detector duties on owners of rented units. A monitored detector in the cellar or hall adds to those duties; it does not replace the detectors each apartment must have.
  • Fire alarm systems. Larger buildings and some mixed-use buildings fall under fire-code requirements of their own, handled by fire alarm installation in Ridgewood.
  • Landmarks. Inside the four city historic districts, outdoor equipment on a building needs Commission approval, as explained above.
  • Permits. Outside the landmark rules, we know of no city fee an owner pays to put a burglar alarm in a building, and none appears on our quotes.

Alarm Installation in Ridgewood: Questions Answered

These are the questions owners of Ridgewood flat-houses, their tenants and the shopkeepers below them ask once they start thinking about a building rather than an apartment. General alarm questions are answered on the Brooklyn alarm installation hub; these are about Ridgewood.

Owning the whole building

I own a six-family flat in Ridgewood, live in one apartment and rent the other five. Which parts of the building are mine to alarm, and which belong to my tenants?

Everything outside the apartment doors is yours: the street door and vestibule, the stair hall, the cellar and everything in it, the roof door or hatch, the yard and any sidewalk cellar doors. Your own apartment is yours too. Each tenant's apartment, behind its door, is the tenant's home, and we do not put sensors inside it, or on its door, unless that tenant asks for them and holds the codes. In practice that gives you a building system covering the common spaces and your apartment, with the tenants free to add their own protection, either as separate partitions on your panel with codes that only work on their own door, or as a system of their own.

With five households using the front door and stairs day and night, what in a six-family can stay armed around the clock?

More than most owners expect. The cellar doors, the yard door, any sidewalk cellar doors and the roof door or hatch are opened by almost nobody except you, a super or a contractor, so they can be armed permanently and switched off only with a code. The stair hall and the street door cannot: they are in constant use, and arming them would mean a siren every time a tenant came home with groceries. For the hall, the useful tools are a camera at the street door, a door that actually latches, and, if you want it, a chime or a text when the street door is held open for longer than a minute or two at night.

Our cellar has a boarded-up opening in the front wall from the days when the building burned coal. Should an old opening like that be on the alarm?

Yes, if anything at all could move it. Ridgewood's flat-houses were heated by coal ranges and kerosene burners until central heat arrived, Wikipedia says, mostly in the 1930s, and cellars of that era often have openings at the front or under the stoop that were later boarded or bricked over. A properly bricked opening is just wall; a board over a hole, a rusted steel plate or a little door behind the areaway railings is a way in. At the survey we look at each one from both sides. Where it can be sealed properly we suggest that first; where it cannot, it gets a contact or a shock sensor, named in the app so an alert says exactly which opening moved.

The front, the back and the row

Our six-family sits in the middle of a row, and the yards at the back meet the yards of the buildings on the next street. How does an intruder actually reach the back of a row like this?

Over the fences. Mathews flats were built on lots about 100 feet deep with an open yard behind each, and a row of them backs onto another row, so the back of a building is reachable from a dozen other yards and from every building whose cellar or back door opens onto them. The June 2026 burglar QNS described went through a gate and broke a rear basement window; the windows he went for on the same block were first- and second-floor ones. On the back of a flat-house we put contacts on the yard door and every cellar window, glass-break where windows do not open, and, if the owner wants early warning, a beam or an outdoor motion detector aimed along the yard rather than across it, so neighbors' cats and the yard next door do not trigger it.

The raised basement apartment in our building sits half below the sidewalk behind the iron railings. How is a unit at that level protected?

Its front windows are at the height of a passer-by's knees, behind an areaway that the Landmarks Commission's Ridgewood North report describes with stoop and areaway ironwork, and they are the easiest windows in the building to reach unseen at night. If the basement unit is rented, its windows are the tenant's, and the tenant should know that they are the first ones we would cover in that apartment: contacts on every window that opens, glass-break covering the ones that do not, and window gates or bars only of a kind that can be opened from inside without a key. If the basement is the owner's own space, or empty, we put it on the building system. The areaway gate itself can take a contact too, so that someone stepping down from the sidewalk is noticed before they reach the glass.

Every building on our street looks the same from the sidewalk. How do the police know which door to go to at night?

This is a real problem in Ridgewood, because the rows were built to one design and many buildings are indistinguishable at 2 a.m. The central station passes on whatever the account says, so we write more than the number: cross street, side, buildings from the corner, store or not, door color. We also make sure the house number is visible from the street at night, and on buildings outside the landmark districts, or with the Commission's approval inside them, an outdoor strobe at the back flashing during an alarm helps anyone coming through the yards pick out the right building.

Our block of flats was built in one go by the same builder, so every building's cellar door, hall and roof hatch are in the same place. Does that make a burglar's job easier?

In one way, yes: anyone who has been inside one Mathews flat knows the layout of every other building on the row, and continuous roofs lead from one hatch to the next. The bicycle thief Bushwick Daily described in 2017 got into three Ridgewood apartment buildings over a month, which is the kind of repetition a uniform row invites. In another way it helps you: neighbors who own identical buildings can protect them identically, and a roof-hatch contact on every building in a row means a hatch forced anywhere along the roofs is noticed.

Stores, accounts and people at home

Our corner building has a store leased to a tenant with its own alarm company, and four apartments above. How do the two systems avoid stepping on each other?

By agreeing on a line before either is installed. The store's alarm covers the store: its front glass, its gate, its back door and whatever part of the cellar is the store's. The building's alarm covers everything that is not the store: the street door to the apartments, the stair, the roof, the yard and the building's part of the cellar. Where the cellar is shared, decide in writing whose system protects it, and put a contact on any door between the two spaces. What causes trouble is two sirens on the same cellar door, or neither.

In September 2026 an apartment door near Linden Street and Cypress Avenue was kicked in while two men were inside. What can an alarm do when the intruder comes in while you are home?

It can make the moment the door gives way the moment help is called. amNewYork reported that the men were held at gunpoint and tied up with duct tape, which is a robbery, not a burglary, and no alarm stops a determined armed intruder. What a system can do is this: with the apartment door on an instant zone whenever the household is home, the siren sounds and the central station is signaled as the door frame splits, before anyone is in the room. A panic button and a duress code that disarms while silently reporting let someone send for help unseen. And because the weak point in that report was the door itself, we also look at the frame, the strike and the lock: a reinforced strike plate and longer screws are cheap, and they buy the seconds an alarm needs.

How can we check, before there is ever a real alarm, that the central station has our building recorded correctly?

Ask for the read-back test we do at the end of every Ridgewood installation, and repeat it once a year. With the account in test mode, a signal is sent from the panel, and the operator reads back what they would give the police: the hyphenated number, the street, "Ridgewood, Queens," the ZIP Code, the cross street, the description of the building and which space the alarm covers. You listen and correct anything that is wrong. Repeat it whenever the store tenant, the call list or the building changes.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Alarm Installation in Ridgewood

We could not open Google's AI Overview, People Also Ask, Answer the Public or Yelp from the environment this page was written in, so we are not quoting any particular AI answer. What follows are generic claims that AI summaries and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr commonly make about home alarms, each set against what we find in Ridgewood buildings.

"Ridgewood is a Brooklyn neighborhood"

Plenty of directories still say so, and the history explains why. But the city, the Post Office and Wikipedia all put Ridgewood in Queens, and its police are the 104th Precinct.

For an alarm account it matters, because the account is what the central station reads to the police. A Ridgewood building recorded as "Brooklyn" with its hyphen dropped is an address that may not exist, or may exist somewhere else.

That is why our Ridgewood accounts carry the word Queens, the ZIP Code and the cross street, and why we read them back with the central station before we leave.

"An alarm system protects your home"

Every generic description of an alarm imagines one household behind one front door. In a Ridgewood six-family there are six households, one stair, one cellar and one roof, and the question that decides the design is who is responsible for which space.

An apartment kit does not see the cellar door, the roof hatch or the yard, which are the parts the local reports keep returning to.

So the first question we ask a Ridgewood owner is not how many sensors they want, but which spaces are theirs.

"Wireless systems install in an hour with no drilling"

Cost sites often present wireless alarms as quick, tidy jobs, and in a single apartment they can be. In a three-story building of brick with plaster walls and a panel in the cellar, the distance and the masonry between a roof-hatch sensor and the panel are a real engineering question.

We test the signal from the top landing, from the roof door and from the yard before we commit to a panel location, and add a repeater where the test says one is needed.

Wireless is still right for most of a Ridgewood building; it is just not an hour's work.

"Put sensors on every door and window"

This is the standard advice, and in a single house it is roughly right. In a six-family it is wrong in both directions: too much in the halls, where sensors on the street door would sound every time a tenant came home, and too little in the cellar, where advice written for houses rarely mentions sidewalk cellar doors, old boarded openings or the areaway.

The 2023 pharmacy attempts on Myrtle and Flushing avenues were aimed at cellar gates and cellar doors. The June 2026 morning ended at a rear basement window.

Our coverage list for a flat-house starts in the cellar and works up, and it leaves the street door to the intercom and a camera unless the owner specifically wants it armed at night.

"Any installer can put up an outdoor siren and cameras"

Not on roughly thirteen hundred Ridgewood buildings, which sit inside the Landmarks Preservation Commission's four historic districts. There, equipment on the outside needs the Commission's permission, and its guidebook keeps it off primary facades unless there is no alternative and limits small buildings to two cameras per twenty-five feet of frontage.

A siren box drilled into cast-stone trim can leave the owner with a violation and a repair that cannot easily be matched.

We put outdoor sirens and strobes at the back of landmarked rows, bring cameras in at the doorway or under the stoop, and tell the owner before the job about anything that will need a permit.

"Professional monitoring means police arrive quickly"

Generated answers often present monitoring as a direct line to the police. A central station is people reading screens and making phone calls; how quickly anyone arrives depends on the police, on what else is happening in the precinct, and on whether the address and description the operator reads out lead straight to the right door.

The 104th Precinct covers Ridgewood, Glendale, Middle Village and Maspeth from its station house on Catalpa Avenue. We make no promise about response times; nobody honestly can.

What an owner can control is everything before the call: a clear account, a checked address, two keyholders nearby, and sensors on the openings that matter.

"Alarm installation costs $X nationally"

National averages on cost sites mix single-family houses in the suburbs with city apartments, and none of them describes a six-family with a cellar, a roof hatch and a store.

Our own prices for Ridgewood are the published Brooklyn list, because the neighborhood is served from our Brooklyn office. The per-unit line of that list, $400 to $800 for each apartment set up with a door sensor, motion detector and monitoring, is there for owners who want every apartment covered; the common-space system alone usually lands in the wireless range of $600 to $1,200 or above it, depending on the count.

The only reliable number is the one written down after a technician has been in the cellar and on the stair.

How It Works

Our Installation Process

A single apartment and a whole six-family go through the same seven stages. What makes a Ridgewood job different is that the survey starts in the cellar and ends on the roof, and that the job is not finished until the central station has read the address back.

1. Talk first, about the building

A phone call about how many apartments there are, whether the owner lives in one, whether there is a store, who has keys to the cellar and the roof, and who should get alerts, followed by four photos: the front, the cellar door, the roof door or hatch and the back from the yard.

2. Survey from the cellar up

A technician meets the owner at the cellar door, checks every opening to the outside, the damp, the boiler and the cellular signal, then climbs the stair, tests a sensor at the roof door or hatch and finishes in the yard.

3. A one-page plan

Before equipment is chosen, the technician writes down which spaces are the owner's, which are a store's and which are offered to tenants, what is armed around the clock, and who hears which alert.

4. Written price the same day

Each cellar opening, the roof contact and any repeater, every partition and any old wiring that tests good become lines on a Brooklyn-list quote, handed over in writing at the end of the visit.

5. Install the cellar, roof and partitions

Contacts and glass-break in the cellar, the yard door and any sidewalk doors, the roof contact and repeater, the panel in a dry high spot, and keypads for the owner and any store or tenant partition.

6. Program, test and read back the address

Modes, codes, temporary contractor codes and the alert list are set and tested opening by opening, and the central station reads back the hyphenated address, Queens, the cross street and the building description.

7. Hand over, then come back

Each person gets a code or the app, the owner gets a one-page sheet for the cellar door, and a return visit a few weeks later adjusts anything the building's first month has shown.

Twice a year: a twenty-minute building check

Put the system in test mode. Open the cellar door, the yard door, the sidewalk cellar doors and the roof door one at a time and confirm each one reports by name. Look at the battery status of the roof contact and any repeater, which work hardest in cold weather. Check that the people on the call list still have keys and the right phone numbers. Delete the codes of contractors who have finished and tenants who have moved. And once a year, ask the central station to read the address back again.

Ridgewood Alarm Installation FAQ

Does a Ridgewood job cost more than a Brooklyn one because the building is in Queens?
+

No. Ridgewood is served from our Brooklyn office on Troy Avenue, and we quote it on the same Brooklyn price list we use for Bushwick across the line: from $600 for a basic system, $600 to $1,200 for a typical wireless system, from $2,500 for complete protection, plus sales tax. What changes the price is the count of openings and partitions in your building, not which side of Wyckoff Avenue it stands on.

If I alarm only the shared parts of my six-family — the cellar, the halls, the roof door and the yard door — which line of your price list does that fall under?
+

Usually the wireless range of $600 to $1,200, because the common spaces of a flat-house are a cellar door or two, the cellar windows, the yard door and the roof door, with a repeater if the roof is out of range. A building with sidewalk cellar doors, many cellar windows or a smoke and carbon monoxide detector on the panel can go above that range, and adding your own apartment as a partition moves it toward the complete-protection figure from $2,500. The written price after the survey lists each line.

We are buying a six-family in Ridgewood and close next month. Can you look at the building before the closing so the alarm is in our budget?
+

Yes, if the seller agrees to let us in. A survey before closing is the same visit as any other: cellar, stair, roof and yard, with a written price at the end. It also tells you whether an old alarm in the building is worth keeping, so you know whether you are budgeting for a takeover or a new system. We need the seller's or the agent's permission and someone to open the doors.

Can the roof door get an alarm contact without turning it into a locked door that tenants cannot use in a fire?
+

Yes. An alarm contact only reports that a door has opened; it does not lock anything, so it never changes how a roof door works as a way out. What must not happen is anyone adding a lock or a chain that stops the door being opened from inside in an emergency. If the owner wants the roof door to sound locally when it opens, we fit an alarm that sounds without restricting the door, and we leave the question of locking hardware to the building's fire-safety obligations.

I spend a month abroad every summer. Can one of my tenants be the person the central station calls about the building while I am away?
+

Yes, if the tenant agrees and has keys to the street door and the cellar. They become a keyholder on the call list with their own passcode for the central station and, if you want, an app login that sees only the building's spaces, never your apartment. They do not need a code that disarms anything; a keyholder's job is to meet the police at the door. When you are back, you can take them off the list from your phone.

Where should the keypad for the building system go: at the front door, at the cellar door, or in my apartment?
+

Wherever the owner actually enters the protected spaces. If you live in the building, the usual place is just inside your own apartment door, with the building and your home as two buttons on the same keypad. If you live elsewhere, it goes at the cellar door, on the inside, where only someone who has already unlocked the cellar can reach it. We avoid putting a building keypad in the front hall, where every tenant and visitor walks past it.

Should my tenants be told before the technician starts working in the halls and the cellar?
+

We recommend it, and we can give you a short notice to post in the hall. Most of the work is in the cellar and on the top landing, and the noise is a drill for a few minutes at a time, but tenants who see a stranger on the roof stair will reasonably worry. A notice with the date, the company name and the technician's name also lets a tenant check who is in the building, which is exactly the habit you want.

Our building already has a video intercom at the street door. What does an alarm add to that?
+

The intercom controls who is let in at the front. The alarm watches everything else: the cellar doors, the yard door, the sidewalk doors and the roof, which no intercom sees, and it reports at night when nobody is answering the intercom at all. The two work well together, and many owners put the intercom's camera and the alarm in the same app so that a cellar alert can be checked against who came through the front door.

Each of our six apartments has its own electric meter. Whose electricity runs the building alarm?
+

The owner's. The panel and any repeater are plugged into an outlet on the building's house circuit, the one that lights the hall and the cellar, never into a tenant's apartment, so a tenant cannot switch it off and does not pay for it. A panel draws very little, and its backup battery carries it through an outage. If the cellar has no house-circuit outlet in a sensible place, an electrician adds one before we mount the panel.

A tenant has asked whether their Ring doorbell on the apartment door can join the building's system. Can it?
+

It can share an app with the building if both are from compatible makers, but we usually keep it separate. The doorbell is the tenant's: it records their callers and their comings and goings, which the owner has no business seeing. The building system covers the common spaces. If the tenant wants more, they can have their own partition on the panel with codes that only they hold, or install their doorbell alongside it independently; either way, neither side sees the other's alerts.
