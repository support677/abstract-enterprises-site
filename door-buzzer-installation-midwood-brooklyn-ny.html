The Neighborhood

Why Midwood Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Midwood sits in the middle of Brooklyn, south of Flatbush and Ditmas Park and north of Homecrest and Sheepshead Bay, roughly between Ocean Parkway and the blocks east of Ocean Avenue. When the Department of City Planning rezoned about 80 of its blocks in 2006, between Avenue H, Ocean Avenue, Avenue P and Kings Highway, and Coney Island Avenue, it described the area plainly: mostly two- and three-story "detached and semidetached houses," with attached row houses on some blocks, and apartment buildings "generally four to eight stories" clustered along Avenue K, Ocean Avenue, Kings Highway and parts of Avenue N. The commercial streets it named were Coney Island Avenue, Avenue M between Coney Island and Ocean avenues, and Avenue J from Coney Island Avenue to East 16th Street. That is the map a buzzer installer works from here, and each part of it produces a different kind of job.

The houses come first. Bisnow's profile of the neighborhood put it in one line: "There are few rental buildings, with most rentals residing in multifamily homes." In practice that means a two-family or a three-family on East 18th or East 23rd Street, where the owner lives on one floor, a tenant or a married child lives on another, and everybody shares a porch door or a front vestibule. A single mechanical bell or a single chime in the front hall cannot tell the two households apart, so the tenant upstairs either hears every ring meant for the owner or none at all, and somebody walks down a flight to open the door. One Brownstoner thread about a three-family house began with exactly that situation after a renovation: the owner wanted "to install a new bell and intercom system" because a contractor had knocked out the working one. Another, about a three-family, came from an owner who wanted to buzz people in remotely and learned that "Hardwired systems must be installed by a low voltage electrician."

The apartment buildings come next. The Brownstoner record for 1710 Avenue H describes "the six-story, 37-unit elevator building" from about 1937 with "an elaborate entrance with crenellated top and arched door." CityRealty lists 1947 Ocean Avenue, between Avenue N and Avenue O, as a 1927 co-op with six floors and 77 apartments. A Brownstoner listing at 1600 Ocean Parkway, where Ocean Parkway meets Avenue P, from 1939, mentioned "an intercom phone for visitors" in its Art Deco lobby. Buildings of that age and size have usually lived through two or three generations of entry equipment, and what is in the lobby now is often a mixture: a panel from one decade, handsets in the apartments from several, and a transformer somewhere in the cellar that nobody has looked at since it was plugged in. A tenant review of a Homecrest building on East 14th Street, headed "Midwood needs help," complained that the exterminator "rarely rings the tenants bells," which is a small reminder that in a building where the buttons do not work, the people who need to get in simply stop trying.

Then there is the calendar. Bisnow described Midwood as largely Orthodox Jewish, with Jewish bakeries, bagel shops, food stores and kosher restaurants on Avenues M and J, and noted that most shops there close on Friday evening and all day Saturday for the Sabbath. Many households here do not operate electric devices on Shabbat and Jewish holidays, and that changes how a street door should work, how a door station's camera is placed, and when an installer can even be in the building. Habitat magazine reported in July 2024 on a co-op shareholder who was "forbidden from using electric devices on the Sabbath" and faced an "electronic keypad beside the front door that requires residents to enter a code to open the door," and an attorney it quoted warned that a co-op that "flatly rejected your request" could face a discrimination claim. In Midwood, a buzzer that works only by pressing a button is a design mistake, not a feature.

Finally, the doors themselves. In May 2018, CBS New York reported that police said a man "followed the 81-year-old victim into the building where he lives" at East 10th Street and Foster Avenue in Midwood and attacked him. The report does not say how the door was opened, and a buzzer alone does not stop someone walking in behind a resident. But an entry system that lets residents see and talk to a visitor before releasing the door, and a door that closes and latches by itself every time, take away the two easiest ways in: the door propped for a delivery and the door that never quite shuts.

Repair or Replace in Midwood

Need Repair in Midwood? Keep the Riser, Replace the Ends, or Start Over: How We Decide Before Quoting

Most Midwood calls that start with "we need a new intercom" turn out to be one of three jobs. The first is a system with one failed part: a transformer that has given out, a strike worn past adjusting, a door panel with a dead speaker, or a handful of apartment handsets that stopped working while the rest carry on. The second is a system that still works but no longer suits the building: a co-op whose handsets are a mixture of three brands and nobody can buy parts for any of them, a two-family that was one home when the bell was installed, or a lobby whose residents now want to answer on their phones. The third is a building or house where there is nothing to keep, because the old wire was cut in a renovation, the panel was removed, or there was never anything beyond a mechanical bell.

Photos usually tell us which group a building belongs to. Send a picture of the entrance from the sidewalk, a close-up of the panel or button, a photo of any apartment station you can reach and, if you can get to it, the cellar where the old equipment lives, to (347) 934-8335 or on WhatsApp. Where a repair and a replacement are both reasonable, you get both prices in writing and a plain recommendation. We will not sell a new system to a building that one part would fix.

ButterflyMX's October 2026 guide for small buildings puts the principle plainly: "Start with diagnosis before buying equipment." Brick Underground's intercom repair guide, quoting Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith, explains why: "It's all in the wire connections," and a total system overhaul is "unlikely" to be needed. Detached two- and three-family houses: one porch door, separate households

The most common Midwood buzzer job is a detached house with two or three households and one way in. Typically the owner lives on the ground floor, a tenant or a relative lives upstairs, and sometimes a third apartment sits on the top floor or in a finished basement. The porch door opens into a front hall with the owner's apartment door on one side and the stair on the other. Everyone passes through the same two doors.

Each household needs its own button, its own answering station and the ability to talk to a visitor and release the street door without involving the other. The design decision is which door the system releases. In most houses it is the porch or front door, with the apartment doors off the hall staying on their own keys. In some, the owner prefers that the tenant's button release only the hall door at the foot of the stair, so the owner's part of the front hall stays private. We settle that before anything is priced, because it changes where the strike goes and how much cable is needed.

The mistake we see most is a pair of store-bought video doorbells, one for each household, stuck side by side on the porch. Each family then has a camera and a chime but no release, so somebody still walks down to open the door, and the porch door ends up unlocked during the day for deliveries. A proper two- or three-button system with a strike is what lets the family upstairs answer its own door.

Semi-detached and attached rows: a party wall and a short run

Semi-detached pairs and attached rows are usually brick, with a small stoop or porch and a cellar that runs the depth of the house. The distance from the front door to the cellar is short, which helps: a pair can drop from the door station through the floor of the vestibule, run along the cellar ceiling and rise to the upstairs apartment through a closet or the back of the stair. On houses with a masonry front, the door station goes on the wood frame or the plain masonry beside the door, never across the joint with the house next door.

The thing to watch is ownership. A semi-detached pair often has two owners, and the cable, the transformer and the strike should stay entirely on one side of the party wall. We do not run a buzzer through a neighbor's cellar, even when it would be shorter, because the next renovation next door can cut it and servicing it then depends on someone else's goodwill.

Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park houses: wood porches and a Landmarks rulebook

The houses in the historic district are free-standing, many with wood porches, bay windows and dormers, in Arts and Crafts, Colonial Revival and Dutch Colonial Revival styles. The Historic Districts Council's record of the district says "The overwhelming majority of houses were constructed and occupied by 1914." Whether a house holds one household or two, the doorway rules are the same. Because the district is landmarked, a new intercom panel generally needs a Landmarks permit, which the Commission's staff can issue when the installation meets its criteria. The panel goes on or beside the door opening, the finish is neutral, and the cable is concealed.

On a wood porch, concealment is easier than on a brownstone. A pair can run down inside the door casing, through the porch floor and into the cellar, with nothing visible but the station. The practical question is what to fasten the station to. Wood trim is acceptable to the Commission; turned columns, brackets and decorative moldings are not places to drill. We measure the casing before choosing a station, because the narrowest stations fit where a standard panel would overhang.

Prewar elevator co-ops: an old riser, a lobby and a board

The six-story buildings on Ocean Avenue, Avenue K, Avenue H and Avenue N were built with a lobby, a street door, an elevator and, in buildings like 1600 Ocean Parkway, a lobby intercom phone for visitors. 1717 Avenue N, with a 1937 certificate of occupancy and architects Kavy & Kavovitt, is described by Brownstoner as a "prewar elevator apartment" with an Art Deco entrance. In these buildings the cable from the lobby to every apartment already exists, usually in a riser that runs up a stair hall or a pipe chase, and the first question is whether it can carry a new system.

We test every pair before promising anything. Aiphone describes its GT Series as a "multi-tenant intercom system for new builds and retrofit projects" with the advantage of "using existing wire and infrastructure," and its upgrade guidance says that "If the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused." Where a pair fails, we replace that one run, often in a small painted raceway along a hall ceiling, rather than rewiring the building. The board receives a written price that separates the shared parts, the lobby panel, the strike, the closer and the power, from the work inside each apartment, so it can be split the way the proprietary lease says.

The common mistake is replacing the lobby panel alone with something modern and leaving sixty handsets of mixed ages upstairs. A new panel that does not speak the old handsets' language leaves the building with a new face and the same complaints. Either the panel and stations are chosen to work together, or the handsets are replaced in the same job.

Postwar co-ops near Ocean Parkway: staff at the desk, and the hours when there is none

Larger buildings from around 1950, such as the six-story, 84-unit co-op at 1119 Ocean Parkway with a doorman, elevators and a live-in super, add a staffed lobby to the mix. The entry system there has two jobs. When the desk is staffed, visitors may be announced by the doorman. When it is not, the panel has to reach every apartment directly. A building that assumes the desk will always be covered usually ends up with a panel nobody has tested in years.

We plan for both modes: a lobby panel that calls apartments, a desk station that can call any apartment and release the door, and a super's station or phone that does the same after hours. Package handling, which is where most lobby trouble starts, is part of the plan from the first visit.

Shop-and-apartment buildings: the residential door beside the store

On Avenue J, Avenue M, Kings Highway and Coney Island Avenue, the typical building has a store across most of the ground floor and a narrow residential door at one side leading straight to a stair. The apartments above need a buzzer; the business often needs its own for a back or side delivery door; and the two must never be able to release each other's doors.

We keep them as two separate systems even when their cables share a cellar. The residential panel calls only the apartments and its power supply sits on the landlord's circuit. The store's station calls the counter or the manager's phone and releases only the store's door. Clear labels in the cellar show which is which, so the next electrician to work for the store does not disconnect the tenants' buzzer.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ask Who Lives Behind the Door, Ask What Day It Is, Then Follow the Old Pair to the Cellar

These notes describe how our technicians approach a buzzer installation in a Midwood house or apartment building. They are our standard practice, not a story about any one address.

Ask who lives behind the door. Before touching anything, the technician asks the owner or the super who actually comes and goes through this door. In a house, that means how many households, which floors each one uses, whether a married child or a tenant has the top floor, and whether there is a basement apartment with its own side entrance. In an apartment building, it means how many apartments are occupied, whether any have been combined, who handles packages, and whether a doorman or a super is ever at the desk. The old bell plate or the lobby directory is compared with those answers, and in a building whose directory was last updated years ago, the answers win.

Ask what day it is. In Midwood, the technician also asks about the calendar. Does anyone in the house or building keep Shabbat? Should installation days avoid Friday afternoon, Saturday and the holidays? Should the street door keep a key cylinder that works without any electricity? Is there a mezuzah on the doorpost where a station would usually go? These questions take a minute and save a reinstallation. A system that the household cannot use on the one day a week it most wants the door to work is not finished, however neat the wiring.

Read the door. Next comes the door itself. Does it swing shut and latch on its own, as HPD requires of entrance doors in apartment buildings? Is it wood, steel, or aluminum and glass? Is the frame sound enough for a strike, or has it been patched after old break-ins? Is there a storm door or an outer porch door in front of it, and which one should the visitor stand at? In the historic district, where is the plain wood casing that a station can be fixed to, and where are the turned columns and moldings that must not be drilled?

Follow the old pair to the cellar. Last, the technician goes downstairs and finds the transformer and the old cable. Where does it rise: a stair hall, a pipe chase, a closet stack, the back of a kitchen? Was it cut in a renovation upstairs? Each surviving pair is tested. The transformer's outlet is traced, because a buzzer plugged into a tenant's outlet or a super's apartment goes dead whenever that circuit is switched off.

Everything from that visit goes on the written price: the button count, the station on each floor or in each apartment, the closer, the strike, the pairs that will be reused and the ones that will be replaced, the Landmarks details if the house is in the district, the days we will and will not work, and the route the new cable takes. Nothing is discovered on installation day.

Shabbat and Yom Tov

A Street Door That Works on Shabbat: Planning a Midwood Entry System for Observant Households

No other part of a Midwood buzzer job is asked about less and matters more. A large share of the neighborhood's residents are Orthodox, and many do not operate electric devices from Friday evening to Saturday night or on Jewish holidays. For them, a street door that opens only when someone presses a release button, enters a code on a keypad or holds a fob to a reader is a door that does not open on Shabbat. That is not a small inconvenience. It can mean waiting outside for a neighbor, leaving the door unlocked for a day, or asking a non-Jewish neighbor to let them in. We plan every Midwood system with that in mind, whether or not the person ordering it asks, and we take our guidance on what is permitted from the household and its rabbi, not from ourselves.

What halachic sources say about building entrances. Peninei Halakha, a widely used work of Jewish law, has a section on intercoms, security monitors and doorbells at building entrances. It says a Jew should not press the intercom to ask a guard to open the door, describes waiting out of a camera's range and knocking so a guard sees and opens, allows following a non-Jew who is entering, and records a dispute over ringing an electric doorbell, which some permit only under pressing circumstances and with a change in the usual manner and others forbid. It also discusses whether a Jew may stand where a security camera will record him. Different communities and rabbis rule differently, and we do not offer rulings. What these sources make clear for an installer is that the push button, the keypad, the camera and the motion-activated light are each a separate question, and a well-planned door gives a household choices on all four.

Keep a key that needs no electricity. The single most important decision is to keep a mechanical way in. With an electric strike, the door's own lock and key keep working exactly as before; the strike only adds a second way to release the latch. With a fail-secure strike, the door stays locked when the strike has no power and still opens from outside with a key and from inside with the lever. That means a resident can come home on Shabbat, use a key and walk in without touching anything electric. Habitat magazine's July 2024 article on co-ops and religious accommodation described a shareholder facing an electronic keypad as the only way in, and noted that a New York Times column on the same question said a request for a side-door key should generally be honored unless that entrance poses a credible danger. Attorney Andrew Lieb told Habitat: "If the co-op flatly rejected your request, you can sue for discrimination because they are effectively making the building unavailable to observant Jews." For a board, the cheapest way to avoid that argument is a key cylinder on the door from the start.

Be careful with fobs and readers as the only way in. In 2015, VIN News reported that Orthodox Jewish residents of LeFrak City in Queens sued over new key fobs "which provide entry for renters through an electronic signal" and over motion-sensor stairwell lights. A building that replaces keys entirely with fobs creates the same problem at the street door. Fobs and codes are useful for the other six days, and for staff and contractors, but in Midwood we recommend them alongside keys, never instead of them.

Think about the camera and the lights. A video door station turns on its camera when someone presses its button. Some stations also have motion sensors that wake the screen or switch on an infrared light when anyone approaches, and some entrance cameras record continuously. For a household that wants to avoid triggering devices on Shabbat, the station can be set to wake only when its button is pressed, the porch light can be put on a timer set before Shabbat, and an entrance camera can be placed so that a resident using the key does not walk straight into its field of view. These are settings and placement choices, and we make them with the household.

Leaving the door unlocked is the riskiest answer. Some buildings have dealt with Shabbat by leaving the street door unlocked for the day. In 2016, CBS New York reported on a Crown Heights building on President Street whose front door "was left unlocked for the Sabbath," where residents said observant tenants did not use electronics and surveillance video showed a man following a young woman into the lobby. That was not Midwood, and it is not a reason to fear a whole day of the week. It is a reason to keep a key cylinder on the door, so the door can stay locked on Shabbat like any other day.

We do not book installation days in observant households or buildings on Friday afternoons, on Shabbat or on Jewish holidays unless the household asks us to, and we leave the street door locking normally before any of those begin. Landmarked Porches

The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District on March 18, 2008. Its houses, in the Historic Districts Council's description, are "characteristic of suburban residences erected by builders between 1890 and 1930," in Arts and Crafts, Colonial Revival and gambrel-roofed Dutch Colonial Revival styles. For a buzzer installer, that means free-standing houses with porches, wood trim around paneled front doors, and owners who rightly care about how the front of the house looks. It also means a permit for most new door stations. What follows is how we read the Commission's rules for a buzzer job. It is not legal advice, and the Commission answers questions directly at info@lpc.nyc.gov.

New panels can be approved at staff level. The Commission's Permit Guidebook chapter on health, safety and utility equipment says: "Staff can issue permits approving light fixtures and intercom panels if the installation meets the following criteria." That avoids a public hearing for an ordinary door station. The criteria are about where the panel goes, how big it is, what it is fastened to and what it looks like, not about whether the technology is audio, video or app-based.

Location: at the door. The guidebook says intercom panels and doorbells "must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening." An intercom may also go on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway. On a Midwood porch house, that usually means the wood casing beside the front door. Where the porch is deep and the door is hard to see from the walk, a station on the porch side of the casing, facing the steps, keeps it within the rule and where visitors expect it.

Quantity: one panel per door opening. A house that wants a button for each of two households at the front door gets one panel with two buttons, not two panels. A house whose second household uses a side door can have a station at each opening, each calling the right apartment. We plan for this from the first sketch, because owners who add a second doorbell camera beside an existing station are often surprised to learn it may not fit the rule.

Size and finish: compatible and discreet. The guidebook asks that the "Design, size, and finish of installations must be compatible with the architecture of the building," and that equipment be as small as possible and blend with the surrounding material where it can. On painted wood trim, a slim station in a neutral finish, or painted to match where the manufacturer allows, reads as part of the door. A large stainless panel does not.

What we submit. The Commission asks for manufacturer cut sheets for intercoms, and elevation drawings are not required for an in-kind replacement. No permit is needed for ordinary maintenance and repair. So the existing button or plate matters: replacing a dead station with a similar one in the same place is a simpler matter than adding a new one where nothing has been. We photograph and measure what is there before proposing anything, prepare the cut sheets and photos the application needs, and do not mount a new panel on a district house until the owner has the permit.

Cable inside, not on the porch. On a wood-frame house, the cleanest route is down through the casing or behind the trim, through the porch floor or the foundation, and into the cellar. That leaves nothing visible but the station itself, which is exactly what the Commission wants and what most owners want too. Where an old bell wire already comes through the casing, we test it first; a sound old pair is a route nobody will ever see.

Prewar Lobbies

The lobby panel goes where the old one was, if it can. The cleanest installation in an ornamental lobby reuses the old panel's opening, or covers it with a trim plate, so no new hole goes into stone, tile or decorative plaster. Habitat magazine's 2017 article on new buzzers quoted Dan Arnold of Academy Mailbox on the easy case: "If it's sheetrock, you can make a few holes and snake it down to the basement." He added that marble or granite makes the work harder, though nothing is insurmountable. In a Midwood prewar lobby, the walls are rarely sheetrock, which is one more reason the existing opening and the existing cable path are worth so much.

The riser decides the system. In most of these buildings the cable from the lobby to the apartments rises in a single path, often along the stair hall or in a pipe chase, and branches off to each apartment on its floor. If every pair tests sound, a two-wire system can run on it, and the job is mostly at the two ends: the lobby panel and the apartment stations. In a co-op of 37 or 77 apartments, that is a schedule, not a single day. Where the old and new systems can run side by side, we install the new riser connections and apartment stations first and switch the lobby over last, so residents are never without a way to answer the door for more than the time it takes to swap the panel. Where they cannot, the switchover is done in the shortest possible window, announced to residents beforehand, and the street door keeps locking throughout.

A Midwood co-op deciding between audio and video should weigh that. Video lets a resident on the sixth floor see whether the person who said "delivery" is holding a box; audio leaves them guessing. Where a building wants couriers kept out of the main lobby altogether, the panel can be set up to release only a vestibule or package area.

The Rules

Self-Closing Doors, Section 50-a and the Duty to Keep It Working: What New York Expects of Midwood Owners and Boards

Midwood's buildings run from single-family houses that no entry rule touches to co-ops with dozens of apartments, so the rules land differently from one door to the next. Here is how they apply in plain terms. None of it is legal advice.

Larger buildings must have talk and release. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law applies in New York City, and its §50-a requires every class A multiple dwelling built or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments to have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets tenants talk with a visitor and release the door. Older buildings of that size must install both when tenants occupying a majority of the apartments ask or consent in writing; the work has to begin within 90 days and be finished within six months. Most Midwood two- and three-family houses are well under eight apartments, so a buzzer there is a choice. Most of the apartment buildings on Ocean Avenue, Avenue K and Kings Highway are well over it.

Doors have to close and latch by themselves. HPD's guidance says: "Apartment doors and hallway doors are required by law to swing closed and latch by themselves." Its inspectors check self-closing doors in the public areas they pass through, including the building entrance, and a self-closing door violation "must be corrected within 14 days from receipt of the Notice of Violation by the property owner." Under Local Law 71 of 2022, HPD also selects 300 class A multiple dwellings every year for proactive self-closing door inspections. For a buzzer installation, that makes the closer part of the job. A strike that releases perfectly on a door that drifts open behind every visitor is not a working entry.

A broken intercom is a housing violation, not an inconvenience. Habitat magazine's February 2024 article on co-op intercoms said buildings built or converted after 1968 with eight or more units must have a two-way voice intercom and that "The city's housing maintenance code also requires a functioning intercom." Nancy Kourland of the Lasser Law Group told Habitat: "As a result, the co-op has a legal obligation to repair or replace the intercom." The remedies the article listed were a written demand to the board, a 311 complaint and an HP proceeding in housing court. ButterflyMX's summary of HPD practice gives the general correction periods: 90 days for class A violations, 30 days for class B and 24 hours for most class C conditions. If your building has received a violation, tell us its class and date when you ask for a quote, because the deadline shapes the schedule.

Everyone must be able to leave. Any door with a buzzer still has to open from the inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. With an electric strike that is automatic: Allegion's guide explains that "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. Magnetic locks hold the whole door, so the building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT" 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, release when power is lost or the fire alarm or sprinklers activate, and hardware listed to UL 294. On Midwood house doors and lobby doors we use strikes almost without exception, and a strike also keeps the mechanical key working, which matters on Shabbat.

Landmarks applies on top, in the district. For houses in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park Historic District, the Commission's rules on panel location, quantity, finish and concealed cable apply in addition to everything above. Outside the district, there is no Landmarks step.

Rules that apply to Midwood business entry installations

  • Free exit at all times. Every business door with a buzzer, code or reader must still open from inside without a key or code; an electric strike keeps the inside lever working, so it is our default.
  • Sensor release for magnetic locks. Where a magnetic lock is the only workable option, the building code calls for an egress-side sensor, a push-to-exit device near the door, release on power loss and fire alarm activation, and UL 294 listed hardware.
  • Fire doors take fail-secure hardware. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes so they stay latched in a fire; stair and corridor doors in Midwood's larger mixed-use buildings are often rated, and we check the label before choosing hardware.
  • Accessible reach. Buttons, keypads and readers on public doors stay between 15 and 48 inches above the floor, inside the 2010 ADA Standards' reach range.
  • Landlord approval in writing. Work on a shared street or corridor door starts only once the landlord or managing agent has approved it in writing.
  • Two systems, two power supplies. Where a store and apartments share a building, each system gets its own supply on its own circuit, labeled in the cellar, so turning off the store's power never silences the tenants' buzzer.

If a business also wants a camera on its entrance, it can be installed on the same visit and priced with the buzzer, which saves a second trip and a second set of holes in the frame.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Midwood: Questions Answered

Questions Midwood owners, boards, tenants and shopkeepers bring to us, together with ones raised in Brownstoner forum threads, Habitat magazine, Brick Underground, halachic guidance on building entrances and the city's own rules, answered for the houses and buildings here.

Co-ops and apartment buildings on the avenues

Our 1920s Ocean Avenue co-op still has the lobby wall niche where an old intercom phone once hung. Can the new panel be built into that niche?

Often it can, and it is usually the best spot in the lobby. A niche that once held a house phone already has a cable path behind it, it is out of the way of the door swing, and it was designed into the lobby, so a panel set there looks as if it belongs. We measure the niche's depth and width before choosing the panel, because a flush-mount panel needs more depth than a surface-mount one, and the back of an old niche is often plaster on masonry rather than an open cavity.

Two checks decide it. First, the niche has to be where a visitor arriving at the door will see it and be able to reach the buttons, which for public use means between 15 and 48 inches off the floor under the 2010 ADA Standards. A niche placed for a doorman's convenience inside the inner lobby may be the wrong side of the door for an unattended panel. Second, the panel's camera, if it has one, has to see a visitor's face from where they will stand. Where the niche fails either test, we use it for the panel's power and wiring and mount the panel itself beside the door.

Our co-op board was told that any app-based intercom needs a new data cable pulled all the way to the front door. Is that always true?

No, though it is true often enough that boards hear it as a rule. Habitat magazine's 2017 article on new buzzers noted that many smartphone-compatible systems "rely on internet cables and often need new wiring at the building entrance." Some panels do need a network cable at the door. Others connect over cellular service and need only power and the door release. Still others are hybrid systems that run on the building's existing two-wire riser and add phone answering through a gateway in the cellar, so the door needs no new data cable at all.

For a Midwood prewar co-op, the hybrid route is often the most economical, because it keeps the old riser working for the apartment stations and adds phone answering for residents who want it. A cellular panel suits a door where running any cable is hard, but the signal has to be tested at the mounting point before anyone relies on it; a recessed lobby entrance between masonry walls can be a weak spot. We price the options side by side so the board can see what each one actually requires.

The transformer for our building's old intercom is plugged into an outlet inside the super's apartment. Should the new power supply go there too?

No. A buzzer's power supply should sit on a circuit the building controls, in a common space such as the cellar or a utility room, not in anyone's apartment. A supply plugged into an outlet in an apartment goes dead whenever that apartment's breaker trips, its power is shut off for work, or the occupant unplugs it to use the outlet, and the whole building loses its entry system without anyone knowing why. It also means the super's apartment has to be entered whenever the system is serviced.

We move the supply to a common location on its own labeled circuit, with battery backup so the system rides through a short outage. If there is no suitable outlet in the cellar, a licensed electrician adds one before we install; that is named on the written price beforehand. We do not do 120-volt electrical work ourselves.

The apartment handsets in our six-story building are a mixture of brands from different decades, because broken ones were swapped one at a time. Does that mean the system cannot be extended?

Usually it means the system should not be extended any further. A mixture of handset brands on one riser is a sign that each repair was done with whatever part was available, and that the system is now held together by compatibility nobody can guarantee. Adding more of the same, or a new lobby panel on top of it, tends to produce intermittent faults: some apartments ring, some do not, some can talk but cannot release.

The better path in most Midwood co-ops is to keep what is good about the building's system, which is normally the riser cable, and replace both ends together: a new lobby panel and matching apartment stations designed to work on that cable. The board then has one system, one manufacturer's parts and one set of instructions for residents. Where the budget does not allow every apartment at once, we can plan a system whose new stations work alongside the old ones for a short period, but we say plainly how long that arrangement is likely to hold.

Our building's old intercom has a separate wire for every apartment rather than a shared pair. Does that make reusing the cable easier or harder?

It depends on what you install next, and it is worth knowing before the quote. Older audio systems were often wired with a multi-conductor cable or a bundle in which each apartment had its own wires back to the lobby panel. That cable is plentiful and usually in better shape than people expect, because it carries very little current. Modern two-wire systems need just one good pair per apartment, so a building wired this way often has spare conductors to choose from when one tests poorly.

Where it gets harder is in the lobby and the cellar, where all those individual wires meet. Every one has to be identified, tested and relabeled, and in a building with dozens of apartments that is a careful morning's work. We do it before the price is final, because the result tells us how many runs can be reused and how many have to be replaced, and that is most of the difference between a modest job and an expensive one.

Houses on the side streets

The right-hand doorpost of our front door already holds a mezuzah, exactly where an installer wants to put the door station. Where does the station go instead?

The mezuzah stays where it is, and the station goes elsewhere. In most Midwood houses that means the opposite side of the door casing, the wall beside the door on the latch side if the mezuzah is on the hinge side, or a position lower on the same post where it does not crowd or cover the mezuzah. We ask about the mezuzah at the first visit, look at where visitors approach from, and choose a slim station that fits the space left. If the household's rabbi has a view on spacing, we follow it.

The same care applies inside. Apartment stations are often mounted beside an apartment's entrance door, which also carries a mezuzah, and in houses with several interior doorways we check each one. None of this adds cost; it simply means the layout is drawn with the mezuzah in it from the start rather than discovered when the drill is already in hand.

Our house in the Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park district has a wood porch with turned columns and no masonry anywhere near the front door. What can a door station be fastened to?

The plain wood trim around the door is usually the answer. The Landmarks Commission's guidebook asks that intercom panels go "within or adjacent to a door opening" and that installations be compatible with the building's architecture, discreet and as small as possible. On a porch house, the flat face of the door casing or a plain board beside it meets those terms. Turned columns, brackets, balusters and decorative moldings are the parts of a porch that give the house its character, and they are the parts we never drill.

We measure the casing first, because some Midwood porch casings are narrow, and choose the slimmest station that fits. Its cable runs inside the casing or behind the trim, down through the porch floor and into the cellar, so nothing shows. The permit application includes the manufacturer's cut sheet and photos of the doorway, and the station goes up only after the permit is issued.

Shabbat and holidays

Will the camera on a new video door station record an observant resident walking past it on Shabbat, and can it be set up so it does not?

It depends on the station and how it is configured, and most can be set up to avoid it. A typical video door station turns on its camera only when its call button is pressed, so a resident who walks past and uses a key does not wake it. Some stations, though, have motion detection that wakes the screen or starts recording when anyone approaches, and some buildings add a separate entrance camera that records all the time. Those are the devices that raise the question.

For a household or building that wants to avoid them, we disable motion wake on the station, put any entrance camera where a resident using the key is not walking straight into its view, and set the porch or vestibule light on a timer set before Shabbat rather than a motion sensor. Peninei Halakha's discussion of building entrances treats standing where a security camera will record oneself as a question in its own right, and communities rule differently on it, so we set up the devices the way the household or board asks rather than deciding for them.

Some buildings leave the street door unlocked for Shabbat so observant residents can get in. Is that safer or riskier than other arrangements?

Riskier, and it is not necessary. A door left unlocked for a day lets in anyone at all, and the other residents of the building may not even know it is open. In 2016 CBS New York reported on a Crown Heights building whose front door "was left unlocked for the Sabbath," where surveillance video showed a man following a woman into the lobby. That is the scenario a working entry system is supposed to prevent.

The safer arrangement is a street door that stays locked every day of the week and opens for observant residents with an ordinary mechanical key. An electric strike leaves the door's own lock and key working, so the buzzer and the key coexist on the same door. Habitat's 2024 reporting suggests that boards are expected to make that kind of key access available, so in Midwood we plan it from the start rather than waiting for a request.

Shops and mixed buildings

Couriers keep arriving on Saturday while our Avenue J store is closed for Shabbat. What should our delivery buzzer do then?

Nothing that opens the door. The delivery station can ring unanswered, or a phone-answering station can be set so that it does not forward calls during the hours the store is closed. The door stays locked on its strike, with no timed or remote release, and a small sign at the station giving the store's hours saves couriers a wasted ring. Most regular carriers learn the hours within a few weeks.

The arrangement to avoid is any that depends on someone remembering to lock the back door on Friday afternoon. With a closer that pulls the door shut and a fail-secure strike that keeps it latched without power, the door locks itself every time it closes, so the store is secure over Shabbat whether or not the last person out thought about it.

Our Coney Island Avenue building has a store on the ground floor and four apartments above, all sharing one cellar. When the store's lease turns over, does anything on the apartment buzzer need to change?

It should not, if the two systems were installed separately, and that is the reason we install them that way. When a store changes hands, the new tenant's contractors often clear out the old tenant's equipment in the cellar, and if the apartments' buzzer transformer is plugged into the store's outlet, or its cable shares a junction box with the store's wiring, it goes into the dumpster with everything else. The apartments find out the next time someone rings.

The protection is simple: the residential system's power supply on the landlord's own circuit, its cable in its own path, and clear labels in the cellar reading which equipment belongs to the apartments. If your building's buzzer currently runs off the store's power, the turnover is a good moment to separate it.

Rules and inspections

HPD now picks 300 buildings a year for proactive self-closing door inspections. If ours is chosen soon after a new buzzer goes in, what about the entrance will the inspector actually look at?

Mainly the door, not the buzzer. HPD's guidance describes inspectors checking that apartment doors and hallway doors, including the building entrance, "swing closed and latch by themselves." For the street door, that means the closer, the hinges and the latch, and whether the door completes its swing and catches every time without anyone pushing it. A new strike that holds the latch properly helps; a strike installed on a door whose closer is weak or whose frame is out of line does not fix anything.

So when we install a buzzer in a Midwood apartment building, the closer is adjusted or replaced as part of the job, the strike is aligned with the latch, and we test the door closing from fully open several times before we leave. If the door was already failing to latch before the installation, we say so on the written price and include the closer work, because a self-closing violation has to be corrected within 14 days of the notice.

A Midwood two-family needs something different, a button for each household, an answering point on each floor and an electric strike so the upstairs family can open the front door. That is a buzzer, and its cost depends on the door, the frame and the cable route, not on the price of a chime.

Comparing the two leads owners to buy a doorbell when they need a buzzer, and then to wonder why the tenant upstairs still walks down to open the door.

"A smartphone intercom costs $5,000 to $7,000 to install, plus a small monthly fee per unit"

Habitat magazine's 2017 article on new buzzers gave about $5,000 to $7,000 to install a ButterflyMX system, with a monthly charge of $2.50 per unit, as an example from one building.

That was one product in one building nine years ago. A Midwood co-op's cost depends on how many apartments it has, whether its riser can be reused, how many entrances the panel must cover and whether the board wants app answering for everyone or only for some. Subscription terms also vary from product to product and change over time.

A board should compare written quotes for its own building, with any recurring fees listed separately, rather than a figure from an article about another one.

"Wire a modern buzzer by making a few holes and snaking the cable down to the basement"

Habitat quoted Dan Arnold of Academy Mailbox: "If it's sheetrock, you can make a few holes and snake it down to the basement."

That is true of sheetrock, and Arnold himself said marble or granite makes the work harder. Midwood's prewar apartment buildings have plaster on masonry, terrazzo floors and ornamental lobbies, and its older houses have plaster on lath. A cable route through those walls is planned around closets, pipe chases and stair halls, not improvised through the front rooms.

The cheapest route is usually the one that already exists, which is why we test the old riser and the old bell pair before proposing new cable anywhere.

"Hardwired systems must be installed by a low-voltage electrician"

A reply in a Brownstoner thread on smart buzzers for a three-family house said exactly that.

The spirit is right: a hardwired buzzer is a job for a licensed professional. The detail is more specific. Entry systems are low-voltage work, which AESS does under New York State license #12000287431. Any new 120-volt outlet or circuit for the power supply is separate electrical work for a licensed electrician.

In Midwood houses and co-ops the low-voltage side is almost all of the job, and when a new outlet is needed, it is named on the written price before work begins.

"A modern intercom requires no building wiring"

ButterflyMX describes its intercom as weatherproof, vandal-resistant and requiring "no building wiring," and cellular call boxes are widely sold as needing no data cable at the door.

The panel may need no data cable, but it still needs power, a strike in a sound frame and a closer that shuts the door. In a co-op, residents who want a wired station in their apartment, because they do not own a smartphone or do not want to depend on one, still need a pair to that apartment.

And in a building with Shabbat-observant residents, an app-only system makes no difference to the people who will not use it on Shabbat. The key cylinder on the door matters more to them than any app.

"Electric strikes on double doors mean opening up the walls quite a bit"

In a 2007 Brownstoner thread about glass-paneled double doors, a renovator who had done two sets warned: "You'll need to open up the walls, probably quite a bit," with a channel behind the astragal where the leaves meet.

That describes how many older installations were done. In Midwood, paired doors turn up at the entrances of some prewar apartment buildings and porch houses, and the release can often go into the inactive leaf with its wire brought through a power transfer hinge, or onto an electrified lock body that avoids cutting the astragal at all.

Where the doors are too fragile or valuable to alter, the release can move to an inner vestibule door, with the outer pair on a key. The right answer comes from looking at the doors, not from a forum thread about someone else's.

"A virtual doorman can replace the buzzer"

Habitat's 2017 article cited about $15,000 a year for a call-center virtual doorman in a 28-unit building, and some summaries present such services as a substitute for an entry system.

A virtual doorman is a staffing service that still relies on a panel, a camera, a strike and a closer at the door. It adds people watching remotely; it does not remove the hardware, and it does not change §50-a's requirement that residents of covered buildings be able to talk with a visitor and release the door from their apartments.

For most Midwood co-ops, a well-installed panel with video and apartment stations, and a super's station for after hours, gives residents what they need without an annual staffing contract.

FAQ

Midwood Door Buzzer Installation FAQ

Our building has one entrance on Ocean Avenue and another on the side street. Can both door stations call the same apartments, and does the side door need its own release?
+

Yes to both. Two door stations can call the same apartments, and each apartment's release opens the door the call came from, so a resident never unlocks the wrong entrance. The side door needs its own strike, closer and wiring back to the power supply. If residents rarely use the side door, it can instead be kept on a key and left off the system, which costs less; we price both so the board can choose.

Can the new apartment stations in our co-op go into the same wall openings the old handsets used, so no apartment gets fresh holes?
+

Usually, yes. We note the size and position of the old handsets during the walkthrough and choose stations whose mounting plates cover the old openings. Where a new station is smaller than the old handset, a trim plate hides the difference, and the cable already in the wall is reused if it tests sound. Apartments whose old handset was removed years ago are the exception and are named on the price.

Do you install door buzzers in Ditmas Park, Fiske Terrace, Beverley Square and East Flatbush as well as Midwood?
+

Yes. Those neighborhoods are served at the same Brooklyn rate as Midwood, as are Flatbush, Homecrest and Flatlands. Crews are dispatched from our Troy Avenue office in Brooklyn, and the same planning, written pricing and testing apply wherever the house or building is.

Can one household keep a wired station on the wall and also answer the door on a phone?
+

Yes, and in a two-family it is often the best arrangement. The wired station keeps working when the internet is down and needs no phone, while the app lets the household answer from another room or from work. Many systems ring both at once, and either one can talk to the visitor and release the door. We set up the app for each household before we leave.

A resident of our co-op uses a wheelchair and struggles with the heavy lobby door after buzzing a visitor in. Can the entry system work with an automatic door opener?
+

Yes. An automatic door operator is a separate device mounted on the door, but it can be connected so that pressing release on an apartment station both unlocks the strike and opens the door. We coordinate with a door-hardware specialist for the operator itself and handle the connection to the buzzer. The closer and the operator have to be matched, so this is planned with the board before anything is ordered.

Will an electric strike make our lobby door harder to push open for residents carrying shopping or a stroller?
+

No. A strike only releases the latch; it does not change how heavy the door feels. What decides that is the closer, and an old closer set too strong is the usual reason a lobby door is hard to open. When we install a strike, we adjust or replace the closer so the door opens without strain but still swings shut and latches every time.

Another company installed our building's buzzer two years ago and it has never worked right. Will you take over a system you did not install?
+

Yes. We start by testing what is there, because an unreliable new system often has a correctable cause, such as a power supply too small for the number of stations, a strike not aligned with the latch or a poor connection in the cellar. You get a written report and price for putting it right. If the equipment itself is unsuitable for the building, we say so and quote the alternative.

Can the speaker volume on our lobby panel be turned down at night so it does not echo up the stairwell?
+

Usually, yes. Most current panels let the speaker and ringback volume be set separately, and some allow different levels by time of day. We set the volume during installation so a visitor can hear the resident clearly at the door without the sound carrying up an open prewar stair. Apartment stations have their own ring volume that each resident controls.

What should an observant household tell the technician before the walkthrough?
+

Which days and hours to avoid, including Friday afternoons and holidays; whether there is a mezuzah where a station might go; whether anyone wants the camera to stay off unless the button is pressed; and whether the street door must keep a working key. With that, the layout and settings are planned around the household from the first drawing, and installation days are booked around the calendar.

Is there anything we should not do to the old system before the walkthrough?
+

Please do not cut, remove or disconnect anything, even wires that seem dead. The old panel, handsets, transformer and cable tell us which pairs go where, and a pair that looks unused may be the one that can carry the new system. If something is unsafe, such as a loose cover or exposed wiring, tell us and we will deal with it at the visit.

Our board wants residents to keep a working buzzer until the very last day. Can the old and new systems run side by side for a while?
+

Often, yes. Where the riser has spare pairs, or where the new system can be wired in parallel, we install the new apartment stations first while the old system carries on, and switch the lobby over last. Residents then lose the ability to answer only for the short time it takes to swap the panel. Where the old and new systems cannot share the wiring, we announce a short switchover window in advance and keep the street door locking throughout.

Do we get a record of which wire goes where once the job is done?
+

Yes. Every button, pair and station is labeled in the cellar and at the panel, and the owner or board receives a written wiring record and a test sheet showing each apartment or household called from the sidewalk, answered and released. That record is what lets the next technician, ours or anyone else's, service the system without guessing.
