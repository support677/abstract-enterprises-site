The Neighborhood

Why Vinegar Hill Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Vinegar Hill is one of the smallest neighborhoods in Brooklyn, about six blocks squeezed between DUMBO, the Brooklyn Navy Yard fence and the Con Edison site on the river, with Farragut Houses and the expressway to the south. Its oldest streets were made a city historic district on January 14, 1997, in three separate pieces: the Front Street row from 225 to 249 with 202 and 204 Front, the houses at 69 to 77 Gold Street with 280 to 286 Water Street, and the Hudson Avenue blocks from 49 to 79 with 312 Water Street. What matters for a buzzer is not the history itself but what it left at the doors.

Most of the houses here were built for one family or for a family over a shop. The Landmarks Preservation Commission's designation report describes Hudson Avenue as a busy commercial strip in the nineteenth century, and notes that newcomers in the 1970s converted most of the storefronts to residential use. Brownstoner's Suzanne Spellen described the typical older building here as three stories of brick with two floors of apartments over a storefront. So a house that now holds two or three households often still has the front door of a one-family house or a shop, with a doorknocker, a single bell button or nothing at all. When the house is divided, sold or renovated, the owner suddenly needs a button for each household, a way to talk to the visitor and a release that opens the street door from upstairs.

The second thing that shapes the work is that so many of those doorways sit inside the historic district. A new intercom panel on a landmarked building needs a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The Commission's Permit Guidebook lets its staff approve one if it is installed "within or adjacent to a door opening," with "a maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel" per door opening, in a design, size and finish that is "neutral and discreet." The same chapter says equipment "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces," which rules out the obvious spot on some Front Street houses whose original cast-iron fences and gates are named in the designation report. Getting the panel position right before anything is bought is a large part of a Vinegar Hill job.

The third is construction. Vinegar Hill's houses are being renovated, and city records show it. When we searched the Department of Buildings' DOB NOW permits on November 11, 2026, 69 Hudson Avenue had an interior renovation permit issued in December 2024 with an estimated cost of $268,000, covering new ceiling finishes and kitchen and bathroom renovations, plus structural work to sister old floor joists. 59 Hudson Avenue had a permit issued in September 2025 for a new bathroom on its second, third and fourth floors. 54 Hudson Avenue had permits in 2025 for alterations to its commercial space. Every one of those jobs is a moment when ceilings and walls are open and buzzer cable can reach every floor for a fraction of the cost of fishing it later.

Six facts that shape a Vinegar Hill buzzer installation

  • The district covers three separate clusters. The 1997 designation takes in parts of Front Street, Gold and Water Streets and Hudson Avenue; a house one door outside those boundaries follows different rules from its neighbor.
  • Landmarks staff approve one intercom panel per door opening. It goes within or beside the opening, or on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway, in a neutral, discreet finish.
  • Low-voltage buzzer wiring needs no electrical permit. NYC Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 exempts wiring designed to run under 50 volts, which covers the buzzer itself, though not a new 120-volt outlet for its power supply.
  • Renovations are open right now. DOB NOW shows permits at 69, 59 and 54 Hudson Avenue between 2024 and 2025, the cheapest moments to wire a buzzer to every floor.
  • The condos are crossing the eight-unit line. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom with voice and remote release in post-1968 buildings of eight or more apartments, which reaches the 2002 and 2005 condos but not a three-family row house.
  • The towers next door are NYCHA's. Farragut Houses' entry doors and intercoms are managed by NYCHA, which reported completing security upgrades there in 2023 that included new intercoms.

Repair or Replace in Vinegar Hill

Need Repair in Vinegar Hill? Keep the Old Bell, Replace the Plate or Install a New System

Plenty of Vinegar Hill installation calls start as repair calls. A bell that went quiet after a kitchen was gutted, a voice intercom in a 2002 condo whose handsets are no longer made, or a two-family house where the upstairs tenant has been coming downstairs to open the door for years. Before any equipment is chosen, we decide which of three paths fits, and we say so plainly.

The first path is a repair, when one part is the whole problem: a bell transformer that has failed, a strike that has worked loose in an old wooden frame, a cracked button. The second is a partial replacement: a new door station and power supply on bell wire that still tests sound, keeping the indoor chimes or handsets that work. The third is a full installation, for houses that never had more than a doorknocker, houses whose wire was lost in a renovation, and condos ready to move from voice to video. If repair is all your house needs, we price it as a repair, and our Brooklyn door buzzer team handles both kinds of work.

Buildings whose ground floor was a store until the 1970s conversions, with the street door still set in the old shopfront between display windows or bulkheads.

The 1870s apartment house

Four-story Italianate buildings like 312 Water Street, built as apartments, with first-story security grilles and a fire escape, and several households behind one door.

Condos built since 2002

Greek Revival brick row houses: a stoop, a narrow opening and a district that watches the doorway

The houses on the north side of Front Street between 225 and 249, and the row at 69 to 77 Gold Street, are what most people picture when they think of Vinegar Hill. Their doorways are narrow, set in brick, and often reached by a stoop with an iron railing. Most were built for one family. Today many are divided into a parlor-floor owner's apartment and one or two rentals above, or into floor-through apartments, and the front door has had at most a single bell button added sometime in the last century.

The mistake here is buying the panel first. A three-button station that fits a doorway in a newer building may be too wide for the brick pier beside a Greek Revival door, and a surface-mounted box with conduit running down the brick to the cellar is exactly what the Landmarks guidebook steers away from. We start with the opening: is there room within it, on the reveal or the frame, for a slim station? Is there plain brick beside it where the station can be anchored into mortar joints? Can the cable go straight back through the masonry into the front hall, so that no more than a few inches of conduit, if any, shows outside? Only once those answers are known do we choose the station.

Inside, the work is usually easy. These houses have a straight stair and a front hall, and cable from the door can drop into the cellar and rise through the stair wall or a closet to each floor. Where a parlor floor has original plaster and moldings, we route through the cellar and up behind cabinetry or inside closets rather than cutting the front parlor.

Wood-frame houses: a different wall, a different anchor

The designation report records several frame houses on Hudson Avenue: 51, a clapboard house built around 1840 to 1847 by a carpenter and shipbuilder; 53, a wood-frame house with a brick front; 55, a frame house with a brick facade and an exposed clapboard side wall; and 70, with clapboard above a stucco base. On a frame house, the material beside the door may be painted wood trim rather than brick, and the frame of the door itself is often softer and older than a steel or hardwood frame in a newer building.

That changes two things. The station is screwed into solid wood trim or blocking, never into thin siding that will split, and the Landmarks guidebook allows equipment to be attached at wood as well as at mortar joints and plain masonry. And the electric strike goes into a frame that may need a hardwood block or a reinforcing plate behind it before it can hold. We look at the frame closely on the walkthrough, and if it needs reinforcing, that is a named line on the quote, not a surprise on install day.

Houses with a former shop floor: the street door opens into a living room

Brownstoner noted that the storefronts on these houses survive "in varying degrees of original authenticity." Some still have their display windows behind brick bulkheads, like 55 Hudson Avenue; others had the shop entrance sealed, like the first story of 53 Hudson Avenue closed with cinder block. Where the former shop door is still the way into the house, it often opens straight into what is now a living room or kitchen, with no vestibule between the sidewalk and the household.

For those houses we almost always recommend video rather than audio. If pressing release lets a visitor step directly into your front room, you want to see who it is first, and a camera at the door shows the whole person and the sidewalk behind them. The station can usually sit on the wooden pier or frame of the old shop entrance, which counts as wood or metalwork rather than a decorative surface, and the cable runs back through the frame into the room.

The 1870s apartment house: several households, one door and the eight-unit line

312 Water Street, described in the designation report as a four-story Italianate apartment house of around 1869 to 1876, with first-story security grilles and a fire escape, is the kind of building that was meant for several households from the start. A building like this may be well under the eight-apartment line in Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, so no law forces an intercom, yet its tenants expect one. If old bell wire already reaches each apartment, we meter it before deciding whether a two-wire system can reuse it.

Lofts, the firehouse and shops: a garage door, a loading door and a dining room

The former firehouse at 225–227 Front Street, Constitution Engine Co. No. 7, has a replacement wooden garage door in what was the apparatus opening, according to the designation report, and was converted to housing in 1976. The old factory and warehouse buildings toward Bridge Street and Plymouth Street now hold apartments, studios and offices. And Hudson Avenue's restaurants, Vinegar Hill House at 72 Hudson since 2008 and Café Gitane at 70 Hudson since 2018, have back and side doors that take deliveries. Each of these needs its own entry plan: a personnel door beside a garage door, a loft lobby with a directory, a delivery door with a buzzer kept apart from any apartment upstairs.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Measure the Doorway, Find the Wall Behind It, Then Choose the Station

This is how our technician spends the first part of a visit to a Vinegar Hill house, before any equipment is chosen.

First, the doorway. With a tape, the technician measures the reveal inside the opening, the face of the frame and any plain brick or wood beside it, and photographs each surface. On a district house those three numbers decide which stations can be considered at all, because the panel has to fit within or beside the opening without covering a decorative surface. A station that is a quarter-inch too wide for the frame is a station we do not order.

Second, the wall behind. From inside the front hall, the technician checks what is directly behind the chosen spot: a plaster wall with a cavity, solid brick, a stair stringer or a radiator. The best position outside is useless if the cable has to come out of the wall into a finished parlor. We look for the spot where the cable can pass straight through the masonry and land inside a closet, a stair wall or the cellar ceiling.

Third, the route down and up. In the cellar, the technician finds the best place for the power supply, near an outlet and away from damp walls, and traces how a cable can rise to each floor: alongside the stair, through a closet stacked above another closet, or with the plumbing if a renovation has opened a chase. On a house that is mid-renovation, this step is a conversation with the general contractor about when each ceiling closes.

The Historic District

A New Door Station on a Landmarked Vinegar Hill Doorway: What Landmarks Staff Approve and What We Prepare

Installing a buzzer where there was none before is a new piece of equipment on the facade, and inside the Vinegar Hill Historic District that is work the Landmarks Preservation Commission reviews. The good news is that the Commission has written down what its staff can approve without a public hearing, and a carefully planned door station fits inside those rules. The Commission's Permit Guidebook, in its chapter on health, safety and utility equipment, sets out the criteria.

  • Where the panel goes. "Light fixtures and intercom panels, as well as doorbells, must be installed within or adjacent to a door opening." The guidebook adds that "an intercom can also be installed on a fence or wall in front of the entranceway."
  • How many. "A maximum of two light fixtures and one intercom panel can be installed per door opening." One door, one panel, whatever the number of households.
  • What it looks like. The "design, size, and finish of installations must be compatible with the architecture of the building (i.e., neutral and discreet)."
  • What it is attached to. Equipment "must be attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood, or metalwork," and "must not be attached through cast iron or on any decorative surfaces."
  • How the cable travels. Conduit "must be concealed by going through plain masonry or wall cladding behind the installation." If that is impossible, "staff may approve a maximum of approximately ten inches of visible conduit," painted to match the surface behind it.
  • What is filed. A complete application includes manufacturer cut sheets and, at visible facades, color specifications or paint cards.

Two more points from the Commission matter to Vinegar Hill owners. The guidebook says a permit is not required for "ordinary maintenance, repair, and retrofitting" of health, safety and utility equipment, so fixing an existing bell is a different matter from adding a new panel. And the Commission's permit-types page says that "while there is no fee to submit an application, there are fees to cover costs associated with issuing permits for certain kinds of work"; the city's business portal lists the Permit for Minor Work as no fee. What kind of permit a given doorway needs is the Commission's decision, made on the application.

Where the Front Street fences come in

The designation report describes an original cast-iron fence and gate at 237 and 239 Front Street, with a wrought-iron gate under the stoop. A fence in front of the entranceway is one of the places the guidebook allows an intercom, but the same chapter forbids attaching equipment through cast iron. So on a house with an original cast-iron fence, the fence is not the easy answer it looks like, and the station usually belongs in or beside the doorway instead. On a house whose areaway fence is a later replacement in plain metal, the answer may be different. We point out which is which on the walkthrough, and the Commission's staff make the final call.

What we prepare, and what stays with the owner

  • We prepare the manufacturer cut sheet for the station, a photo of the doorway marked with the exact position, the finish we propose, and a short description of how the cable enters the building and how much conduit, if any, will show.
  • The owner files. The permit belongs to the building, and the owner, or the architect or expediter already working on a renovation, files it with the Commission.
  • We wait for the permit. The station goes on the doorway once the owner has the approval. Indoor cable, the power supply and indoor stations can often be installed first, so the job finishes quickly when the permit arrives.
  • Nothing is ordered on a guess. If the Commission's staff ask for a different finish or position, we change the order before it is placed, which is why we do not order a doorway station until the plan is agreed.
Renovations

Open Walls on Hudson Avenue: Wiring the Buzzer While Your Renovation Is Under Way

The cheapest door buzzer in Vinegar Hill is the one whose cable goes in while the house is open. City records show how often that moment comes. The DOB NOW permits we read on November 11, 2026 include, at 69 Hudson Avenue, an interior renovation "with restoration of floors, stair treads at existing location, new ceiling finishes, kitchen and bathroom renovations," issued in December 2024 with an estimated cost of $268,000, followed in January 2025 by structural work "sistering existing wood floor joists to remove slope," and in March 2025 by a heat pump air-conditioning system. At 59 Hudson Avenue, a permit issued in September 2025 covers general construction for a new bathroom on the second, third and fourth floors. At 54 Hudson Avenue, permits in January and April 2025 cover alterations to an existing commercial space, with mechanical, structural and plumbing work.

Every one of those jobs opens ceilings, floors or stair walls on several floors. While they are open, a cable from the street door to every floor is a few hours' work. Once the plaster is back and painted, the same cable has to be fished through finished walls, and in an old house with solid brick party walls and joists running the wrong way, that can take a day and still leave small openings to patch.

What to do, and when, in the renovation schedule

  • Before demolition. Tell us how many households the house will hold when it is finished, not how many it holds now. A house being turned from one family into an owner's duplex and a rental needs two buttons and stations on more than one floor.
  • At rough-in. We pull cable from the doorway to the cellar and up to each indoor station position, set back boxes at the height you choose, usually near the kitchen and at the top of the stair, and leave a spare pair to each floor for later.
  • At the door. If the street door or its frame is being restored or replaced, the frame is prepared for the electric strike before it goes back in, so no one cuts a restored frame afterward.
  • At finish. Indoor stations go on after the painters, the doorway station after the Landmarks permit and the facade work, and the system is tested before you move back in.
Condos and Small Apartment Buildings

From Voice to Video in Vinegar Hill's Newer Condos and Rentals

Vinegar Hill's newer buildings are few, small and well documented. Commodore's Court at 85 Hudson Avenue is listed by Compass as ten units on five stories, built in 2002, with an elevator, bike room, common roof deck and a "Voice Intercom"; other listings call it a 9-unit building. The Vista on Vinegar Hill is "a seven story, 35 unit condominium at 206 Front Street, completed in 2005," whose building page lists a part-time doorman, a video intercom, a residents' lounge, a planted courtyard and "a private garage" on the lower level. A rental listing at 68 Gold Street advertises an elevator, video intercom, bike storage and private parking.

These buildings share three things that shape an entry project. They have an elevator, so the riser cable runs in a shaft or a stacked closet rather than up a stair wall. They have more than one door residents use: the lobby, and often a garage, a courtyard gate or a side door. And they are run by condo boards or managing agents who must approve the work and pay for it from a reserve.

The questions a board should settle before choosing equipment

  • What the riser will carry. We meter the existing pairs from the lobby panel to each apartment. If they test sound, a two-wire video system can often reuse them, which is the cheapest path from voice to video. Aiphone, for example, says its GT Series "offers the advantage of using existing wire and infrastructure."
  • Which doors get stations. A garage door, courtyard gate or side door that residents use every day needs its own station or reader, or it becomes the door that gets propped.
  • How residents answer. A video monitor in each apartment works without phones or Wi-Fi and has no subscription. Phone answering is convenient for owners who travel, but cloud services often carry a per-apartment or per-door monthly charge, and Swiftlane's 2026 guide puts those at "$5–$15 per unit per month or $30–$100 per door per month."
  • Who manages it. Someone has to hold the administrator login, add a new owner's name to the directory and delete a seller's. We set the system up in the board's or managing agent's name, never in ours, and hand over the login at the end.
Rules and Responsibilities

Which Vinegar Hill Buildings Must Have a Buzzer, Which Approvals Apply and Who Orders the Work

  • Newer buildings of eight or more apartments must have an intercom. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires a self-locking main entrance door and an intercom allowing voice communication and remote release in Class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments. That covers the condos built on Hudson Avenue and Front Street since 2002.
  • Older buildings install one when a majority of tenants ask. For buildings put up before that date, the same section requires the door and intercom once tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing, with installation started within 90 days and completed within six months.
  • Small houses are the owner's choice. A two- or three-family row house or frame house is outside §50-a's mandate. Owners put in a buzzer because tenants, buyers and their own households expect one, not because a statute requires it.
  • The historic district reviews the doorway. A new intercom panel on a landmarked building needs a Landmarks permit; the staff-level criteria are set out in the section above.
  • The buzzer wiring needs no electrical permit. Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 exempts wiring designed to operate under 50 volts. A new 120-volt outlet or circuit for the power supply is electrical work for a licensed electrician.
  • Fire doors stay latched. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes, and that "fail secure products are locked when power is removed," so a rated door into a stair or cellar stays latched in a fire while still opening from inside by the lever.
  • Magnetic locks need the full package. Allegion also notes that "electromagnetic locks are only available fail safe." On the rare Vinegar Hill door where one is truly needed, the building code's sensor-release rules apply in full: a sensor on the egress side, a "PUSH TO EXIT" button 40 to 48 inches above the floor within 5 feet of the door, release on fire alarm activation and UL 294 listed equipment.

None of this is legal advice for a particular building. An owner answering a tenants' request, a board planning a new lobby panel or an owner reading the Landmarks rules for a doorway should confirm the details with the managing agent, the architect, a lawyer or the Commission itself. What we provide is a system that meets the requirement that applies and paperwork showing what was installed.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Vinegar Hill: Questions Answered

Old shopfronts and narrow doorways

Our Hudson Avenue house kept two doorways from its shop days, the house door and the old store door beside it, and we use both. If Landmarks staff allow one intercom panel per door opening, should each door get its own station, or can one station release both?

Either can work, and the choice depends on who uses each door. If the old store door now leads to a separate household or a ground-floor office, give it its own station: one panel within or beside that opening, ringing only the people behind it, with its own release. The guidebook's limit is one panel per door opening, so two doorways can carry one each. If both doors lead into the same household, a single station at the door visitors actually use is usually better, with a second release wired to the other door so the household can open whichever one a delivery is standing at.

What we avoid is a station placed between the two doors so that visitors cannot tell which door will open. On a narrow old shopfront, the station belongs in or beside the door it serves, and the indoor station should say which door is ringing. We draw both options on the photo of your shopfront, and the one you choose is what goes into the Landmarks filing.

The wooden pier beside our Front Street door is only about four inches wide, and the rest of the opening is brick with stone trim. Can a station that slim still hold a camera and buttons for three households?

Yes. Narrow video stations made for door frames and mullions are built for exactly this, with a camera at the top and two to four buttons stacked below, and several fit within a face of about four inches. The station is screwed into solid wood, which the Landmarks guidebook lists alongside plain masonry and metalwork as an acceptable surface, and the cable goes straight back through the pier into the hall, so nothing has to cross the brick or the stone.

The question to settle first is whether the pier is original decorative woodwork or plain trim. The guidebook says equipment must not go on decorative surfaces, so a carved or molded pier is not the place for it. If yours is plain, the slim station is usually the most discreet answer on the whole doorway. If it is decorative, the next choice is within the opening on the reveal, and we measure both on the walkthrough.

Renovations, permits and timing

Our general contractor closes the ceilings in three weeks, but we have not chosen a door station and our Landmarks filing is not in yet. Can the cable go in now and the station be chosen after approval?

Yes, and that is the order we prefer. Inside the house, the cable from the doorway to the cellar and up to each floor does not depend on which station you choose, as long as we pull a cable that every station we might propose can use. We rough it in while the ceilings are open, set the indoor back boxes, prepare the door frame for the strike if the frame is out, and cap and label every end.

Outside, nothing goes on the doorway until the Landmarks permit is in hand. If you need the door to work before that, a temporary button can be fitted inside the vestibule or on the door frame's inner face, where it changes nothing on the facade, and replaced by the approved station once the permit arrives. The quote shows the rough-in and the finish as two visits, so you know what each costs.

A DOB permit for our gut renovation is already open. Does adding a buzzer mean amending that permit or filing anything new with the Department of Buildings?

For the buzzer itself, normally not. The wiring for a door station, indoor stations and a strike runs well under 50 volts, and Administrative Code §28-105.4.7 says an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate below that level. The buzzer does not change the building's use, egress or occupancy, which is the language the renovation permits on Hudson Avenue all repeat.

Two things can bring other trades in. If the power supply needs a new 120-volt outlet in the cellar, that is electrical work your licensed electrician does under the renovation's electrical filing. And if the doorway is in the historic district, the station needs its Landmarks permit, which is separate from DOB. Your architect or expediter will know whether anything in the renovation's own filings needs updating; we give them the equipment list so they can check.

We would like the cable pulled now, during our renovation, but the buzzer itself installed in a year or two when the budget allows. Will cable left in the walls that long still be usable?

Yes, if it is the right cable and it is protected. Low-voltage cable left capped in a dry wall does not wear out in a year or two. What ruins it is being cut, painted over or buried without labels by the next trade. So we pull a cable suited to video as well as audio, leave a generous loop in each back box, cover every box with a blank plate, label both ends and give you a one-page sketch of where each run goes.

When you are ready, the installation is a short visit: the station on the door, the strike, the power supply and the indoor stations, with no walls to open. The rough-in is priced on its own now, and the finish is quoted when you call, at the Brooklyn list price at that time.

Condos and small buildings

Our five-story condo's voice intercom dates from 2002, and only some owners want video. If the lobby panel goes to video, must every apartment change its handset on the same day?

On many systems, no. Several multi-tenant systems let video monitors and audio handsets sit on the same lobby panel, so the board can replace the panel and power supply as one building project and let each owner choose a video monitor or a simpler audio station for their own apartment. The deciding factor is the riser cable: we meter each pair from the lobby to the apartments, and if it carries the new system's signal, apartments can be changed one at a time.

What cannot be phased is the lobby panel itself, because the old handsets and the new panel rarely talk to each other across brands. So the usual plan is one installation day for the panel and every apartment's station, audio or video as each owner chooses, with the board's share and each owner's share shown separately on the quote. Owners who chose audio can move to video later without the lobby being touched again.

Our ground floor is a restaurant with a private dining room upstairs, and guests reach it through the house's own street door. Can they be buzzed up to that room without getting access to the apartment on the top floor?

Yes, by deciding which doors inside the house lock and who releases them. The street door gets a station that rings the restaurant, the restaurant releases it for guests, and the door from the stair to the top-floor apartment stays locked from the stair side while always opening from inside the apartment. The apartment keeps its own button at the street door, ringing only upstairs, so its visitors are never answered by the restaurant.

Because a dining room can fill with guests at once, the doors they use to leave must open with a single push or turn from inside, and if a magnetic lock is ever proposed on that route, the full sensor-release package applies. The building owner approves the plan in writing, since the stair and the street door serve both the restaurant and the household.

Mail and the doorway

Our house is now three households but still has one mail slot in the door. Can mailboxes for each household go up when the buzzer goes in, and does Landmarks treat them like the station?

They can go up on the same visit, and the Landmarks guidebook has its own criteria for them. Its staff can approve mailboxes and mail key lockboxes installed "within or adjacent to the door opening," attached to a gate or door underneath the stoop or on a plain stoop wall beside the opening, or on an areaway fence or a freestanding post in the areaway. That is separate from the limit of one intercom panel per door opening, so a station and a mailbox group can both be planned for the same doorway.

The practical step is to plan them together so they do not compete for the same piece of plain brick. We mark both on the photo of your doorway, the station at the height a visitor reaches easily and the mailboxes where the carrier can reach them, and the owner files them in one Landmarks application. If the carrier needs to enter a locked vestibule to reach the mailboxes, that is a separate question for the Postal Service about its access lock.

"Installing a video doorbell in New York costs $363.94 to $727.88"

Contractor+'s New York cost page gives that range, broken into three phases: remove the old doorbell, connect the wires to a bracket and set up the app. For a one-family house with working bell wire and a door that only needs a ring, that may be close.

A Vinegar Hill buzzer is a different job. "A video doorbell may need a permit, typically around $150–$300"

The same page says that "installing a video doorbell may require a permit depending on local regulations" and that the permit "is typically around $150-$300." In New York City, that mixes up two different authorities.

What a Vinegar Hill doorway may need is a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission, if the house is inside the 1997 district, and the Commission says there is no fee to submit an application, with the Permit for Minor Work listed by the city as no fee.

The real cost of a landmarked doorway is not a permit fee but planning time: the right station, the right position and a complete application with cut sheets, which we prepare for the owner to file.

"Many video doorbells are designed for easy DIY installation"

Contractor+ answers "Can I install a video doorbell myself?" with "Yes," and for a doorbell that clips onto existing wires in an unlandmarked house, it often is easy.

On a doorway inside the historic district, a do-it-yourself doorbell is still new equipment on the facade. The guidebook allows one intercom panel per door opening, attached at mortar joints, plain masonry, wood or metalwork and never through cast iron or on a decorative surface. A doorbell screwed into a molded frame or onto an original iron gate is the kind of installation that review is there to prevent.

And a doorbell alone still does not open the street door. The moment an owner wants the door released from upstairs, the job involves the frame, a strike and a power supply, which is where installers earn their price.

"Labor typically runs $50–$100 an hour"

Swiftlane's guide gives that hourly range for intercom labor, with installations running "from a single day to several days for complex deployments."

An hourly figure is the wrong way to think about an old house. Fishing cable through a finished nineteenth-century wall can take far longer than the same run in a renovation with the ceilings open, and the hours depend on the house, not on the installer's rate. Our Brooklyn list prices the job, not the hour, and the price is fixed in writing before the work starts.

"An electrician would install it"

That was one reply in a Brownstoner thread from the owner of a Brooklyn three-family asking for smart buzzer advice, and it is a common assumption. Another reply in the same thread said that unless the system is hardwired, "an electrician does not install them but a homeowner can."

Door buzzers are low-voltage systems, and in New York they are installed by low-voltage contractors; ours work under New York State license #12000287431. Cost pages assume the old bell wire is there and ready. Contractor+'s second phase is simply "Connect the doorbell wires to the new device," and many summaries say a video doorbell can run on the existing doorbell wiring.

In Vinegar Hill's houses, that wire may be decades old, may have been cut during an earlier renovation, or may never have reached the upper floors. The Brownstoner commenter who wired a doorbell camera said "the doorbell wiring was too old," which is a common finding in houses of this age.

A previous owner ran a bell wire in surface conduit down the brick of our facade. Can it come off when the new buzzer goes in?

Our house is inside the district, but its front door and frame are modern replacements. Does that change what can go on the doorway?

Almost never. The cable stays on your side of the party wall, running from your doorway through your own front wall, cellar and stair. We bought a door station online and it turned out wider than our door frame. Can you still install it somewhere on the doorway?
+

Sometimes, on plain masonry beside the opening or on a fence or wall in front of the entrance, which the Landmarks guidebook also allows. On a narrow Greek Revival doorway there may be no plain surface wide enough, in which case we will tell you before the visit rather than mount it badly. If it can be used, we install it as labor-only work at a flat price. If not, the return window with the seller is usually the better option than forcing it onto the facade.

Is preparing the information for the Landmarks application included in your quote?
+

Yes. The cut sheet for the station, the marked photo of its position, the proposed finish and the note on how the cable enters the building are part of planning the job, and we hand them to the owner or architect with the quote. Filing the application and corresponding with the Commission stay with the owner, the architect or an expediter, since the permit belongs to the building. We are glad to answer the Commission's staff if they have a question about the equipment.

Can the strike and power supply be installed during our renovation and the door station added in the same visit as the other trades' final touches?
+

Yes. We fit the strike while the door frame is out or prepared, mount the power supply in the cellar once its outlet is in, and come back for the finish visit when the painters and any facade work are done and the Landmarks permit is in hand. On that visit the doorway station and the indoor stations go on and the whole system is tested from the sidewalk.
