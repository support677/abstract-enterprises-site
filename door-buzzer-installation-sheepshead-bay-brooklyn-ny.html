The Neighborhood

Why Sheepshead Bay Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Sheepshead Bay runs from Ocean Parkway on the west to Nostrand and Gerritsen Avenues on the east, and from Kings Highway down to the water. Most people know it for the fishing boats at the piers and the restaurants on Emmons Avenue. For anyone planning an entry system, the more useful fact is how the housing was built. NYC Parks' history of the piers says brick apartment buildings replaced the wooden houses in the 1950s, and that by 1960 the neighborhood was the fastest-growing community in Brooklyn. Many of those buildings were organized as co-ops, and many of their front doors still carry a version of the system they opened with.

Listing records show what that looks like block by block. 1625 Emmons Avenue is a six-story co-op from 1962 with 148 apartments. 1516 Voorhies Avenue went up in 1961 with 95 apartments on six floors, an elevator and a live-in super. 3221 Emmons Avenue is a seven-story elevator co-op from 1960, 2770 Ocean Avenue a six-story building of 106 apartments from 1963, and 2701 Ocean Avenue a six-story elevator building of 63 apartments. The NYBits directory lists 41 co-op buildings in the neighborhood, 17 of them walk-ups. In buildings like these the entry system is not a gadget; it is one panel at the lobby door, a power supply and controller somewhere in the cellar, and a riser that carries the call to every apartment on every floor.

That arrangement has two weak points here that it does not have in most of Brooklyn. The first is age: the riser in a 1961 building has been spliced, extended and reused through every replacement since, and nobody has a drawing of it. The second is water. Brownstoner reported in December 2020 that the Special Sheepshead Bay District along Emmons Avenue "was particularly devastated by Hurricane Sandy," and City Limits described a house at the bay's edge where "Sandy defied sandbags." The cellar is exactly where an old entry system keeps its power supply and its junctions, so a new installation here starts with a question most installers never ask: how high has the water come in this building?

The newer buildings bring their own work. 3112 Emmons Avenue, a condo of 75 apartments finished in 2006, lists an intercom among its amenities. The Vue at 1809 Emmons Avenue, finished in 2017 with 58 apartments, lists a pool, on-site parking, a bike room and a restaurant, which means a lobby door plus several other doors that residents expect to open with the same system. Condo boards in buildings this young are usually deciding whether to extend the developer's system or replace it, not whether to have one.

Then there are the houses. Listings show townhouses on Voorhies Avenue, lots as narrow as 19 by 76 feet on East 22nd Street, and two-families throughout the numbered streets between Avenue U and Avenue Z. Near Plumb Beach, Bklyner described the Sheepshead Courts in 2015 as "bungalows densely packed together on tiny, pedestrian-only streets." A house on a court has no curb in front of it at all, which changes where a visitor stands and where a door station should go.

Finally, the human side, without exaggeration. News 12 Brooklyn reported on April 17, 2026 that tenants at 3205 and 3191 Emmons Avenue described years of conditions going unfixed, and the management company's spokesperson said repairs often depend on city sign-off and access to individual apartments. Public HPD records list open "Bell/Buzzer/Intercom" complaints filed in 2026 at 2970 Ocean Avenue and 1601 Ocean Parkway. A broken buzzer in a building this size is not one household's problem; it is every apartment walking down to the lobby, and a street door that someone eventually props open.

Repair or Replace in Sheepshead Bay

Need Repair in Sheepshead Bay? Fix the Failed Part, Rebuild on the Old Riser or Start Over

A lot of calls about a "new buzzer" in Sheepshead Bay turn out to be about a system that still half works. The lobby panel rings some apartments and not others. The handsets talk but the door does not release. Everything died after a storm and was patched back together. Before any equipment is chosen we decide which of three paths fits, because the cheapest correct answer is not always a new system.

The first path is a targeted repair, when one part is the whole problem: a power supply that has failed, a strike that has worked loose in an aluminum frame, a closer pressing the latch so hard against the keeper that the strike buzzes and the door still will not open. Brick Underground's repair guide lists the usual suspects as the lobby panel, the apartment station, the door strike and the power supply, which "may be in the basement or lobby ceiling." The second path is a new lobby panel, controller and apartment stations on riser cable that tests sound. The third is new cable as well, for lines that fail their tests, buildings whose old system was a different technology, and houses that never had more than a bell.

If a repair is all you need, we say so and price it as a repair, and our Brooklyn door buzzer team handles both kinds of work. Here the deciding checks are usually in the cellar: what condition the riser junctions are in, whether the old power supply sits where water reaches, and how many apartment lines still pass a test.

Postwar elevator co-ops: one lobby door, a hundred apartments and a riser nobody has drawn

The six- and seven-story co-ops from 1960 to 1963 are the core of our work here, and they share a layout. A street door opens into a vestibule with the mailboxes and the lobby panel; an inner door opens into the lobby and the elevator. The riser rises from the cellar in one or more vertical lines, each feeding a stack of apartments, and every apartment has a station by its entry door. Over sixty years that riser has usually been reused for two or three systems, with junction boxes added wherever the last installer needed one.

Our method in these buildings is fixed. We test every pair on every line before we price, because the cost of a 95- or 148-apartment job depends far more on how many lines pass than on which panel goes in the lobby. We find where the old power supply, amplifier or controller sits, which in a building near the bay is usually the cellar, and we plan the new one on a wall above the flood line or on the first floor. We check both lobby doors, because the street door has to latch on its own every time against a closer that has been adjusted for decades. And we agree with the board or the managing agent how the lobby will be covered on switch-over day, so no apartment is without a way to answer.

The common mistake is replacing the lobby panel and the apartment stations and assuming the riser will cope. In a building this size, one bad splice can make calls ring the wrong floor or drop out on a whole line. The test sheet we leave behind shows each line's result, so the board can see exactly what was reused and what was replaced.

Large doorman co-ops: a desk, a panel and the hours when the desk is empty

The Atlantis at 3235 Emmons Avenue is listed as a seven-floor, 172-unit doorman elevator co-op, and other buildings here have a doorman for part of the day. A listing for 1625 Emmons Avenue mentions an evening doorman and a live-in super, while CityRealty lists no doorman at all, which shows how often the arrangement changes. In these buildings the entry system has two jobs: let the desk announce visitors and release the door when it is staffed, and let visitors reach apartments directly when it is not.

That usually means a lobby panel that can call both the desk and the apartments, a desk station or console, and a clear rule for who answers at which hours. It also means thinking about packages. A former tenant at 2770 Ocean Avenue, a six-story building of 106 apartments from 1963, wrote on openigloo that packages are "left in an unsecure area if the doorman takes his many breaks" and that "Any packages that arrive over the weekend get locked away when the doorman leaves." A package room door on the entry system, opened by a courier code rather than a key, answers both complaints.

Walk-up co-ops: a board that is also the super

The walk-up co-ops are smaller buildings without an elevator, and they often have a live-in super or a managing agent on call. The entry system has to be something a board member can run: a directory that can be changed from a laptop or the panel itself, codes that can be issued and deleted, and no monthly service fee unless the board chooses one. A two-wire system on the old cable, with audio or video handsets, is often the right fit, and phone answering can be added for the shareholders who want it.

The vestibule is where walk-ups go wrong. A street door with the panel outside, a vestibule with mailboxes, and an inner door that also locks can leave a visitor trapped between two doors if only one releases. We set up which door the panel releases, and how the mail carrier gets in, before the order goes in.

Condos from 2006 onward: a developer's system and doors the offering plan never mentioned

3112 Emmons Avenue, a 75-apartment condo finished in 2006, lists an intercom among its amenities, and the Vue at 1809 Emmons Avenue, 58 apartments finished in 2017, lists a pool, on-site parking and a bike room. In buildings like these the lobby system usually works; what the board wants is more doors on it, phone answering for owners who rent their units out, or a replacement for a model the maker has stopped supporting. We find out what the developer installed, whether the building owns the admin access, and whether the cable to each apartment is the kind a newer system can use, before anyone talks about replacing it.

Houses and two-families: two households, one front door and a narrow lot

On the numbered streets between Kings Highway and the bay, a lot as narrow as 19 by 76 feet on East 22nd Street holds a house with one front door and often two households. The job is a two-button station at the door, each button ringing only its own floor, each household able to release the door, and, where the second household has its own side entrance, a separate button there. Semi-detached houses add a constraint: the stoop and the wall beside the door may be shared with the neighbor, so the station and its cable stay on your side.

Houses that were raised or rebuilt after Sandy often have their front door at the top of a new stair, their living floors above a ground level built to take water, and no cellar at all. In those houses we run the cable inside the house above the flood level rather than along the ground floor, and the power supply goes in a closet on a living floor.

Bungalows on the courts: a door several houses in from the street

Brownstoner's 2015 history describes bungalows built in the 1920s in "an oft-forgotten corner of Brighton Beach and Sheepshead Bay," "Originally intended exclusively for warm-weather use," and many were never repaired after Sandy. Bklyner described the Sheepshead Courts as "bungalows densely packed together on tiny, pedestrian-only streets," where rebuilding "requires agreement and cooperation among all of the residents." For a buzzer, that means a visitor may stand at the street end of a court and not know which door is yours, and a house may have no cellar, no attic and walls with little room for cable. A small wired station at the door, or a weatherproof station at the court's entrance that rings one house, are both possible; which one fits depends on who owns the walkway and the gate.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Read the Cellar Walls Before You Read the Lobby Panel

This is how our technician spends the first hour in a Sheepshead Bay co-op, before anything is priced.

The visit starts at the sidewalk, because that is where a visitor starts. Is the panel easy to find from the street, can a visitor read the directory, and does the street door shut and latch on its own after someone walks through it? Then the vestibule and the lobby: the inner door, the mailboxes, where couriers leave parcels and where the desk sits if there is one.

Then the cellar, which in a building near the bay is the part of the visit that changes the plan most. We look for the old power supply, the amplifier or controller, the junction boxes where the riser lines begin, and the lowest point where cable enters the wall. We look at the walls themselves for the line where water stood, and at whether anything electrical was moved up after the last flood. We ask the super where water reached, because he usually remembers.

Only then do we go upstairs to meter the riser from the top floor down, apartment line by apartment line. The test tells us which lines can carry a new system and which need new cable. The cellar tells us where the new controller and power supply have to go so they are still working after the next storm. Both go on the written price before the board sees it.

Flood-Aware Installation

Above the Water Line: Where a Sheepshead Bay Entry System's Power, Controller and Cable Should Live

Most door buzzer installations in Brooklyn never have to think about water. Here it is the first thing we think about, because the parts that keep an entry system alive are the parts old installations put in the cellar. The lobby panel on the street door is built to sit outdoors. The power supply, the controller, the amplifier on older systems, the backup battery and the junctions where every riser line begins are not, and a cellar is where they have traditionally been fixed to the wall.

The city's rules on flood-resistant construction set the principle. Appendix G of the New York City Building Code says, in section G304.1.1, that in flood hazard areas "Utilities and attendant equipment shall be located at or above the design flood elevation," or be built to keep water out. The text we reviewed has no separate clause for low-voltage or communications equipment such as a door buzzer, so we do not claim it legally governs where a buzzer controller goes. We follow the principle anyway, because the cost of following it at installation time is small and the cost of ignoring it is a building whose front door stops working the day the water goes down.

In practice that means four decisions, made before the price is set. First, where the controller and power supply go: on a cellar wall as high as the ceiling allows, in a locked enclosure on the first floor or in the super's workroom, depending on what the building has above the flood line. Second, how the riser lines are met: we bring the junction up to the same height rather than leaving it where the old one sat. Third, what the backup battery runs: it should keep the lobby door's release and the panel working through an ordinary outage, and it has to sit as high as the controller. Fourth, how cable crosses the cellar: along the ceiling and beams, not along the floor or the foot of the wall.

Recovery is the other half. Bklyner's Sandy anniversary coverage cited the city's analysis that, in Southern Brooklyn high-rises, repairs to electrical, heat and elevator systems took two to four weeks and sometimes longer. During that time residents of a locked building still need to get in. That is one reason we usually recommend a fail-secure electric strike on the street door rather than a magnetic lock: as Allegion's guide explains, fail-secure hardware stays locked when power is removed, and the key in the lock still works, so residents can let themselves in with a key when nothing electrical works. A magnetic lock, which Allegion notes is only available fail-safe, does the opposite and leaves the door unlocked.

None of this changes which system a building chooses. It changes where the parts go, which is decided on the walkthrough and written on the quote, so the board knows what it is paying for and why.

The Law and the Records

Which Sheepshead Bay Buildings Must Have an Intercom, What It Has to Do and What the City Records Show

New York's intercom requirement turns on a date that matters more in Sheepshead Bay than almost anywhere else in Brooklyn. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a says that every Class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments "shall also be equipped with an intercommunication system" at its self-locking main entrance door, one that lets residents speak to a visitor and release the door from their apartment.

Buildings older than that line are treated differently. Under the same section, a pre-1968 building must install the self-locking door and the intercom if tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request it in writing, with installation started within 90 days and completed within six months. That is the rule that covers most of the large co-ops here: 3221 Emmons Avenue from 1960, 1516 Voorhies Avenue from 1961, 1625 Emmons Avenue from 1962 and 2770 Ocean Avenue from 1963 all went up a few years before the 1968 line. The condos at 3112 Emmons Avenue (2006) and 1809 Emmons Avenue (2017) fall after it. Co-ops add a layer, because shareholders are also the owners, and in practice their boards replace entry systems because shareholders expect a working door, not because the law compels them to.

Separately from §50-a, the city's Housing Maintenance Code obligations apply to the buzzer or intercom a building already has, and sources disagree on the exact threshold, so we do not quote one. What is clear from public records is that tenants here use the complaint system. Augrented, a third-party site that republishes city data, lists an open HPD "Bell/Buzzer/Intercom" complaint at 2970 Ocean Avenue for apartment D1, received June 9, 2026, at a building it shows with 453 HPD violations against a neighborhood average of 16.1. It lists another open complaint of the same kind at 1601 Ocean Parkway for apartment 6D, received May 20, 2026, and a closed 2020 complaint at 1670 Sheepshead Bay Road that was verified during inspection.

For an owner or board, the practical lessons are short. A new installation should give two-way voice and remote release to every apartment, because that is what the law describes for buildings it covers and what residents assume everywhere. The street door has to close and lock on its own after every visitor, because a release on a door that drifts open defeats the system. And every apartment should be tested and recorded at handover, because a single dead station is exactly what an HPD complaint is about.

We are installers, not lawyers. If your building is facing a complaint or a violation, your managing agent or attorney decides how to answer it; what we provide is the installed system, the test results for every apartment and the paperwork that shows when the work was finished.

Planning With a Co-op Board

Planning a Lobby System With a Board, a Part-Time Desk and More Than a Hundred Apartments

Most large entry jobs in Sheepshead Bay are ordered by a co-op board, and the board's real question is rarely which brand. It is what the building will look like on the other side: how residents will answer, who will manage the directory, what happens to the people who do not want to change, and what it will cost every year after the installation is paid.

Habitat's May 2017 report on new buzzer systems set out the three choices boards still face: hard-wired talk-and-release systems, some with video; telephone-based systems on a dedicated line, which let residents answer on a landline or cell phone but offer no video; and smartphone-based systems with video on residents' phones. Dan Arnold of Academy Mailbox told Habitat, "A lot of our calls now are people who want to abandon the hard-wired intercom units in their apartments." In a building where many shareholders are retired, though, the station on the wall is often what people trust, and a system that supports both is usually the answer.

The running cost is where the choice bites. Habitat quoted Nicholas Silvers of Tavros Capital putting a ButterflyMX installation at roughly $5,000 to $7,000 plus a monthly charge of $2.50 per unit. Rules that apply to business entry installations around Sheepshead Bay

  • Anyone inside can always get out. A business door with a buzzer, code or reader still opens from inside without a key. Allegion's guide explains that with an electric strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure, which is why a strike is our default on commercial doors.
  • Magnetic locks bring conditions. The building code's sensor-release provisions require a sensor on the egress side, a manual unlocking device 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door marked "PUSH TO EXIT," unlocking on loss of power and on fire alarm activation, and listing to UL 294. A magnetic lock is not a cheaper strike; it is a different system with its own requirements.
  • Fire-rated doors take fail-secure hardware. Allegion notes that fire doors need fail-secure strikes under NFPA 80, so the door stays latched in a fire while still opening from inside.
  • Controls have to be reachable. The 2010 ADA Standards set the high forward reach at 48 inches maximum, so the call button and keypad on a business door go at or below that height.
  • The Lundy's building is a landmark. Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Sheepshead Bay: Questions Answered

Water, cellars and outages

The riser cable in our co-op's cellar sat in floodwater during Sandy and the old system kept working afterward. Can a new system go on that cable, or did the soaking ruin it?

There is no way to tell by looking. Cable that was underwater can work for years and still be weaker than cable that stayed dry, because water that got into a jacket or a splice can corrode the copper slowly. That is why we meter every pair from the cellar to the apartments before pricing, and why cable that was in the water gets the closest look. ButterflyMX's replacement guide puts it plainly: wiring "cannot be confirmed from photos or building age."

In practice the weak points are not the long runs inside the walls but the places where cable meets something: the junction boxes and splices near the cellar floor, and the terminals at the old power supply. Often the riser lines test well once those low connections are cut out and remade higher on the wall. Lines that fail are replaced individually, and every failed line is named on the quote with its price before you order, so there is no surprise on installation day.

If the power is out across the neighborhood for days, as it was after Sandy, how do residents get into a building with a new entry system?

With a key, if the system is set up the way we recommend. A fail-secure electric strike on the street door keeps the door locked when power fails, and the ordinary lock cylinder in the door still works, so residents let themselves in with their keys exactly as they would without any buzzer. What stops working is the call from the lobby and the release from the apartments, until power or the battery returns.

The backup battery in the power supply covers ordinary outages, not a multi-week one; we state on the quote what it runs and keep it mounted as high as the controller. A magnetic lock behaves the other way: it is fail-safe only, so in an outage it releases and the street door stands unlocked. In a building where the 2012 outage lasted weeks, that difference is a decision the board should make knowingly, and we explain it before anything is ordered.

Co-ops, condos and shareholders

In our 95-apartment co-op some shareholders want video on their phones and others want to keep a plain station on the wall. Can one system give each apartment a different choice, and who pays for the extras?

Yes, on most current systems. The lobby panel, the controller and the riser are shared; what sits in each apartment can differ. One apartment can have an audio station, the next a video station, and a third can answer on a phone, all on the same lobby panel. Some systems also let an apartment have both a wall station and phone answering.

Who pays is the board's decision, not ours, but the written price can be split to make it easy: the shared equipment and the standard station for every apartment as one figure, and each upgrade as a separate line a shareholder can choose. Swiftlane's guide notes that cloud systems can carry subscriptions of "$5 to $15 per unit per month," so if phone answering on a given system carries a fee, the quote says so and says whether it is charged per apartment or for the building.

Is a 150-apartment co-op cheaper per apartment than a small walk-up?

Usually yes, because the most expensive pieces are shared. A large building and a small one both need one lobby panel, one controller, one power supply and one street-door release; what grows with the building is the number of apartment stations, the length of the riser and the directory. That is why our Brooklyn list puts multi-unit systems from about $2,500 to past $10,000 rather than at a price per apartment.

What can turn that around is the riser. A building whose lines all test well needs no new cable, while a building with failed lines on several stacks needs cable run floor by floor. Brick Underground's repair guide quotes Pete Carmello of Lock and Tech estimating that a 100-unit building could take about two days to replace, and a building where lines fail takes longer. The riser test on the walkthrough is what tells you which kind of building you have.

Our condo has a pool, a bike room and on-site parking as well as the lobby. Should those doors be on the lobby's entry system, and can the pool door open only in summer?

They usually should be on one system, because residents expect one code, card or phone to open every door they are allowed through, and the board wants one place to remove a resident who sells. A system with access schedules can open the pool door to residents only during the hours and months the board sets, keep the bike room open to residents all year and let the parking door be used at any hour, all from the same directory.

What changes from door to door is the hardware. A glass pool door, a steel door to a parking area and a lobby door may each need a different strike or lock, and an outdoor door near the water needs hardware rated for the weather. We list each door with its release and its price, so the board can add the amenity doors now or later.

Houses, courts and the waterfront rules

Our bungalow is on one of the pedestrian courts near Plumb Beach, several houses in from the street. Can a visitor ring us from the street end of the court, and who owns a station that several neighbors share?

A visitor can ring you from the street end if a station is put there, either a station that rings only your house or a panel with a button for each house on the court. The cable has to get from that point to each house it calls, along the court, under the walk or along a fence line, which is the main cost, so a single-house station at your own door is cheaper and simpler. A lit house number and a sign at the court's entrance often solve the "which door?" problem without any shared equipment.

A shared panel is a shared decision. Bklyner noted in 2015 that rebuilding on the courts "requires agreement and cooperation among all of the residents," and an entry panel is the same. Before we install one we need written agreement on who owns it, who can change its directory and who pays if it fails. Without that, we recommend a station at each house's own door.

Does the special zoning district along Emmons Avenue, or the Lundy's landmark, limit what can go on our building's front door?

The Special Sheepshead Bay District, created by the City Planning Commission in 1973, covers about 20 blocks along Emmons Avenue between Sheepshead Bay Road and Knapp Street. Its rules, as the Department of City Planning describes them, deal with what can be built and used: residential density, building heights such as three stories or 35 feet within 75 feet of Emmons Avenue in one subarea, and which commercial uses are allowed. We found nothing in that description about door stations, intercoms or locks, so a buzzer on an existing building inside the district is ordinary work.

The Lundy's building at 1901 Emmons Avenue is the exception. The Brooklyn Eagle described it in 2017 as Sheepshead Bay's only designated landmark, and reported that the Landmarks Preservation Commission told the tenant to remove most of the proposed facade sconces. Anything mounted on its exterior, including a door station, should go through the Commission first. Inside the building, behind the facade, the work is ordinary again.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Sheepshead Bay

Ask an AI assistant or a search summary about replacing a building's buzzer and you get confident answers assembled from national cost guides and vendor blogs. Some of it is right in general and wrong for a neighborhood of sixty-year-old co-ops a few blocks from the bay. Here are seven claims we see repeated, each checked against its source and against what we find in buildings here.

"Installing an intercom system costs an average of $2,500"

That is HomeAdvisor's national figure, with a range of $1,500 to $15,000 and a split between wireless systems at $150 to $1,500 and hard-wired ones at $1,000 to $15,000. As a national average it is reasonable. As a guide for a Sheepshead Bay co-op it says almost nothing, because it blends one-family houses with apartment buildings.

Here the spread is wider and more predictable. A house or bungalow buzzer sits at $400 to $900 on our Brooklyn list, a video system for a two-family or small building at $700 to $1,800, and a lobby system for an apartment building or co-op from about $2,500 to past $10,000.

What moves a building inside that range is the riser test and the number of doors, which no average can know. That is why the price is fixed after the walkthrough, not before.

"Modern IP and video intercoms cost $1,000 to $5,000 or more per entry door"

Swiftlane's guide prices systems by the door, which makes sense for an office or a gated development. It misleads in a Sheepshead Bay elevator co-op, which may have one lobby door and 148 apartments behind it.

In those buildings the door is the smallest part of the cost. The apartment stations, the length of the riser and how many of its lines need new cable decide the total, and a per-door figure hides all of it.

Per-door pricing does fit the newer condos with several amenity doors, where each extra door, its hardware and its reader is a line on our quote.

"Software subscriptions commonly add $5 to $15 per unit per month"

Swiftlane's figure is accurate for many cloud systems, and boards should take it seriously. For a building of 148 apartments it works out to between $740 and $2,220 a month, every month, on top of the installation.

The summaries rarely mention that a wired system with no subscription is still an option. Our Brooklyn list includes a $0 monthly choice, and phone answering can be added for only the apartments that want it on systems that allow it.

Before the board votes, we put the installation price and the yearly running cost side by side for each option, so a cheap installation with an expensive subscription is not mistaken for a bargain.

"New York law requires every apartment building to have an intercom"

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires an intercom in Class A buildings of eight or more apartments erected or converted after January 1, 1968. Older buildings must install one if tenants occupying a majority of the apartments request it in writing.

That distinction is not academic here. The large co-ops at 3221 Emmons, 1516 Voorhies, 1625 Emmons and 2770 Ocean Avenue were built from 1960 to 1963, just before the date, while the condos at 3112 and 1809 Emmons Avenue came long after.

Whatever the law requires of a particular building, the systems we install give two-way voice and remote release to every apartment, and the test sheet records each one.

"A cloud intercom requires no building wiring"

ButterflyMX describes its intercom as one that "requires no building wiring," meaning residents answer on their phones and no riser is needed for the call. For a building whose riser has failed throughout, that is a real advantage.

But the panel at the street door still needs power, the door still needs an electric strike or lock wired to the panel, and a network or cellular connection has to reach the lobby. In a co-op whose cellar floods, the power supply and network gear behind that panel still have to be mounted above the water.

So "no wiring" means no riser wiring. It does not mean no installer, no cellar work and no door hardware.

"Magnetic locks are the modern, secure choice for a lobby door"

Magnetic locks are common on commercial doors, and the summaries often present them as an upgrade. Allegion's guide is clear that electromagnetic locks "are only available fail safe," meaning they unlock when power is removed.

In a neighborhood that has lived through a multi-week outage, a lobby door that stands unlocked whenever the power is off is a poor default. The code's sensor-release requirements, including a "PUSH TO EXIT" device within five feet of the door, also add cost a strike does not.

For most Sheepshead Bay street doors we recommend a fail-secure strike, which stays locked without power while the key still opens it and the lever still lets everyone out.

"If the existing wires are in good condition, they can be reused"

That line from Aiphone's upgrade guide is true, and reusing sound cable is the biggest single saving on a co-op job. The trouble is the word "if." Brick Underground's repair guide quotes Daniel Vasquez of Paragon Security and Locksmith: "It's all in the wire connections."

In a Sheepshead Bay cellar those connections often sit low on the wall, where floodwater reached them, and the riser may have been spliced through several earlier systems. Cable that looks fine can fail a test, and cable that looks tired can pass one.

So we test before we promise. The quote names every line that passed, every line that failed and what replacing each failed line costs.

Our building's entrance has a canopy on posts out to the sidewalk. Can the door station go on one of the posts instead of beside the door?
+

Sometimes. A post can hold a weatherproof station if the cable can run inside it or along it without being exposed, and if the station stays within reach for every visitor. The drawback is that a visitor buzzed in at the post still has to walk to the door before the release times out, so the release time is set longer. In most buildings we recommend the wall beside the door, and we explain the difference on the walkthrough.

How long will the backup battery keep our lobby door and panel working when the power goes out?
+

Long enough for an ordinary outage, not for days. The quote states what the battery is meant to cover. For a long outage, the fail-secure strike we recommend keeps the street door locked and still lets residents in with their keys.
