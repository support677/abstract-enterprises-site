The Neighborhood

Why Bronx River Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Bronx River is a place most maps do not draw, which is the first thing a camera installer has to understand about it. Search for it and the encyclopedias send you to the river itself. The Soundview article on Wikipedia treats Bronx River as one of the historic community names inside Bronx Community Board 9, and the people who live here use it for the blocks on the east bank around East 172nd to East 174th Streets, between the river, the Cross Bronx Expressway and the Westchester Avenue El. Wikipedia's history of the wider area explains how it was built. The land east of the Bronx River stayed in Westchester County until New York City annexed it in 1895. The IRT Pelham Line was extended along Westchester Avenue in 1920, and one- and two-family houses followed it. The large site that became the housing development, Wikipedia says, was still largely undeveloped before construction began after the Second World War.

That development is the center of the neighborhood. NYCHA's Bronx River Houses were completed on February 28, 1951, on a 13.94-acre site bounded by East 174th Street, Harrod Avenue and Bronx River Avenue, and Wikipedia gives the population as 3,025. A secondary source describes nine 14-story buildings; the apartment count varies from source to source, so we do not lean on a single figure. What matters for this page is the development's long history with cameras. The Bronx Times, profiling the late tenant leader James Graham, credited him with pushing for mandatory guest sign-ins, security cameras in lobbies and elevators, and steel and bulletproof glass, and reported that by the early 1990s every building in Bronx River Houses had bulletproof glass and cameras in its lobbies and elevators. Wikipedia adds that in 1999 the NYPD completed the installation of 135 closed-circuit television cameras at the Houses. An older mirror of that article goes further and calls it the first housing project in the country placed under round-the-clock police watch with camera coverage of its common areas; we could not confirm that anywhere else, so we repeat it only as a claim.

The lesson residents draw from that history is practical. The cameras in the lobbies, elevators and grounds of a NYCHA development belong to NYCHA and its partners, and changes to them come through NYCHA's own programs, not from a private company a tenant hires. The camera a resident can put up is the one on their own front door, and NYCHA has said exactly what is allowed there: a wireless video camera or video doorbell, for security purposes only, attached over the peephole or stuck to the door with adhesive, with no holes and no physical alterations. That one rule shapes most of the camera questions we expect from the towers, and we explain below how we work inside it.

The doors themselves have been a long-running worry. In the NYC Comptroller's 2018 investigative survey of NYCHA entrances, a tenant leader is quoted saying that seniors had been robbed in the buildings "because doors do not lock," that doors were "mostly broken," and that an intercom NYCHA had planned for Bronx River had "yet to happen." The same survey found that across the Bronx, 24 percent of 767 development front doors were open and 20 percent of 276 rear or side doors were unlocked, and that citywide only 1,887 of 3,538 entrance doors, 53 percent, had a camera placed by the entrance. Online reviews of the complex collected by Wanderlog mention safety concerns including malfunctioning elevators and unlocked doors. None of that is something a resident can fix with a camera of their own, but it explains why so many families here want a view of the hallway outside their own door, recorded on a device they control.

The Bronx River Addition, finished by NYCHA in 1966 as a separate state-funded development for senior residents, adds another kind of caller: the adult child who wants to know a parent is safe. City Limits reported in February 2024 that the Addition building at 1630–1632 East 174th Street was no longer in use, after NYCHA deemed it uninhabitable in 2022 because of a faulty heating system and moved out the 60 seniors who had lived there. In October 2025, New York YIMBY reported that the Preservation Trust had selected Fifth Avenue Builders for more than $93 million in renovations across 226 apartments in two Addition buildings, and NYCHA's February 2025 announcement listed new kitchens and bathrooms and new heating, plumbing and electrical systems. Neither source says whether cameras or intercoms are part of that work. News 12 Bronx, covering the plan, quoted tenant association president Norma Saunders on peeling paint, mold and plumbing problems. We will not guess at what the renovation includes; residents should ask the tenant association and the Trust directly.

Around the towers, the housing is private and much older in feel. Real estate summaries describe the Soundview side of the river as mostly multi-unit rowhouses and tenement-style apartment buildings near the El on Westchester Avenue, with brick high-rise condos and some Greek Revival rowhomes in the mix. Listings in the Soundview-Bruckner area on Redfin describe a three-story two-family with two three-bedroom apartments, and another two-family with a nice-sized backyard and a common driveway. Those details are the whole camera plan in a sentence. A two-family house has two entrances to watch, an owner who often lives on one floor and a tenant on the other, and a backyard and driveway that may be shared with the next house. Every one of those facts decides where a camera can point, who gets to see its picture, and where the recorder should sit.

The streets add their own reasons. News 12 Bronx reported that in July 2026 a double shooting happened just after 11:45 on a Saturday night near Bronx River Avenue and East 174th Street, and that the shooter was riding a motorized scooter. The neighborhood is patrolled by the NYPD's 43rd Precinct, with the public housing served by Police Service Area 8. Bronx Community Board 9's FY2026 district statement asks for an Argus camera on Bronx River Avenue between East 174th and East 177th Streets to deal with illegal dumping. And the Scherman Foundation and the Bronx Times have both written about the Houses sitting within 100 feet of a proposed widening of the Cross Bronx Expressway, quoting residents who say many windows cannot be opened because of the polluted air. A camera on a house here has to cope with night scenes on busy streets, fast movement, road grime and a public that is understandably sensitive about being watched.

We could not pull HPD, 311 or Department of Buildings records for camera or entry-door complaints at Bronx River addresses, we found no landmark district covering these blocks, and our searches turned up no named businesses on East 174th Street or Bronx River Avenue to describe. Where this page talks about how houses are built here, it is drawing on the listings and summaries above and on what we find in buildings of that type generally. The tower door: small device, strict rule

For a resident of the towers, the job is short and the limits are firm. NYCHA's guidance allows a wireless camera or video doorbell attached over the peephole or fixed to the door with adhesive, for security purposes only, with no holes and no alterations. That excludes anything that needs a screw, a wire through the door frame or a power cable run from an outlet across the hall. It means a battery-powered device, chosen so that it sits flat and stays put on the door you actually have, set to cover the space directly outside your door and no further. We do not drill, we do not run wires, and we do not touch the hallway. We help you get a device that fits, put it on cleanly, set its motion zones and connect it to your phone and your WiFi, and show you how to recharge or change its battery without taking it off the door.

The house wall: your property, your neighbors' privacy

For an owner, the building is yours to work on, and the questions shift to construction and courtesy. Brick, frame and siding take different mounts. Cable has to get from each camera position back to one recorder without leaving loose wire on the front of the house. And every camera that looks across a shared driveway or a low backyard fence can see the house next door. New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a, often called the backyard camera law, lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera that intrudes on their yard. So the plan starts with what you have a right to watch: your doors, your windows, your side of the drive, your yard. We mark each camera's view on a sketch and change the angle or add a privacy mask before you sign anything.

The business lot: empty at night, busy by day

Wikipedia describes the western edge of Soundview, along the river, as mostly storage, warehousing and automotive repair, and that is the commercial camera work we expect closest to Bronx River. A repair lot or a storage building is busy all day and empty all night, which flips the usual priorities. The cameras need to see the gate, the doors and the parked vehicles clearly after dark, the recorder needs to survive somebody walking in to take it, and the people who need to check the cameras are often the owner at home and a manager on the road. Commercial cable also travels further and through tougher surfaces: block walls, steel roll-down doors and open ceilings. We plan those jobs with the owner on site, door by door.

Brick fronts and the houses built after the El

The one- and two-family houses that Wikipedia says followed the 1920 extension of the Pelham Line, and the rowhouses near Westchester Avenue, are mostly masonry at the front. Brick takes a camera well if the anchors go into the face of the brick and not the mortar joint, because mortar is softer and crumbles under a bracket that is pulled and pushed by wind on a tall mount. We drill slowly with masonry bits, check the dust for whether we have hit sound brick, and set sleeve anchors sized for the mount. Where the cable must pass through the wall, we drill once, at a slight downward slope toward the outside so water cannot run in, sleeve the hole, and seal it. A camera on a brick front almost always looks better and lasts longer in a weatherproof junction box, which hides the cable connection and gives the anchors a wider footprint.

Siding and frame houses

Some houses here have siding over wood framing, or a frame rear addition on a brick front. Siding is the easiest place to make a mess: a camera screwed straight into vinyl or aluminum can crack it, and water will follow the screws in. We use a mounting block made for siding so the camera sits on a flat, sealed base, and we look for the framing behind it so the screws hold in wood, not just in the cladding. Cable through a frame wall usually drops neatly into the basement, which is where we prefer to route it in a two-family, because it keeps the wire inside the owner's space and away from the tenant's floor.

Shared stairs and vestibules

A three- or four-family house has a stair that every household uses and that the owner controls. A camera there is useful for the front door and the mailbox area, but it must be placed so that when an apartment door opens, the camera does not see into the apartment. We mount stair cameras high, near the top of a flight, pointed down the run of the stair and along the vestibule, and we walk each landing with the camera live on a phone while someone opens each door. If any door shows its interior, we move the camera or mask that part of the picture. The recorder for a house like this belongs somewhere only the owner can reach, usually the basement or the owner's own apartment.

Block walls and open ceilings in commercial buildings

The storage and repair buildings near the river are more likely to have concrete block walls and open ceilings than plaster and drywall. Block is strong but hollow; anchors need to go into the solid parts or use fixings made for hollow block. Cable runs are long, so we carry them on J-hooks or in conduit rather than letting them lie on top of ducts or lights, and where a run has to pass from the lot to an inside office we core-drill the block once, in the right place, rather than chasing the cable around doorframes. Exposed cable on the outside of a commercial building goes in conduit, because a cut cable is the cheapest way to blind a camera.

What a lot camera has to do that a house camera does not

A camera over a family's front door is mostly looking at faces at close range in a lit doorway. A camera over a repair lot is looking at a gate, a row of parked customer cars and a fence line forty or fifty feet away, at three in the morning, with no one there to switch a light on. That changes almost every choice. The cameras need either good low-light sensors or their own light, aimed so they see across the lot rather than into a streetlight. The gate camera and the bay door camera need narrower, closer views so a person walking through fills enough of the picture to be recognizable. The recorder needs to be somewhere a thief cannot simply carry away with the stock, on battery backup so a cut power line does not stop it, and the owner needs to be able to check the lot from home without logging into the business computer. We plan those jobs from the gate inward: where does a person or vehicle enter, where do they have to pass, and what is the closest point a camera can see them clearly.

Shops with apartments above

The shopfronts on Westchester Avenue often have apartments over them and a shared entrance or side door. A shop owner can cover their own door, counter, register and stockroom, but a camera at a shared entrance belongs to the building owner's decision, and the apartments upstairs are off limits. If you lease a shop, check your lease and ask the landlord before we mount anything on the facade, and send us their answer in writing. Inside the shop, we place the register camera to see hands and the counter top, the door camera to see faces as people come in, and the stockroom camera to see the back door, and we keep the recorder in a locked cabinet or a back office rather than under the counter where it is easy to grab.

Rules and practical limits for business cameras

  • No general camera mandate. As security industry guidance on NYC building rules notes, there is no general city law that requires cameras in apartment buildings, and the same is true of most shops. A business installs cameras because it wants them, which means the owner sets the scope.
  • Common areas belong to the landlord. A tenant business, like a tenant household, needs the landlord's permission for equipment in shared spaces and on the outside of the building.
  • Light first, then camera. Industry guidance makes the point bluntly: a camera aimed at an unlit corridor produces no usable footage. We check the lighting at every camera position, and tell you where a light is needed before a camera can do its job.
  • Fire-rated walls and ceilings. Where camera cable passes through a fire-rated wall or floor in a commercial building, the opening has to be sealed with a rated fire-stopping material. We seal what we open.
  • New power is an electrician's job. Our cameras run on low-voltage cable, usually powered from the recorder or a switch. Where a business wants a new outlet or a new circuit for equipment, that work is for a licensed electrician, and our quote says so.
  • Private places stay private. We do not mount cameras in restrooms, changing areas or anywhere else people expect not to be watched, and we tell you plainly if a requested view crosses that line.

Field Notes: Standing on the Sidewalk Before Touching a Bronx River House

We do not publish stories about a particular customer's house unless we can document them, so this is not a tale about one address. It is the routine we follow at a two-family house of the kind that lines the blocks around the Houses, before any equipment comes off the van.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Bronx River: Questions Answered

Living in the towers

I rent in one of the Bronx River Houses towers. What kind of camera am I allowed to put on my apartment door?

NYCHA's guidance is specific: a resident may attach a wireless video camera or video doorbell to their own apartment door, as long as it is for security purposes only and it is attached over the peephole or fixed to the door with adhesive, with no holes and no physical alterations. In practice that means a battery-powered peephole camera or an adhesive-mounted doorbell that connects to your WiFi. Anything that needs screws, a wire through the frame or power from an outlet in the hall is outside the rule. We help you pick a device that sits properly on your door, set its motion area so it watches your doorway rather than your neighbors', and connect it to your phone.

The tenants want better coverage in our NYCHA lobby and elevators. Can a resident group pay AESS to add cameras there?

No. The lobbies, elevators, stairs and grounds belong to NYCHA, and camera work in those spaces is part of NYCHA's own capital and maintenance programs. Elsewhere in the Bronx, a 2025 NYCHA partnership deal for the Northwest Bronx Scattered Sites included more than 1,900 new cameras in hallways, stairways, elevators and outdoor areas, which shows the scale and the channel that work goes through. A resident group's route is the tenant association, NYCHA's repair and capital requests, and the elected officials who fund capital work, not a private contractor. We would rather tell you that plainly than take a deposit for a job we cannot do.

Will a camera stuck on with adhesive stay put on a metal apartment door through a Bronx winter?

It will if the surface is prepared and the device is light enough for its mount. Adhesive pads fail for three reasons: the door was not cleaned of grease and paint dust first, the pad was pressed on in the cold and never bonded, or the device is heavier than the pad is rated for. We clean the spot, fit the mount at room temperature, press it firmly for the time the pad's maker asks for, and let it cure before the camera goes on. We also position it away from where the door is pushed or bumped, and show you how to take the camera off its mount to charge it without peeling the pad.

Owners and tenants in private houses

We own a three-family house and want a camera on the shared stair. How do you aim it so it never sees into a tenant's apartment?

We mount it high near the top of a flight and point it down the run of the stair and across the vestibule, so it sees people coming and going and the mail area but not the doorways face-on. Then we test it the only way that proves anything: with the camera live on a phone, someone opens each apartment door in turn while we watch. If any door shows its interior, we move the camera or add a privacy mask over that part of the picture. Brick Underground's guidance on hallway cameras makes the same point from the tenant side: even with permission, a camera must not be able to see into anyone's apartment.

Our house shares a driveway with the house next door. How far down it can our cameras look before we are recording the neighbors?

Your camera can watch your doors, your windows, your car and your half of the drive. Where it starts to show the neighbor's side door, their back windows or their yard, you are into territory New York's Civil Rights Law §52-a was written for: it lets residential owners and tenants sue over a neighbor's camera that intrudes on their property. On a shared driveway we usually mount the camera on your own house, angled along your side, and add a privacy mask over the neighbor's door and windows. If you and your neighbor want shared coverage of the whole drive, put that agreement in writing first.

The owner lives downstairs and a tenant lives upstairs. Which apartment should the recorder sit in?

The owner's, or a part of the house only the owner reaches, such as a locked basement room. The recorder holds every camera's footage, including views of both entrances, so whoever can touch it can delete, unplug or remove it. It should be on a battery backup, out of sight from any door or window, and connected to the owner's internet so the owner controls remote access. If the owner lives elsewhere, the basement is usually best, in a locked cabinet. A tenant who needs to see the front door gets that through their own app login, not through physical access to the box.

Can the tenant upstairs watch the front-door camera on their own phone without seeing the yard and the driveway?

Yes, with most recorders we install. The owner keeps the main account, which can see everything and change settings, and we create a separate user for the tenant that can view only the cameras the owner chooses, usually the front door and the stoop. That user cannot delete footage or change camera settings. If the tenant moves out, the owner deletes that user in a minute and the system carries on. We set this up at handover and show the owner how to add or remove users later.

The street outside

Someone speeding past on a scooter at night: what will a camera on our house realistically capture?

Usually a clear record of when it happened and which way they went, and only sometimes a recognizable face. A wide camera over a door is designed to see a large area, so a person thirty feet away on a moving scooter fills very little of the picture, and at night most cameras slow their shutter to gather light, which turns fast movement into a blur. A narrower camera aimed at the point where people have to pass close to the house, with good light on that spot, does much better. We will tell you honestly which of your positions can identify people and which can only show what happened.

People keep dumping trash bags on our stretch of Bronx River Avenue. Will a private camera help, and is the city doing anything?

The city has been asked. Bronx Community Board 9's FY2026 district statement lists a request for an Argus camera on Bronx River Avenue between East 174th and East 177th Streets to address illegal dumping. A private camera on your own house can still help you document dumping in front of your property: the time, the vehicle and the direction it came from. For that, the camera needs to see the curb in front of your house from close range, with light on it, rather than looking up and down the whole avenue, and you should keep the footage when you report it.

We live a block from the Cross Bronx Expressway. Does traffic grime affect cameras?

It does over time. Residents quoted by the Bronx Times and the Scherman Foundation describe air near the expressway as bad enough to keep windows shut, and the same film settles on camera domes and lenses. A dirty dome scatters light, which shows up first at night as glare around streetlights and headlights. We choose positions under an eave or overhang where we can, angle domes so rain helps rinse them, and show you how to clean the lens safely with a soft cloth. On a house close to the highway, plan on wiping the lenses a few times a year.

Businesses by the river

Our repair shop near the river leaves customers' cars outside overnight. How do you cover a lot like that?

From the gate inward. One camera sees the gate or entrance closely enough to show a person or vehicle coming in. Wider cameras on the building cover the rows of parked cars, mounted high so nobody can reach them and aimed across the lot rather than into a streetlight. A camera on the office or bay door covers the most likely way into the building. The recorder sits inside on battery backup, away from the door, and the owner gets the app on their phone. If the lot is dark at night, we tell you where a light is needed, because no camera can show what it cannot see.

Our warehouse walls are concrete block. How do you get camera cable from the loading door back to the office?

Through the wall in one planned opening, then along a protected path. Inside, cable travels on J-hooks or in tray across the ceiling, clear of lights and heaters. Where it has to pass through a block wall, we drill or core-drill once in the right place and seal the opening, using rated fire-stopping where the wall is fire-rated. Outside, any cable within reach goes in metal conduit. We map the route with you on the walk-through, so cable does not cross the loading floor or run along a roll-down door where it would be pinched.

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Bronx River

What follows are claims we see repeated in quick online answers about cameras in New York and in this part of the Bronx, set against what the public record and our field practice say. Marketplace cost guides from Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers; they are useful for a rough idea, but none of them has seen your door, your driveway or your lot.

"Renters can put a doorbell camera on their door like anyone else."

For a NYCHA resident in Bronx River Houses or the Addition, the rule is narrower than that. NYCHA has said residents may attach a wireless camera or video doorbell to their apartment door only for security, only over the peephole or with adhesive, and with no holes or physical alterations.

That rules out the standard wired doorbell camera, which needs screws and a power wire, and it rules out anything mounted on the frame or the hallway wall. A device that breaks the rule can be taken down.

In a private building the landlord and the lease decide; industry guidance on NYC rentals describes doorbell cameras as a gray area that most landlords tolerate but leases can restrict. Ask first, and get the answer in writing.

"Every NYC building with cameras has to post a notice under Local Law 50."

You will find this in several vendor blogs, stated as settled law. We tried to confirm it against the city's administrative code and could not, so we do not repeat it as a rule on this page.

That does not mean notice is a bad idea. In a three-family house or a shop, a plain sign that cameras are in operation tells tenants and customers what to expect and discourages the people the cameras are there for.

If you are a landlord who needs a firm answer on notice, ask your attorney or the city before relying on a blog, including ours. We will install cameras that are visible and clearly placed either way.

"You never need a license to install security cameras in New York."

The sources disagree. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. The Department of State's installer application, meanwhile, says installing passive, non-detecting security devices does not require licensure.

Where exactly a camera system falls between those two depends on what is installed, and online answers tend to flatten that into a confident yes or no. We are not going to settle it in a paragraph.

What we can say is simple: AESS does this work under NYS license #12000287431, and we carry insurance. If a board, landlord or insurer asks who installed your cameras, the answer is a licensed company.

"NYC law requires cameras in apartment buildings."

It does not, as a general rule. Security guidance on NYC building compliance states that there is no general city law mandating cameras in apartment buildings. The law people usually have in mind is about intercoms.

Under Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a, multiple dwellings built or converted after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors and a two-way intercom, and guidance on that law applies it to buildings of eight or more units.

So a two- or three-family house in Bronx River has no camera obligation, and the owner chooses what to cover. The cameras in NYCHA lobbies and elevators exist because NYCHA and the NYPD put them there, not because a law forced every building to.
