The Neighborhood

Why Tremont Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Tremont was one of the first parts of the west Bronx to fill in, and its buildings show it. The Lehman College neighborhood guide says a postmaster made up the name in the mid-nineteenth century as a nod to three local estates, Fairmount, Mount Eden and Mount Hope, and that an east-west streetcar between the north-south rail and elevated lines let Tremont grow earlier than its neighbors. When the municipal building for what was about to become the Borough of the Bronx opened at East Tremont and Park Avenues in 1897, it confirmed the area as the borough's civic center. In the early 1900s, wooden farmhouses and open lots gave way to five- and six-story brick tenements, filled by Irish, Italian and Eastern European Jewish families looking for more room and air, with the Third Avenue elevated as their lifeline. In the 1950s the Cross Bronx Expressway was cut through just south of East Tremont Avenue, separating Tremont from Claremont and Crotona; in the 1970s the Twin Parks scatter-site buildings were set into the gaps. Wikipedia draws today's Tremont between East 181st Street on the north, Third Avenue on the east, the expressway on the south and the Grand Concourse on the west, describes it as very hilly, with stair streets climbing between blocks, and notes that it is predominantly Dominican with a large, long-established Puerto Rican and African American population.

The buildings that matter for a camera job are mostly apartment houses. Wikipedia says Tremont is dominated by five- and six-story apartment buildings, and the listings bear that out: StreetEasy shows 2055 Anthony Avenue as a six-story, 78-unit building from 1920, 1930 Grand Concourse as a five-story, 62-unit building from 1922, and, at the small end, 2065 Valentine Avenue as a two-story, two-unit house from 1901. Between East Tremont Avenue and East 179th Street, Morris Avenue holds the landmarked row of bow-fronted two-family houses and tenements that John Hauser designed between 1906 and 1910. Twin Parks, with about 2,250 apartments split between buildings near Bronx Park and buildings near Webster Avenue, brought 1970s towers by architects including Richard Meier, Giovanni Pasanella and Skidmore, Owings & Merrill. Newer still are buildings like the eight-story affordable building at 2026 Walton Avenue, which lists security cameras among its amenities, and the Webster Avenue Residence at 411 East 178th Street, an eight-story, 170-unit supportive and affordable building opened in 2018. Permits filed in 2026 for a twelve-story, 80-apartment building at 220 East 179th Street and a four-story mixed-use building with seven apartments at 459 East Tremont Avenue show the building has not stopped. Across almost all of this stock, people rent. That is the single fact that shapes a Tremont camera job more than any other: the owner decides what goes in the vestibule and on the landings, and the tenants live with it.

The record of what goes wrong is specific. Wikipedia's figures for 2022 show the 48th Precinct, which covers much of Tremont along with the 46th, reporting 447 robberies, 252 burglaries, 467 grand larcenies and 304 vehicle thefts, though overall crime there fell 60.9 percent between 1990 and 2022. In April 2026, News 12 and Hoodline reported tenants at 1920 Walton Avenue saying late-night parties and drug use had taken over their lobby and hallways, with the super cleaning up after it and sometimes facing confrontations when he did. News 12 visited a newer building on Park Avenue, Tremont Renaissance, which had been advertised with round-the-clock security, and found its front door propped open with a cinder block. CBS New York aired surveillance video of an assault inside a bodega on East Tremont Avenue as police asked for help finding the man. In July 2026 the Citizen app logged a basement fire in a three-story building at 255 East Tremont Avenue. And the housing record is heavy: one HPD data site puts the average Tremont building at 82.1 recorded violations, and lists an open bell, buzzer and intercom complaint from July 2026 at a 35-unit, 1931 building at 805 Fairmount Place. A tenant's 2026 comment to the Rent Guidelines Board described a Tremont building with 338 open HPD violations and a Housing Court order to correct issued that May.

None of that means a camera is required. The city's Housing Maintenance Code, Administrative Code §27-2045, makes owners of multiple dwellings keep a working intercom or buzzer, and the Multiple Dwelling Law requires self-closing, self-locking doors and a two-way intercom in buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after January 1, 1968. There is no general city law that requires cameras in an apartment building. So every camera in Tremont is a choice, made by an owner who usually does not live in the building, for a building full of people who did not pick it. Our job is to make that choice useful: cameras placed where trouble actually comes in, cable that does not crack old plaster or keep a fire door from closing, recordings kept long enough to matter, and a plain written rule for who looks at them.

Tremont facts that decide where a camera goes

  • Walk-ups set the pattern: five- and six-story prewar apartment houses, many with a single stair, a cellar, a rear yard and a roof bulkhead, make up most of the residential stock.
  • Renters, so owners decide: tenants generally cannot put cameras in hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces without the landlord's permission, and a doorbell camera on an apartment door is subject to the lease.
  • A landmarked row: the Morris Avenue Historic District, designated by the Landmarks Preservation Commission on July 15, 1986, means exterior cameras on those fronts need an LPC permit before work begins.
  • Two precincts: the 48th Precinct, at 450 Cross Bronx Expressway, and the 46th share Tremont, which matters when you hand over footage and want it to reach the right detective.
  • Construction keeps arriving: new filings on East 179th Street and East Tremont Avenue add buildings whose camera wiring can be planned before walls close.
  • The avenue is changing: construction on the Tremont Avenue busway began in August 2026, with bus-priority rules from 6 a.m. to 8 p.m. that affect where a work van can stop.

What Tremont owners tend to add next. Once the vestibule and stair cameras are working, owners often want the street-door picture on the intercom panel and the door release tied to the same record, or a reader on the cellar door so the super can see who used it. We leave a labeled spare port on the recorder and, where a door upgrade is likely, a spare cable at the street door and the cellar, so the cellar ceiling only has to be opened once.

Either a camera has stopped showing a picture, often the one on the roof landing or in the cellar, sometimes all of them after a power cut or after the building's internet line was swapped, or something has happened in the building and the clip has to be pulled before the recorder writes over it. Who Watches

Who Sees the Clip: Building a Camera System a Tremont Walk-Up Can Live With

In a neighborhood of houses, the person who pays for a camera and the person it protects are usually the same. In a Tremont walk-up they are rarely the same. The owner signs the quote, often from an office in another part of the city. A managing agent may hold the contract. The super lives in the building, or close to it, and is the one who actually opens the app at night. The tenants walk past the cameras every day, did not choose them, and want to know two things: that the cameras are not aimed at their doors, and that if something happens to them, the footage exists and someone will look at it. A system that ignores any of those people runs into trouble. Tenants who feel watched tape over lenses or complain. A super with no login cannot help anyone at 2 a.m. An owner whose recorder is unlocked in the super's apartment has no idea who has erased what. The fix is not more cameras. It is a plan, written down, that says what each camera watches and who may see it.

Brick Underground has pointed out that a landlord generally has no obligation to share surveillance footage with a tenant. That is the legal floor, and it is not where a sensible building stops. When a package vanishes from a vestibule or a door is kicked in, the owner and the tenant want the same thing, which is a clear record of who came in. A short written viewing rule turns that shared interest into a routine: who reviews footage, how a tenant asks for a review, how fast a clip is saved, and who decides whether it goes to the police, an insurer or nobody. We help owners write that rule as part of every building job, and we set the recorder's accounts to match it.

Aim at paths, not at doors

The quickest way to lose tenants' trust is a camera at the end of a hallway that looks straight into an apartment every time the door opens. In a Tremont walk-up the hallways are short and the doors face each other, so this happens easily. We aim landing cameras down the stair rather than along the corridor, so the picture shows everyone climbing to that floor without settling on any one door. Where a corridor view is needed, the recorder's privacy masks black out each doorway in the picture permanently, and we show the owner what is blacked out before we leave. The camera still answers the question that matters, who came up to the fourth floor at 3:10 a.m., without becoming a log of who visits apartment 4C.

Keep the recorder where nobody can quietly clear it

In many older Tremont buildings the recorder from a previous system sits on a shelf in the super's apartment or open on a cellar workbench. Anyone who can reach it can unplug it, take the drive or, with the password taped to the lid, delete the night that mattered. We put the recorder, the switch and the battery unit in a locked metal box or a locked room the owner controls, off the floor, away from the boiler and away from any spot that has taken water. The super keeps his phone login. The drive stays where only the owner's key reaches it.

Decide in advance how long footage lives

A recorder keeps footage until the drive fills and then starts writing over the oldest days. How many days that is depends on the drive, the number of cameras and how they record. Complaints in a walk-up often surface late: a tenant comes back from a trip to a broken lock, an insurer asks for footage two weeks after a leak, a detective calls after a canvass. We size storage so the street door, vestibule and cellar keep at least a month, and we show the super how to lock a clip the moment a problem is reported so it survives no matter how full the drive gets.

Tell people the cameras are there

We have seen installers state flatly that a city law requires residential owners to post camera notices in common areas, and other installers state just as flatly that no such rule exists. We do not tell owners either one; check with your own counsel if it matters to you. What we can say from experience is that a small notice in the vestibule saying the entrance and stairs are recorded, and who to contact about footage, heads off most of the suspicion that otherwise greets new cameras in a rented building. It also tells the person deciding whether to prop the street door open with a brick that the brick will be on video.

Stoop to Roof Door

Following the Path Through a Five-Story Tremont Walk-Up

Most Tremont apartment houses share a plan that has barely changed in a century. A stoop or a few steps lead up from the sidewalk to an outer street door. Behind it is a small vestibule with the mailboxes and the intercom panel, then an inner door into a narrow lobby. A single stair, sometimes two, turns at every landing up to a roof bulkhead door. Below, a cellar stair leads to the boiler room, the meters, the super's workshop and a door to the rear yard, which is often where the fire escapes come down. On the hilly blocks between the Concourse and Webster Avenue, the cellar may open at grade on the low side of the lot, making it a second street-level entrance in all but name. The point of a camera plan is to cover every way into that chain of spaces with the fewest cameras that still record a face. This table is the layout we start from and then adjust to your building.

Why the inside of the vestibule beats the outside of the street door

An owner's first instinct is a camera on the facade over the street door, where everyone can see it. On a Tremont block that camera fights the light all day: in the afternoon the open sidewalk behind a visitor is far brighter than their face, and at night the streetlight and the store signs downstairs do the same. It also sits within reach of a broom handle. A camera inside, looking at the inner door from the lobby, sees people lit from the front by the lobby lights, at close range, at the exact moment they enter the building. Where an owner wants the deterrence of a visible outside camera too, we add one, but the camera that identifies people is almost always the one inside.

How cable climbs a building with no empty walls

Prewar walk-ups were not built with room for wiring, and the plaster in their stairwells is old enough to crack if it is chased carelessly. The cellar ceiling is the main highway: cable runs across it from the recorder to a point below the stair, the lobby or the street door, and comes up through a small number of drilled and sealed openings. To reach the landings, we look first for a path already there, an old buzzer riser, an abandoned dumbwaiter or pipe chase, the space behind a stair stringer, or a closet stacked floor above floor. Where none exists, a painted metal raceway tight to the corner of the stairwell carries the cable up with screws into mortar rather than across open plaster. Every hole through a floor or a rated wall is sealed with firestop. Nothing is fastened to, or run through the hinge side of, a stair or corridor door in a way that could keep it from closing on its own; after the Twin Parks fire in January 2022, which spread because self-closing doors failed, the City Council toughened the penalties for faulty door closers, and no camera job should give an owner a new reason to worry about them.

Powering a dozen cameras from one locked box

Most walk-ups have no outlets on the landings and no spare circuit in the hallway, and running new line-voltage power up a stairwell is an electrician's job and a permit question. We avoid it by using cameras that take their power over the same network cable that carries the picture. A small building runs on the ports built into the recorder; a six-story building with cameras on every landing, in the cellar and in the yard usually needs a separate powered switch beside the recorder. The whole system then runs from one outlet in the equipment box, on a battery unit, so a short power cut does not black out the stairs.

Morris Avenue

Cameras on the Bow Fronts: Working Inside the Morris Avenue Historic District

The stretch of Morris Avenue between East Tremont Avenue and East 179th Street is the one part of Tremont where a camera on the front of a building is a matter for the Landmarks Preservation Commission. The Historic Districts Council describes the district as two-family row houses and tenements designed by John Hauser and built in five campaigns between 1906 and 1910, most of them three stories tall with curved bow fronts that tie the row together, varied shades and patterns of brick, and different wrought-iron railings along the areaways. Google Arts & Culture's entry credits builder August Jacob with putting the row up over four years, at a time when the extension of the subway in 1904 had set off a rush of speculative building in the Bronx. The Commission held its hearing in 1982 and designated the district on July 15, 1986. Sources disagree about which neighborhood the row belongs to; Wikipedia's Tremont article includes it, while some other listings put it in Mount Hope. For a camera job the label does not matter. The designation does.

NYC Business sums up the rule plainly: if you own a landmark or a building in a designated historic district, you need a permit from the Commission before doing any work. The Commission's permit guidebook has a chapter on health and safety equipment that names security cameras alongside window bars, lighting and intercom panels, and says staff can approve cameras when the installation meets the criteria in Section 2-20 of the Commission's rules. In practice, that means a small, discreet camera with its cable hidden, placed so it does not damage or cover the features that make the row worth protecting. On these houses, those features are exactly where a camera would naturally go: the curve of the bow, the brick patterns, the stoop and the areaway railings.

Where a camera can sit on a Hauser row house

The curved bow is the wrong place for a camera. Mounts do not sit flat on a curve, the brick there is the most visible on the street, and a box stuck to it reads as an intrusion from a block away. The better positions are the flat return beside the entrance, the underside of the stoop or the door hood where one exists, the inside of the vestibule looking out, and, for the lower door, the areaway beneath the stoop, which is lower, shadowed and far less visible from the sidewalk. We drill mortar joints rather than brick faces wherever we can, because a hole in a joint is small, hidden and can be repointed away, while a hole in a face brick is permanent. Cable comes through the wall directly behind the camera and runs inside, so nothing shows on the front.

The back of the house and the inside of the door

Many Morris Avenue owners find that most of what they need can be done without touching the front at all. A camera inside the vestibule, looking at the street door through its glass, records everyone who comes up the stoop. A camera at the back of the house covers the yard and the rear door. Interior work and the rear are still part of the property, and whether a given rear installation needs a permit is a question for the Commission, not for us, but the front is where the Commission's interest concentrates. We prepare the photos, a marked-up elevation and the camera's dimensions and color so the owner or their architect can file, and we do not mount anything visible until the permit is in hand.

Two households, one row house

These were built as two-family houses, and many still are, with an owner on one floor and a tenant on the other, or two tenants and an owner who lives elsewhere. The camera plan has to settle who sees which view. A common arrangement is a recorder in the owner's space, the front stoop and areaway cameras shared with both households through separate app accounts, and the yard camera visible only to the household that uses the yard. That is set up once, written into the lease where appropriate, and changed when a tenant moves.

Prewar walk-ups: the cellar does the heavy lifting

In a walk-up from the 1920s, almost every cable run begins in the cellar. The ceiling there is usually open joists or a plaster ceiling with old penetrations, crowded with heating pipes, water lines and the remains of every phone, cable and buzzer system the building has had. We trace what is live before touching anything, hang new camera cable on its own supports away from hot pipes, and come up into the building through as few openings as possible. Plaster in the halls is often over brick or clay tile; we fasten into the masonry behind it with the right anchors rather than trusting the plaster. Cameras on the landings are small domes that sit close to the ceiling and are hard to knock out of aim with a mop handle. The recorder goes in a locked box in the cellar or a closet the owner controls, raised off the floor.

Concourse elevator buildings: bigger lobbies, more doors

The elevator buildings along the Concourse were built to impress, with tiled or terrazzo lobbies, ornamental plaster and sometimes a courtyard. They also have more ways in: a main entrance, a service door, a cellar door, sometimes a courtyard gate and a garage. The lobby camera faces the inner door; a second camera covers the mailroom or package area; the elevator landing on the ground floor shows who boards; and the service and cellar doors each get their own view. We avoid drilling original tile, finding grout lines or plain plaster nearby instead, and we route cable through the cellar to come up behind walls rather than across the lobby face. If the owner wants a camera in the elevator cab, the picture has to travel down the hoistway by a method the elevator contractor approves, with that contractor present.

Small frame houses: easy cable, tight lots

The few older frame houses that survive among Tremont's apartment buildings are the simplest camera jobs in the neighborhood in one way and the trickiest in another. Cable runs easily through an attic, a crawl space or a basement, and wood trim takes a mount without fuss. But the lots are narrow and hemmed in by taller buildings, so a camera aimed carelessly records the windows next door. We frame each view on the stoop, the side passage and the yard of the house itself, and keep the picture off the neighbors with careful aiming and, where needed, privacy masks.

1970s towers and newer buildings: concrete, block and spare conduit

The Twin Parks buildings and other towers from the 1970s were built of concrete and masonry with interior stair towers; Twin Parks North West, at 333 East 181st Street on Tremont's northern edge, has no external fire escapes at all. Cable there follows whatever conduit and sleeves the original builders left, and where none exist we use a surface raceway or a single planned core through a block wall, sealed with rated firestop. Newer buildings are easier still, often with ceilings that lift out and spare conduit to every floor, but they come with their own catch: a developer-installed system that the owner inherits without documentation. We start those jobs by finding out exactly what was installed, who holds the passwords and whether the original installer's warranty still applies.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Tremont: Questions Answered

Tenants, supers and owners

Our Walton Avenue building lists security cameras as an amenity. When something is taken from outside my door, can I make management show me the recording?

Not by right, in most cases. A landlord generally has no legal obligation to share surveillance footage with a tenant, and an amenity line in a listing does not change that. What works is asking fast and in writing: email or message management the same day with the date, the rough time and what happened, and ask them to save the footage from the vestibule, the stair and your landing before it is recorded over. If you report the theft to the police, tell the officer the building has cameras; detectives can request the footage from the owner. A well-run building has a written rule for exactly this request, and if yours does not, a tenant association can reasonably ask for one. A doorbell camera on your own apartment door, where your lease allows it, gives you a record you control.

At 1920 Walton Avenue the super said he faced confrontations cleaning up after nights of partying in the lobby. Can a super look at the lobby on his phone before he walks down into it?

Yes, and in a building with that kind of trouble it is one of the most useful things a camera system does. The owner gives the super a live-view login on his phone covering the lobby, the vestibule, the stair foot and the cellar door. Before he goes down, he looks. If people are there, he can wait, call the managing agent or call 311 or 911 depending on what he sees, rather than walking into the middle of it alone. The same cameras record, so the owner has a dated record of how often it happens to bring to the precinct or to management. We place the lobby cameras high and in hard domes, since a camera that keeps an eye on a gathering spot is the first thing someone will try to knock down.

Our building is under a Housing Court order to correct violations. Can our tenant association put up its own hallway camera to keep a record of whether repair crews actually come?

Not in the hallway without the owner's permission. Hallways, stairs and lobbies are common areas the landlord controls, and tenants generally cannot install cameras there on their own. What tenants can do is keep their own record: dated photos and videos of conditions taken on their phones, notes of when crews arrived and left, and, inside their own apartments, a camera aimed at the work being done there. Those records are what courts and HPD inspectors work from. If the association wants building cameras, the request goes to the owner or the managing agent in writing; many owners under pressure to show progress are willing, and a camera on the cellar door and the stair can document contractor visits for both sides.

The Morris Avenue Historic District

The house next to ours on Morris Avenue already has a camera on its front. Since the row was built as matching houses, does that mean we are allowed one in the same spot?

No. Each property needs its own Landmarks permit, and the neighbor's camera may have been approved, approved in a different form, or never applied for at all. A camera that went up without a permit is not a precedent; it is a violation waiting to be noticed. If the neighbor did get a permit, ask to see it, because it shows the size, color and position the Commission accepted on a house of the same design, and a matching application is easier to prepare. We use that as a starting point, take our own photos, and file details for your house.

The wrought-iron areaway railings are part of what the district protects. Would clamping a camera to ours avoid drilling into the brick?

It avoids the brick but not the Commission. The varied wrought-iron railings are named by the Historic Districts Council as one of the features that give the row its character, so a camera on one is still exterior work that needs a permit, and a clamp can scratch or stress old ironwork in a way a single mortar-joint anchor does not. A railing also moves when people lean on it or lock bikes to it, which knocks the camera's aim. The better spot is usually inside the areaway, under the stoop, on a plain wall surface that is shadowed and barely visible from the street, with the cable going straight through the wall into the house.

Cellars, fires and the front wall

Our cellar meter room has to stay open to the utility's meter readers, and people we do not know go in and out. Can a camera cover that room?

It can, and it is a sensible place for one. A camera mounted high in the corner opposite the meter room door records the face of everyone who comes in and what they touch. It does not interfere with anyone's right to reach the meters, and it gives the owner a record when a meter is tampered with or a panel is opened by someone who should not have opened it. We mount it where it will not be in the way of the meters or the panels, run its cable on its own supports rather than on the service equipment, and keep it off any space that should not be recorded, such as a super's living quarters next door.

After the July basement fire in a three-story building on East Tremont Avenue, we wondered: if smoke fills our cellar, will the cameras and recorder that did not burn still work?

Sometimes, but do not count on it and do not test it by switching things back on. Smoke leaves soot and residue that can coat lenses, get into a recorder's fans and circuit boards and keep corroding long after the fire is out, and a recorder that sat in a smoky room may fail weeks later. After any fire or smoke event, leave the recorder off until it has been inspected, and if the footage from the time of the fire matters, get the drive pulled and copied first. Going forward, keeping the recorder in a closed metal box off the cellar floor, and saving important clips to the owner's phone or computer when they happen, means a cellar fire does not take the only copy with it.

The fire department connection and its sign are on the front wall right beside our street door. Can a camera mount near it?

Near it, yes; on it or over it, no. The connection, its caps and its sign have to stay clear and visible for firefighters, and nothing should be fastened to the connection or block the sign. We place the street-door camera on the other side of the door or above the door frame where it covers the entrance without crowding the connection, and we route its cable through the wall behind the camera rather than across the front where it could be mistaken for, or tangled with, the fire service equipment.

Shops on the avenue

The busway construction on Tremont Avenue will have crews in front of our store for months. Should our outside camera be set differently while the street is torn up?

Yes, for a few practical reasons. Construction dust settles on lenses, so an outside camera may need wiping every week or two during the work rather than twice a year. Barriers, equipment and crews moving all day will set off motion alerts constantly, so we narrow the alert zone to your doorway and turn off alerts on the street portion until the work is done. If a work zone or barrier blocks the camera's view of your door, we can temporarily re-aim it or add a view from inside the glass. Recording stays continuous throughout, since a busy work zone is also where disputes over damaged awnings, signs or gates come up, and a dated recording settles them.

Owners, Tenants and the Law

The Rules Behind a Tremont Camera Job, in Plain Words

A camera job in Tremont touches a handful of rules, and most of them are simple once you know which apply. They come from five places: the state's installer licensing law, the city's housing rules, the Landmarks Preservation Commission, the lease, and New York's privacy laws.

Who may install. New York's General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security or fire alarm systems without a license. Sources disagree about how far that reaches: the Department of State's installer application says installing "passive," non-detecting security devices does not require a license, while some installers argue that any video surveillance system does. You do not have to settle that argument. AESS holds NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance, and we send copies to owners and managing agents who ask. An owner mounting a camera on their own property is not in the installing business. New line-voltage power, such as a new outlet on a landing, is electrician's work, and the quote says so when a job needs it. In our experience a low-voltage camera job does not usually need a Department of Buildings permit, but work that touches fire alarm wiring, door hardware or new power can change that, and we flag it at the walk-through.

The housing rules. The Housing Maintenance Code requires owners to keep a working intercom or buzzer, and the Multiple Dwelling Law requires self-closing, self-locking doors and a two-way intercom in buildings of eight or more apartments built or converted after 1968. No general city law requires cameras. A camera is not a substitute for a working intercom or a door that latches, and it will not clear a violation for either one. What the rules do require of a camera job is that it not break anything they protect: no cable that holds a self-closing door open, no mount that blocks an exit sign or a sprinkler, no hole through a rated wall or floor left unsealed.

Landmarks. On Morris Avenue between East Tremont Avenue and East 179th Street, the Landmarks Preservation Commission requires a permit before work begins on a building in the district. Its permit guidebook lists security cameras among the equipment it reviews and says staff can approve them when they meet the criteria in its rules. We prepare the photos and mounting details and wait for the permit before installing anything visible. Tremont Baptist Church at 324 East Tremont Avenue is an individual landmark, designated in 2000, and the same permit rule applies to it.

Leases and common areas. Tenants generally may not put cameras in hallways, lobbies or other shared spaces without the landlord's permission. A doorbell camera on an apartment door sits in a gray area that many landlords tolerate and some leases forbid; read yours, and ask in writing. Inside their own apartments, tenants can generally use cameras aimed at their own space, with the usual care around guests and anyone who works there.

Where a camera may look. New York does not forbid recording what anyone can see from public space, such as a sidewalk, a stoop or a vestibule. Civil Rights Law §52-a lets residents sue a neighbor whose camera is aimed into their backyard or other place where they reasonably expect privacy, and Penal Law §250.45 makes it a crime to record people in places like bathrooms and bedrooms without consent. In Tremont, where rear yards back onto each other and fire escapes pass right by other people's windows, we frame every view on your own doors, stairs and yard, and black out anything else. We set cameras to record video, not sound.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Installation in Tremont

Search summaries, AI answers and cost sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr tend to describe camera installation as if every property were a suburban house with an owner inside it. These are claims we see repeated about cameras in buildings like Tremont's, written as summaries rather than quotations, and what the sources and the work show instead.

"New York City apartment buildings are required to have security cameras."

This one comes up constantly, usually mixed up with the intercom rules. The Housing Maintenance Code requires a working intercom or buzzer in multiple dwellings, and the Multiple Dwelling Law adds self-closing, self-locking doors and a two-way intercom in post-1968 buildings of eight or more apartments.

There is no general city law that requires cameras in an apartment building. A lease, a financing agreement or a regulatory agreement may require them for a particular building, and some new buildings, like 2026 Walton Avenue, include them by choice.

For a Tremont owner, the practical point is the reverse of the myth: cameras are optional, but a camera will not cover for a dead intercom or a street door that does not latch, and fixing those comes first.

"Tenants can install their own cameras anywhere they feel unsafe."

Inside their own apartment, aimed at their own space, tenants generally can. That is where the freedom ends.

Hallways, stairs, lobbies and yards are common areas the landlord controls, and tenants generally may not put cameras there without permission. A doorbell camera on an apartment door is a gray area that depends on the lease and the landlord.

In a mostly rented neighborhood like Tremont, the useful move for worried tenants is a written request to the owner, ideally from a tenant association, naming the doors and spaces that need coverage.

"Cameras on historic buildings are banned, or else nobody checks."

Both versions miss. On Morris Avenue, inside the landmark district, a permit from the Landmarks Preservation Commission is required before work begins.

The Commission's own guidebook lists security cameras as equipment its staff can approve when an installation meets the criteria in its rules, which favors small cameras with hidden cable placed away from significant features like the bow fronts and railings.

The real risk is installing first and applying later, which can mean taking the camera down again.

"A camera system in New York costs about $1,500 installed."

Angi publishes an average of about $1,509 for New York, with a typical range of $797 to $2,222. As an average across every kind of property, that is not unreasonable.

It says little about a Tremont building. The cost of a walk-up job is driven by cable paths: whether the cellar ceiling is open, whether an old chase reaches the landings, how much prewar plaster and brick stand in the way, how many rated walls need firestopping, and how many people need their own accounts. Two buildings with the same number of cameras can differ greatly in labor.

That is why we quote line by line, in writing, after seeing the building.

"Wireless cameras solve the problem of old buildings with no wiring."

Wireless cameras skip the picture cable, which sounds ideal for a building with plaster walls and no chases.

In practice, brick walls and old floors between a router and a fifth-floor landing weaken the signal, a building full of tenants' routers crowds the airwaves, and every wireless camera still needs power, which means an outlet, a battery that has to be charged or a cable anyway. A camera that drops off the network at the moment something happens is worse than no camera, because people assume it was recording.

For building systems we wire the cameras and keep wireless for the rare spot where a cable truly cannot go.

"Tremont Avenue is already covered by city cameras."

Two real stories get blurred into this claim. The Tremont Avenue busway will use camera enforcement for its bus-priority rules, and the Bronx Times reported a grant for NYPD cameras on East Tremont Avenue.

That NYPD camera grant covers East Tremont Avenue in Throggs Neck, between Bruckner Boulevard and Harding Avenue, miles east of Tremont. Busway enforcement cameras exist to catch vehicles in the bus lane, not to watch building doors.

Neither one sees your vestibule, your stairs, your cellar door or your store counter.

"Any recorder keeps weeks of footage automatically."

Some do; many do not. How long footage lasts depends on the drive size, the number of cameras, their resolution and whether they record continuously or only on motion.

An old recorder with a small drive and a dozen cameras may hold only a few days, and a recorder whose drive has quietly failed may hold nothing while still showing a live picture.

We size storage for the days you need, set the recorder to warn when a drive or camera fails, and show the super how to lock a clip the moment a problem is reported.
