The Neighborhood

Why Cadman Plaza Properties Need Security Camera Repair

Cadman Plaza is a park before it is a neighborhood. The 10.4-acre green, named for the Reverend Samuel Parkes Cadman and laid out after the city condemned the land in 1935, runs from Borough Hall toward the Brooklyn Bridge between Cadman Plaza West and Cadman Plaza East. The people who call us about cameras here live and work around its edges: in the late-1960s and early-1970s co-op towers and townhouses on the west side, in the new condominium over the Brooklyn Heights Library, in the walk-up buildings along Henry and Clark Streets, and in the law offices, shops and office towers that cluster around the courts and Borough Hall to the south. Those buildings were put up decades apart, by very different owners, but their camera systems share a trait. Almost none of them were designed as one system. They grew a camera at a time.

The biggest of them shows why that matters. Cadman Towers, at 101 Clark Street and 10 Clinton Street, was finished in 1971 as a Mitchell-Lama co-op of two poured-concrete towers, 32 and 13 stories, with about 420 apartments, 50 duplex townhouses at street scale, garages for roughly half the units and an elevated plaza, much of it set behind six-foot concrete walls. Next door, 75 Henry Street rose to 32 or 33 stories by 1973, depending on the source, with a full-time doorman, a garage and its own plaza, while the Whitman Close townhouses beside it were built to echo the brownstones across the street. 140 Cadman Plaza West, Cadman Plaza North, went up in 1967 at 27 floors and about 250 units. The Center for Brooklyn History describes the whole group as one urban renewal complex built on the site of the old Brooklyn Bridge trolley terminal. None of those buildings had cameras on the drawings. Every camera in them was added later, run through concrete that was never meant to take new cable, and wired back to wherever there was room.

That history is now meeting a tight budget in at least one building. In April 2024 Cadman Towers became the first Mitchell-Lama co-op approved to convert to a Housing Development Fund Corporation, with more than three quarters of shareholders in favor. The Real Deal reported that "millions of dollars' worth of deferred maintenance has piled up" and that the building, with a $62 million mortgage coming due, "can't borrow more to pay for repairs." THE CITY added that resale prices stay capped and that new buyers now come through a lottery on Housing Connect. For a camera system, that combination means two things: boards need repairs priced one item at a time so they can choose what to fix first, and more apartments are changing hands, which brings new shareholders who want to know which camera sees their door and who can look at it.

Power is the second pressure. On June 23, 2025, Con Edison asked customers in northwest Brooklyn, naming Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn, DUMBO, Vinegar Hill and Boerum Hill among others, to conserve energy while crews repaired equipment, and said it had "reduced voltage by 8 percent in the area to protect equipment and maintain service." Two months later, on August 23, 2025, a contractor struck a high-voltage feeder at 365 Bridge Street in Downtown Brooklyn, starting a transformer fire that left nearly 230 residents without power; one resident told News 12 that of the building's four elevators, "three of them went out." Neither event was inside the Cadman Plaza towers, but both show the kind of low voltage, sudden loss and restart that camera recorders, network switches and power boxes in nearby buildings have to ride through. After an event like that, some cameras come back and others do not, and the reason is usually a power supply or a switch port, not the cameras.

The third pressure is the street. The 84th Precinct, based at 301 Gold Street, covers Brooklyn Heights, Downtown Brooklyn and the blocks around the park. In February 2026 the Brooklyn Eagle reported on officers honored for curbing retail theft in Brooklyn Heights and Downtown Brooklyn, with the precinct's new commanding officer saying one cosmetics store "is a place that gets hit constantly." In 2022 the Brooklyn Eagle reported that thieves smashed the glass front door of the AT&T store at 160 Montague Street before dawn and took phones and watches, and that the store had no video of the break-in. News 12 reported that on February 26, 2024, two men walked into a store on Henry Street, "ordered an employee to move from a register" and left with $670 of merchandise, and that on September 29, 2025, a man in an Amazon uniform walked into a Brooklyn Heights school building on Joralemon Street and took a package with a $1,500 laptop inside. The lesson for owners is plain. Around Cadman Plaza, a lobby or storefront camera is often the only record of what happened, and it only helps if it was recording, aimed and timestamped correctly that day.

Residents have been thinking along the same lines for years. In 2017 DNAinfo reported that the Brooklyn Heights Association received a $2,500 grant from the borough president's office toward street-facing cameras on homes along Joralemon Street, about two per block, with a spokesman saying cameras "could one, be a deterrent, and two, help police apprehend perpetrators." Cameras put up in that era are now nine years old or more, which is about when drives, power adapters and the first generation of phone apps start to give out. And in November 2025 News 12 reported that the path lights in Cadman Plaza Park had been out for weeks, with residents using flashlights to cross it. A camera on a building that faces the park and relies on that light to see at night loses most of its picture when the lights go dark.

Who has to fix a broken camera is its own question here, because so many systems belong to someone other than the person who notices the problem. No city law requires a rental building's cameras to work, as the reality check further down explains. In a co-op the board or the managing agent decides; in a condo, the management office; in an office tower, the landlord or the tenant, depending on whose system it is. We book the repair with whoever controls the recorder and can approve the price, and we write the cause and the cost plainly enough to be forwarded to a board, a landlord or an insurer. Many Cadman Plaza buildings also pair the repair with a look at the door intercom or the access control on the same visit, since the same desk usually watches both.

Concrete towers over a garage: the run is the repair

A camera system in one of the Cadman Plaza co-op towers is really a set of long cables. NYC Urbanism describes Cadman Towers as saw-toothed poured concrete, with large windowless concrete walls, an elevated plaza and a bridge over Clark Street; 75 Henry Street and 140 Cadman Plaza West are concrete high-rises of the same era. When cameras were added to buildings like these, installers could not open walls or chase cable into slabs, so they used whatever path existed: an electrical or telephone riser, a spare sleeve through a floor, the garage ceiling, exposed raceway along a corridor. The result is that a camera on the top of a 32-story tower or at the far corner of a garage can be several hundred feet of cable away from the recorder, with joints, splitters, adapters and patch points along the way, and each of those is a place where the picture can be lost.

That changes how we diagnose. On a short run in a house, a dark camera is usually the camera. On a long tower run it is just as often the cable, a joint in a riser closet, an amplifier or balun that failed, or the power at the far end being too weak to start the camera, even though the voltage looks fine at the recorder. We test at both ends, measure power at the camera itself, and use a tester or analyzer on the run before recommending any new camera. When a riser cable has genuinely failed along its length, the replacement goes up the same path where there is room, and the written price says how many floors it covers before any cable is pulled. Where the building has core holes or sleeves with spare capacity we use them; we do not drill new holes through structural concrete without the building's approval and the super or engineer's say on where it is safe.

Townhouse rows inside the complex: a private camera on a shared wall

The duplex townhouses at the foot of Cadman Towers were designed, NYC Urbanism notes, to give the complex a street scale, and Brownstoner described the Whitman Close row at 75 Henry Street, between Clark and Middagh, as roughly sixteen two-story units echoing the brownstones across the street. Owners in these rows often have two or three cameras of their own, covering a front door, a garden or a gate, on a small recorder inside the house. The complication is the wall. The camera may be mounted on brick or concrete that belongs to the co-op, and the cable may pass through a space the co-op controls. Before we remount or replace a camera on an outside wall, we check with the owner whose wall it is and whether the co-op's alteration rules apply, and we keep the replacement in the same holes where that is sound, so there is nothing new for the building to approve.

The 2020s condominium: newer equipment, different failures

One Clinton, at 280 Cadman Plaza West, opened in 2022 as a 36-story tower of about 134 condominiums, designed by Marvel Architects for the Hudson Companies, with the Brooklyn Heights Library on its lower floors. A building that new usually has network cameras, managed switches and a recorder or video management software chosen by the developer's installer, and its problems are rarely worn-out hardware. They are a firmware update that changed how the cameras talk to the recorder, an admin login held by the installer who left when the building was handed over, a switch configuration nobody documented, or a camera that was replaced under warranty with a slightly different model. Repairs here start with getting the management office the access and documentation it should have received.

Walk-ups on the side streets: short runs, crowded cellars

The small brick apartment buildings along Henry Street and the streets toward the Heights are the opposite of the towers. Runs are short and systems are small, often four to eight cameras on a vestibule, a hallway, a back yard and a cellar door, with the recorder on a shelf in the cellar or in a closet off the stairs. Their faults are ordinary and fixable: a drive near the end of its life, a power adapter that failed, a cable cut during plumbing work, a recorder sitting in a damp corner beside the boiler.

Court Street's landmarked office towers: a suite inside someone else's building

South of the park, the Borough Hall Skyscraper Historic District, approved in September 2011, takes in 21 buildings along Court Street between Montague and Livingston Streets, among them 32 Court Street, described in the designation coverage as Brooklyn's first true skyscraper. The Montague-Court Building at 16 Court Street, finished in 1927 at 35 stories, is Brooklyn's tallest office building. Camera repairs in these towers usually involve a tenant's own cameras inside a suite, sometimes recording to a small recorder in a closet and sometimes to the tenant's server, on a network the landlord's IT team or a telecom closet controls. The building runs the lobby cameras and the freight elevator. We work inside the suite, coordinate any closet or riser access with the building manager, and leave the landlord's equipment alone unless the landlord asks us to look at it.

Brooklyn Paper reported in 2016 that the 75 Henry Street co-op owns Pineapple Walk, the strip of stores and the Park Plaza Restaurant between Cadman Plaza West and Henry Street. Stores like those, and the shops on Montague and Henry Streets, usually own the cameras inside and over the door, record to a box under the counter or in a back room, and rent the wall the outside camera hangs on. Their repairs are fast and practical: a camera over the register that has drifted, a door camera that turns faces into silhouettes against the bright street, a recorder that quietly filled its drive. NYC Urbanism notes that Cadman Towers was built with garages for roughly half its units, and 75 Henry Street has its own. Field Notes

Field Notes: Following a Tower Camera From the Desk Screen to the Far End of Its Cable

These notes describe how our technicians approach a large camera system in a concrete residential tower like the ones around Cadman Plaza. They set out standard practice, not a story about any particular building.

Start where the building watches. The technician begins at the desk monitor or the screen the super uses, noting which channels show video, which are dark, frozen or grey, and what the recorder says about its drive and clock. Desk staff are asked when each problem started.

Find the head end and its power. Next comes the room where the cables meet: a garage office, a cellar room, a telecom closet or a cabinet behind the desk. The recorder, the power box or switch, and the battery backup are checked under load, because a power supply that sags at night or a switch that rebooted after a dip can take a whole floor of cameras with it.

Trace before touching. Unlabeled cables are toned out and labeled as they are identified.

Measure at the far end. For any camera that is weak or intermittent, the technician measures power and signal at the camera itself, not only at the recorder. On long runs this is where most of the answers are.

Price before parts, and leave a map. You hear the cause and get a written price before anything is replaced. When the work is done, every camera is checked live and in playback, cables traced are labeled at both ends where reachable, and a short list of cameras, channels and locations goes on your job record so the next repair starts from a map instead of from scratch.

Fault by Fault

Distance, Power, Layers and Light: How Cadman Plaza Camera Systems Break and What Each Repair Involves

Five causes account for most of the camera failures we find in the buildings around the park. Each leaves its own signs, and knowing which one you have is what keeps the repair to the part that actually failed.

Distance: what a long run does to a picture

Every cable loses some signal and some power over its length, and the towers around Cadman Plaza have some of the longest camera runs we see in Brooklyn. On older coax systems, a long run shows up as a picture that gets softer, darker or more washed out the farther the camera is from the recorder, often with the color fading first. On network systems powered over the data cable, the limit is sharper: a standard network cable run is rated to about 100 meters, roughly 328 feet, and a camera beyond that, or one fed through a chain of patch points and cheap couplers, may boot, drop, reboot and drop again, especially in cold weather when its heater or infrared draws more power. A coax run may need a proper balun, an amplifier or a fresh connector at a loose joint; a network run may need a small switch or extender in a riser closet partway up. We test before recommending any of those, because a single bad joint can look exactly like a run that is simply too long.

Power: dips, restarts and the battery backup that gave up

Camera systems rarely die in an outage. They are damaged by what happens around it. A recorder that loses power while writing can corrupt its drive's file system. A switch can come back with one dead port, or with every port reset. A cheap central power box can fail when the power returns, or start sagging at night when every camera switches on its infrared. Low voltage, like the 8 percent reduction Con Edison applied across northwest Brooklyn on June 23, 2025, is harder to see: equipment keeps running, but a power supply that was already marginal may start restarting the recorder or dropping the cameras on the end of the longest runs. The repair is to find which link failed, replace it, and put the recorder and the switch on a battery backup that has been tested under the real load. Power supply repair typically runs $150 to $400 on our Brooklyn list, and a drive swap runs $200 to $450 by capacity.

Layers: fifty years of cameras added one at a time

The Cadman Plaza complex went up between 1967 and 1973, and its camera systems have been added to, swapped out and patched ever since. A typical tower has analog cameras from one decade, HD-over-coax cameras from the next, a handful of network cameras on a separate switch, a hybrid recorder trying to make sense of all three, and a few cameras that were disconnected years ago but are still on the wall. When something fails in a system like that, we trace, label and list the cameras first, fix the fault, and tell the board which layer is closest to the end of its life. A hybrid recorder that runs the older coax cameras next to new network cameras runs $700 to $1,200 installed on our Brooklyn list.

People and accounts: the logins outlive the staff

Many camera problems around Cadman Plaza are not hardware at all. A recorder's admin password was set by an installer who is gone, a super who retired, or a managing agent the board replaced. Phone viewing was tied to one person's email. The building changed internet providers and nobody updated the recorder. In a co-op where apartments are changing hands through a new lottery, new shareholders and staff need access while old ones should lose it. These repairs are mostly configuration: recovering admin access through the manufacturer's procedure, which can need proof that the building owns the system, then setting up named accounts for the people the board approves, and recording who has which login. FirstService Residential's advice to boards is to decide who may see footage, keep access as narrow as possible and log who looks at it, and a repaired system is a good moment to put that in place. With no parts, that work is often covered by the $150 service call.

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Repair in Cadman Plaza: Questions Answered

These are the situations shareholders, boards, managing agents, desk staff and business owners around the park describe when they call about cameras in concrete towers, shared walls and leased suites. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Concrete towers and long cable runs

The cameras near the top of our tower look grey and washed out, while the lobby cameras look fine. Is it the cameras or the distance?

Usually the distance, or something along it. On older coax systems the signal weakens over a long run, and the cameras farthest from the recorder are the first to lose contrast and color. A loose or corroded joint in a riser closet, a failed amplifier or balun, or a power supply that is too weak at the far end can make it worse. We test the run and measure at the camera before recommending anything. The fix is often a connector, a balun or a small amplifier placed at the right end; a reterminate runs $150 to $350 on our Brooklyn list. A new camera on the same weak run would look the same.

Our building is solid concrete. Can a dead hallway camera be swapped for a wireless one so nobody has to drill?

Sometimes, but rarely in the towers. Wireless cameras need a strong, steady signal to a router or access point, and concrete floors and walls block most of it, so a wireless camera in a tower corridor often drops out or records in fits and starts. It also still needs power, which means a cable or an outlet anyway. Where the old camera's cable is still in place, the cheaper and more reliable repair is almost always a wired camera on that cable, even if the old camera was analog and the new one connects through an adapter. Where there is no usable cable, we look for an existing riser or sleeve before considering any wireless link, and we tell you the trade-off in writing.

The camera cable in our stair tower passes through the wall into the hallway. When it is replaced, does the hole need anything special?

Yes. Stair towers and corridor walls in high-rises are usually fire-rated, and any hole through them should be sealed with a fire-rated sealant or a fitting made for the purpose so the wall keeps doing its job in a fire. Old installations sometimes left an open hole or stuffed it with ordinary foam. When we replace a cable through one of those walls, we seal the penetration properly and point out any others we see. If the building's super or engineer has a standard product they use, we use that one.

Lobbies, desks and the people watching

Our doorman watches all sixteen cameras on one screen, and the pictures are too small to recognize anyone. Is that the recorder or the monitor?

Usually neither is broken; it is the layout. A sixteen-way split on one monitor shrinks every camera to a thumbnail. Most recorders can drive a second output, cycle through cameras, or show a large view of the main door beside smaller views of the rest, and many can pop up a camera full screen when it detects motion. Where the monitor itself is old or has a low resolution, or the cable from the recorder is the wrong type, the picture can also look soft. We set up a layout that matches what the desk actually needs to watch, and if the monitor or its cable is the weak link, we price that one part.

There are cameras in our lobby that may not be connected to anything. How can we tell which ones actually record?

By matching each camera on the wall to a channel on the recorder, which is part of most visits we make to older buildings. We list every camera we can see, compare that to the channels the recorder shows and records, and trace the ones that do not match. Some turn out to be dead cameras still on their brackets, some are connected to a second old recorder nobody remembers, and a few are dummy housings. You get a written list, and anything that is just clutter can be taken down if the building wants it gone. A lobby with cameras that look live but are not is worse than one with fewer cameras that all work.

A neighbor put his own camera over his apartment door in our co-op hallway, and it has stopped working. Is it his to repair or the building's?

It depends on whether the co-op approved it. If the board allowed it, the camera is usually the shareholder's to maintain, under whatever conditions the approval set. If it went up without approval, the hallway is still the building's common space, and as one manager put it on a Habitat board forum, shareholders "don't own any portion of their space outside of their interior doors," so the board decides whether it stays at all. We take repair instructions on hallway equipment from the managing agent or the board, not from the shareholder alone, and we do not tie a private camera into the building's recorder without the building's written say-so.

The park side, the plaza and the townhouses

Our building faces Cadman Plaza Park, and since the park's path lights went out our cameras show nothing past the fence at night. Can that be fixed on our side?

Partly. Those cameras were almost certainly using the park's light rather than their own infrared, which typically reaches only part of the way across a wide open space. On our side, we can check whether the infrared is working at all, clean or replace it if it is not, aim the camera so it covers your own entrance and fence line well rather than the far side of the park, and recommend a camera with better low-light performance for that position. What a building camera cannot do is light the park. If the long view matters, a steady light on your own property near the door helps more than any camera setting, and that is a building decision we can advise on.

Our townhouse at the base of the complex has a camera on the garden wall, and the wall belongs to the co-op. Who has to approve the repair?

If the camera goes back in the same spot on the same holes, most co-ops treat it as the owner's ordinary maintenance, but check your alteration rules. If the camera moves, needs new holes, or its cable needs a new path through a wall the co-op owns, expect to need the board's or managing agent's approval first. We ask before the visit, keep the replacement in the existing mounting where the masonry is sound, and give you a short written description of the work you can send to the building if it asks.

Boards, budgets and records

Our co-op converted to an HDFC and money is tight. If half our cameras need work, which repairs should come first?

Start with the head end and the doors. If the recorder, its drive or its power is failing, every camera is at risk, so that comes first. Next come the cameras at the entrances people actually use: the lobby door, the garage gate, the service and delivery door, the package area. Then the cameras covering places where problems have already happened. Corridor and stair cameras on upper floors usually come last. We price each item separately on the written estimate so the board can approve the first group now and the rest later, and we mark which items are urgent and which can wait without real risk.

We received a lawyer's letter asking the building to preserve lobby video of a fall. What has to happen to the recorder?

Act before the recorder overwrites that period. Most recorders keep a set number of days and then record over the oldest footage, so the first step is to export the requested time window, from every camera that might show it, to a USB drive in the recorder's own format and a common video format, with the recorder's clock noted against the real time. We can do that on a booked visit, leave the original on the recorder, and give you a short written note of what was exported, from which cameras and when. What to hand over, to whom, and how long to keep it are questions for your attorney.

Offices near the courts

Our office near the courthouses wants its own camera at reception. The building already covers the corridor. Can our camera stay separate from the landlord's system?

Yes, and in most leased suites it should. A small recorder in your own closet, or recording to your own server, keeps your footage under your control and your camera off the landlord's equipment. AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Security Camera Repair in Cadman Plaza

These claims turn up in AI-generated answers and in national cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr. Each is paraphrased, then set against what the work looks like in concrete towers, leased office suites and shops around the park.

"Expect to pay somewhere between $680 and $2,145 to fix your cameras"

HomeAdvisor's Brooklyn page for surveillance camera installation or repair gives a typical range of $680 to $2,145, an average of $1,416 and a low-to-high spread of $160 to $3,500, and AI summaries tend to repeat the typical range as the cost of a repair.

Those figures blend new installations with repairs. A repair around Cadman Plaza is usually one fault: a joint in a riser, a dead drive, a power box that failed after a dip, a switch port, a lost login. On our Brooklyn list most single faults cost $150 to $700, and only a recorder replacement, a partial rewire or a long riser run replaced over many floors reaches $700 to $1,500.

The planning number for a building with one problem is the $150 service call plus the one failed part, written down before the work. For a co-op watching every dollar, the useful document is an itemized list it can approve one line at a time, not an average built from other people's new systems.

"Wireless cameras are the easy answer when a building is hard to wire"

AI answers often suggest replacing a hard-to-reach wired camera with a Wi-Fi one, as if skipping the cable solves the problem.

In a poured-concrete tower it usually creates a new one. Concrete floors and walls block most Wi-Fi signal, so a wireless camera in a corridor, stair or garage often drops out or records in pieces, and it still needs power from somewhere. Meanwhile the old camera's cable, already run through the building at someone's expense years ago, is often perfectly usable.

The cheaper, steadier repair in the Cadman Plaza towers is nearly always a wired camera on the existing cable, with an adapter if the old camera was analog and the new one is not. Wireless links have a place, but only after the existing paths have been ruled out.

"Your landlord or co-op is required to keep the cameras working"

Answers about broken building cameras often state, without qualification, that the building must repair them.

It is not that simple. Brick Underground has reported that "there's no city law requiring rental buildings to have functioning security cameras." The same article quoted attorney Jeffrey Reich that a landlord on notice of criminal activity "may be obligated to take reasonable safety precautions," and that fixing the camera "would certainly be a reasonable action." It also suggested that "NYPD involvement may be the deciding factor in getting your building management to repair those cameras."

In practice, around Cadman Plaza, the person who controls the recorder decides, whether that is a co-op board, a condo office, a landlord or a store owner. A written note of what failed and what it costs to fix, sent to that person, gets more repairs approved than any general statement about obligations. We write that note as part of every visit.

"A high-rise system is just a bigger home system"

Many answers give the same troubleshooting steps for a thirty-story co-op as for a two-camera house: check the cable, restart the recorder, replace the camera.

A tower system differs in kind. Its runs can be several times longer than a house's, it may have more than one head end, its cables share risers with other building systems, and its recorder may serve a desk, a management office and dozens of phones at once. Restarting the recorder in a building like that can interrupt recording for the whole property and lose the footage someone needed.

The right approach is to save any needed footage, then test from the head end outward and measure at the far end of the run. That is slower than swapping a camera, and much cheaper than swapping several cameras that were never the problem.

"Anyone who lives in the building can ask to see the footage"

Some answers suggest residents can simply ask the super or the desk to play back video whenever something happens.

Well-run buildings handle it more carefully. FirstService Residential's advice to New York co-op and condo boards is that access usually goes to the property manager or a security provider, that residents should not receive copies without the board's and counsel's approval, and that the manager should keep a log of requests and of who viewed footage. Its executive Christina Forbes put it simply: "Limiting access protects the privacy of residents and helps prevent potential misuse."

A repair is a good moment to match the system to that policy: named logins instead of one shared password, viewing-only accounts for desk staff, and export rights for the few people who need them. We set that up as the board directs; the policy itself is the board's decision.

"Power problems only matter when the lights go out"

AI answers about camera outages focus on full blackouts and suggest a battery backup as the whole answer.

Partial power problems cause as much trouble. When Con Edison reduced voltage by 8 percent across northwest Brooklyn on June 23, 2025, the lights stayed on, but equipment on marginal power supplies can misbehave under conditions like that, and a recorder that restarts mid-write or a switch that resets can leave cameras off-line after the power is back to normal. A transformer fire like the one at 365 Bridge Street in August 2025 shows how quickly a building can lose service altogether.

The fix is to know what happened to each link in the chain: power box, switch, recorder and drive. After any power event, check playback for the hours around it. If a camera did not come back, the cause is usually a power supply or a port, and that is a modest repair.

"A new building's cameras won't need repairs for years"

Owners in newer buildings are sometimes told that recently installed systems are maintenance-free.

Newer systems break differently, not less. In a condominium like One Clinton, opened in 2022, cameras and recorders are network devices that depend on firmware, switch settings, accounts and an internet connection. A firmware update can change how a camera talks to the recorder, an installer can leave with the admin password, and a network change elsewhere in the building can cut off remote viewing overnight.

The protection is documentation: who holds the admin login, which switch ports feed which cameras, which firmware versions are running, and who to call. If the developer's installer never handed that over, a repair visit is the time to rebuild it, and we leave a copy with the management office.
