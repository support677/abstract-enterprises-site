The Neighborhood

Why Claremont Properties Need Structured Cabling Installation

Claremont is a neighborhood of big buildings with many owners, old buildings with layered wiring, and brand-new buildings whose networks are being designed right now, and each of those facts changes how a cable gets from the provider's line to the place it is needed. When we matched the city's PLUTO property records to the census tracts in and around Claremont Village and the blocks east of the Grand Concourse that border Claremont Park, the area held about 14,750 homes on 677 residential lots. Sixty-six buildings with fifty or more apartments hold roughly 9,492 of those homes, close to two out of every three. The rest are spread across about 299 walk-up apartment buildings, a few dozen private elevator buildings, and 278 one- and two-family houses that together hold only about 524 homes. In a place shaped like that, the typical cabling customer is not a homeowner with an attic. It is a tenant, a managing agent, a co-op board, a nonprofit housing operator, a general contractor, or a shopkeeper renting the ground floor of somebody else's building.

The age of those buildings matters as much as their size. The same property records put about 6,042 of the area's homes in buildings finished between 1901 and 1929, mostly the five- and six-story apartment houses that CityNeighborhoods.nyc describes along Clay, Fulton and Webster Avenues, and about 4,758 in buildings from 1940 to 1967, the decades of the big public housing towers. Wikipedia records that the Butler Houses, six buildings of 21 stories between East 169th and East 171st Streets, were completed in December 1964, that the five 21-story Webster Houses followed in September 1965, and that the Morris Houses were completed in August 1965. The Neighborhoods newsletter describes that cluster of developments as Claremont Village, home to more than 10,000 residents. A walk-up from 1925 and a tower from 1965 were both built long before anyone imagined a data network, but they were built very differently, and a cable route that works in one will not work in the other.

Then there is the third kind of building, the one going up now. The Bronx Times reports that The Heartwood, a 19-story building at 155 East 173rd Street in the Claremont neighborhood, will replace the 1959 Grand Concourse Library with a new 17,500-square-foot library and 113 affordable rent-stabilized apartments, about 15 percent of them for formerly homeless households. The New York Housing Conference describes 3500 Park Avenue in the Claremont Village neighborhood as 115 studios and two-bedroom apartments, 69 of them for formerly homeless New Yorkers and 46 for low-income seniors and families, with a live-in super and about 20 on-site workers. New York YIMBY reported in October 2026 that another project, Sol On Park, had broken ground at 3728 Park Avenue in Claremont Village. Every one of those buildings needs a telecom room, a riser, cable to staff offices and front desks, access points in community rooms, and cable for cameras and entry panels, and all of it is cheapest and neatest when it goes in while the walls are still open.

Who controls the building decides who can say yes to a cable. The four Claremont Village developments and nearby sites belong to NYCHA, which runs its own projects; its PACT releases for other Bronx developments describe building-wide free Wi-Fi, new intercoms and hundreds of cameras installed as part of owner-led renovations. Optimum's Big Apple Connect page lists Butler, Webster, Morris I and II, Morrisania, Morrisania Air Rights, Claremont Parkway-Franklin Avenue and Claremont Rehab among the Bronx developments where residents get free home internet with one combined modem and Wi-Fi router per household. The same property records show about 56 lots with roughly 2,257 homes held under HDFC owner names, which usually means a co-op board decides. The private buildings answer to an owner or a managing agent, and every shop on Webster Avenue, Third Avenue and East 170th Street answers to a landlord. We do not drill until the right person has agreed in writing.

Getting service into a building is its own step. Two provider comparison sites disagree sharply about ZIP 10457, which covers much of Claremont. ISPReports, working from federal broadband data as of December 2025, shows Optimum available at 99.6 percent of addresses and Verizon fiber at 93.4 percent. InternetProviders.ai, using June 2026 federal data, puts Verizon Fios at about 65 percent and tells readers to confirm availability at their address. BroadbandNow says fiber reaches 84.7 percent of the Bronx as a whole. In practice, whether fiber reaches a particular apartment in an older building usually depends on whether the owner has let the provider build a path through the cellar and up the building. A Verizon community forum thread from a tenant in an older apartment building describes exactly that: the landlord would not allow holes through the hallway, and the replies pointed out that the provider still needs a path for its fiber and will not reuse another company's conduit. A well-planned building riser solves that problem for every provider at once.

Inside the apartments and shops, the network problems we hear about are ordinary ones made worse by the buildings. A free gateway left by the front door because that is where the coax came out of the wall. A back bedroom behind two plaster-and-masonry walls where the Wi-Fi drops every evening. A cellar where four providers' lines, a dead phone frame and a camera recorder share one damp corner. A store whose card terminal runs on Wi-Fi because nobody ever pulled a cable to the counter. A staff office in a supportive building where the case managers' computers sit on the same network the residents use. None of those is solved by a faster plan. They are solved by a cable that goes from the right place to the right place, tested and labeled so the next person can find it.

We also want to be honest about what we could not find. We found no published DOB, 311 or HPD records that track data cabling in Claremont, no Reddit threads from Claremont residents about it, and no Yelp complaints about cabling contractors that we could tie to the neighborhood. CityNeighborhoods.nyc, which we use for its boundary description and its note that East 170th Street and Webster Avenue form a local hub, disagrees with NYC Parks and Wikipedia on several historical dates, so we do not rely on it for anything else. Where this page describes walls, cellars and risers, it draws on the building ages and types in the public record and on what we find in buildings of those kinds, not on an inspection of any particular address.

Claremont facts that shape a cabling plan

  • Big buildings dominate: 66 buildings with 50 or more apartments hold about 9,492 of roughly 14,750 homes in the area we studied, so most jobs involve a cellar, a riser and somebody other than the occupant who approves the work.
  • Two building eras: about 6,042 homes in buildings from 1901 to 1929 and about 4,758 from 1940 to 1967, which means prewar plaster and masonry on one block and 1960s concrete towers on the next.
  • Public housing with its own internet program: Optimum lists Butler, Webster, Morris I and II and other nearby NYCHA developments for Big Apple Connect, which supplies one combined modem and router per household at no monthly charge.
  • Board-run buildings: about 56 lots with roughly 2,257 homes are held under HDFC owner names, so a co-op board, not a single landlord, signs off on shared pathways.
  • New construction near the park: The Heartwood plans 113 apartments and a 17,500-square-foot library at 155 East 173rd Street, and 3500 Park Avenue added 115 supportive and affordable apartments in Claremont Village.
  • No electrical permit for data wiring: under Local Law 128 of 2024, wiring under 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data no longer needs an electrical permit when done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person.

What usually follows good cabling in Claremont. Once a building has a labeled cellar cabinet and a tested riser, owners and boards tend to ask about cameras at the street door, a modern entry panel or fob readers at the lobby within a year or two. All of those can ride the same pathways, so at the first walk-through we note where spare cable or an empty sleeve could go and leave room in the cabinet, even if nobody has asked for it yet.

Desk Offline, Store Terminal Dropping, or a Whole Floor Lost Its Connection? Quick Help in Claremont

Some problems cannot wait for a planning visit: a front desk in a staffed building that lost its connection during the overnight shift, a card terminal on Webster Avenue that keeps dropping at the busiest hour, a floor of apartments that went dark after another contractor worked in the cellar. Before calling, take two minutes. Is the provider's box lit? Does the router show internet? Is it one jack, one apartment, one floor or the whole building? Did anyone work in the cellar, the riser closet or the ceiling recently, and has a provider's technician been in the building? One dead jack usually points to that cable or its connector; a whole floor usually points to a switch, a power strip or a cable in the riser; everything dead usually points to the provider, the router or power. If water reached any equipment, unplug it at the outlet and leave it off. Then call (646) 490-0629. We book within 2 hours and tell you honestly when the earliest visit can be.

Who Controls What

Five Kinds of Owner, Five Kinds of Cabling Job in Claremont

In a neighborhood of single-family houses, the person who calls us is the person who decides. In Claremont that is rarely true. The tenant who wants a wired desk does not own the wall. The super who has the cellar keys does not sign the contract. The nonprofit that runs a new building may not own the land, and the shop on Webster Avenue shares its cellar with the apartments upstairs. So before we talk about cable types or prices, we work out which of five situations a job falls into, because that decides where the cable may go, who has to agree, and who keeps the records afterward.

Private walk-ups: the cellar decides the route

Most of Claremont's prewar walk-ups receive every service in the cellar, usually on the wall closest to the street. Over the decades, each phone company, cable company and alarm installer added its own box there, and in many buildings nobody can say which lines still carry anything. The cleanest plan starts by giving the building one organized point of entry: a labeled backboard or a small locked cabinet where every provider's line ends, and a riser path that climbs to each floor in a protected sleeve rather than as loose cable stapled up a stairwell. From there, each apartment's line can be connected for whichever provider a tenant chooses. The owner gets one place to look when something fails, and providers stop drilling new holes through the front of the building every time a tenant switches service.

Elevator buildings: work with the core, not around it

A private elevator building from the 1920s or the postwar decades usually has a stair and elevator core running from the cellar to the roof, and a closet or two on each floor that once held telephone equipment. Those closets are the natural place for a floor's cable to meet the riser. We look at what is already in them, which pipes and power share the space, and whether the floor-to-floor openings are already sleeved and sealed. Where they are not, new sleeves go in with proper firestopping, because a riser is exactly the kind of opening that lets smoke travel between floors if it is left open. The managing agent gets a riser diagram that shows every sleeve and every cable, so the next contractor does not have to guess.

HDFC co-ops: a plan the next board can read

An HDFC board is often a handful of volunteer shareholders, and boards change. A cabling plan for an HDFC building has to make sense to people who were not at the meeting where it was approved. We write it in plain language, phase it so the shared parts can be done first and individual apartments later if the budget requires it, and leave records that explain what each sleeve, cabinet and cable is for. Shareholders who want their own drops can then hire the work separately, using pathways the board already approved, instead of each one asking for a new hole.

NYCHA towers: what we will and will not do

The Butler, Webster and Morris Houses are NYCHA property, and their risers, hallways and walls are NYCHA's to change. Building-wide networks in public housing are owner projects; NYCHA's PACT releases for other Bronx developments describe free Wi-Fi, new intercoms and cameras installed as part of renovations led by NYCHA and its partners. We do not open walls, add sleeves or touch shared equipment in a NYCHA building at a resident's request. What a resident can do inside an apartment without altering the building is limited, and we explain those limits honestly on the phone before anyone books a visit. A tenant association or community program that needs a network in a NYCHA space should start with NYCHA's property management, and we are glad to talk with whoever NYCHA designates.

New nonprofit-run buildings: the operator inherits everything

When a developer and a general contractor finish a new building, they hand it to an operator, often a nonprofit that also provides services on site. That operator lives with every cabling decision made during construction. We try to involve the operator's property manager and IT contact early, so that the front desk, the staff offices, the community room and the cameras are cabled the way the people who run the building need them, and so the closeout package lands with someone who will keep it.

Shops: your space is yours, the cellar usually is not

A store on Webster Avenue or East 170th Street controls its own sales floor and back room, but the cellar below, the meter room and the route to the street usually belong to the building. Before quoting, we confirm with the landlord or managing agent where the provider will deliver service, which part of the cellar the shop may use, and whether a certificate of insurance is required. Inside the shop, the plan is the shop's own: a locked cabinet, cable to the counter and the office, and guest Wi-Fi kept apart from the register.

Before the Walls Close

Cabling Claremont's New Buildings While They Are Still Being Built

Claremont is one of the parts of the Bronx where new affordable and supportive buildings keep arriving. The Heartwood at 155 East 173rd Street, which the city's developer selection in November 2025 assigned to Settlement Housing Fund and Kalel Companies, still needs a rezoning, a transfer of city property, financing and a temporary library before construction begins, according to 6sqft. 3500 Park Avenue is already open. Sol On Park at 3728 Park Avenue broke ground in October 2026, according to New York YIMBY. News 12 covered the unveiling of another new building, Claremont House, in 2023. For the low-voltage side of buildings like these, the difference between doing the work at the right moment and doing it after the walls close is the difference between a neat, documented system and years of surface raceway.

The telecom room is the heart of the building

Every provider, every floor and every staff system meets in one room, so its size, power, ventilation and security decide how well the building runs for decades. We look for enough wall space for several providers' equipment, rack space for the building's own switches and recorders, a dedicated outlet or two on the right circuit, a way to keep the room within the temperature the equipment needs, and a door that locks. In a building with a front desk staffed around the clock, the desk usually needs to reach the room quickly without the room being open to everyone who walks by.

Airtight buildings need planned penetrations

The Heartwood is described as aiming to meet Passive House standards, which would make its library the first New York Public Library branch to do so. Buildings built to that standard depend on an airtight envelope, and every cable or conduit that passes through an exterior wall or the air barrier has to be planned, sleeved and sealed in a way the envelope designer has approved. That is one more reason the low-voltage plan should be settled early: a provider's technician who drills a fresh hole through the wall two years after opening can undo part of what the building was designed to do.

Staffed buildings need more than apartment jacks

A supportive building like 3500 Park Avenue, which the New York Housing Conference describes as having about 20 on-site workers and a live-in super, needs cabling for the people who work there as well as the people who live there. That usually means wired desks at the front desk and in every staff office, cable for cameras at the entrances, hallways and common rooms, access points in community and program rooms, cable for the entry panel and any fob readers, and a separate network for staff that residents cannot reach. We plan those as distinct systems sharing one pathway, so a problem in one does not take down the others.

Closeout is where many projects fail

A low-voltage contractor who finishes and leaves without records hands the operator a building full of cable nobody can identify. Our closeout package lists every run with its label and test result, shows the riser on a diagram, includes photos of every penetration before it was covered, and names the equipment in the telecom room. When a new property manager or IT vendor arrives years later, that package is what lets them work without opening walls.

Inside One Apartment

What a Claremont Tenant Can Wire Without Changing the Building

Most of the people who live in Claremont rent, and plenty of them want nothing more than a dependable wired connection to one desk, one television or one game console. That is a reasonable request, and it is often possible without touching anything the landlord owns. The limits are real, though, and we would rather explain them on the phone than send a technician who has to say no at the door.

Surface routes that leave no damage

A tenant's cable usually travels on the surface: along the top of the baseboard in a paintable raceway, around a door frame, and down to a surface-mounted jack or straight to the device. The raceway is fastened in a way that can be removed when the tenant leaves, and we pick a route that avoids radiators, heating pipes and anywhere furniture will crush the cable. A forum post from a renter elsewhere captures the request we hear most: a line to each bedroom and two to the living room, with drilling "absolutely out of the question." The answers that renter got are the same ones we use: surface raceway, careful routing at doorways, and no cable through heating or air ducts.

If you have Big Apple Connect

Optimum's Big Apple Connect page says each participating NYCHA household gets one free gateway, which is a modem and Wi-Fi router in one box, and that a free Wi-Fi extender is available only if an Optimum technician recommends one. Its fine print lists fiber service at up to 500 megabits per second in both directions and cable-network service at up to 500 down and 20 up. That gateway belongs to the program, and the apartment belongs to NYCHA. The useful thing to know is that a short cable from the gateway to a device that does not move, such as a desk computer or a television, takes that device off the crowded airwaves and leaves more room for phones and tablets. If you need anything beyond a cable on the surface, the conversation starts with Optimum and NYCHA, not with us.

When the owner says yes

Some private owners welcome a tenant's request, especially when the tenant pays and the owner gets a tested, labeled jack that the next tenant can use too. When that happens, we put the agreement in writing before the visit: which walls may be opened, how holes will be patched and by whom, and who owns the cable afterward. That protects the tenant's deposit and the owner's building. If a landlord's managing agent has its own contractor rules, we follow them.

Structured Cabling for Claremont's Architecture

The cable is the same from building to building. What changes is the route: what the walls are made of, how floors are joined, where the old pathways are, and how much of the building we are allowed to touch. These are the kinds of buildings the public record describes in Claremont, and how each one shapes the work.

Prewar Walk-Ups

Five- and six-story apartment houses from the 1910s and 1920s on blocks like Clay, Fulton and Webster Avenues, with plaster on masonry or clay tile, a cellar entry and a stair hall that climbs the whole building.

Private Elevator Buildings

Larger prewar and postwar buildings, including the Grand Concourse frontage at the neighborhood's western edge, with an elevator core and closets on each floor.

1960s Public Housing Towers

The 21-story Butler and Webster Houses and the Morris Houses, completed in 1964 and 1965, built of concrete and controlled by NYCHA.

HDFC Co-ops

Older buildings now owned by their shareholders through housing development fund corporations, where a volunteer board approves shared work.

New Affordable and Supportive Buildings

Recent and planned buildings such as 3500 Park Avenue and The Heartwood, designed with telecom rooms, staff spaces and community rooms.

Houses on the Side Streets

A small number of one- and two-family houses, from 1890s and 1900s houses on streets like College, Teller and Findlay Avenues to two-family houses built in the 1990s along Claremont Parkway and Crotona Place.

Prewar walk-ups: find the old paths before making new ones

A 1920s walk-up usually holds more pathways than it seems to. Gas and water risers were once run in chases that may now be partly empty, some buildings have a disused dumbwaiter or a shaft beside the stair, and earlier phone companies often left a stack of sleeves near the front of the building. Before drilling anything, we look for those paths, check what is still in them, and decide whether one can carry a new riser safely and legally. Where none exists, a new sleeve stacked through each floor at the same spot, usually in a stair-hall corner or a closet, gives the building a permanent path. Interior walls in buildings of this age can be plaster on wood lath, plaster on clay tile or plaster directly on brick, sometimes within the same apartment, which is why we scan and use an inspection camera before choosing where a jack goes.

Elevator buildings: closets, cores and sealed floors

In an elevator building, the cable for each floor normally meets the riser in a closet near the core. We check what already shares that closet, keep data cable away from power and elevator equipment, and make sure every opening through the floor is sleeved and sealed with a rated firestop. Along the Grand Concourse frontage, where the property records show most buildings date from the 1920s, the hallways can be long and the ceilings high, so we plan the hallway runs carefully: above a hallway ceiling where there is space, in a painted surface raceway along the top of the wall where there is not.

Concrete towers: owner projects only

Poured-concrete towers from the 1960s leave no hidden spaces to fish a cable through; every new path is a cored hole or a surface raceway. In the NYCHA towers of Claremont Village, those decisions belong to NYCHA. We describe the buildings here because they house a large share of the neighborhood, not to offer work inside them that their owner has not asked for.

HDFC co-ops: phased work and plain records

HDFC boards often pay for shared improvements out of limited reserves. A sensible cabling plan for one of these buildings splits the work into phases: first the cellar entry and the riser, then floor connections, then individual apartments as shareholders request them. Each phase stands on its own and is tested and documented when it is finished, so a board that pauses between phases is never left with half a system nobody understands.

New buildings: plan with the drawings, finish with the records

For new construction, the cabling plan should start in the drawings, continue through rough-in and finish with a closeout package, as described above. The detail that matters most in Claremont's new buildings is the split between systems: residents' connections, the operator's staff network, cameras and entry panels, and any library or community space with its own users. Each gets its own panel in the telecom room and its own labels, even when the cables share a pathway.

Rules that apply to business cabling in Claremont

  • The low-voltage permit exemption.   • Alarm and camera licensing. Installing or servicing security systems in New York requires a state license; our low-voltage work is done under NYS license #12000287431.

  • Seal every penetration. Holes through walls and floors are sealed the same day against fire, smoke, water and pests, and photographed for the record.
  • Building rules come first. NYC contractor guides list freight-elevator reservations, delivery windows, noise limits and ceiling-access rules among the building requirements that shape a cabling job, including buildings that only allow ceiling work after 6 PM. Your Questions Answered

Structured Cabling Installation in Claremont: Questions Answered

New buildings near the park

The Heartwood will put a public library and 113 apartments in the same tower. Should the library and the apartments share one telecom room?

They can share a room, but they should not share a network, and many designers give a public library its own lockable space inside or beside the main telecom room. The library's computers, public Wi-Fi and staff systems are run by the library system, while the apartments, the building's cameras and the operator's offices are run by the housing operator, and each party needs to reach its own equipment without touching the other's. In practice that means separate service entrances or at least separate provider handoffs, separate patch panels and switches, separate labeling schemes, and a clear line on the drawings showing who owns which pathway. If the two share a room, a partition, separate racks with their own locks, and an agreed access procedure keep everyone honest. We would raise this at the drawing stage, because it is far harder to divide a room after both parties have moved in.

Our new building's emergency generator backs up the community room so it can serve as a cooling center. Should the network equipment in that room be on generator power too?

Usually yes, at least for the parts that make the room useful in an emergency. The Bronx Times describes The Heartwood's planned generator as providing backup power and cooling to amenity spaces and the community room, and 6sqft says those spaces can serve as cooling centers. A cooling center whose Wi-Fi goes dark when the street loses power is far less useful to the people sitting in it. The access points in that room, the switch that powers them and the provider's equipment that feeds the building all need power for the network to work, so the electrical engineer should know which network equipment needs a generator-backed outlet, and the telecom room should have at least one. A battery unit on the switch covers the seconds between the outage and the generator starting. We list those power needs in our plan so the electrician can price them; the outlets themselves are electrical work.

A general contractor building on Park Avenue asked when our low-voltage crew needs to be on site. At what stage do you come in?

Earlier than most contractors expect, and in several short visits rather than one long one. We want to review the drawings before slabs are poured so riser sleeves land where the cable has to climb; walk the building after the structure is up to confirm the sleeves are really there; pull cable during rough-in, once framing and the other trades' main lines are in but before insulation and drywall; return after painting to terminate jacks and mount devices; and finish with testing and the closeout package. The most expensive mistake on a new building is a low-voltage crew that first arrives after the walls are closed, because every cable then has to be fished or put in a raceway. If the general contractor shares the schedule, we will mark exactly which weeks we need to be in the building.

Our social-service staff keep confidential files on office computers in the same building as the residents they serve. What in the cabling protects those files?

The cabling creates the separation that the network settings then enforce. Staff desks get wired connections that run back to their own patch panel and switch in a locked part of the telecom room, so there is no physical path from a resident's apartment or a community-room access point to a staff computer. Wall jacks in staff offices are labeled for staff use and kept out of public areas, and any staff Wi-Fi runs on its own access points or its own encrypted network that residents are not given. The equipment cabinet is locked, and the port map shows exactly which jacks are on the staff network, so nobody can plug a resident's device into one by accident. Data policies, passwords and the organization's software are the operator's IT vendor's job; our part is making sure the physical network gives them clean lines to work with.

Walk-ups, towers and the paths between floors

Our building's fire escape passes the rear windows of every apartment. Can a network cable be run along it to reach the upper floors?

No, and a responsible installer should refuse. A fire escape is an emergency exit, and nothing should be fastened to it, threaded through it or hung from it that could snag someone climbing down in the dark or slow the escape. Cable on a fire escape is also exposed to weather, sunlight and anyone who walks past, and fastening anything to the ironwork can damage the coating that protects it from rust. If the only practical riser is on the outside of the building, it belongs on the wall itself, in a properly fastened exterior conduit placed well away from the fire escape and the windows that serve it, and only with the owner's approval. In most walk-ups an interior route through a stacked sleeve or an old chase is better anyway, and that is where we start looking.

Our 1920s building has a telephone closet on each floor full of the phone company's old copper blocks. Can the new building network share those closets?

Often, yes, and they can be the best spots in the building, because they were placed where cable needed to meet each floor. The first step is finding out what in them is still live. Some old copper pairs may still carry a landline, an elevator phone or an alarm communicator, and cutting them could disconnect something important. We trace the pairs, mark what is live, and with the owner's agreement leave the provider's working equipment in place and remove only what the provider confirms is abandoned. The new network then gets its own labeled panel or wall space in the same closet. If the closet has an open hole through the floor where the old cable climbed, we sleeve and firestop it as part of the job.

Our building sits on the slope between the Grand Concourse and Webster Avenue, so the cellar is below ground at the front and nearly at street level at the back. Does that change where service should come in?

It can help. NYC Parks notes that the lawns of the old Claremont estate ran down to the Mill Brook, which is now Webster Avenue, and the land still falls from the Concourse side toward Webster. A building on that slope may have a rear cellar wall with a window or door near grade, which can be a drier, more accessible place for a provider's entry and for the building's main cabinet than a front wall below the sidewalk. We look at where water collects, where the providers' existing lines arrive, and where the riser can start with the shortest protected run. If the best entry point is not where the providers come in today, the cellar plan can bring their lines to a new backboard without asking them to rebuild their service.

Renting in Claremont

The technician left our free gateway on the floor by the front door, right where the coax comes out of the wall. Can it be moved to the middle of the apartment without another service call?

The gateway itself can usually move a short distance with a longer coax or fiber jumper, but the provider's outlet should stay where the provider put it, and some programs prefer their own technician to make changes. The usual answer is the other way around: leave the gateway near its outlet, but lift it off the floor onto a shelf or a small wall mount, then run a network cable along the baseboard in a slim raceway to the middle of the apartment, where a cabled access point or the device that matters most is connected. That puts the strong signal where you live without touching the provider's line. If you are in a NYCHA apartment with Big Apple Connect, ask Optimum first, since the equipment belongs to the program.

Can a network cable go along the radiator pipes or through the sleeve where the heating pipe passes into the next room?

Not along the pipes and not through their sleeves. Steam and hot-water pipes in a Claremont walk-up get hot enough to damage cable over time, they expand and move as the heat cycles, and the sleeve around a heating pipe belongs to the heating system and is often the landlord's responsibility to keep sealed. A cable squeezed in beside the pipe also makes that seal worse. If the next room is where the cable needs to go, the cable travels along the baseboard and around the door frame instead, at a sensible distance from the radiator. In a heating season in an old building, that small detour saves a cable that would otherwise be failing by spring.

Our apartment has three old jacks from past providers, and nobody knows which, if any, still works. Can you tell us which to keep?

Yes, that is quick work. We test each jack to see what kind of cable is behind it, whether it is connected to anything, and where it goes, using a tone generator to follow it to the cellar or to another jack. Old phone jacks often carry wire that cannot handle a modern network; old coax outlets may still connect to a provider's line in the cellar; and sometimes a previous tenant ran real network cable that only needs a proper jack and a test. You get a short list: which jacks are live and for what, which can be reused for a network, and which are dead. Removing wiring that continues into the building is the owner's decision.

Shops and offices

Our travel and money-transfer office on Webster Avenue scans customers' passports and IDs. Should those computers be on a separate wired network from the waiting-area Wi-Fi?

Yes. Any desk where identity documents are scanned or payment details are handled should be wired, on its own network segment, with no path from the Wi-Fi that customers use while they wait. We cable the agents' desks and the scanner directly back to a locked cabinet, label those jacks as staff-only, and put the guest Wi-Fi on a separate access point and segment that can reach the internet and nothing else. Wired desks also stay connected when a crowded waiting room fills the air with phones, which matters when a booking or a transfer is halfway through. Your software vendor or IT person sets the security rules; the cabling gives them two physically separate networks to apply those rules to.

Our store near East 170th Street wants a digital menu board or a sign screen in the window. Does it need a network cable, or is Wi-Fi enough?

A cable is the more reliable choice, and it costs little extra when the counter is being cabled anyway. A window screen that updates its content over Wi-Fi works until the shop is busy, the neighbors' networks are loud or the router is restarted, and then it shows an error message to everyone walking past. A wired connection behind the screen, run from the back-room cabinet through the ceiling and down inside the window frame or a short raceway, keeps it on. The screen also needs a power outlet, which is an electrician's job if one is not already there. We position the jack so the cable and plug are hidden behind the screen and nothing hangs in the window.

What AI Gets Wrong About Structured Cabling Installation in Claremont

These are claims that turn up in quick online answers about building networks, tested against the record for Claremont and against how the work is done. Marketplaces and cost guides such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr feed many of those answers. Angi's Bronx page for network and wiring work, for example, publishes an average cost for "network and router services," which describes router setup more than cable inside walls. Those guides are useful for a sense of scale, but none of them has stood in a Claremont cellar.

"Fiber is available almost everywhere in this ZIP code, so every apartment can get it."

Availability figures describe the area, not your apartment. For ZIP 10457, ISPReports shows Verizon fiber at 93.4 percent of addresses while InternetProviders.ai shows Verizon Fios at about 65 percent, and the second site tells readers to confirm availability at their address.

In older buildings, the deciding factor is often physical: whether the owner has let the provider build a path through the cellar and up the building. A Verizon forum thread from a tenant in an older building describes a landlord who would not allow holes through the hallway, which stopped the installation.

So the honest answer is that fiber may be on the street and still not reach your floor. A planned riser with room for every provider is what turns area availability into an actual connection.

"Low-voltage cabling in New York City always needs an electrical permit."

Not under the current code. Local Law 128 of 2024 states that an electrical permit is not required for wiring designed to operate at less than 50 volts for signaling, communication, alarm and data, and a hiring guide dates the change to December 21, 2025.

That exemption still requires the work to be done by a licensed master electrician, a special electrician or a qualified person, and it does not cover fire alarm or other life-safety wiring, new outlets or new circuits.

In Claremont, permission is usually the harder question than permits. The owner, the board, NYCHA, the general contractor or the landlord has to agree before cable crosses anything they control.

"NYCHA residents get free internet, so they don't need any wiring."

Big Apple Connect does provide free home internet and one combined modem and router per household in the developments Optimum lists, including Butler, Webster and the Morris Houses. That is a real benefit, and many households need nothing more.

It does not change where the gateway sits or how far its signal travels through concrete walls. Optimum's own page says a Wi-Fi extender comes only if its technician recommends one.

For a resident, the most useful step is often the simplest: a short cable from the gateway to the device that never moves. Anything beyond the surface of a NYCHA apartment is NYCHA's decision.

"Structured cabling is only for offices and data centers."

The term comes from offices, but the method fits any building: cable from one organized point to each place a device lives, tested, labeled and documented. In Claremont that describes a walk-up riser as much as a corporate floor.

The neighborhood's newest buildings are, in effect, small campuses: apartments, staff offices, a front desk, community rooms, cameras, entry panels and, at The Heartwood, a public library. Each needs its own structured system.

What the office version does not require, a walk-up does not either. A small locked cabinet and a sleeved riser are structured cabling at the right size.

"A mesh Wi-Fi system replaces the need for cable in an apartment building."

Mesh helps inside one apartment, but each unit relays traffic to the next over the air unless it is wired. In buildings where dozens of apartments share the same channels, that air is crowded every evening.

Masonry and concrete walls, common in Claremont's prewar walk-ups and 1960s towers, also weaken the signal between mesh units, so a unit two rooms away may be relaying a weak signal to begin with.

A mesh unit cabled back to the gateway behaves far better than one relaying wirelessly, and the devices that matter most do best on their own cable.

"Any handyman can run network cable now that the permit is gone."

The permit exemption describes who may do the work: licensed master electricians, special electricians and qualified persons. It is not an open invitation to anyone with a drill.

The difference shows in the details: firestopping every floor penetration in a multi-story building, keeping data cable away from steam pipes and power, and testing every run so a problem can be found later.

A riser that works on the day it is installed but leaves an open hole between floors, or a cable that fails a test a year later, costs a building far more than doing it correctly once.

"Old phone and cable wiring in a building can carry a modern network."

Some of it can be reused, usually as a pull line to bring new cable through the same path. Very little of it can carry a modern network on its own.

Old telephone wire and coax in a prewar Claremont building may also still carry something important, such as an elevator phone or an alarm communicator, so it should be traced and tested before anyone reuses or removes it.

What we never do is assume. A run that looks fine but tests poorly causes the slow, intermittent problems that waste hours on the phone with a provider who can see nothing wrong.

Our Installation Process for Claremont Buildings

A single drop to one desk and a full riser for a walk-up go through the same eight steps. In Claremont the second step matters most, because so many buildings answer to someone other than the person who called.

1. Send the address, building type and photos

Street and building, whether it is a walk-up, an elevator building, a new building or a storefront, which places need a wired connection, and photos of the entry point and the current equipment.

2. Confirm who controls the building

The owner, managing agent, HDFC board, nonprofit operator, general contractor or landlord agrees to the scope in writing before any visit that involves drilling.

3. Walk the building from the entry point inward

The cellar entry and old provider boxes first, then the riser, hallways, closets, ceilings and walls, noting pipes, old wiring and fire-rated openings.

4. Write the drop-by-drop plan and price

Every drop with its cable, route, penetrations, sealing and price, plus the cabinet, switch, access points and battery, signed before work begins.

5. Build and protect the pathways

Only the planned openings, after scanning; cable on hooks or in conduit, clear of steam pipes, power and gas.

Every penetration firestopped the same day, jacks and panels terminated to one standard, labels on both ends of every run.

7. Test every run on an analyzer

Each cable tested end to end; anything that fails is fixed and retested before the crew leaves, and the network is checked on the finished cabling.

8. Hand over the port map and results

A printed port map in the cabinet, test results, penetration photos and a written summary sent to whoever runs the building.

FAQ

Claremont Structured Cabling Installation FAQ

Can one cable bring both a landline phone and internet to the same wall plate?
+

Usually the better answer is two jacks on one plate, each with its own cable back to the cabinet. Most landlines today come from the internet provider's gateway rather than the old copper, so the phone line starts at a port on that box and can travel on its own network cable to a jack beside the data jack. Splitting the pairs of one cable between phone and data is possible but limits the data side and confuses anyone who works on it later. A second cable costs little when the first is already being pulled, and it keeps both services clean, testable and clearly labeled.

We own two buildings next to each other on the same block. Can a cable between them let both share one internet service?
+

Often, yes, if both buildings are under the same ownership and the provider's terms allow it. The link usually runs through a shared cellar wall or between the cellars, sleeved and sealed on both sides, and for anything beyond a short distance or between separate electrical services we use fiber, which does not carry electrical faults from one building to the other. Each building then gets its own switch and labels, so either can be separated later if one is sold. If the only route is across a yard or over a roof, the outdoor section needs rated cable in conduit, and both buildings' records should show where it runs.

Our building has solar panels on the roof. Does the solar system's monitoring need its own network cable?
+

Many solar inverters report their output over Wi-Fi or a cellular connection, and that works until the cellar or the roof is far from the router. If the monitoring keeps dropping, a network cable from the building's cabinet to the inverter or its communication box is the more reliable fix. We run and test that data cable and label it at both ends, but we do not touch the solar system's electrical wiring, its disconnects or its inverter settings, which belong to the solar installer. If the inverter sits on the roof, the cable comes down through a sealed, protected path rather than an open hole in the bulkhead.

How many years will the cable you install stay useful before it needs replacing?
+

Well-installed copper and fiber cable inside a building commonly stays in service for many years, and what usually retires it is a change in what the building needs, not the cable wearing out. The things that shorten its life are the ones a careful installer avoids: crushing it with tight staples, running it along hot pipes, leaving it loose where people step on it, and exposing it to water in a cellar. That is why we keep cable on proper supports, away from heat, and in conduit where it is exposed. Leaving spare sleeve space and pull strings in the riser also means the building can add newer cable later without opening walls.

Is plenum-rated cable required in the hallway ceilings of our building?
+

It depends on what the space above the ceiling is used for. Plenum-rated cable is required where the space above a ceiling carries air for the heating or cooling system, because that air can spread smoke from a burning cable jacket. Many hallway ceilings in older Claremont buildings are not air spaces at all, and in risers between floors the rule is a riser rating instead. We check how each ceiling is used during the walk-through, choose the jacket rating for every space the cable crosses, and list it in the plan, so the cable meets the rule for that space rather than a guess.

Can you leave empty conduit to each apartment so a future provider can pull its own line?
+

Yes, and in a riser job it is one of the most useful things an owner can buy. An empty conduit or innerduct with a pull string, run from the cellar entry to each floor or each apartment, lets a provider's technician pull fiber or cable without drilling new holes through the building. We label each one at both ends, cap the ends, and show them on the riser diagram, so the super can point a technician to the right pathway. For a new building, this belongs in the rough-in, while the walls are open and the cost is lowest.

A jack in our office was damaged when a desk was moved. Can you replace just the jack without running new cable?
+

Usually, yes. If the cable behind the jack still has enough slack and was not pulled out of the wall or kinked, we cut back to clean cable, terminate a new jack, and test the run end to end to confirm it still performs. If the test shows the cable itself was damaged, the run is replaced from the panel to the jack, because a patched cable inside a wall becomes a weak point. Moving the jack a little higher or to the side of the desk, or adding a short protective cover, helps keep the same thing from happening again.

Our community room needs a projector and a laptop at the front of the room. Can the connection be built into the wall instead of loose cords?
+

Yes. The usual setup is a wall or floor plate at the front of the room with the connections the presenter needs, a cable inside the wall or ceiling to the projector or screen, and a network jack for the laptop and for a wired access point that can handle a full room of phones. Projectors and screens also need power where they mount, which is an electrician's part of the job. Planning it this way means nobody tapes cords across the floor before each meeting, and the room can be rearranged without anyone tripping over a cable.

A cable has to cross a doorway in our apartment. How is it protected so nobody trips on it?
+

We do not lay cable across the floor of a doorway. The usual route goes up the side of the door frame inside a slim raceway, across the top of the frame, and back down the other side, where it continues along the baseboard. The raceway is painted or chosen to match the trim, and it is fastened so it can be removed cleanly if the lease requires. If the door frame is in poor shape or the raceway would block a door closer, we look for another wall to go around rather than under the door.

Our nonprofit's funder wants a written scope of work before releasing money for the network. Can your plan serve that purpose?
+

Yes. Our drop plan is already a written scope: it lists each drop, the cable and route, every penetration and how it is sealed, the cabinet and equipment, the testing method, what is handed over at the end, and the price of each line. We can organize it by phase or by funding category if the funder asks, and we can provide the closeout package and test results afterward as proof that the work was completed as described. If the funder needs a specific form or certificate of insurance, tell us at the start so the paperwork is ready.

A previous painter covered our wall jacks and the cable along the baseboard with paint. Does painted cable still work?
+

Paint on the outside of a cable jacket usually does no harm to the signal, but paint inside a jack can stop the plug from seating and corrode the contacts. We test each painted run first. If the cable passes, the jack is replaced with a clean one and the run is labeled; if the cable was crushed by layers of paint and fasteners or fails the test, it is replaced. For the future, a raceway along the baseboard can be painted with the wall without the cable itself being painted, and jacks can be masked or removed before the painters arrive.

How do you reach an access point location on a double-height ceiling, such as a library reading room or a tall lobby?
+

With the right lift and a plan that avoids going back up. High ceilings are reached with a rolling scaffold or a lift sized for the room, scheduled when the space is closed or quiet, and the cable is pulled, the mount is set and the access point is installed in one trip. Where possible we place high access points so they can be serviced from a catwalk, a wall or a lower soffit instead of the ceiling's highest point. For a new building, the drawings should show those locations early, so the cable is in place before the ceiling is finished.
