The Neighborhood

Why Gowanus Properties Need Access Control

The canal explains almost everything about Gowanus doors. A tidal creek and marsh was dredged and walled into the Gowanus Canal in the 1860s, and for a century the blocks on either bank filled with coal yards, gas works, stone and cement makers, foundries and warehouses that used the water for shipping. The industry left behind some of the most polluted water in the country; the Environmental Protection Agency added the canal to its Superfund list in 2010 and has budgeted roughly half a billion dollars for the cleanup, which has run in phases since 2020. The canal is about 1.8 miles long and still takes combined sewer overflow from fourteen outfalls when heavy rain overwhelms the sewers. In practical terms: the ground near the water is low, the street drains can back up in a cloudburst, and old buildings here were built for trucks and barges rather than for residents.

Then the rules changed. In November 2021 the City Council approved a rezoning of 82 blocks of Gowanus with only one member voting against it. Coverage at the time described room for nearly 8,500 new apartments, about 3,000 of them affordable, with mixed-use towers up to 30 stories close to the canal and up to 17 stories along Fourth Avenue, new waterfront open space, $174 million for sewer upgrades and $200 million for nearby public housing. The first big rentals actually came earlier, under a separate approval: Lightstone’s twin 12-story buildings at 363 and 365 Bond Street, about 700 apartments, could not be built with cellars because of the flood-prone site, and 365 Bond opened in June 2016. Since the rezoning, larger campuses have followed, such as Gowanus Wharf, whose nine-story Union Channel building has 224 apartments and a 24-hour attended lobby and whose 15-story Douglass Port building on Douglass Street held a lottery for 65 of its 260 apartments in early 2026. These buildings are named only to describe the building type; none of them is an AESS client, and nothing on this page describes work in them.

The old Gowanus did not disappear. City Planning’s May 2021 vision plan for the Gowanus Industrial Business Zone, the strip roughly from Third to 16th Streets between the canal and Third Avenue, counted nearly 300 businesses and about 4,000 private jobs: manufacturing, construction, warehousing, auto body shops, big-box stores, arts and offices. Artists still work in former factory buildings along Second, Third and Fourth Avenues; Hyperallergic’s report on the 26th Gowanus Open Studios in October 2022 described a clay studio in a former ironworks and multi-tenant studio buildings with wood and print shops, and noted that landlords were tightening tenant screening and insurance requirements, including an alarm system for some tenants. That mix is the second half of the access problem: dozens of small tenants in one old building, each with a key to the same street door.

Put the two halves side by side and Gowanus looks unlike any other Brooklyn neighborhood we work in. On one block a developer needs a door system that will be live on the day a temporary certificate of occupancy arrives and will register 200 tenants in a few months. On the next block a studio manager needs forty separate people to get in at 2 a.m. without forty copies of one key, and a contractor needs drivers to collect trucks at 5 a.m. without a lockbox on the fence. All of them share low ground and the same storms. Keys do not cope with any of it. A credential system with schedules, expiry dates and equipment mounted above the water does.

The crime record here is mostly ordinary, and that is useful to know. Brooklyn Paper reported in 2008 that thieves forced open two doors to get into a Fourth Avenue apartment building near Sixth Street and took a bicycle, and in 2015 that a burglar took tools, a box of door handles, smoke detectors, strobe lights and a security camera, about $2,800 worth, from a parole office still being fitted out on Second Avenue near Fifth Street. An NYPD appeal from January 2020 asked for help identifying a burglary suspect at a building on Ninth Street. Each story is about a door that was weak, propped or not yet controlled, which is exactly the gap a reader, a closer and a door-position contact close. We use them as examples of entry risk, not as statistics.

Gowanus in numbers we checked before writing this page

  • Boundaries and district: roughly Wyckoff Street to the Gowanus Expressway and Bond Street to Fourth Avenue, ZIP code 11215 for much of it, inside Brooklyn Community District 6, whose board office is at 250 Baltic Street and whose full-board meetings are held at the Van Alen Institute, 303 Bond Street.
  • Two precincts: the NYPD lists the 76th Precinct at 191 Union Street as serving parts of Gowanus; police appeals for incidents on the Park Slope side of the canal, such as a 2020 burglary on Ninth Street, come from the 78th Precinct at 65 Sixth Avenue.
  • The canal: about 1.8 miles long, on the EPA Superfund list since 2010, with fourteen combined sewer overflow points and a cleanup budget of about $506 million; the larger of two sewage holding tanks was finished in March 2025.
  • The rezoning: 82 blocks, approved in November 2021, nearly 8,500 apartments allowed, roughly 3,000 affordable, and towers up to 30 stories near the water.
  • The industrial zone: nearly 300 businesses and about 4,000 jobs in the Gowanus part of the Southwest Brooklyn Industrial Business Zone, according to City Planning in 2021.
  • Transit: the F and G at Carroll Street, Smith–Ninth Streets and Fourth Avenue–Ninth Street, the R at Union Street, and the Smith–Ninth Streets platform, 87.5 feet above the street, the highest station in the subway system.

Owners who add readers in Gowanus usually want cameras on the same doors, and new buildings often want the visitor intercom planned at the same time; we coordinate all three on one schedule so the lobby wall is opened once.

Building Specialists

Access Control for Gowanus’s Architecture

Five kinds of building make up most of what we see in Gowanus. Each one fails in a different way when entry control is planned badly.

Canal-side rental towers

Nine to thirty stories, often with a garage, retail on the ground floor, amenity floors and a public waterfront path behind. Many were designed with no cellar, so lobby equipment rooms sit at or near grade. The work is lobby, garage, package room, amenity, roof and stair control on one database, planned during construction.

Fourth Avenue mid-rises

Mid-rise apartment buildings along a wide, busy avenue, many with stores at street level and a second entrance on a side street. Two street doors, shop tenants with their own hours, and residents who expect a quiet lobby.

Former factories turned studio buildings

Brick or concrete industrial buildings on Second, Third and Fourth Avenues divided into many small studios, often with one street door, one loading door and a long shared corridor. Dozens of tenants, short leases, late hours.

Low industrial and auto buildings

One- and two-story garages, body shops, contractors’ shops and warehouses with roll-up doors, a personnel door and often a fenced yard. Early starts, vehicles in and out, and hardware that takes abuse.

Rowhouses and small walk-ups

On the residential blocks toward Bond, Nevins, Carroll and Union Streets, older houses and three- to six-unit walk-ups sit between the new buildings. Small systems, owner-landlords, and the multiple-dwelling rules that start at three apartments.

Conversions in progress

Industrial buildings being turned into apartments or offices under the rezoning. They pass through every stage on this page in a couple of years: empty building, construction site, partly occupied, finished.

Flood-Aware Placement

Keeping Gowanus Door Systems Above the Water

Gowanus floods two ways. The first is the storm surge: during Hurricane Sandy in October 2012 the canal rose over its banks and pushed its contaminated water into nearby streets and into the basements of homes and businesses, and in the weeks after, residents at public meetings complained about landlords who still had not pumped out their cellars. FEMA maps much of the low ground near the canal as a special flood hazard area, and a 2021 journalism project on the rezoning, Climate Concerns NYC 2021, citing a flood-risk overview, counted 111 Gowanus properties with a better than one-in-four chance of flooding. The second way is the cloudburst. The canal’s combined sewers overflow during heavy rain by design, and when the system is full, water backs up at street drains and low doorways long before any surge.

For door systems, the code points the way. Appendix G of the NYC Building Code requires electrical systems and equipment in flood hazard areas to be elevated to or above the design flood elevation, or built to keep water out under the ASCE 24 standard, and the NYC Electrical Code (section 110.11) puts panelboards, disconnects and circuit breakers above that elevation too. Permit applications in A-zones must certify that electrical service equipment is located or built to prevent water entering it. A door controller and its power supply are not service equipment, but they are electrical equipment fed from the building’s panels, and if they sit on the floor of a ground-level room they will be under water when the panel above them is still dry.

Our routine in Gowanus is simple. Controllers, lock power supplies and batteries go in a room above the design flood elevation, or at least on the highest wall position the room allows, inside closed enclosures. Only the parts that have to be at the door stay low: the reader, the lock, the door contact and the exit button. Those are chosen in outdoor or wet-location ratings and wired so that a soaked reader cannot short the controller. Cable from the door rises to the equipment rather than dropping to a cellar. And the battery is sized to carry the locks through the length of outage the building expects, so the doors stay secure if utility power to a flooded block is cut.

New buildings make this easier because the design flood elevation is already on the drawings. Older buildings are harder: the only spare space may be a cellar that took water in 2012. In those buildings we look for a closet on the first floor above grade, a spot in the building office, or a cabinet high on the wall of the stair hall, and we run cable up rather than down. The cost difference is usually a few extra hours of labor and a longer cable run, and it is cheap next to replacing a controller full of canal water.

From Construction to Move-In

A Gowanus Building’s Doors, Phase by Phase

Most of the work we are asked about in Gowanus right now is in buildings that are not finished yet. The rezoning allowed many new buildings at once, and each passes through the same four phases. Planning entry control phase by phase avoids two common failures: a construction site that runs for months on padlocks and a shared code, and a new building whose permanent readers go live the week tenants arrive, with nobody trained to run them.

Phase 1 — Site security while the frame goes up

Before there are walls, the risk is theft of tools and materials, which is exactly what happened at the Second Avenue parole office fit-out in 2015. At this stage the useful hardware is a keypad or reader on the construction gate or site office, with a code for each subcontractor that the general contractor can switch off when the trade finishes. Cameras usually matter more than locks here. We install what the general contractor wants and remove it cleanly at the end.

Phase 2 — Rough-in, while walls are open

This is when the permanent system is cheapest to build. Cable goes to every controlled door, back boxes go in at reader height, the door supplier is told which frames need a strike prep or an electric hinge, and the equipment room gets its power circuit and its space on the wall above the flood line. Missing this window means fishing cable through finished drywall later.

Phase 3 — Trim-out and partial occupancy

Readers, locks and controllers are installed and tested, and the system often has to run in two modes at once: finished floors and the lobby locked to future residents, unfinished floors still open to trades on their own credentials. Every contractor credential is given an end date, so access does not survive the punch list.

Phase 4 — Lease-up and handover

Tenants arrive over weeks or months. Credentials are issued as leases start, the leasing team and the managing agent are trained, and the developer’s construction accounts are closed. The building receives a door list, wiring records and administrator logins in its own name.

Commercial Access Control

Business Access Control in Gowanus

Gowanus business doors fall along a handful of corridors, and each corridor has its own pattern of hours, vehicles and visitors.

  • Second Avenue and the streets toward Hamilton Avenue. The heart of the industrial business zone: contractors, auto and truck businesses, storage and warehousing, with yards behind fences and roll-up doors on the street. Drivers start early, and keys tend to end up in lockboxes. Keypads with per-driver codes and gate releases on a schedule are the usual answer.
  • Third Avenue. Big retail at Third Street, where the Whole Foods Market opened in December 2013 around the landmarked 1872–73 Coignet Stone Company Building, plus gas stations, supply houses, studios and newer offices. Staff doors, stockrooms and roll-up loading doors share the avenue with heavy traffic.
  • Fourth Avenue. A wide avenue now lined with mid-rise and taller apartment buildings with shops at the base, close to the R at Union Street and the F, G and R at Fourth Avenue–Ninth Street. Ground-floor shops want staff readers; the residential lobbies above want a separate system or a separate group.
  • Union, Carroll, Third and Ninth Street bridges. The streets that cross the canal carry foot and bike traffic between Carroll Gardens and Park Slope, and the new buildings near them have retail and amenity entrances facing the water.
  • Bond and Nevins Streets. The blocks closest to the canal’s west and east banks, where the first big rentals went up and where studio, event and office spaces sit in older buildings between them.
  • Douglass and Degraw Streets at the head of the canal. Gowanus Wharf’s campus and other new buildings near the head end, where the city is building a sewage holding tank under the cleanup plan.

Businesses here ask us for three things more than anything else: stop handing out keys to people who come and go, know who opened the back door at night, and open the door for the right people at the right hour without someone standing there. A reader with a controller does all three. A shop or studio with one door can start with a single controlled entrance and add the stockroom, office or loading door later on the same system.

Rules that apply to Gowanus doors

  • NYC Building Code 1010.1.9.8 (sensor release of electrically locked egress doors). A sensor on the egress side unlocks the door on approach; a manual “PUSH TO EXIT” button 40 to 48 inches high within five feet of the door cuts lock power for at least 30 seconds; the door unlocks on power loss and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation; the locking system is listed to UL 294.
  • NYC Building Code 1010.1.9.9 (electromagnetically locked egress doors). Door hardware with a built-in switch releases the magnetic lock with one hand in one motion, the lock releases on loss of power, and the system is listed to UL 294.
  • Appendix G and Electrical Code 110.11. In flood hazard areas, electrical equipment goes at or above the design flood elevation or is built to keep water out; the section above explains what that means for controllers and power supplies.
  • Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a. Class A multiple dwellings erected or converted after January 1, 1968 need self-closing, self-locking entrance doors kept locked when no attendant is on duty, and an intercom at the main entrance once a building has eight or more apartments. Roof doors must be self-closing but not self-locking.
  • Housing Maintenance Code §27-2043. Every apartment entrance door needs a lock that opens from outside with a key, plus the heavy-duty latch, deadbolt and chain guard the code lists; an apartment keypad never replaces the key.
  • Local Law 63 of 2021, the Tenant Data Privacy Act. Owners of class A multiple dwellings who use keyless entry must give tenants a written data policy, collect only minimal data, destroy authentication data within 90 days unless anonymized, never sell it, and may not make smart access the only way into an apartment.
  • The 2015 state rule on shared entrances. New buildings that receive tax or zoning benefits for affordable housing must give affordable units the same common entrances and common areas as market-rate units, which affects how a mixed-income building’s groups are set up.

None of this is legal advice. It is the checklist we design against, and for building-specific questions we work with your architect, expediter or counsel.

Your Questions Answered

Access Control in Gowanus: Questions Answered

These questions come from the building types on this page: buildings in design or under construction, new rentals in lease-up, studio and maker buildings, yards and shops, and conversions. Each answer opens with the short version.

New and unfinished buildings

We are still in design on a building near the canal. What should the architect reserve for access control on the drawings?

A room above the flood line, a power circuit, conduit to every controlled door and the right door preps. Those four things are cheap on paper and expensive to add after the concrete is poured.

The equipment room comes first. Ask the architect to show where the access controllers, lock power supplies and batteries will live, and to put that space above the design flood elevation already on the drawings, next to the telecom equipment. A dedicated circuit for it, ideally on emergency or standby power if the building has it, saves an electrician’s visit later. Then ask for conduit or a clear cable path from that room to each door that will be controlled: lobby, vestibule, package room, garage, amenity spaces, roof, bike room, stairs that open to the street, and any door onto the waterfront path.

Finally, the door hardware schedule. Every controlled door should be listed with its lock type, so the frames arrive from the supplier already prepared for an electric strike or with an electric hinge or power transfer for an exit device. We are happy to mark up the hardware schedule and the door list for the architect at no charge when we are quoting the installation; it is the single most useful hour in the whole project.

Our building is a construction site now and the first residents move in next spring. Which doors need permanent readers before the temporary certificate of occupancy, and which can wait?

Every door that residents will use from day one, and every door that separates them from areas still under construction, has to be finished first. Doors to spaces that are not yet open, such as an amenity floor still being fitted out, can follow.

In practice that means the street entrance and vestibule, the package room, the elevators or lobby doors that lead to occupied floors, and the stair doors at street level go in first, along with the fire-alarm release and the exit hardware the Building Code requires on those doors. The doors between occupied floors and floors still being built need attention too: trades will want to use the same stairs and elevators, so their credentials should open only the floors they are working on, and residents’ credentials should not open the construction floors at all.

Garage, roof deck, gym, lounge and bike room readers can be installed later, as long as cable and back boxes went in during rough-in. We usually split a new building into two programming phases on the same system so the second phase is a matter of adding doors rather than starting over. Your expediter will know what the temporary certificate inspection requires for egress doors; we make sure the locks on those doors do what the code says before the inspector arrives.

During lease-up hundreds of tenants will move in over a few months. Can each tenant’s phone credential switch on by itself on their lease start date?

Yes, on most current cloud platforms, if the start and end dates are entered with the credential. Many platforms can also take that information from property-management software instead of retyping it.

A credential with a start date can be created weeks before move-in, when the lease is signed. The tenant downloads the app or receives the invitation, but the credential does nothing until 12:01 a.m. on the start date, and can be limited to the lobby, the elevator to their floor and the package room. Movers can get their own credential for the move window on the booked day. If the move-in date shifts, the leasing team changes one date rather than reissuing anything.

Two things make a lease-up go smoothly. Keep a stock of physical fobs or cards for tenants who do not want, or cannot use, a phone credential; under the Tenant Data Privacy Act, smart access cannot be the only way into an apartment, and a physical credential is the simplest backup for the building doors as well. And give the leasing office its own administrator role that can issue resident credentials but not change door schedules or delete logs, so the system stays the way the building intended while dozens of people are being added each week.

Mixed-income buildings, garages and campuses

Our building has lottery apartments and market-rate apartments. Can the access system give some tenants the gym or roof and not others?

The system can, but the law and your regulatory agreement decide whether you may. We set up whatever groups your counsel and the building’s agreement allow, and we start from the assumption that entrances and common areas are shared.

The 2015 state legislation that ended separate “poor doors” says affordable units in buildings that receive the relevant tax or zoning benefits shall share the same common entrances and common areas as market-rate units. In access-control terms, every resident group should open the same street doors, lobby, mail and package rooms and the same elevators. Whether a fee-based amenity such as a gym membership or a storage cage can be sold separately is a question for your attorney and for the agency that oversees the affordable units, not for an installer.

Technically, a building’s residents usually sit in one group for the base-building doors, with optional add-on groups for things a tenant has chosen to pay for, such as parking or a storage unit, regardless of which kind of lease they hold. That keeps the default access identical for everyone and makes any difference visible and explainable. If your agreement or counsel says the amenities must be shared too, the add-on group simply disappears.

Monthly parkers who do not live here use our building’s garage. How do we keep them out of the residential lobby?

Treat the garage as its own zone with its own exits, and put a reader on every door between the garage and the building. A garage credential should open the vehicle entrance and nothing else.

Buildings like the first canal-side rentals, which put a large garage on much of the ground floor, often have a door from the garage into the residential elevator lobby. That door needs a reader that accepts only residents, and a door contact so the system notices when it is held open. Monthly parkers who do not live in the building get credentials, tags or plate access for the vehicle gate and the garage’s own pedestrian exit to the street. If the garage operator is a separate company, it should administer its parkers in a separate group or a separate system, so the building never has to manage strangers’ credentials.

Elevators deserve a check as well. If an elevator stops at the garage level, it should require a resident credential at that stop, or the garage landing should be served only by a separate elevator. Fire service operation always overrides these settings; the elevator contractor confirms that when the reader is connected.

Several buildings on our campus share one amenity club. Can one credential open the club in the next building without opening that building’s lobby?

Yes, if the buildings share one access platform or their platforms can share credentials, and the club has an entrance that does not run through the other lobby. The second condition is the one that is easy to miss.

Multi-building campuses along the canal are planned with shared amenities: Gowanus Wharf, for instance, advertises a club shared across its buildings. On one platform this is straightforward: residents of each building are in their own group, and a separate “club” group, which everyone can belong to, opens the club doors and nothing else. The problem is physical. If the only way to the club is through the next building’s lobby, a club credential will need to open that lobby door too, and then the lobby is effectively shared.

The fix is an entrance to the amenity space from outside, or a vestibule with two controlled doors: one that opens to club members and one beyond it that opens only to that building’s own residents. If the buildings were built with different access systems, ask whether they can be put on one platform before deciding; running two databases for the same residents leads to people who have moved out keeping access in one of them.

Our ground floor opens onto the new public walkway along the canal. How should the doors onto that walkway be controlled?

As exits that are always free from the inside and controlled from the outside, with the walkway hours set by whatever the building’s waterfront agreement requires. The building should not assume it can lock the public out of the path itself.

The rezoning requires owners of waterfront sites to build a public promenade that will eventually run about seven acres along the canal, and the details of public access, such as hours and gates, are set for each site in its own approvals. Your architect or owner’s counsel will know what applies to your site. The building’s own doors onto that path are another matter: residents and staff use them to reach the water, and from the outside they are an entrance into private space.

We normally treat them like a secondary street entrance. From inside, free egress with exit hardware that meets the Building Code; from outside, a reader that accepts residents, perhaps only during daylight hours if the building wants, plus a door contact and a camera because these doors face a public path at night. Readers and exit devices on that side face weather and spray off the water, so they are chosen in outdoor ratings and mounted with the flood elevation in mind.

Studios, workshops and yards

Our studio leases run month to month. Can a studio-holder’s access stop on the day their lease ends unless it is renewed?

Yes. Give every credential an end date that matches the paid-through date, and extend it when the lease is renewed. Then nobody has to remember to delete anyone.

In a studio building with dozens of short leases, the biggest risk is the former tenant who still has a key or fob months after leaving. With a credential system, each studio-holder’s credential carries the date their lease runs to. When the studio manager records a renewal, the date moves forward; if nothing happens, the credential stops working at the end of that day for the street door, the corridor and the studio door itself. The manager can see in one list which credentials are about to expire this week.

A word of caution: removing access from a tenant whose lease is still running, for example over a payment dispute, raises legal questions that an installer cannot answer. We set the system up so that dates follow the lease term; decisions about ending access early belong to the building’s lawyer. For residential tenants the law is stricter still, which is one reason studio buildings that also contain apartments are set up with completely separate groups.

An open-studios weekend brings the public through our building. How do we open the studio floors to visitors and keep the rest locked?

Schedule the street door and the participating floors to unlock during the event hours, and leave every other door on credentials. Then staff the entrance rather than relying on the door alone.

Gowanus Open Studios has been running for more than two decades, with its 26th edition in 2022, and many studio buildings take part. An access system can hold a one-off “event” schedule: the street door and the corridor doors to participating studios unlock from, say, noon to six on each day, and lock again on their own at the end. Studios that are not taking part stay locked, as do the roof, the shop, storage rooms and anything else visitors should not wander into. The event schedule overrides the normal one only for those hours and those doors.

Two practical points. Unlocked doors need a person nearby; schedule volunteers or staff at the street door and on each open floor, because a schedule cannot tell a visitor from someone casing the building. And check the egress side: when a floor is busy with visitors, every exit route must stay free, so the event schedule should never lock a door that the fire plan counts on.

Our shared woodshop has table saws and a kiln room. Can a reader keep out members who have not finished the safety orientation?

Yes. Put the shop doors in their own access group and add members to it only after the orientation is recorded. The door enforces what the sign on it only asks for.

Maker spaces and shared shops in the old industrial buildings tend to have several layers of access: the building, the member lounge or bench area, and the rooms with dangerous equipment. A reader on each shop room door, with membership in that group granted by the shop manager when training is complete, means a new member’s credential opens the building and the bench room on day one and the woodshop or kiln room only once they are signed off. Many platforms let a group carry an expiry, so a certification that must be renewed each year lapses by itself.

Two cautions. Readers control who enters; they do not supervise what happens inside, so the shop’s own rules about working alone still apply. And any door on a route that people use to leave the building in an emergency must open freely from inside, whatever the access rules on the outside.

Our drivers collect trucks before 6 a.m. from a Second Avenue yard with a roll-up door and a steel side door. How do they get in without keys in a lockbox?

Give each driver their own code or fob for the side door, on a schedule that starts when the first shift does, and control the roll-up from inside. The lockbox goes in the scrap bin.

Lockboxes on yard fences have one code that everyone knows and a key that can be copied. A keypad-and-reader on the steel personnel door, with a heavy-duty strike or magnetic lock and a door closer that still works in winter, gives each driver a code of their own, active only during their shift window. The roll-up door then opens from a switch inside, so nobody needs an outdoor control for it. If the yard gate must open too, the gate lock or operator can be released by the same credential on the same schedule.

The log is the other benefit. When a truck leaves at 5:12 a.m., the office can see whose code opened the door. If a driver leaves the company, their code is turned off in seconds, and the rest of the crew do not have to learn a new one. Readers in yards take abuse, so we fit metal-housed units and mount them where a reversing truck cannot reach them.

Tools and fixtures were stolen from our renovation before we opened. Would a keypad on the site door have stopped it, or was that a camera job?

Usually both, in that order: a controlled door with a log to narrow down who could get in, and a camera to show what happened. Neither one alone is enough on a busy fit-out.

The 2015 Brooklyn Paper report about the parole office on Second Avenue is a good example of the risk. Over several weeks someone took a drill, a box of door handles, smoke detectors, strobe lights and a security camera that had not yet been installed. On a renovation like that, dozens of people from several trades come and go, and the usual arrangement is a padlock and a key passed around. A keypad with a separate code for each trade, switched off when that trade finishes, narrows the list of possible entrants to a few names and a time window.

A camera covering the door and the materials storage room supplies the rest. Store small, valuable items such as hardware, fixtures and devices in a lockable room with its own controlled door, and do not leave expensive equipment like cameras sitting in boxes on site until the day they are installed.

The old industrial building on our lot is being converted to apartments. Which entrance rules start to apply once people live there?

Once it becomes a class A multiple dwelling, the Multiple Dwelling Law, the Housing Maintenance Code and the Tenant Data Privacy Act all apply to its doors. Plan for them during the conversion rather than after the first inspection.

Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a says that buildings converted to class A multiple dwellings after January 1, 1968 must have self-closing, self-locking doors at street, passageway, court, yard and cellar entrances, kept locked unless an attendant is on duty, and once there are eight or more apartments, an intercom at the main entrance that lets each apartment talk to a visitor and release the door. Roof doors must close by themselves but must not lock in a way that stops anyone leaving. The Housing Maintenance Code then sets the locks on each apartment door, including a key-operated lock that a keypad cannot replace.

If the converted building will use fobs, phones or keypads, the Tenant Data Privacy Act adds a written privacy policy for tenants, limits on the data collected, a 90-day destruction rule for authentication data and a ban on making smart access the only way in. Loading doors and roll-ups that made sense for the old use become residential secondary entrances, so they need the same self-closing, self-locking treatment or a conversion to a fixed wall. We help your architect work out which of the old openings stay and how each one is secured.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Access Control in Gowanus

From our build environment we could not open Google’s AI Overview results for Gowanus searches, so nothing here quotes them. These are generic claims that keep turning up in quick online answers and national cost guides, checked against what Gowanus buildings actually need.

“Put the access panel in the basement with the other equipment.”

Generic installation advice treats the cellar as the natural home for controllers, power supplies and batteries, because that is where the electrical service and the phone lines usually arrive. In much of Gowanus there is no cellar at all, and where there is one, it may have taken canal water during Sandy.

The NYC Building Code’s flood-resistant construction appendix expects electrical equipment in flood hazard areas to sit at or above the design flood elevation or to be built to keep water out, and the Electrical Code puts panels and breakers above that line too. A door controller in a cellar is the first thing to fail and the most expensive thing to replace.

The better default here is a closet or equipment room above the flood elevation, with only the reader, lock, contact and exit button at the door.

“Access control costs a few hundred dollars a door.”

National guides from sites such as Angi, HomeAdvisor and Fixr often blend the price of a battery smart lock for a house with the price of a commercial reader system, and quick answers repeat the low end. A smart lock is a consumer product for one door; a reader, controller, electrified lock, power supply and cable on a building door is a different job.

Our published Brooklyn price for one controlled door is $1,500 to $2,500, which includes the reader, electric strike, wiring and ten programmed fobs. Multi-door systems run $4,000 to $15,000, and a full building with elevator restriction and parking $15,000 to $40,000 and up. Gowanus is charged at that same Brooklyn base.

What moves a Gowanus price inside those ranges is the door and the site: storefront glass that needs an exit device, concrete walls, a controller relocated above the flood line, or a construction schedule with two phases.

“Install the system after construction is finished.”

Plenty of advice treats access control as a finishing trade, like hanging pictures. In a new building that is how readers end up surface-mounted on raceway, cable is fished through finished walls, and doors arrive from the supplier without strike preps or power transfers.

The cheap moment is rough-in, when walls and ceilings are open. Cable, back boxes, door preps and an equipment-room circuit cost little then and a great deal later. With the number of buildings under construction near the canal, this is the most common avoidable cost we see.

Readers themselves can be installed at the end; the planning cannot.

“One shared keypad code is fine for a small building.”

A single code is easy to set up, so it is often suggested for small studio buildings, shops and yards. The trouble is that it never gets changed: every former tenant, driver and subcontractor keeps it, and there is no way to tell from the log who used it.

Individual codes or fobs cost nothing extra on almost any keypad reader, and they make two things possible that a shared code cannot: removing one person without telling everyone else a new code, and knowing whose code opened the door at 3 a.m.

For studio buildings with monthly leases this is the whole point of the system.

“Smart access lets landlords track tenants.”

Online answers sometimes describe keyless entry as a way for owners to see when every tenant comes and goes, and some tenants fear exactly that. In New York City, owners of class A multiple dwellings who use keyless entry are bound by Local Law 63 of 2021.

That law requires a written data policy, limits what can be collected, requires authentication data to be destroyed within 90 days unless anonymized, bans selling it and forbids using it to track tenants’ relationships or movements outside the building. It also says smart access cannot be the only way into an apartment.

We set retention to match the law on residential systems and give the owner the paperwork the law expects. Commercial studio buildings are not covered by that law, but we still recommend keeping only what is needed.

“Any fob system is more secure than keys.”

This claim is half right. A fob that can be deleted is better than a key that can be copied, but many buildings still run on unencrypted 125 kHz proximity fobs, which can be copied in seconds with cheap devices sold online.

If a Gowanus building is getting a new system, it should use encrypted 13.56 MHz credentials or phone credentials. If it has an older proximity system, it is worth checking what the fobs are before assuming they are secure.

The door matters too: a fob reader on a door that does not latch, or that people prop open for deliveries, secures nothing.

“A video doorbell is an access control system.”

Consumer doorbell cameras are often recommended as a cheap entry solution. They show who is at the door and let you talk to them, and some pair with a smart lock. They do not give each person their own credential, keep a reliable entry log across several doors, or meet the Building Code’s requirements for locked egress doors.

For a single-family house or one studio door they can be a sensible piece. For a multi-tenant building, a yard or a new tower, they are a camera at the door, not a door control system.

Many of the buildings we work on use both: a proper reader on the door and a camera or video intercom station beside it.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Look for the Water Line First

This describes how we survey low-lying buildings, not a particular customer. Before we look at a single door in a building near the canal, we look for evidence of water. A tide line on a cellar wall. Rust at the bottom of a steel door frame. A brown stain on a ceiling tile under a bathroom. Swollen baseboards. Old equipment mounted mysteriously high on a wall by someone who learned the hard way.

Then we ask the super or the owner one question: where was the water in 2012? The answer is usually precise — a mark on a pipe, a step on the stairs, a height on someone’s leg. That height, or the design flood elevation on the drawings if the building is new, decides where everything electrical goes.

Only then do we walk the doors. The plan that comes out of that order of work usually has the same readers anyone would choose, but the controllers, power supplies and batteries end up a floor higher than the client expected, and the cable runs up rather than down. On a dry day that looks like extra effort. On the morning after a storm it is the difference between doors that work and doors that need replacing.

FAQ

Gowanus Access Control FAQ

Our insurer asked how our studio building controls entry. What can the access system show them?
+

Most cloud platforms can export a list of every active credential with the doors and hours it opens, plus a log of door events such as forced or held-open doors over a chosen period. Insurers and landlords increasingly ask studio and workshop tenants about alarms and entry control, so we set up a one-page report the manager can download whenever it is requested. What the insurer accepts is their decision; we make sure the records exist and are easy to produce.

Can a film crew or event renter get a code that only works for the hours of their booking?
+

Yes. A temporary credential can be limited to particular doors and to a start and end time, such as the loading door and one studio from 7 a.m. to 9 p.m. on the shoot day. It stops working by itself when the booking ends, so nobody has to remember to cancel it, and the log shows when the crew actually came and went. For multi-day bookings we set one credential per day rather than one long window.

How many hours of battery should a strike and reader have to ride out a storm outage?
+

Size the battery for the outage the building realistically expects, not the minimum. For a low-lying Gowanus building we usually plan for several hours on the lock power supply and the controller, and we work out the figure from the current each lock draws rather than guessing. Fail-safe locks that release on power loss will open when the battery runs out, so the doors that must stay locked longest are fitted with fail-secure hardware that still allows free exit from inside.

Can a parcel locker in the lobby use the same credentials as the front door?
+

Some smart parcel lockers can be linked to an access platform, so residents open the building and collect parcels with the same phone credential, and couriers get a code that opens the street door and the locker bank only. Others run completely separately with their own app. We check the specific locker and access platform before promising integration, and we place the locker inside a controlled vestibule or package room rather than in a part of the lobby anyone can reach.

Can a water sensor near the equipment closet warn the super before the door system is damaged?
+

Often, yes. Many access controllers have spare inputs, and a leak sensor wired to one can send an alert through the same app the building already uses for door events, so the super hears about water on the floor of the equipment room or the lobby closet before it reaches the electronics. An intrusion alarm panel can do the same job if the building has one. A sensor gives warning; it does not keep water out, so it works best alongside equipment that is already mounted high.

Can prospective tenants take a self-guided tour with a code that expires after the visit?
+

Yes, if the leasing team issues a time-limited credential that opens only the street door, the elevator and the model or available apartment for a short window. Many rental platforms can create those credentials automatically after a prospect books a tour. We recommend a camera covering the entrance and the tour route, and a rule that tour codes never open amenity floors, package rooms or occupied corridors.

Can our resident app or property-management software issue the door credentials?
+

Often, yes. Several cloud access platforms connect to common property-management and resident-experience software, so a signed lease creates the credential and a move-out ends it. Whether your combination works depends on the specific products and versions, so we confirm it before quoting. Where there is no integration, the leasing team adds residents in the access app directly, with start and end dates.

Do fire-rated stair and corridor doors in a new tower need special hardware for a reader?
+

Yes. Electric strikes, exit devices and other hardware fitted to a fire-rated door must be listed for use on fire doors, and the frame and door should be prepared by the manufacturer or under its listing rather than cut in the field. Stair doors also have their own Building Code rules about re-entry and unlocking on fire alarm. We specify listed hardware from the start so the door’s label and rating stay valid.

At what height should readers and exit buttons go so wheelchair users can reach them?
+

We mount readers and keypads within the accessible reach range on a clear, level approach, and exit buttons at the 40 to 48 inches the Building Code sets for manual release devices on sensor-released doors. Where a door has an automatic opener, the reader is placed so a person can present a credential and then reach the opener button without backing up. On older industrial doors with a step, we flag the step, since a reader cannot fix the approach.

Can you add readers to doors another contractor installed that are still under that contractor’s warranty?
+

Usually, but tell us first. Cutting a door or frame for new hardware can affect another contractor’s warranty on it, so we ask for the door supplier’s or general contractor’s written agreement, or we fit hardware that uses the existing preps without new cutting. Our own one-year parts-only warranty then covers the access equipment we supplied, separate from the door itself.
