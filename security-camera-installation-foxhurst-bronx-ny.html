The Neighborhood

Why Foxhurst Properties Need Security Camera Installation

Foxhurst is a neighborhood that was built twice, and a camera installer feels that on every job. The name comes from Fox Street, which turns up on nineteenth-century maps of what was then the Town of West Farms. According to CityNeighborhoods, the street grid of Prospect Avenue, Intervale Avenue and Fox Street was fully laid out by the 1890s, and two things made the area easy to reach: Crotona Park, created in 1888, and the White Plains Road subway line, which arrived in 1904. Brick apartment buildings followed, and many of them still stand. Homes.com describes the neighborhood's housing as mostly 1910s midrise brick apartment buildings, with multifamily properties and co-ops mixed in. Those are the walk-ups our crews know well: a street door with a small vestibule, a stair that turns at each landing, plaster over brick, a cellar holding the boiler and the meters, and a rear yard that nobody sees from the street.

Then came the second building period. The Mott Haven Herald, writing about Kelly Street, reported that nearly 70 percent of the housing stock in Community District 2, the Hunts Point and Longwood district that Foxhurst sits in, was destroyed by fire in the 1970s. Rebuilding followed in waves. In 1978, residents on Kelly Street took over three buildings and began rehabilitating them through what the Banana Kelly Community Improvement Association calls sweat equity. Later decades added new apartment buildings and attached houses on lots that had stood empty. Census-style figures from Point2 Homes put the median construction year for Foxhurst homes at 1981. About 23.7 percent of homes date from before the 1940s, 16.1 percent were added between 2000 and 2009, and another 12.3 percent between 2010 and 2019. Homes.com lists, as one example, a two-family house at 1029 Kelly Street built in 1993, and city property records show a seven-story, 14-unit building at 1012 Kelly Street owned by an LLC that carries the Foxhurst name.

For cameras, that history matters more than any brand name. In a 1910s walk-up, the hard part is getting cable up through old plaster and between floors without cracking finishes or weakening the fire separation. In a building rebuilt in the 1980s or put up in the 2000s, the floors may be concrete, the hall ceilings may be dropped, and there may already be camera cable from the developer, sometimes working, sometimes leading to a recorder nobody can find. Many of the rebuilt buildings were rehabilitated by community groups and nonprofits, and many are now co-ops. Homes.com notes that some Foxhurst co-ops are income-restricted and that listings advertise HDFC co-op apartments starting in the $100,000s. In those buildings, cameras in the lobby, halls and yard are a board decision, paid from a building budget that is usually tight, and the system has to outlive the people who voted for it.

The reason owners and boards ask about cameras at all is simple: the trouble in an apartment building tends to happen at the doors, at hours when nobody is watching. CBS New York reported on a 2016 killing on Southern Boulevard near Westchester Avenue in the Foxhurst section, in which police released surveillance photos and video of three men who allegedly entered the building around 3:10 a.m. We mention it not to describe your block, which we would not presume to judge, but because it shows what investigators rely on in a case like that: clear, time-stamped pictures of who came through the entrance and when. A building that has an entry camera framed for faces, with its clock set correctly and enough storage to hold the night in question, can answer that question. A building with a dead camera, or one pointed at a ceiling light, cannot.

Two rules decide who may put cameras where. First, the building owner or co-op board controls the common areas. A lawyer answering a New York tenant on Justia Ask a Lawyer explained that a landlord or co-op board can generally install cameras in lobbies, entryways, hallways and other shared spaces, while private space is a different matter. Second, tenants who put up their own devices in shared areas can get into real trouble. AOL reported in 2012 that a resident of Parkside Houses, a NYCHA development elsewhere in the Bronx, received an eviction notice saying she had breached her lease by installing cameras. So a Foxhurst renter's camera plan normally stays inside the apartment, and anything outside it goes through the owner, the managing agent or the board, in writing.

Foxhurst facts that shape a camera plan

  • Two eras of construction: about 23.7 percent of homes are pre-1940s, while more than a quarter were built between 2000 and 2019, so a single block can mean plaster-and-brick work on one job and concrete on the next.
  • Rebuilt after the fires: nearly 70 percent of the district's housing was lost to fire in the 1970s, which is why so many buildings have been through one or more gut renovations and carry layers of old wiring.
  • Co-op decisions: HDFC and other co-ops are common, and some are income-restricted, so camera plans usually go to a board meeting and have to fit a modest budget.
  • Trains overhead: the 2 and 5 run elevated above Westchester Avenue and Southern Boulevard, which brings vibration, moving shadows and passing headlights into any storefront or upper-floor camera nearby.
  • A main shopping street: CityNeighborhoods calls Southern Boulevard the neighborhood's commercial artery, and stores there sit at the foot of apartment buildings, sharing walls, cellars and sometimes side doors with tenants above.
  • No landmark district: we found no Landmarks Preservation Commission historic district inside Foxhurst; the nearest, the Longwood Historic District, covers about three square blocks bounded roughly by Beck Street and Longwood, Leggett and Prospect Avenues.

What Foxhurst buildings often pair with cameras. A camera at the street door answers who came in; it does not stop the wrong person from getting in. Boards and owners who start with cameras usually ask next about the door itself: a working intercom, which the Housing Maintenance Code expects owners of multiple dwellings to maintain, or a fob reader whose log can be matched against the camera's clock. We leave a spare labeled port and a pull string at the entry so those additions do not mean opening the vestibule ceiling a second time.

In the older walk-ups: start in the cellar and borrow every existing path

A walk-up's cellar is the natural home for the recorder, because power, the internet provider's equipment, the intercom's power supply and the building's other low-voltage lines are already down there. Cable for the street door and vestibule usually comes up a short distance through the floor or along the cellar ceiling to the front wall. The landings are harder. Before we suggest opening any plaster, we look for paths the building already has: a heating riser chase, a closet stacked floor above floor, a former dumbwaiter shaft, or the space behind an old pipe run. When none exists, a slim painted raceway fixed along the stair wall carries the cable up, anchored into the plaster with fasteners chosen for old plaster so it does not crumble. Every hole between floors is sealed with rated firestop once the cable is through, and nothing is fastened to a stair door, its frame or its closer.

In the rebuilt and newer buildings: find what the builder left before drilling

The buildings that went up or were gut-renovated from the 1980s onward often look easier and turn out to need more detective work. Many were built with some low-voltage cable already in the walls: a coax run to the lobby, a few data drops to a closet, sometimes a full camera rough-in that a later owner never finished. Concrete floors mean that a new path between stories may require a core drill, which is noisy, slow and best avoided if the builder left sleeves. So the first visit to a newer building includes a hunt for the existing telecom closet, any recorder hidden in it, and the sleeves or chases between floors. Where old cable is usable, we test it and reuse it; where it fails, we leave it labeled as dead or pull it out, so the next person is not fooled by it.

Attached and two-family houses: small, close and personal

Foxhurst also has newer attached and two-family houses, many built on lots cleared after the fires. These are a different kind of camera job: usually a front door, a front gate or steps, a side passage and a small rear yard, sometimes with a tenant on the upper floor. The houses sit close to their neighbors, so every outdoor camera has to stop at the owner's own lot line rather than looking into the next family's windows. The owner usually wants to see the cameras on a phone; the tenant may want the front door only. We set separate accounts so each person sees what they should and nothing more, and we keep the recorder inside the owner's part of the house.

Old walk-ups: plaster, narrow vestibules and stairs that turn

The pre-war walk-ups set the hardest conditions for clean camera work. Their vestibules are often barely deep enough for two people and a stroller, so a camera mounted inside the vestibule has to sit high and close to the ceiling, out of reach and clear of the door's swing. A better spot is often just inside the inner door, looking back at the vestibule, so it sees each person's face as they come in. The stairs in these buildings usually turn at every landing, which means one camera at the bottom shows only the first flight. Rather than putting a camera on every landing, we often place domes at alternating floors, angled up and down the stair, and check on site that together they leave no stretch unwatched. Plaster is fragile under a hammer drill, so we use slower drilling and anchors suited to old plaster, and we patch and paint any small marks before leaving.

Rebuilt buildings: concrete, drop ceilings and someone else's wiring

Buildings that were rebuilt or newly built after the fires often have halls with drop ceilings or soffits, which make horizontal cable runs easy, and concrete floors, which make vertical runs hard. The builder usually left a path for telephone, cable television or the intercom between floors, and we try to share it where code and space allow. When there is no sleeve and the slab must be cored, we plan one core in a hidden spot such as a closet, size it for the cable plus room for future additions, and seal it with rated firestop afterward. Developer-installed cameras are common in these buildings, and they are not always bad. Many can be kept on a new recorder if they are reasonably current and work properly; others are older analog models that are not worth keeping. We test them and tell you which group each one falls into before quoting.

Co-ops: the board's system, written for the next board

In Foxhurst co-ops, including the HDFC buildings that Homes.com describes, the camera system belongs to the corporation, not to whichever officer pushed for it. That shapes how we set it up. The administrator account is in the corporation's name, with a recovery email and phone number the board controls, and the password is recorded in a sealed note for the board's files rather than in one officer's phone. The managing agent or a named officer gets full viewing and export rights; the super gets the views he needs for the doors, cellar and yard; individual shareholders get nothing by default unless the board decides otherwise. We write all of this up for the minutes. When officers change, the board removes the departing user and adds the new one without calling anyone back. For tight budgets, we size the recorder and switch so cameras can be added in later years without replacing the core equipment.

Houses: covering the lot without covering the neighbors

Attached and two-family houses in Foxhurst sit shoulder to shoulder, and that sets the camera plan. A front camera covers the steps, the door and the gate, angled so its frame ends at the property line. A side-passage camera, if there is a passage, looks along it rather than across into the next house. A rear camera covers the yard and the back door. Infrared lights on cameras close to a neighbor's wall can bounce back and wash out the night picture, so we test after dark and adjust the angle or switch to a camera with a separate light. If the upstairs apartment is rented, the tenant can get a front-door view on their own phone with no access to the yard, the basement or the owner's settings.

Storefronts under the elevated train

A store under the el lives with three things a camera notices: vibration, moving shadow and changing light. When a train passes, the building shakes slightly, and a camera on a loose bracket shakes with it, blurring the clip and setting off motion alerts. We fix cameras into solid structure, not ceiling tiles or thin sign panels, and choose mounts that do not resonate. During the day, the tracks cast stripes of shade across the sidewalk and the front of the store, which can confuse a camera that is set for one level of light; we choose cameras that handle mixed light well and test them when the sun is low and the stripes are strongest. At night, headlights from cars and buses swing across the storefront glass, which is another reason the most useful camera is usually the one just inside the door, aimed at people walking in, not out through the glass.

Shops that share a building with tenants

Most Foxhurst stores are tenants in buildings that also hold apartments. That creates questions a mall store never faces. The cellar may be shared, with the store's stock room on one side and the building's boiler and meters on the other. A side or rear door may serve both the shop and the apartments above. The front of the building belongs to the landlord, not the shopkeeper. So a store's camera plan starts with a short list of whose permission is needed for each position: the store's own interior is the shopkeeper's to decide, while the facade, a shared side door, and any camera in the cellar outside the store's space need the landlord's agreement in writing. Where both the store and the building want coverage of the same door, the cleanest arrangement is usually one camera with two viewers rather than two cameras competing for one wall, and we write down who owns it.

Restaurants and takeout counters

The restaurants and pizzerias on Westchester and Prospect Avenues tend to need four views: the front door, the register or pickup counter, the kitchen door or back exit, and the cellar or storage area if there is one. A camera over the pickup counter that shows hands, bags and the customer's face settles most disputes about a missing order or a wrong charge. Where a restaurant serves through a window or a walk-up counter, a camera inside, above and slightly behind the person working the window, shows who is at the window and what changes hands. Kitchen cameras need housings that tolerate heat and grease, mounted away from the hood and the fryer. Dining-room coverage is usually lighter: a wide view of the room for safety, not a camera trained on each table.

Rules and limits for business cameras in Foxhurst

  • The installer needs a state license. New York General Business Law §69-m says no one may be in the business of installing, servicing or maintaining security systems without a license, and a law firm writing on the rule explains that it reaches closed-circuit television used to detect or monitor intrusion. AESS works under NYS license #12000287431 and carries insurance.
  • Owners working on their own property are treated differently. The NYS Department of State notes that people installing on their own property are not in the business of installing, so they do not need the license. A shopkeeper who hires someone else does need that someone to be licensed.
  • Private spaces stay off camera. Restrooms, changing areas and any room where people expect privacy are never recorded, and we aim and test cameras so their frames stop short of those doors.
  • The landlord decides outside the unit. The facade, a shared side door, a shared cellar and the sidewalk canopy belong to the building owner, and we ask for written agreement first.
  • Doors that must close stay free. Nothing is fixed to a self-closing door, its closer or its latch side in a way that could stop it shutting.
  • Every opening is sealed. Holes through rated walls and floors, including between a store and the apartments above, are closed with rated firestop before we leave.
Field Notes

Field Notes: Reading a Foxhurst Building Before Suggesting a Camera

We do not publish stories about particular buildings or customers, so this is our routine rather than an anecdote.

The first thing we do in a Foxhurst building is work out which century we are in. We look at the hall walls, tap the plaster, check whether the ceiling is plaster or tile, and go down to the cellar to see whether the floor above is wood joists or concrete. That tells us whether this is a 1910s walk-up, a building gutted in the 1980s, or something newer, and it decides how cable will travel. Next we look for what earlier owners left: coax stapled along the cellar ceiling, a dead camera over the street door, a junction box on the third floor, a closet with a recorder and no password. We trace whatever we find before touching it. Then we stand at the street door at the hour the super says is busiest and watch how people actually enter, where they pause, where the light comes from and where a camera would be in someone's way. Only after that do we draw the plan, and we ask the owner or board one more question before pricing it: who will hold the main login five years from now?

Your Questions Answered

Security Camera Installation in Foxhurst: Questions Answered

Pre-war walk-ups

Our walk-up has an old dumbwaiter shaft that was closed off decades ago. Can camera cable go up it?

Often yes, and it is one of the best paths in an old building, because it runs straight from the cellar to the top floor without touching the plaster halls. We open it at the bottom and top, check that it is dry and not blocked, and drop a line through. The cable is fixed to supports inside rather than left hanging, and short branches reach each landing through small sealed openings. Because a shaft like that can carry fire and smoke between floors, every opening we make into it is sealed with rated firestop, and we leave the owner a note of where those seals are.

Our vestibule is so tight the inner door nearly hits the mailboxes. Where can a camera fit?

Usually not in the vestibule at all. A camera squeezed into a tiny vestibule ends up low enough to be knocked, or so close to people that it only sees the tops of heads. The better spot in most narrow walk-up entries is just inside the inner door, high on the wall or ceiling, looking back toward the vestibule so it sees each face as people come through. If the mailboxes are the concern, a second small camera in the lobby can look at them from the side. We check the door's full swing before marking any position so nothing is hit when the door opens.

Rebuilt and newer buildings

Nobody in our building knows where the camera recorder even is. How do you find it?

We follow the cable. Starting at a working or dead camera, we trace its wire with a tone generator and probe through ceilings, closets and the cellar until it ends at a box, which is usually the recorder or a switch that leads to it. In buildings renovated in the 1980s or later, the recorder is often in a telecom closet, a locked cabinet in the cellar, or a ceiling space near the lobby. Once it is found, we check whether it still powers on, whether its drive holds any footage, and whether access can be recovered without wiping it; if not, we explain the choices before resetting anything.

Co-ops and HDFC buildings

Some shareholders worry that lobby cameras will be used to track who visits whom. How do boards usually set limits?

A short written camera policy, adopted at a board meeting, answers most of those worries. It lists where the cameras are, makes clear that none look into apartments, says who may view and export footage, sets how long recordings are kept before they loop, and states the reasons footage may be reviewed, such as a crime, damage or a safety complaint. Justia's lawyer answer notes that co-op boards can generally place cameras in shared spaces like lobbies and halls; a written policy shows shareholders how that power will be used. We supply the technical facts the policy needs, such as storage days and user rights.

Our co-op's officers change almost every year. How do the camera logins survive each turnover?

The main account is created in the corporation's name, tied to an email address and phone number the corporation keeps, not an officer's personal account. Each officer, the managing agent and the super gets their own user with limits, and the master password is recorded once, sealed, in the board's files. When an officer leaves, their user is removed and the new officer is added from the main account in a few minutes. We hand over a one-page sheet explaining exactly how to do that, so the next board does not need us or the last treasurer to get in.

Southern Boulevard, Westchester and Prospect Avenues

Our office upstairs on Westchester Avenue looks straight at the elevated train. Can an inside camera face those windows?

It can, but it is rarely a good idea. A camera facing windows at train level will record passing riders, catch flare from the train's lights and the sky behind it, and set off motion alerts every few minutes when trains go by. Inside an upper-floor office, the useful views are the suite door, the reception area and any cash or records room, aimed away from the windows. If you need to watch the windows themselves for a break-in, a camera beside the window looking along the inside of the glass sees anyone climbing in without filming the train.

Our takeout spot on Prospect Avenue serves through a counter and a small window. Where do the cameras go?

One camera inside, above and a little behind the staff position at the window, shows the customer's face, the order and the money or card changing hands. A second camera above the indoor counter does the same for walk-in customers, and a third covers the front door so every person entering is recorded at head height. If the window opens onto the sidewalk, motion alerts on that camera stay off during business hours and switch on after you close and lower the gate. The kitchen and back door get their own coverage, mounted clear of heat and grease.

Our store and the apartments above share one cellar. Can our cameras cover our part without recording the building's side?

Yes, by aiming each cellar camera so its frame ends at your storage area or partition, and by testing that from the recorder before the job is signed off. If the shared parts of the cellar, such as the boiler room door or the meters, need coverage, that is the landlord's decision, and the landlord may prefer their own camera there or ask to share yours. Where the landlord agrees to share, we can give the landlord or super a separate view of that one camera without access to anything inside your store.

FAQ

Foxhurst Security Camera Installation FAQ

Our store has a painted sheet-metal sign band above the gate. Can a camera be mounted on it?
+

Usually not directly. A thin sign band flexes and vibrates, especially under the elevated train, and a camera fixed to it will shake on every passing train and every gust of wind. The better approach is to fix the camera to the solid masonry or steel behind or beside the sign, sometimes with a bracket that passes through the band to the structure, sealed against water. The facade belongs to the landlord, so any outside camera needs written agreement first, and we confirm what the sign is attached to before drilling.

In our HDFC co-op, does the camera system belong to the corporation or to the shareholders who helped pay for it?
+

If the board approves it and it is installed in the common areas, it is treated as the corporation's equipment, whoever raised the money. That is why we put the main account in the corporation's name and record the master password in the board's files. Shareholders who contributed do not gain personal control over the cameras or footage. If a group of shareholders wants their own system, it has to stay inside their own apartments. Your co-op's lawyer or managing agent can confirm how your bylaws treat contributions.

Can a clip from one incident be locked so the recorder never records over it?
+

Yes. Most current recorders let an authorized user mark a stretch of footage as locked or protected, so it is kept even when the drive fills and older footage is overwritten. It is still wise to export a copy to a USB drive or your phone, because a locked clip lives on the same recorder and is lost if that recorder fails or is stolen. We show the owner or board member how to lock and export a clip during the handover, and we leave enough free space that locked clips do not crowd out normal recording.

Two cameras cover different parts of our turning stairway. Can we play them back side by side on one timeline?
+

Yes. Recorders can play several cameras in sync, so you can follow a person from the second-floor landing camera to the fourth-floor one without hunting for the matching time. This works best when every camera's clock is set from the recorder and the recorder keeps accurate time, which we set up during installation. In a walk-up whose stair turns at every landing, synced playback is often how an owner works out exactly which floor someone left the stair on.

Our rebuilt building has poured concrete floors. Does every camera cable have to be cored through the slab?
+

Usually not. Builders normally leave sleeves or chases between floors for telephone, cable television and the intercom, and camera cable can often share them where space and code allow. We look for those paths first. If there is none, we plan one core in a hidden spot such as a closet stacked over the one below, sized for the cameras now and some extra later, and we seal it with rated firestop. One planned core is far better than several small holes drilled floor by floor.

Our ground-floor apartment windows have security gates. Can a camera sit behind the gate and still see out?
+

A camera inside the window, with the lens close to the glass, can see past the bars of a gate, because the bars are out of focus that close. The catch is at night: an infrared light behind glass bounces back into the lens and washes out the picture, so an indoor window camera needs its own infrared switched off and some outdoor light to see by. Inside your apartment, that camera is your decision; anything fixed to the gate or the outside wall needs the owner's permission.

Someone spray-painted our lobby dome camera. Can it be cleaned, or does it have to be replaced?
+

Often it can be cleaned. Many vandal-resistant domes have a replaceable clear cover, so the painted cover is swapped and the camera inside stays. Where the cover is not replaceable, careful cleaning with a solvent safe for that plastic may restore it, though scratches can leave the picture hazy. Turning on the camera's tamper or scene-change alert means the super hears about it the night it happens, not days later, and mounting the dome higher makes it harder to reach in the first place.

Should our building's cameras run on the super's home internet plan, or have their own line?
+

A line in the building's or corporation's name is better. If the cameras ride on the super's personal plan, remote viewing ends the day he moves, changes provider or swaps the router, and the building has no control over the account. The recorder keeps recording without internet either way; the line only matters for watching from a phone and receiving alerts. A modest plan is usually enough for viewing, and we set the recorder up so a change of router later does not break the connection.

Is a camera at the boiler room door worth it, so we can see which contractor went in and when?
+

In many buildings, yes. Boiler, plumbing, meter and pest-control contractors come and go through the cellar, and when something breaks or goes missing, a time-stamped clip of who entered settles the question quickly. The camera faces the door from outside the room, so it records faces as people approach rather than the equipment inside. Its alerts can be set for hours when no contractor is expected, while it simply records during the working day. It also helps confirm whether a scheduled visit really happened.

Can the recorder send us a daily snapshot from every camera, so we know none has been turned or blocked?
+

Many recorders can email or push a still image from each camera on a schedule, such as once each morning. Glancing at that set of pictures shows at once whether a camera has been knocked toward the ceiling, covered or gone dark. It suits a board member or owner who does not want to open the app every day. We set the schedule and the recipient during handover, and pair it with an alert for cameras that go offline.

If we add a fob reader to the front door later, can its entry log be matched with the cameras?
+

Yes, as long as both systems keep the same time. A fob log tells you which credential opened the door at 2:14 a.m.; the camera shows who was holding it, and whether anyone followed them in. Some systems link the two so a fob event jumps straight to the matching clip; others are simply checked side by side. We set the camera recorder's clock automatically and leave a spare cable and labeled port at the entry, so a reader can be added without opening the vestibule again.

Our walk-up's stair has a skylight at the top. Does daylight from above wash out the landing cameras?
+

It can, especially for a camera on an upper landing aimed up toward the bright skylight, which turns people into dark shapes. We aim the top-floor cameras down the stair, away from the skylight, and choose cameras that handle strong contrast. On lower landings the light is softer and rarely a problem. We check the views at midday on a clear day and again at night, when the skylight goes dark and the stair depends on its own lights, before the job is signed off.
