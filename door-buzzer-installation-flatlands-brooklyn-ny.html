The Neighborhood

Why Flatlands Properties Need Door Buzzer Installation

Flatlands sits in the southeast of Brooklyn, between Nostrand Avenue and Ralph Avenue and from the Bay Ridge Branch rail line down toward Avenue T. It began in 1636 as the Dutch farm town of Nieuw Amersfoort and stayed rural longer than almost anywhere else in the borough, because it had no railroad or good road to the rest of Brooklyn; it was annexed by the City of Brooklyn only in 1896. When building finally came, it came as houses. The blocks south of the rail line are mostly brick and frame one- and two-family homes put up between the 1920s and the 1950s, many attached or semi-detached in long rows, with a driveway, a front yard and a stoop. There is no subway inside the neighborhood; people drive, take buses to the 2 and 5 at Flatbush and Nostrand Avenues, and receive a great many deliveries.

That shapes what a buzzer installation means here. In a Manhattan walk-up, the entry problem is a lobby panel and a riser. In Flatlands, the most common job is a single front door shared by two households, an owner on one floor and a tenant or relatives on the other, with nothing beside the door but a doorbell that rings in one apartment. The city's tax lot file shows why. In ZIP 11234, two-family lots outnumber every other kind of residential building except one-family houses, and of those 6,573 two-family lots, 3,032 were built between 1950 and 1969 and another 1,726 between 1930 and 1949. Those decades predate any idea of a two-apartment intercom being standard equipment in a house.

The other half of the picture is the apartment buildings, fewer but much larger. Kings Highway is lined with six-story elevator buildings from 1938 to 1955 with 66 to 84 apartments each. The Kings Village co-op's buildings at 1165 East 54th Street and at neighboring addresses on East 51st and East 53rd Streets have 156 units apiece and went up in 1965 and 1966. South toward Avenue T are two-story garden apartment complexes from 1955, where tenants enter through a courtyard rather than a lobby. And on Farragut Road, NYCHA's Glenwood Houses holds 20 six-story buildings and about 2,700 residents. Each of these has a different front door, and the city's records show which of those doors keep failing.

Those records are public. In HPD's housing maintenance code violations for the Flatlands neighborhood area, inspectors wrote 14 intercom violations in 2025 and 6 more in 2026 through November 11, all but two of them class B. The addresses are almost all apartment buildings: 2425 Nostrand Avenue has 14 intercom violations on record, 4011 Kings Highway has 13, 1022 East 36th Street has 9, and 4200 Avenue K has 7. Tenants' side of the story shows up in HPD's complaint log: in ZIP 11234, complaints in the bell, buzzer and intercom category rose from 11 in 2023 to 16 in 2024 and 18 in 2025, with 14 more in 2026 so far. These are not huge numbers, but each one is a building where a visitor rang and nobody upstairs could answer, or answered and could not open the door.

Repair or Replace in Flatlands

A call that begins "we need a new buzzer" in Flatlands usually turns out to be one of three situations. The first is a house that has never had one: a doorbell by the front door, maybe a store-bought camera doorbell one household added, and a tenant who comes downstairs for every ring. Door Buzzer Installation for Flatlands's Architecture

Six kinds of building make up almost every Flatlands buzzer installation we plan. They differ in where the entrance is, how cable can reach each household, who signs for the work and what the door or gate is made of, and each one has a mistake that shows up when the job is rushed.

Attached and semi-detached two-family rows

Brick houses built in long rows from the 1930s through the 1960s, with a stoop or a short flight up to a front door that leads to both apartments, a garage or driveway below, and a party wall on one or both sides.

Detached houses with a second apartment

Frame and brick houses on wider lots, often with siding over the original walls, where the second household is upstairs, in a finished basement or behind a separate door off the driveway.

Six-story Kings Highway buildings

Elevator apartment buildings from 1938 to 1955 with roughly 66 to 84 units, a lobby door off the avenue, a vestibule with mailboxes and a riser that has carried intercom cable for decades.

1960s co-op buildings

The six-story, 156-unit buildings of Kings Village, completed in 1965 and 1966, run by one co-op board and managing agent across several buildings.

Garden apartment complexes

Two-story courtyard buildings from the 1950s near Avenue S and Avenue T, and older courtyard buildings elsewhere, where the entrance gate and each stair door are separate points of entry.

Stores with apartments above

Two- and three-story buildings on Flatbush Avenue, Kings Highway, Avenue N and Ralph Avenue with a shop at street level and one to four apartments reached by a narrow residential door beside it.

Attached two-family rows: one front door, two apartments, a party wall on each side

The most common Flatlands house is a brick two-family in a row of identical houses. The front door at the top of the stoop opens into a small hall; one door off that hall leads to the first-floor apartment, a stair leads to the second. The owner often lives on one floor and rents the other, or a grown child lives upstairs. Visitors ring one bell, and whoever hears it goes to the door. A proper two-household buzzer replaces that guesswork with two labeled buttons, one station on each floor and a release in the front door's frame.

The cable route is the part that takes thought. In a row house, the party walls are solid masonry and are not to be cut. The usual path runs from the door frame down into the garage or cellar under the stoop, along the basement ceiling, and up through a closet or the stair wall to the second floor. Where there is no cellar, because the front of the house sits over a garage on a slab, the run usually goes up inside the hall wall or behind the stair stringer. We trace the route before quoting so it does not change on the day.

Detached houses: the second household often has its own door

On the wider lots, houses stand alone with a driveway down one side. The second apartment is often reached by its own entrance, a side door partway down the driveway or a door at the back of the house, while the front door belongs to the owner. That arrangement is good for privacy and bad for visitors, who walk up to the front door, ring the only bell they can find and wake the wrong household.

The right installation gives each entrance its own station. The owner's front door gets a one- or two-button station; the tenant's side or rear door gets its own, answered only in the tenant's apartment, with a release on that door. A small sign or a house number on the tenant's station, visible from the sidewalk, sends visitors down the driveway instead of to the front. Both stations are powered from a supply on the owner's side of the house, so a tenant's power cut does not kill the owner's buzzer, or the reverse.

Six-story Kings Highway buildings: a riser that has carried a buzzer for seventy years

The apartment buildings on Kings Highway between Nostrand Avenue and Utica Avenue are a world away from the houses on the side streets. Built from the late 1930s to the mid-1950s, most are six stories with an elevator and somewhere between 66 and 84 apartments. Several of them show up in HPD's records: 4011 Kings Highway has 13 intercom violations on file and 3502 Kings Highway has 6. In buildings like these, the intercom has usually been replaced more than once, and each replacement has left its own layer of cable in the riser.

The job starts in the basement, where the old system's power supply and junction terminals are, and goes up the riser one floor at a time. Each pair is tested from the basement to the apartment. Where enough pairs are sound, a two-wire system can reuse them, and Aiphone's GT Series, built for "new builds and retrofit projects," is one example of equipment designed for exactly that. Where the riser is full of dead or mixed cable, new cable goes up the existing riser path; the elevator shaft is not an option, because it is reserved for the elevator's own equipment.

1960s co-op buildings: several lobbies, one board, one set of records

Kings Village is a co-op of six-story buildings with 156 units each, completed in 1965 and 1966 according to the city's tax lot file and Compass's building record. Three of its addresses, 1270 East 51st Street and 1190 and 1199 East 53rd Street, each have four intercom violations on file with HPD. A co-op board's decision about an entry system is a decision about several buildings at once, paid for from shared funds, so the planning is as much about records as about hardware.

For a board, we start with an inventory of each lobby: what panel is there, how many apartments answer, how many pairs in the riser are working, and what the power supply and door hardware look like. The board then gets a price for each building and for all of them together, so it can phase the work or do it at once. Every system is set up so the managing agent can update the directory and so the board holds the admin access, not the installer.

Garden apartment complexes: the entrance is a gate

Some Flatlands apartment buildings are arranged around a courtyard. Tenants pass through a gate from the sidewalk into the court, and then into the stair door of their building. The HPD violation written at 1022 East 36th Street on May 5, 2026, which describes an intercom that failed "from east entrance gate to north wall in the kitchen" of an apartment, shows how such a system works: the call starts at the gate, and the apartment's station, here in the kitchen, has to answer and release the gate.

Stores with apartments above: keep the shop's door and the tenants' door apart

On Flatbush Avenue, Kings Highway, Avenue N and Ralph Avenue, many buildings have a shop or an office at street level and a small number of apartments upstairs. The residential door is often a narrow door beside the shop window. The shop has its own hours, its own deliveries and sometimes its own back door; the tenants have theirs. A buzzer installation has to keep those worlds separate.

The residential panel calls only the apartments, and its release opens only the residential door. Its power supply is on the landlord's circuit, not one that goes dark when the store closes or changes hands. If the store wants its own buzzer, for a back door or a door that stays locked during certain hours, it is a separate station answered at the counter. No button in the shop can open the apartments' door, and no tenant's release can open the store.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Ring From the Sidewalk, Find Where Each Household Really Lives, Then Follow the Wire to the Cellar

These notes describe how our technicians approach an installation in the kinds of buildings Flatlands has. They are our standard practice, not an account of one particular address.

Ring from the sidewalk. The technician starts where a visitor starts: on the sidewalk, looking for the bell. Is it obvious which door to use? Is the house number visible at night? If the tenant has a side door, can a stranger tell? Then the technician tries the door the way a visitor would, pushing on it after it closes, to see whether it has latched. On a two-family with a worn latch, this one test often decides more of the job than anything on the wall.

Find where each household really lives. Next comes the count: mailboxes, meters, doorbells and doors. In Flatlands houses, those numbers often disagree. A house listed as a two-family may have a finished basement used by a relative; a three-family may have one apartment empty; a front door may serve the owner while the tenant uses a door nobody mentioned on the phone. The technician asks where each household's apartment door is and where its people spend their time, because a station in the front hall is useless to a family that lives in the back kitchen.

Follow the wire to the cellar. Last, the technician traces whatever old bell or intercom wire exists to its transformer, usually in the cellar or the garage, and tests each pair. The transformer's circuit is noted, along with whose meter it is on. A two-family's doorbell transformer wired into the tenant's panel is common, and it means the owner's buzzer dies when the tenant's power is shut off. The new supply always goes on the owner's side, with a licensed electrician if a new outlet is needed.

The Door Comes First

From 12 to 94 in One Year: What the 2018 Self-Closing Door Law Means for a Flatlands Buzzer Installation

If you chart HPD's self-closing door violations in the Flatlands neighborhood area year by year, one jump stands out. In 2017, inspectors wrote 12 of them. In 2018 they wrote 94, then 137 in 2019, 216 in 2023 and 288 in 2024. Neighborhood buildings did not suddenly get worse doors. What changed was the law. Local Law 111 of 2018 added section 27-2041.1 to the city's housing maintenance code, requiring owners of multiple dwellings to keep their self-closing doors in good repair, and HPD's later rule sets out how it inspects them, including periodic inspections in buildings of more than six units. Since June 12, 2019, these violations have been treated as class C, the immediately hazardous category.

Most of those violations concern apartment entrance doors and stair doors rather than street doors, so they are not a direct count of failed lobby entrances. But they tell an installer two things about this neighborhood. First, the city now looks hard at whether doors close by themselves, and building entrance doors are on the list of doors that must. Second, many Flatlands owners have spent the last few years fixing closers, which means a building's front door may already be in better shape than its intercom, or may have had a closer installed so strong that a visitor buzzed in can barely push the door open.

Either way, every Flatlands installation we price includes two checks at the door before anything else, the closer and the latch, and the price includes what those checks find. If the door itself is past saving, we say so before quoting, so it can be replaced first.

The closer. A hydraulic closer has two speeds, one for the main swing and one for the last few inches, where the latch has to catch. A closer set too slow lets the door stop short of latching; one set too hard makes the door slam and the frame loosen, and on an older visitor or a parent with a stroller it can be a real obstacle. We adjust closers that can be adjusted and replace those that leak, sized to the door's weight and its exposure. A steel lobby door facing the wind on Kings Highway needs a different closer from a wood door under a two-family's porch roof.

The latch and the strike. An electric strike replaces the fixed plate in the frame that the latch catches on. When the release is pressed, part of the strike frees the latch and the door can be pushed open; when it stops, the latch is held again. That only works if the latch lines up with the strike. Doors in seventy-year-old houses have sagged, frames have shifted as stoops settled, and many front doors have been replaced with doors that never quite fit. We check alignment, adjust hinges or move the strike, and choose a strike body that fits the frame without cutting away so much of it that the frame is weakened.

An HPD spokesperson, asked by News 12 in June 2026 about conditions at a Flatlands apartment building on East 35th Street, put the owner's side of this plainly: "it is the property manager's responsibility to follow the City's Housing Code by promptly addressing violations." For an entrance, the housing code asks for a door that closes and locks and, in larger buildings, an intercom that works. A buzzer installation that ignores the first half is only doing half the job.

What Inspectors Test

"From Building Entrance to Return Buzzer in the Foyer": Reading a Flatlands Intercom Violation Before You Replace Anything

HPD intercom violations are written in a fixed style, and in Flatlands the recent ones repay a close read. A violation at 3801 Avenue L on August 1, 2026 orders the owner to "properly repair or replace the broken or defective intercom system from building entrance to return buzzer at east wall in the foyer" of a third-floor apartment. One at 1240 East 38th Street on July 27 names an "intercom, bell buzzer from entrance to building to west wall in the foyer" of a first-floor unit. At 5409 Avenue O in March, the order covers the "buzzer intercom system from west wall at building entrance to return buzzer at north wall in the foyer." Every one of them cites section 27-2005 of the housing maintenance code, the owner's general duty to keep the building in good repair.

Three things follow from that wording, and each one changes how an installation should be planned.

The violation is about a path, not a panel. The phrase "from building entrance to return buzzer" describes the whole chain: the button outside, the cable, the apartment's station and the release that answers back. An inspector who writes it has tested that chain from one apartment and found it broken somewhere. The break may be in the lobby panel, in the riser, in one apartment's station or in the door's strike. Replacing the lobby panel alone fixes the violation only if the panel was the broken link.

The apartment's location matters. These violations name the apartment and the wall where its station sits, usually in the foyer just inside the apartment door, sometimes in the kitchen. That is where the station should stay in a new installation unless the household wants it elsewhere, because that is where residents expect it and where the next inspector will look.

One apartment's violation can mean a whole riser's problem. In older apartment buildings, apartments stacked above one another share a riser. When one apartment's station fails because its pair in the riser has broken, the units above or below it often fail next. Before quoting a new system for a building with a cited apartment, we test the pairs for the whole line, not just the unit named on the violation.

Owners who have received a violation should send us a copy, or the HPD violation number, with the request. The class and the date matter. HPD's correction periods, as ButterflyMX's guide to New York intercom rules sets them out, are 90 days for class A, 30 days for class B and 24 hours for most class C conditions. Of the 20 intercom violations written in the Flatlands area in 2025 and 2026, 18 were class B, so a 30-day window is the usual case, and a replacement meant to clear one has to be planned to finish inside it, including the time it takes to get into every apartment.

A new installation does not clear a violation by itself; the owner still certifies the correction with HPD. What we provide is the work and a dated test sheet showing that every apartment, including the cited one, was called from the entrance, answered, talked and released the door. That sheet is the record an owner wants when certifying, and when the next tenant complains.

The Rules

Which Flatlands Buildings Must Have a Buzzer, What It Has to Do, and What Stays Optional

Flatlands has every kind of building the entry rules treat differently, from one-family houses that no intercom rule touches to 156-unit co-ops. Here is how the main rules land, in plain terms and without legal advice.

Houses with one, two or three households. New York's Multiple Dwelling Law is about multiple dwellings, which in New York City means buildings with three or more households living independently. A one- or two-family house is outside it altogether, so a buzzer there is a matter of convenience and safety, not law. A Reddit r/AskNYC thread from a tenant in a four-unit Brooklyn building captures the common belief about small buildings: the landlord "isn't required to put in a buzzer system or a self-locking front door." For small buildings that predate 1968 and fall below eight units, that is broadly right as far as §50-a goes, though the housing code's repair duty applies to any intercom that is already there.

Apartment buildings of eight or more units. §50-a says that every class A multiple dwelling erected or converted after January 1, 1968 with eight or more apartments must have a self-locking main entrance door and an intercommunication system that lets tenants talk to a visitor and release the door from their apartments. For older buildings of that size, the owner must install the door and the system when tenants of a majority of apartments request or consent in writing; work has to begin within 90 days and finish within six months. The city's tenants' rights guide adds that the owner may recover the cost of providing this equipment from tenants. Most of the Kings Highway buildings and the Kings Village co-op were finished before 1968, which puts them under the majority-request route rather than the automatic one.

Everyone must be able to get out. A door with a buzzer still has to open from inside without a key, a code or special knowledge. With an electric strike that is automatic: Allegion's guide notes that with a strike "you can exit by turning the lever" whether the strike is fail-safe or fail-secure. Magnetic locks are different, because they hold the whole door. The building code's sensor-release provisions call for a sensor on the egress side, a manual release marked "PUSH TO EXIT" mounted 40 to 48 inches above the floor within five feet of the door, release on loss of power and on fire alarm or sprinkler activation, and hardware listed to UL 294.

NYCHA buildings follow NYCHA's program. At Glenwood Houses, entrances are part of NYCHA's own capital work. Its layered access control guideline describes key fobs, a direct-call intercom and new door locks, with electric strikes configured fail-secure and in-unit intercoms listed as an optional upgrade. Residents with a broken entry there should report it to NYCHA; private installers do not work on NYCHA doors without NYCHA's contract.

Your Questions Answered

Door Buzzer Installation in Flatlands: Questions Answered

Questions Flatlands homeowners, landlords, co-op boards and business owners bring to us, plus questions raised in r/AskNYC, ButterflyMX and installer discussions about small-building buzzers, answered for the houses and buildings here.

Houses with two or three households

Our grown children live upstairs in our two-family. Is one shared bell enough, or should each floor still get its own button?

One bell works while everyone in the house knows everyone's visitors. It stops working the day one household starts getting deliveries, a home attendant, a tutor or friends the other floor does not expect. Then the shared bell becomes a relay: whoever hears it goes down, finds out who it is for, and calls up the stairs. That is the situation most Flatlands families describe when they call us, often years after the second floor became a separate household.

Two buttons cost little more than one, because the station, the release, the power supply and most of the cable are the same. Each floor gets a station that rings only for its own button, and both can release the front door. If the family still wants one bell to ring everywhere for some visitors, the station can be set to ring both floors from one of the buttons.

Our attached house's front hall is barely wider than the door. Where can the first-floor apartment's station fit?

Usually inside the first-floor apartment rather than in the shared hall. In many Flatlands row houses the front door opens into a hall a few feet deep, with the first-floor apartment door on one side and the stair on the other. A station on that hall wall is in everyone's way and is not where the first-floor household spends its time. We put it inside the apartment, near its own door or in the kitchen, and run the cable through the wall from the hall.

We rent our finished basement to a relative who comes in through the side door off the garage. Can that door be put on the buzzer?

Yes, and in Flatlands it often should be, because it is the door the basement household uses every day. The side door gets its own one-button station outside, answered by a station in the basement apartment, and its own release in the frame. Visitors for the basement go to that door; visitors for the floors above go to the front.

Our house became a three-family years ago, but the third apartment was never added to the old two-button plate. Is it cheaper to add a button or replace the plate?

It depends on what the two-button plate is attached to. If it is a simple bell plate wired to two chimes, adding a third button means a new plate anyway, because there is nowhere to put the button and no talk or release behind it. If it is a two-unit audio intercom that is still in production or has compatible parts, a third button and a third station can sometimes be added to the same system, which is the cheaper route.

In most of the houses we see, the plate is either a bare bell or an intercom old enough that parts are no longer made, and replacement is the honest answer. A new three-button station, three apartment stations, a release and a power supply sits in our $400 to $900 audio range or the $700 to $1,800 video range, depending on what the household wants. We look at the plate and its wiring from your photos before quoting either option.

Apartment buildings and co-ops

Our Kings Highway building has 84 apartments and was built around 1950. Since it predates 1968, do the tenants decide whether we need an intercom?

In part. Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a requires buildings of eight or more units built or converted after January 1, 1968 to have a self-locking main door and an intercom automatically. For older buildings, the duty arises when tenants of a majority of the apartments request or consent in writing, and then the work has to begin within 90 days and be completed within six months. So in a building of your age, a majority request is what turns an intercom from a good idea into a legal requirement.

In practice, though, almost every six-story building on Kings Highway already has an intercom, installed decades ago and replaced since. Once a system is in place, the housing code's repair duty applies, and that is what HPD's Flatlands violations cite. The more useful question for an owner is usually not whether to have a system, but whether the current one can be repaired reliably or should be replaced before the next round of complaints.

Our co-op has several buildings with identical lobbies. Will one design work for all of them, or does each lobby need its own survey?

One design, but a survey of every lobby and riser. Buildings built from the same plans in the mid-1960s often have the same lobby layout, the same door and the same panel position, so the board can choose one panel, one apartment station and one door hardware set for the whole complex.

What differs from building to building is everything hidden: how many pairs in each riser still test sound, what previous repairs were made, where the power supply ended up, whether a door was replaced and how the closer was set after the city's self-closing door rule took effect. Those differences change the labor, and sometimes the cable, building by building. We survey each one, then give the board a price per building on one common design, so it can compare them and decide on the order.

Gates, courtyards and doors after a violation

Our garden complex's courtyard gate is iron and swings in the wind. Can it hold a lock that a buzzer releases?

Yes, if the gate is made to close properly first. A buzzer release needs a gate that comes to rest in the same place every time, so its latch meets the lock. An iron gate that swings loose in the wind, sags on its hinges or bounces off its stop will not latch reliably no matter what lock is fitted. So the gate work comes first: the hinges are adjusted or replaced, a gate closer strong enough for its weight and exposure is fitted, and a stop is added so it lands where it should.

Then the lock. Gates usually take a gate-rated electric lock or strike built into a box on the post, or a magnetic lock where the gate frame allows it, with the free-exit and release rules that come with magnetic locks. The panel goes on the post at a reachable height, protected from the gate's swing. Cable comes from the building through an underground conduit where one exists, or a new one, and power comes from the building's supply so the gate does not depend on an outdoor outlet.

Families use our courtyard gate for a day care in the morning, but tenants want it locked at night. Can the gate follow a schedule?

Yes. The release can be set to hold the gate unlocked during set hours, for example during morning drop-off, and to lock it automatically afterward, so anyone arriving later has to ring. The schedule lives in the system's controller and can be changed by whoever holds admin access, usually the owner or the managing agent. A manual override lets staff unlock the gate for a delivery or an event without changing the schedule.

Two things should be agreed in writing before the schedule is set: who can change it, and what happens if the power fails during the locked hours. With a fail-secure lock the gate stays locked when the power fails and can be opened with a key, which is what most residential buildings want. With a fail-safe magnetic lock it opens, which is why we rarely recommend one on a residential gate. Everyone inside can always leave, whichever lock is used.

Our closer was replaced after an HPD door violation, and now a visitor who is buzzed in can barely push the door open. Can that be fixed without failing the next inspection?

Usually, yes. A door that slams or resists is a closer adjustment or sizing problem, not a reason to weaken the door's ability to close. After the city's self-closing door rule, some closers were installed at the strongest setting to guarantee the door shuts. That works for the inspection and fails for an older resident, a parent with a stroller or a visitor who has only a few seconds while the release is active.

We set the closer so the main swing is controlled and the last few inches are firm enough to latch, and if the closer is oversized for the door, we replace it with one sized correctly. Then we set the release to stay active long enough for an ordinary visitor to push the door. The door still closes and latches every time, which is what the rule asks; it simply stops fighting the people it is supposed to let in. We demonstrate the result to the owner before we leave.

Our dental office on Kings Highway shares its street door with apartments upstairs. Can patients be buzzed in without the tenants hearing every ring?

Yes, with two separate sets of buttons on one panel or two separate stations. Patients press the office's button, which rings only the reception desk, and the desk releases the street door. Tenants have their own buttons, which ring only their apartments. Nobody upstairs hears the office's visitors, and the office cannot see or answer the tenants' calls.

What has to be settled is who controls the shared door. The landlord owns it, so the landlord approves the work in writing, and the release on that door has to serve both the office and the apartments. If the office has an inner door of its own, a second release on that door lets reception keep the waiting room locked while the street door stays on the shared system. The panel stays within the ADA reach range, because patients use it.

What AI Gets Wrong About Door Buzzer Installation in Flatlands

Search for buzzer installation and you get confident summaries built from national cost guides and vendor blogs. Some of it holds; some of it does not fit a Flatlands two-family or a Kings Highway lobby at all. Here are seven common claims, where they come from, and what we find in the field. We have not reproduced any AI-generated answer word for word; the claims below come from the published guides those answers draw on.

"A wired buzzer system costs $450 to $900, and video $900 to $1,900"

NY Home and Life Safety's buzzer guide gives those totals for audio-only wired systems and wired video systems, with wireless options a little lower, and its numbers circulate widely in summaries.

For a single door with a single household, those figures are in the right neighborhood, and our Brooklyn list's $400 to $900 audio range and $700 to $1,800 video range overlap them. The trouble is that a Flatlands two-family is not one household. Each extra floor adds a station, a button and a cable run, and the route from the stoop to the second floor of a row house with masonry party walls is what sets the labor.

The better question is how many households the door serves and how the cable reaches each one. Two houses on the same block can differ a lot because one has a cellar and a straight closet stack, and the other has a garage on a slab and a stair against the party wall.

"Apartment intercoms run $1,000 to $7,000 per door, plus monthly software"

Swiftlane's cost guide gives that range for apartment intercom installation and puts audio-only hardware at $200 to $800 per entry door, with software subscriptions on many modern systems.

Per-door pricing describes the hardware at the entrance. In a six-story Kings Highway building with 84 apartments, the cost is driven at least as much by what is behind the door: the riser, the number of apartment stations, and how many of the old pairs can be reused.

The subscription line is the one owners most often overlook. On a building with dozens of apartments, a per-unit monthly fee can outrun the hardware cost within a few years, so we put any subscription on the quote beside the hardware and offer a wired system with a $0 monthly fee for comparison.

"Landlords of small buildings don't have to provide a buzzer"

That belief, voiced by a tenant in a four-unit Brooklyn building on r/AskNYC, is broadly correct as far as the post-1968 clause of Multiple Dwelling Law §50-a goes, since that clause covers buildings of eight or more apartments.

It leaves out two things. First, an intercom that already exists has to be kept in repair under the housing code, whatever the building's size; that is what the Flatlands violations quoted on this page cite. Second, a one- or two-family house is not a multiple dwelling at all, so the question there is not law but whether the household wants to stop walking downstairs.

For most Flatlands owners, a buzzer is not about compliance. It is about a tenant or a parent who should not have to take two flights of stairs to see who rang, and a front door that should not be left unlocked for a delivery.

"A video doorbell is a modern replacement for a buzzer"

Summaries often treat smart video doorbells and building intercoms as interchangeable.

A video doorbell shows a visitor and lets one household talk to them. It does not release the door, and on a two-family it cannot give each household its own button without a second doorbell. The household that answers still has to go down and open the door, which is exactly what a buzzer exists to avoid.

A video doorbell can be part of the answer, for the owner's own door or alongside a buzzer, but on its own it is a camera with a chime. A real buzzer needs a release in the frame and a station on each floor.

"Old buzzer wiring has to be ripped out"

Many summaries treat any decades-old wire as a reason to start over.

Aiphone designs its GT Series for "new builds and retrofit projects," with the advantage of "using existing wire and infrastructure," and its upgrade guidance says sound wires can be reused. In a 1950s apartment building whose riser carries pairs to every apartment, reusing sound pairs saves a lot of opening up of walls and hallways.

The answer is to test rather than assume. We test each pair; sound pairs are reused, failed pairs are named on the price with their cost, and the owner decides with full information.

"A magnetic lock is the easy fix for a door that won't latch"

Magnetic locks hold a door without a latch, so they look like a shortcut for a sagging front door. Allegion notes they "are only available fail safe," meaning they release when power is lost.

That makes them harder, not easier, to install correctly. The building code calls for an egress-side sensor, a push-to-exit device, release on power loss and fire alarm activation, and listed hardware. On a two-family's front door with no fire alarm system, a magnetic lock can leave the door unlocked exactly during an outage, when the house is most exposed.

Fixing the door so it latches, and fitting a fail-secure strike, is usually cheaper and more secure. We reserve magnetic locks for doors and gates where a strike truly cannot be fitted, and install them with every required part.

"Wireless kits are the easy answer for a house"

HomeAdvisor's intercom guide says wireless systems are the best option for people who want to do it themselves, and wireless buzzer kits are sold as needing no wiring.

A wireless kit removes the cable between the door station and the indoor units, but not the release. The door still needs a strike in the frame, power to that strike and a closer that shuts the door, none of which comes in the box. And radio signal through a brick party wall, a steel-framed stair or a basement ceiling can be weak, which shows up as missed calls.

Where a cable route truly cannot be found, a wireless link between the door and the indoor stations can be the right choice, but it is tested at the actual locations before anyone relies on it, and the release side is wired properly.
