The Neighborhood

Why Rockland County Properties Need Cat6 Repair Built Around Retrofit Cable

Rockland grew fast, and it grew before anyone wired houses for data. The Historical Society of Rockland County records that homes were going up at a rate of more than a thousand a year in the 1950s, driven by the Tappan Zee Bridge opening in 1955 and the completion of the Palisades Interstate Parkway and the Thruway in the same decade. The county’s population went from 89,276 in 1950 to 229,903 in 1970. That boom produced the streets of raised ranches, split-levels, colonials and capes that still make up most of Clarkstown, Ramapo and Orangetown, and a generation of low-rise offices, strip plazas and medical buildings along Route 59, Route 304 and Route 303. None of it was built with Cat6 in the walls. Every run of Cat6 you find in those buildings today was added afterwards.

That one fact explains most of the Cat6 repairs we do in the county. Cable that goes in during construction is pulled through open framing, terminated on a bench-like workflow and tested before the drywall closes. Cable added to a finished building is fished, stapled, tucked and squeezed through whatever path is available: down an exterior wall cavity, along a garage ceiling, through the heating chase of a split-level, behind baseboard, above a dropped ceiling that was later replaced with drywall. Each of those paths has a weak point — a staple driven too hard, a sharp bend at a top plate, a jacket nicked on a nail, a run spliced with a coupler when it came up short. The link works for months or years, then a renovation, a move of furniture, a new router location or a hot summer finds the weak point.

The second pressure is the network itself. Across Rockland, internet service has been moving to fiber; Optimum’s own installation guidance tells customers on its faster plans to use “Ethernet CAT 6 or higher” and a switch with a 10-gigabit port, and a comparison site claims Optimum fiber now reaches 91.5 percent of Rockland homes. When a household or a small office upgrades to a multi-gigabit plan, every Cat6 run in the building is asked to carry more than it ever has. Links that negotiated a gigabit without complaint suddenly refuse 2.5 or 5 gigabits, or drop back to 100 megabits under load. The plan is not at fault, and usually neither is the cable type — it is a bad termination, an untwisted pair or a cheap coupler that a gigabit link tolerated.

The third is water. Rockland is not on the coast, but it floods. On July 9, 2023 a storm dumped more than eight inches of rain on the county, according to the Rockland County Times, and flash flooding damaged homes, closed Route 9W and much of the road network in Stony Point; Rockland was one of the counties named in the federal disaster declaration that followed. In Spring Valley, a 52-unit complex at 9 Park Avenue that had flooded repeatedly, including a major flood in 2019, was ordered vacated, repaired or demolished in May 2026, ABC7 reported, with its basement apartments already off-limits. Lower levels in the county’s split-levels and raised ranches are exactly where the router, the switch and the cable bundle usually live. When water reaches them, the terminations corrode first.

The last is turnover. Rockland property changes hands and changes use constantly — houses sold to new families who finish the basement, offices in Pearl River and Orangeburg re-tenanted, plazas on Route 59 sold and re-leased, the Palisades Center itself sold at a foreclosure auction in February 2026. Each change adds another layer of cable from another installer, rarely with labels. So a Rockland Cat6 repair begins with two questions most repair calls skip: who installed this run, and what has been done to the building since?

Building Specialists

Cat6 Repair for Rockland County’s Architecture

In Rockland the house or office type predicts where a retrofit run was squeezed through, and so where it is most likely to fail. These are the building types behind most of our county calls, and what each one tends to do to Cat6.

Raised Ranches

Entry on a landing between two floors, living space above, a den, laundry and garage below. Cat6 usually climbs from the lower level through a closet, a heating chase or the garage ceiling, and the tight turns and staples along that climb are where we find damage.

Split-Levels

Three or four half-floors stacked around short stairs. Runs between levels cross the joint where one floor system meets the next, often through a narrow gap above a foundation wall, and finished family rooms hide the path.

Colonials and Capes

Two full floors or a floor and a dormered attic. Cat6 is commonly fished down exterior walls or run through knee-wall attics, where insulation work, summer heat and later dormer additions stress the cable.

River-Village Houses

Victorian and older houses in Nyack, Piermont and Haverstraw, many in the 11.5 percent of county homes built before 1940. Plaster, balloon framing and surface raceway put the weak points at plates, staples and baseboard runs.

Two-Family and Small Multifamily

Houses split into units in Spring Valley, Haverstraw, Monsey and the village centres. Shared basements, a single entry for both units’ internet service and runs that cross from one unit’s space into the other’s complicate every repair.

1960s–1980s Office Buildings

Low-rise suites in Pearl River, Orangeburg, Nanuet and Suffern, re-tenanted many times. Expect three generations of cable above the grid, panels with nobody’s labels and Cat6 jacks on Cat5e cable that was never replaced.

Medical and Dental Practices

Converted houses and small office buildings along Route 59, Main Street in New City and around the hospital campuses. Exam-room jacks behind cabinetry, carts that hit floor-level plates, and patient hours that limit when work can happen.

Strip Plazas and Shopping Centres

Single-storey retail along Route 59 and Route 303 and around the Palisades Center and Nanuet Town Centre. Point-of-sale and camera runs pass through stockrooms and over neighbouring units, and tenant turnover leaves cable of unknown origin.

What we check on every Rockland retrofit run

Who installed it, and how? Cable put in by an internet provider’s technician, a previous owner or a renovation crew tends to have a recognisable signature: field-crimped plugs instead of jacks, a run coming out of the wall through a grommet, colour-coded but unlabelled cable, a cheap keystone that takes a punch tool badly. We look for those signatures first, because they tell us what else on the property was probably done the same way.

Is there a joint we cannot see? When a retrofit run came up short, the usual fix was a coupler. When a basement was finished after the cable was in, the usual fix for a run in the way was to cut it and join it. A certification tester shows a joint as a reflection at a measured distance, so we can tell you whether there is a hidden connection and roughly where it sits before anything is opened. Joints inside closed walls and ceilings are the most common single cause of Rockland multi-gig failures we see.

Has the run been stapled, nailed or crushed? Cable stapled tightly to joists and studs is deformed at every staple. One or two hard staples rarely kill a link; a dozen along a basement ceiling can push return loss and crosstalk past the Cat6 limit. Trim carpenters and picture hangers add nails. Furniture and doors crush runs that were laid on floors or under thresholds. The tester’s distance reading tells us which of those to look for.

Has water reached it? On lower levels we check the bottom of the wall for tide marks, open the lowest jacks and the panel, and look for green corrosion on contacts. Water that has reached the inside of a jacket travels along it, so the reading on the tester matters more than how dry the outside looks.

Is the cable really solid-copper Cat6? At any cut we look at the conductors. Silver showing through the copper colour means copper-clad aluminium, which cannot certify and runs warm under PoE. We also check printed jacket markings, because some retrofit runs are Cat5e with Cat6 jacks on the ends, which explains a lot of disappointing speed tests.

Homeowners who are already opening walls often ask us to look at the rest of the low-voltage wiring. Our low-voltage installation service in Rockland County handles the doorbell, intercom and control wiring that tends to share the same paths.

Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in Rockland County: Questions Answered

These are the questions Rockland homeowners, practice managers, landlords and the IT people who look after them ask once a Cat6 link starts misbehaving. They are grouped by what usually set the problem off: an internet upgrade, the layout of the county’s houses, water on the lower level, the way businesses here change hands and use, and the paperwork side of a repair. Shorter practical questions sit in the FAQ further down. If your question is not here, call the Rockland line and ask; the answer usually takes a couple of minutes and costs nothing, and it often tells you whether a visit is needed at all or whether a new cord or a different router port will do.

After a fiber or multi-gig internet upgrade

We moved to a multi-gig fiber plan in Rockland, but the wired office still tops out around one gigabit — is the Cat6 in the wall the limit?

Usually not the cable type, often the way it was finished. Cat6 is rated to carry 2.5 and 5 gigabits over the full 100-metre channel under the IEEE 802.3bz standard, and 10 gigabits over shorter runs, so a typical house or office run is long enough. What stops a link from stepping up is everything around the cable: a router or switch port that only does one gigabit, a computer network adapter that only does one gigabit, a patch cord marked Cat5 or with no marking at all, a jack punched down with an inch of untwist, or a coupler joining two lengths inside the wall. A gigabit link tolerates a surprising amount of that. A multi-gig link negotiates down or drops.

We sort it in order. First we confirm that both ends of the link — the port on the router or switch and the adapter on the computer — actually support the higher speed, because there is no point repairing a cable for a port that cannot use it. Then we test the run itself with a link tester that shows what speed it will negotiate and a certification tester that shows whether it meets the Cat6 limits, with any failure placed at a distance. Most fixes are a re-terminated jack, a new cord or the removal of a hidden coupler. Only when the run itself fails — it is Cat5e under Cat6 jacks, it is copper-clad aluminium, or it has been crushed — does it need replacing.

The internet installer put the new box in a different room, and two wall jacks stopped working that same day — what happened to them?

Almost always nothing happened to the cable. In most Rockland houses with retrofit Cat6, all the runs come back to one spot — a closet, the lower-level utility room, the back of a desk — where the old modem and router sat, sometimes with a small switch. When a fiber installation moves the internet equipment to another room, those home runs are still sitting at the old spot, now connected to nothing, or connected to a switch that no longer has an uplink. The jacks look dead because nothing is feeding them.

The repair is to bring the new internet connection to where the cable gathers, or to bring the cable to where the internet now arrives. Often there is an existing spare run between the two rooms that can carry the uplink; we find it with a tone generator and test it before using it. Where there is none, we either run one new Cat6 link between the two locations or recommend moving the router back to the cable hub, whichever leaves fewer cords trailing across the house. Ask your provider to note where their equipment can and cannot go before they arrive, and send us a photo of the old setup; it makes the fix quick.

The fiber crew drilled a new entry hole and now one basement run tests short — could the drill have caught it?

It is possible, and it is easy to check. A run that suddenly reads shorter than it should on a tester, or shows an open or shorted pair at a particular distance, has a fault at that distance. If that distance lines up with the new hole — the length of cable from the jack to the spot where the drill went through the rim joist or the foundation wall — the drill is the likely cause. Bits that go through a wall cavity or along a joist bay can nick or sever a cable lying against the far side.

The repair depends on what the bit did. A nicked jacket with intact pairs may still pass, though we usually recommend repairing it because moisture enters at the nick. A cut pair needs either a rated splice at an accessible spot or a replacement of the run from that point. We record the reading, photograph the damage and give you a short note you can pass to the provider if you want to raise it with them.

Raised ranches, split-levels and finished lower levels

The Cat6 from our raised ranch’s lower-level den to the bedrooms upstairs failed after we finished the basement ceiling — can it be reached without tearing out the new drywall?

In most cases yes, or with one small, patchable opening rather than a strip of ceiling. The first step is a tester reading from both ends of the run, which gives the distance to the fault. If the fault sits at a jack or a plate, nothing has to be opened. If it sits in the ceiling, the distance and the route — usually along the joists from the utility room toward the stairwell, then up through a wall — narrow it to a short section. Finishing work is a common cause: a drywall screw or a ceiling-light box driven into a run that used to hang free.

If the damage is at a reachable spot, we make one neat access hole, repair the cable with a rated connection or cut in a short replacement section, and leave the opening ready for a patch or covered by a blank plate or an access panel if you would rather keep the spot reachable. If the run is damaged in several places, or was pinched along the length of a soffit, it is often cleaner to abandon it and pull a new run along a different route — through a closet stack, along the garage, or up the exterior wall cavity — than to chase each fault.

Why do the jacks on the half-level above the garage in our split-level fail more than any others in the house?

Because that is usually the hardest-working stretch of cable in a split-level. To get from the utility area to the rooms over the garage, a retrofit run typically crosses the garage ceiling, which is unheated or barely heated, stapled to the joists or the underside of the subfloor, and close to a garage door opener, lights and storage hooks. The cable sees cold in winter and heat in summer, and the people storing things in the garage rafters do not know it is there. The joint where the garage ceiling meets the house floor system is also where installers make their tightest bends.

We test those runs first and look for the signature of the cause: damage spread along the run suggests staples or crushing, a single fault at one distance suggests a nail, hook or bend. The lasting repair usually moves the cable off the garage ceiling surface and into a protected path — a sleeve, a raceway or a route inside the conditioned part of the house — and, where it has to pass through the wall between the garage and the living space, restores the fire seal around it.

A previous owner left a plastic junction box in our New City basement ceiling with several Cat6 runs joined inside it — can it stay?

It can stay if it holds category, but it rarely does. Those boxes are usually where a past owner joined runs that came up short or rerouted cable when finishing the basement, using couplers, punch-down blocks for telephone wire, or twisted-and-taped joints. A Cat6 link can include a properly rated connection, but each one adds loss and reflection, and the cheap couplers and telephone blocks we usually find in those boxes are not Cat6 parts at all. The links through them often still pass data at low speed and fail when asked for a gigabit or more.

We test every run that goes through the box. Those that pass with margin can stay as they are, with the box labelled and left accessible. Those that fail are either remade inside the box with a proper Cat6 connection — acceptable on a short-term basis and only where the box remains accessible — or replaced with a continuous run from the jack to the network location, which is the repair we recommend whenever the route allows. Joints should never end up buried behind finished drywall where nobody can reach them again.

Our mesh Wi-Fi points are plugged into Cat6 jacks but still show a wireless link back to the router — is the wiring to blame?

Sometimes, and it is worth ruling out. Mesh systems that support wired backhaul fall back to a wireless connection between nodes if the Ethernet link is missing, unstable or too slow, and the app may only show that the node is “connected”. In split-levels and raised ranches the jacks used for mesh nodes are often the oldest retrofit runs in the house, and a run with a bad pair or a hidden joint can negotiate at 100 megabits or flap on and off, which the mesh treats as unusable.

We check each node’s jack with a link tester to see what speed it actually negotiates and whether it stays up, then certify the run if it looks marginal. A failed termination or cord is fixed on the spot. If the runs are healthy, the problem lies in how the mesh is set up — for example, nodes connected through a switch that does not pass their traffic properly — and we tell you that rather than replacing good cable.

Water on the lower level

Our router and switch sit on the same lower-level wall as the sump pump — where should the Cat6 terminations go so the next flood does not reach them?

Up, and away from the pump. In many Rockland raised ranches and split-levels the network gear ended up beside the sump because that is where the utility room is and where the cable entered the house. The terminations — the patch panel or the keystone bank, the switch and the router — are the parts that corrode first when water rises or when the pump area stays damp. Cable itself tolerates brief wetting better than contacts do.

The usual repair is to remount the patch point and the equipment on a board or a small wall cabinet at least a few feet above the floor, on the wall furthest from the pump, and to bring the runs up to it with a service loop rather than letting them sag to the floor. Where runs were cut short to reach a floor-level location, we extend them with a short continuous section from a new, raised termination point and test the result. Anything already showing corrosion is re-terminated or replaced, and the old floor-level hardware is removed.

The basement units in our Spring Valley building are shut because of flooding, but the Cat6 to the upper floors passes through them — can it be rerouted around?

Usually, yes. In older Spring Valley, Haverstraw and Monsey buildings, Cat6 for upper units and common areas often runs from a telecom point in the cellar up through basement apartments or storage spaces. When those spaces flood or are closed, the runs inside them become both unreliable and inaccessible. Whether to repair them in place depends on whether anyone may enter the closed space and whether the cable there has been wet.

Where the closed space cannot be used, we look for a path that avoids it — a stair hall, a hallway ceiling on the first floor, an exterior chase, or a short new riser from a relocated termination point — and pull new Cat6 along it, testing each link before the old cable is abandoned and then removed where accessible. The owner or managing agent approves the route first, and any work in common areas follows the building’s rules. Tenants keep their service while the new path is built; the switch-over takes minutes per unit.

How Rockland businesses use and change their space

Exam-room jacks in our New City practice keep failing after the cleaning crew moves the carts — is there a repair that stops it?

Yes. Medical and dental rooms put network jacks at floor level behind or beside cabinetry, and mobile carts, chairs and cleaning equipment hit them every day. The repeated knock loosens the keystone in the plate, cracks the jack housing, or pulls the patch cord sideways until the plug latch breaks or the contacts in the jack spread. The run behind the wall is usually fine; the failure is at the front of the jack.

We re-terminate or replace the damaged jacks, swap in patch cords with protected latches, and change how the plate meets the room: angled plates that keep cords flush, plates moved up to counter height where cabinetry allows, or a short surface raceway so the cord leaves the wall where carts do not reach. Repaired links are tested and labelled so your front desk can tell the cleaning crew which plates to avoid. We can schedule the work before patients arrive or after the last appointment.

We added a PoE camera to the small switch in our Clarkstown home office and now the desk connection drops — can PoE on one port affect another port’s Cat6?

Not through the cable, but through the switch. A PoE camera draws power from the switch, and small desktop switches have a limited power budget and sometimes a modest power supply. When a camera, an access point or a video doorbell starts drawing near that limit — at night when infrared lights come on, or in cold weather when a heater in the housing switches on — some switches restart or briefly reset their ports, and every connection on the switch drops for a moment. That looks like a cable fault at the desk.

We check the switch’s power budget against what is connected, test the camera’s run for a fault that might make it draw abnormally, and test the desk’s run so you know it is clean. The fix is usually a switch or injector with enough power for the devices, or moving the camera to a separate PoE source. If the camera’s own run is damaged, which can cause repeated power negotiation, it is repaired at the same visit.

Our Suffern office’s Cat6 was put in by the internet provider when we opened — when it fails, is the provider responsible or are we?

It depends on where the provider’s responsibility ends, and that point is usually earlier than people expect. Providers generally look after the line into the building and their own equipment — the fiber terminal, the gateway or the modem — and sometimes the first cable to it. Cable they ran beyond that point as a courtesy or for a fee during the original install is often treated as inside wiring, which belongs to the property owner or tenant. Your service agreement or the provider’s support line will say where the line is drawn.

Practically, we start by testing. If the fault is in the provider’s equipment or the line to it, you have a clear reading to show them. If it is in the inside wiring, we repair it. Either way, the result is documented so there is no argument about who should fix what next time. If you rent, your lease may say whether the landlord or the tenant owns the cabling, which is worth checking before the repair is booked.

Why the certification tester is the most important tool on a Rockland call. A basic continuity tester shows that each of the eight conductors reaches the right pin, and nothing else. It cannot see a staple-flattened section, a hidden coupler, an inch of untwist at a jack or a corroded contact that still conducts. Those are exactly the faults retrofit Cat6 accumulates, and they are what stops a run from carrying a gigabit or more. A repair that ends with a row of green lights on a continuity tester has not been checked against the standard the cable was bought for.

What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in Rockland County

How this section was written: Google’s AI Overview could not be opened from the tools used to research this page, so none of the headings below is quoted from it. Each is a claim that retailers, internet providers’ help pages, forums and cost guides commonly publish, and that answer engines tend to repeat. Under each is what we actually find in Rockland houses and offices, with the source for any figure.

“Faster internet plans need Cat6A or better in the walls”

When a multi-gig plan arrives, the advice that follows is often to rewire with a higher category. Provider pages add to the impression: Optimum’s fiber installation guidance lists “Ethernet CAT 6 or higher” among the things customers need for its fastest speeds.

Cat6 is “or higher” already for most homes. The IEEE 802.3bz standard runs 2.5 and 5 gigabit Ethernet over Cat5e or Cat6 up to 100 metres, and 10 gigabits over Cat6 is supported on shorter runs. A Rockland house run is rarely long enough for distance to matter. What blocks the speed is a weak jack, a cheap cord or a hidden coupler.

So before anyone quotes you a rewire, test the run. If it certifies, a better cord or a re-terminated jack is the whole job. If it fails, the reading says where, and replacing one run costs far less than replacing them all.

“If the internet company installed the cable, they will fix it”

Many people assume a cable run by the provider’s technician stays the provider’s problem, and online answers often point owners back to their provider for anything to do with the connection.

Providers generally maintain their line and their own equipment. Runs added inside the building, even by their technician, are commonly treated as inside wiring that belongs to the owner. In Rockland, where so much Cat6 was added during an internet install years ago, owners are often surprised to find the provider will not touch it.

A test settles it either way. A clean inside run with a dead gateway goes back to the provider with a reading in hand; a damaged inside run gets repaired the same day without waiting for a provider appointment.

“Cable behind a finished ceiling can’t be repaired, only replaced”

General advice for a dead in-wall Ethernet run usually jumps to running a new one, or to giving up on wired and buying a mesh system, on the assumption that a fault behind drywall cannot be reached.

A tester’s distance reading turns “somewhere in the ceiling” into a spot. In the raised ranches and split-levels that dominate Clarkstown and Ramapo, many lower-level faults are a drywall screw, a light box or a coupler at one point along an otherwise healthy run.

One small opening, one repair, one patch is often all it takes. Replacement is the right answer when the run is damaged in several places or is the wrong cable, and the test tells you which case you are in.

“Rockland doesn’t flood, so basement cabling is safe”

Flood risk in the New York area tends to be discussed as a coastal and river-front problem, and an inland suburban county can sound safe by comparison.

The record says otherwise. The July 9, 2023 storm dropped more than eight inches of rain and caused flash-flood damage to homes across northern Rockland, according to the Rockland County Times, and Rockland was among the counties in disaster declaration DR-4723. In Spring Valley, a complex that flooded repeatedly had its basement apartments closed and was ordered vacated, repaired or demolished in May 2026, ABC7 reported.

Lower levels are where Rockland houses keep their network gear. Raising the termination point above the floor costs far less than replacing corroded hardware after the next storm.

“Lightning is the main threat to Ethernet in the Hudson Valley”

Advice for the region often leads with surge protection, and it is a real issue for long runs between buildings in the northern counties.

Rockland had the fewest lightning pulses of the six Hudson Valley counties in Earth Networks’ 2020 New York report — 64,575 against Orange County’s 275,059 — and it is a county of compact suburban lots more than outbuildings and long runs between them.

Surges still happen, and runs to a detached garage or a pole camera deserve protection. But in our Rockland work the larger share of faults comes from inside the building: terminations, renovations, staples, water and moved equipment.

“A Cat6 repair should cost about what a new drop costs”

Cost guides publish per-drop prices for new Cat6 — Fixr’s January 2025 guide puts a drop at $125 to $250 including materials and labour — and those numbers get applied to repair calls.

A new drop in open framing is a known quantity. A repair in a finished Rockland house is diagnosis first: which run, which fault, where along it, and whether the run is worth saving. The time goes into testing and finding, then into a small, careful fix.

That is why we price it as published time on site — $195 an hour with a three-hour minimum — plus replacement runs from $175 each only when a run cannot be saved, the figures on our Rockland repair page.

“Any handyman can fix Ethernet now that the county licenses contractors”

Rockland’s home improvement licensing has been in the local news through 2026, and it is easy to read a county licence as a sign that anyone holding one can do any work in a house.

Chapter 286 regulates the business side of home improvement — licensing, written contracts, change orders, cancellation rights. It does not teach anyone how to terminate Cat6 to category or read a certification test, and it excludes electrical work altogether.

Ask whoever repairs your cable what they will test the link with and what result you will receive. A licensed low-voltage contractor should be able to show you a saved Cat6 test, not a row of lights.

How we approach a retrofit run in a finished house — general practice, not a single job

We start where the cable gathers. Can we send photos of the jack, the network closet and the router by WhatsApp before you come out to Rockland?
+

Your Rockland repair is still booked and quoted through the direct line or the form.

Do you need our Wi-Fi password or internet account details to repair the Cat6?
+

No. Testing and repairing cable does not involve logging in to your router or your provider account. Our testers connect to the jack and the far end of the run directly. The only time access to the router helps is when we need to see which port a run lands on or what speed the router reports, and then you or your IT provider can show us the screen. We never change network settings without being asked.

Can the old cable be used to pull its replacement through the wall of our split-level?
+

Often, yes. If the old run is not stapled, glued or tied along its path, we can fasten the new Cat6 to it and pull the new cable through the same route as the old one comes out. It is the least disruptive way to replace a run in a finished house. If the old cable is stapled to framing or has been crushed into a tight spot, it will not slide, and we fall back to fishing a new route; we test the old run's path before deciding.

If a run is being replaced anyway, can a second Cat6 be pulled alongside it for later use?
+

Yes, and it is usually worth doing. Once a path has been opened or a pull is under way, a second cable adds little time and gives you a spare for a future camera, access point or a failed run. The spare is terminated and tested like any other link, or left coiled and labelled at both ends if you prefer. The extra drop is priced at the published replacement-drop figure and shown on the written quote.

Will the rest of our home network stay up while one Rockland run is being repaired?
+

Almost always. We work on one link at a time, so only the jack being repaired is out of service while its termination is remade or its cable is replaced. If the repair is at the patch panel or switch end, a few seconds of disconnection may be needed when a cord is moved, and we warn you first so a video call or an alarm communicator is not interrupted at a bad moment.

Is there a charge for a second trip if the repair needs a part that was not on the van?
+

Second trips are rare because we ask about the property and the fault before booking and carry the common parts for Rockland retrofit repairs. If a matching plate, an unusual module or a long cable route needs something we do not carry, we tell you on the first visit, and any return time is billed at the same published hourly rate, shown on the written quote before it is booked.

Can you repair the Ethernet run to a TV or games console behind a wall-mounted TV?

Yes. Wall-mounted TVs in Rockland living rooms often hide a Cat6 jack behind the screen, and the cord and jack there get bent hard when the TV is mounted tight to the wall.
