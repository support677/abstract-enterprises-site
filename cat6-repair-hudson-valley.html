The Neighborhood

Why Hudson Valley Properties Need Cat6 Repair That Accounts for Weather, Distance and Age

Inside a city building, Cat6 lives in a climate-controlled box. Across Westchester, Rockland, Orange, Putnam, Dutchess and Ulster, a lot of it does not. It runs from the main house to the detached garage, from the office to the pole camera at the edge of the lot, from the farmhouse to the barn, from the school’s main building to the portable classroom, from a distribution warehouse’s office to the guard shack at the truck gate. Every one of those runs crosses a boundary that indoor cable was never designed for — a change of temperature, a change of building, a change of electrical ground, or a stretch of open air and soil. That is where the region’s Cat6 faults concentrate, and it is why the repair has to start by asking what the cable has been through, not only what the tester says.

Weather is the first reason. The Hudson Valley gets real thunderstorm seasons, and a strike nearby does not have to hit a building to wreck its network. Voltage induced on a long copper run between two structures arrives at the switch as a surge, and the casualties are usually the switch ports, the PoE camera at the far end, and the terminations in between. Winter brings the opposite problem: unheated outbuildings, crawlspaces and garages cycle through freezing and thawing, buried conduit fills with water that freezes, and cable that was fine in October develops a fault by February. Summer attics in older farmhouses and the high ceilings of steel warehouses push the other way, raising cable temperature and with it the loss on long runs.

Distance is the second. Valley sites spread out. A Cat6 link that runs the full length of a warehouse, or up a hill to a well house, is often close to the 100-metre channel limit before anything goes wrong, so a small amount of damage or heat is enough to tip it into failure. The same distances shape the repair itself: a technician who drives an hour to a site in northern Dutchess or western Ulster should not be driving back for a part. Planning what to bring from a phone description and a few photos is part of doing Cat6 repair properly in this region.

Age is the third. Much of the region’s commercial plant was installed in waves: the corporate campuses and office parks along I-287 and Route 9 from the 1970s and 1980s, re-cabled with Cat5e and then Cat6 as tenants came and went; the former IBM sites in Kingston and Poughkeepsie; the brick and stone buildings of Kingston’s Stockade and Newburgh’s East End, now offices, practices and apartments; and, with construction starting in 2020 and continuing since, the large distribution buildings along I-84 and Route 17K. Each wave left its own kind of Cat6 — good cable badly terminated, cable that was never genuine Cat6, runs extended with couplers, and closets nobody has labelled since the last tenant left.

Finally, animals. Mice, squirrels and other rodents get into barns, sheds, pool houses, attics and drop ceilings across the region, and cable jackets are easy chewing. Damage is often hidden, partial and spread along a run, which makes it a poor fit for guesswork and a good fit for fault location by distance. Put those five pressures together and you get the valley’s version of a Cat6 repair: test it, locate it, fix it for the conditions it actually lives in, protect the parts that leave the building, and leave a record you can compare against after the next storm.

Material and method checks we make on every valley repair

Is the cable rated for where it actually runs? Indoor communications cable carries markings like CM, CMR or CMP for indoor spaces. Outdoor and buried runs need cable made for sunlight and water. One of the most common causes of a slow Cat6 decline in the region is indoor cable run along an exterior wall, across a roof or through buried pipe, where the jacket cracks in sunlight and water reaches the pairs. If the cable is wrong for the location, re-terminating it buys months, not years.

Where does the run cross between buildings or electrical services? When a copper run connects two structures with separate grounding, any difference in ground potential and any induced storm surge travel along it. We look at how that run enters each building, whether there is protection and bonding at the entry, and whether a link of that length and exposure should be fiber instead. Our fiber optic installation service in the Hudson Valley handles the cases where it should.

What temperature does the run see? Insertion loss rises with heat, so a long Cat6 run in a summer attic, under a warehouse roof or bundled tightly with other PoE cables can fail a test it passed when it was installed. In winter, cable in unheated spaces stiffens and the terminations of flexed runs crack. We record where the run goes so the repair matches the conditions.

Is it genuine solid-copper Cat6? We check the conductors at the cut. Silver at the core means copper-clad aluminium, which cannot certify and fares badly with PoE and in outdoor runs. We also check for stranded patch cable used as a permanent run, and for couplers hidden above tiles or inside boxes.

What is chewing on it, and where? When the fault-location reading shows damage spread along a run rather than at one point, rodents are a leading suspect. We trace the run, check where it lies on beams or insulation, and plan the repair so the new length is in conduit or off the surfaces animals travel along.

Compliance callouts for business Cat6 repairs in the valley

  • Protection at building entry. The National Electrical Code requires listed primary protection for communications circuits exposed to lightning or power contact where they enter a building, with grounding near the entrance (Article 800, as summarised by EC&M). Cat6 between buildings is exactly the kind of run that exposure rule was written for.
  • Abandoned cable. The accessible portions of abandoned communications cable must be removed unless tagged for future use (section 800.25 in the edition EC&M discussed). Replacing a run means taking the old one out where it can be reached.
  • County and municipal licensing. Westchester requires home improvement contractors to hold a county licence through its Department of Consumer Protection, and Rockland licenses trades under its own county code, including Chapter 250 for electricians and electrical inspections. Permits, where needed, come from the local building department, so requirements change from town to town. AESS works under NYS licence #12000287431.
  • Digging. Any repair that means excavating a buried run starts with the free one-call notice to have utility lines marked, given at least two and no more than ten working days ahead, as Central Hudson’s Dig Safely guidance sets out.
  • Watershed land. Parts of Westchester, Putnam, Orange and Ulster lie in New York City’s reservoir watersheds, where DEP regulations govern land disturbance and stormwater. A short trench repair is rarely affected, but a long new route across open ground deserves a check first.

Your Questions Answered

Cat6 Repair in the Hudson Valley: Questions Answered

These are the questions we hear from facility managers, farm and estate owners, school and municipal IT staff, office-park tenants and homeowners once a Cat6 link has started misbehaving north of the city. They are grouped by what usually caused the problem: storms and power, cold and heat, animals and outbuildings, outdoor runs and PoE devices, the region’s older commercial buildings, and distance. Shorter practical questions are in the FAQ further down.

After a storm or a power event

Why did a storm kill the switch ports for the barn and the yard cameras but leave the office desk ports working?

When several ports fail together during a storm, the switch is the first suspect and the cable runs that leave the building are the second. A nearby strike induces voltage on long copper runs, especially ones that run outdoors, across a parking lot or to another structure. That energy travels to the switch, where the port electronics are the weakest point, and to the device at the other end. Ports that fed only short indoor runs to desks often survive while ports that fed the yard camera, the barn and the gate do not, which is a useful clue in itself.

We sort it out in a set order. First, the dead ports are tested with a known-good cable and device, which tells you whether the switch hardware survived. Then each affected run is tested on its own against its category limit, looking for burned or open conductors, damaged terminations and protectors that sacrificed themselves. Then the far-end device is checked. Often the answer is a mix: two switch ports gone, one camera gone, and every cable still passing. Sometimes a termination or protector has failed open and needs replacing. Either way, you replace only what the testing shows is damaged.

Which Cat6 runs should be tested after a strike near the property, even if they still seem to work?

By testing every run that could have been exposed, not just the ones that obviously stopped. A surge can leave a link working but weakened: one pair partly damaged, a keystone contact pitted, a protector degraded. Those links may light up and pass traffic, then fail weeks later or run at reduced speed. The runs most at risk are the ones that leave the building, the longest ones, those that share a pathway with power or with a downspout or gutter, and those that feed outdoor PoE devices.

We make a short list with you from the site layout, then certify each run on it and compare the results with any earlier test reports you have. Margins that have collapsed since the last test, resistance imbalance between pairs and failures on a single pair all point to surge damage. Links that pass with healthy margins are left alone and documented. That record also helps if you are making an insurance claim, because it separates storm damage from cabling that was already failing.

The power came back after an outage, but some Cat6 links never did — what failed?

Usually not the cable itself, but something the cable connects. Outages in the valley can be long, and the moment power returns is hard on equipment: surges on restoration, devices restarting in the wrong order, and PoE switches trying to power every port at once. A switch that comes back with a reduced power budget, or ports that failed during the restoration surge, can leave cameras and access points offline while the cabling is perfectly fine. A camera that needs more power than its port now offers will simply stay dark.

The cabling does fail in some cases, and the pattern is recognisable. Runs that were already marginal — a loose termination, a pair with a nick in it, an outdoor plug with a little water in it — can fail completely when equipment powers up and draws current through them again. So we start at the switch: port status, PoE allocation, any logged faults. Then we test the runs feeding the devices that stayed down. That order keeps you from paying to replace cable when a switch reset, a port move or a power budget change is the real fix.

Cold, heat and the seasons

Why does a Cat6 link to an unheated garage or barn only fail in the coldest weeks of winter?

Because something in it moves when it gets cold. Cable and connectors contract in freezing temperatures, and a termination that was barely seated, a conductor nicked during installation, or an outdoor plug with a hairline crack can open up just enough to drop the link when the building drops below freezing. The fault comes back when the space warms up, so by the time someone looks in spring, everything works again. Water in an outdoor connector or an entry box makes this much worse, because it freezes and pushes the contacts apart.

The most reliable way to fix it is to test the link while it is cold, or to find the weak point regardless of temperature. A certification test usually shows a marginal termination even on a mild day, as a poor return-loss or crosstalk reading at a particular end or distance. We repair that point, replace outdoor plugs with connectors and boxes suited to cold and damp, add a drip loop where cable enters a wall, and make sure any cable in the unheated space is rated for the conditions. If the run was indoor cable stapled to an outside wall, replacing that section is usually the honest answer.

Our outdoor Cat6 runs through buried conduit — can ice in the pipe damage it?

Yes. Buried conduit very often fills with water, through joints, condensation or an open end, and if any of it sits above the frost line, that water can freeze around the cable. Electricians discussing exactly this on Mike Holt’s forum describe the danger plainly: freezing water crushes the conductors, damaging the insulation or jacket, and conductor failure follows. Cat6 depends on the precise geometry of its pairs, so pressure that would not bother a power cable can be enough to fail a crosstalk or return-loss test.

The repair depends on what the testing shows. If the fault sits at one distance, typically where the conduit runs shallowest or where it rises to the building, that is where the damage is. Indoor-rated cable in a wet conduit is not a repair candidate; it gets replaced with cable made for wet locations, and the conduit is drained, sealed at the ends or re-routed where it keeps filling. If the run is long, between buildings with separate power, or keeps failing, we discuss fiber in the same conduit as the longer-lasting fix.

A Cat6 run crosses from the original school building into its addition and keeps failing at the joint — why there?

Because that is where the building moves. Schools, libraries and town halls across the valley have often grown in stages, with a wing added in one decade and a gym or classroom block in another. Where an addition meets the original structure there is usually an expansion joint, a change in ceiling height and a fire-rated wall, and the cable that crosses it was frequently pulled tight, squeezed through a sleeve sized for the old telephone wiring, or tied hard to the structure on both sides. As the two parts of the building expand, contract and settle at slightly different rates, the stress concentrates on that one short stretch of cable.

The fault-location reading tends to give it away, putting the damage at the same distance on every run that crosses the joint. We open the ceiling there, look for stretching, kinks or crushing, and replace the damaged length with a little slack on each side so the joint can move without pulling on the pairs. Each side is supported independently, the penetration through the rated wall is firestopped again to its rating, and the repaired links are certified, with results in the format the district or municipality asks for. When several runs cross the same joint, we plan them as one repair rather than several visits.

Animals, outbuildings and outdoor runs

Mice got into the barn ceiling — how much of the chewed Cat6 has to be replaced?

Less than you might fear, if the damage can be located. Rodent damage is rarely one clean cut. It is usually a series of bites along a stretch of cable where it crosses a beam, a joist or insulation that animals travel along. Some bites only scuff the jacket; others reach the pairs. A cable with exposed but unbroken pairs may still pass a basic continuity test while failing its category, and it will get worse as moisture and dust reach the conductors.

We test the run and use the fault-location reading to find where along it the damage sits, then look at that stretch. If the damage is confined to a short, reachable section and the link’s job allows it, a rated in-line repair can bring it back to a passing test. If the bites are spread along the run, or the run feeds something important like a camera or a farm office, we replace that length or the whole run in conduit or on supports away from beams and insulation. Repairing chewed cable without changing where it lies just schedules the next repair.

Someone ran indoor Cat6 along the outside of the house to the shed — it still works, so does it need replacing?

Eventually, yes, and usually before it fails at the worst moment. Indoor communications cable is not made for sunlight or standing water. Over a few seasons the jacket on an exposed run hardens, cracks and lets water reach the pairs; stapled sections split where the staples pinch; and the plug at the shed end, often a field-made connector, corrodes. Before the link fails completely, it tends to slow down, drop out in wet weather and fail certification. A copper run between two structures with no protection at the entry also leaves both ends exposed to storm surges.

If the link still passes its category, there is no emergency, but it is worth planning a replacement on a better path: outdoor-rated or direct-burial cable, or conduit, a proper entry with a drip loop at each building, and protection where the run comes inside. If the shed or studio is far enough away, or has its own electrical service, fiber with media converters is often the more durable link. Either way, we test the existing run first, so you know how much life it has left.

Water got into the plug at a pole-mounted camera — can the run be saved, or does it have to come back to the building?

Often the run can be saved, and the repair is at the camera end. Field-made plugs at pole and wall cameras are a classic failure point outdoors: water gets in around the cable, wicks into the plug and corrodes the contacts. The camera starts rebooting in wet weather, then drops altogether. If the water stayed at the plug, cutting back past the corroded section and terminating with a connector designed for outdoor use, inside a weather-sealed box or boot, is enough to restore the link.

The question is how far the water went. Moisture can wick along the cable under the jacket for some distance, especially if the cable was cut open or the end was left pointing upward without a drip loop. We cut back, test, and keep cutting back only while the readings improve. If return loss or insertion loss still fails after the end has been remade, the moisture has travelled into the run and that length needs replacing. On a tall pole, the plan also covers how the new end is reached, whether by ladder, by lift, or with the help of the site’s own equipment.

Our Cat6 to cameras and access points in a high-bay warehouse keeps getting damaged — what is hitting it?

Usually the operation. In tall distribution buildings, Cat6 to cameras, scanners and access points runs high along racking, joists and structure, and the things that damage it are the things that work at height: lift baskets, forklift masts, pallets lifted too high, and contractors fitting lights or sprinklers. Cable laid across the top of racking or tied tightly to structure gets pinched, crushed and abraded. At the device end, plugs made in the field are often loaded by the weight of the cable hanging off them.

We locate each fault by distance so the lift only goes up where the damage is, repair the termination or replace the damaged length, and look at the pathway. Supporting cable on proper hangers clear of moving equipment, protecting vertical drops near aisles, and giving each device a short, supported service loop removes most of the cause. Where runs are close to the 100-metre limit because the building is so long, we may recommend a closer switch location rather than a repair that will always be marginal. Lift hire, where the site has none, is shown as its own line, as on our Orange County repair pricing.

The region’s older commercial buildings

Our office-park suite shares a telecom room with the tenant next door — can a Cat6 repair be done without touching their equipment?

Yes, if the work is planned with the landlord and the room is mapped first. Many of the region’s office parks put one telecom room on each floor or wing, shared by several tenants, with panels from different fit-outs side by side and patch cords that wander between racks. The risk is not your repair; it is someone pulling or re-punching a neighbour’s live link by mistake because the labels are wrong or missing.

We start by confirming with building management which panels and pathways belong to your suite. Then we trace your failing links with a tone and probe and by testing from your outlets, so we know exactly which ports are yours before anything is unplugged. Repairs happen only on those ports, and any cords that cross to another tenant’s equipment are left as found and noted for management. When we leave, your ports are labelled at both ends, so the next technician on either side of the room does not repeat the guesswork.

The failed Cat6 in our brick townhouse office runs along the baseboards and door casings — what is the least visible way to replace it?

Usually a different route rather than the same one. In older brick and stone buildings like those in Newburgh’s East End or Kingston’s Stockade, solid masonry walls leave nowhere to fish cable, so earlier installers ran it along baseboards, picture rails and door casings. Re-running a replacement along the same path keeps every visible length and every fixing in the woodwork. Before choosing, we look at what the building offers: a cellar or crawlspace under the room, a closet or stair void that runs between floors, an old chimney chase that is no longer used, or the back of built-in cabinetry.

Where one of those exists, the new run comes up through the floor at the desk or inside the closet, and the surface cable is removed. Where none does, a slim surface raceway painted to match, following the line of the trim, is far tidier than loose cable and protects the run from furniture and cleaning. In listed or historic interiors we agree the route and any fixings with you before work starts, and we avoid drilling original mouldings or masonry joints where an alternative exists. The failed run is tested first so you know the fault is not simply at a jack, which would make the whole question unnecessary.

How is a Cat6 run repaired where it passes through a thick stone wall and the old sleeve is packed full?

Carefully, because the wall is usually the hardest part of the run to replace. Stone and brick walls in the valley’s older buildings can be very thick, and the one sleeve or hole through them tends to carry everything: phone lines, coax, old Cat5e, and the Cat6 that has failed. When the sleeve is packed, pulling a new cable through it risks damaging the cables that still work, and drilling a new hole through historic masonry is a decision of its own.

First we confirm the fault is actually inside the wall. Often it is at one side or the other, where the cable bends sharply out of the sleeve, and that can be repaired without touching the section inside. If the damage really is within the wall, we look at what in the sleeve is abandoned: removing dead cable often frees enough space to pull a replacement. Only when there is no other path do we discuss a new penetration, its size and location, and how it will be sealed against water and pests afterwards.

AI Overview Reality Check

What AI Gets Wrong About Cat6 Repair in the Hudson Valley

A note on method: Google’s AI Overview could not be viewed from the tools used to research this page, so nothing below is presented as its exact wording. Each heading is a claim that general how-to guides, forums, retailers and cost sites commonly publish and that answer engines tend to repeat. Under each is what changes when the Cat6 is in a valley barn, warehouse, office park or stone building.

“Ethernet is isolated, so lightning can’t travel along it”

Forum threads on linking outbuildings often include someone saying that Ethernet’s isolation transformers make lightning a non-issue for a copper run between buildings. A thread on the MikroTik forum captures both sides, including one participant calling the lightning concern a myth.

Another participant in the same thread makes the point that matters: Ethernet’s isolation is functional, not a safety rating, and each building has its own grounding electrode. A long run between two structures connects two different ground references and acts as an antenna for induced surges. In a county that, by Earth Networks’ count, sees hundreds of thousands of lightning pulses a year, the damaged switch ports we test after storms are not a myth.

That is why a valley Cat6 repair on an outdoor run includes asking what protects each end. A protector bonded at the entry, or fiber for the exposed stretch, is part of fixing the link, not an upsell.

“Any Cat6 will do outdoors if it’s inside conduit”

Plenty of advice treats conduit as weatherproofing, and indoor cable gets pulled into buried pipe on that basis all the time.

Buried conduit is a wet location. It collects water from joints and condensation, and in this climate that water can freeze. Electricians describe freezing water in conduit crushing conductors and damaging jackets, and cable makers’ outdoor guidance calls for direct-burial or outdoor-rated cable in underground conduit, with slack left to allow for freeze and thaw.

So when a buried run fails, the cable type is the first thing we check. Indoor cable in a wet conduit gets replaced with the right cable, not re-terminated.

“If the cable still lights up, the storm didn’t damage it”

The usual home check after a storm is to see whether the device comes back. If it does, the cabling is assumed fine.

Surges do not always destroy. They can weaken a pair, pit a contact or degrade a protector, leaving a link that works today and fails next month or negotiates a lower speed. A continuity check cannot see any of that.

Certification testing can, especially when there is an earlier result to compare against. That is why we save the results of every repaired link: after the next storm, the comparison takes minutes.

“Chewed cable just needs electrical tape”

Taping over a nicked jacket is common advice for minor cable damage, and on a power cord it at least keeps fingers off the conductors.

On Cat6, tape does nothing for the pairs underneath. Bites that disturb the twist or nick a conductor change the link’s crosstalk and return loss whether or not the jacket is covered, and taped damage in a damp barn keeps letting moisture in.

Rodent damage gets located, cut out and either repaired with rated in-line hardware or replaced, and the new cable is moved off the route the animals use.

“A Cat6 drop costs a couple of hundred dollars anywhere”

National cost guides publish per-drop figures for new Cat6 — Fixr’s January 2025 guide puts it at $125 to $250 including materials and labour — and those numbers are often quoted as if they applied to any cable job.

They describe new drops in open, accessible spaces. A valley repair is mostly diagnosis across buildings and distances: which runs failed, where each fault sits, whether it was storm, frost or animals, and what the repaired link needs to survive. A run to a barn or a pole camera also has outdoor ends and an entry to make good.

So we price the two parts apart: time on site at $195 an hour against a three-hour minimum, and any run that must be replaced from $175 per drop, the figures on our Hudson Valley repair pages.

“Long runs just need a better cable”

When a long Ethernet link misbehaves, the internet often suggests buying a higher grade of cable, as if Cat6A or “Cat7” extended the distance.

Every twisted-pair category is designed around the same 100-metre channel, and higher grades buy speed and headroom within that distance, not more of it. Warehouses and estates in the valley regularly have runs at or past that limit, and heat adds to the loss.

The fix for a too-long run is a closer switch, a shorter path or fiber. We measure length first, so you do not pay for a premium cable that cannot solve the problem.

“You can find a cable fault by eye”

DIY advice usually starts with walking the cable looking for damage, and when a run is short and exposed that can work.

Runs in the valley disappear into barn ceilings, buried pipe, stone walls and warehouse structure thirty feet up. Looking along them means ladders, lifts and opening things up, often in the wrong place.

Fault-location testing reads the distance to the damage from the end of the cable. We go to that distance, look there, and repair there.

Field Notes

Field Notes on Cat6 That Leaves the Building

What we do first, second and last on a valley outdoor run — general practice rather than one particular call

We start at the ends that face the weather. The plug at a camera, the box on the side of a barn, the place where the cable turns into the wall: in this region those are where most outdoor faults live, and they are the cheapest to fix. We open them before anything else and look for water, corrosion, burn marks and cracked jackets.

Then we ask what connects the two buildings electrically. Separate meters, a subpanel, a generator, a protector at one end but not the other. That answer often decides whether we repair the copper or recommend replacing that link with something that does not carry current between buildings.

We test the run from the inside end, read the distance to the fault, and only then walk, climb or dig to it. And before we leave, the result goes on file with the date. On a site that sees storms every summer, the most useful thing we can hand over is a number to compare against next time.

FAQ

Hudson Valley Cat6 Repair FAQ

Can you repair Cat6 in an older house that still has knob-and-tube or other old electrical wiring?
+

Yes, as long as the data work stays clear of it. We do not touch or move electrical wiring, we keep the Cat6 run separated from old power wiring where they share a cavity or joist bay, and we tell you if we see wiring that looks unsafe so an electrician can assess it. Old wiring in a wall can also limit where a replacement run can go, which we take into account when choosing the route.

Can you repair the Cat6 between a Starlink dish or rural internet antenna and the router?
+

Yes. That run usually leaves the house, crosses a roof or a wall and ends in a weather-exposed plug, which makes it one of the most common outdoor Cat6 faults we see in rural parts of the valley. We test it, replace the outdoor termination with a connector made for the cable, check how it enters the building and leave the link tested. The dish or antenna itself stays with your internet provider.

Do you repair Cat6 that feeds a driveway gate, keypad or intercom post?
+

Yes. Gate and entry posts are often fed by a long Cat6 run that was buried shallow or laid in a flexible pipe beside the driveway. We find whether the fault is in the post, the run or the building end, repair the termination or the damaged length, and tell you if the run needs a better pathway before it fails again.

Can you repair a Cat6 run that feeds a well house or pump house?
+

Yes. Well houses and pump houses are small, damp and often unheated, so the Cat6 serving a controller, a sensor or a camera there usually fails from moisture and cold at the termination. We test the run, remake the end inside a sealed enclosure, check the entry for water and make sure the cable is rated for a damp location. If the building has its own electrical service, we also look at how the run is protected where it enters each building.

Will you match the existing jacks and plates in a finished house or office?
+

We try to. Tell us the brand or send a photo of a plate when you book, and we bring matching keystones and inserts where they are still made. In a finished house where only one jack in a room has failed, matching colour and style matters as much as performance, and where an exact match is no longer available we tell you before we start.

Can you repair Cat6 in the crawlspace under an older house?
+

Yes, if the space can be entered safely. Crawlspaces in older valley houses are damp, low and often shared with plumbing and ducts, so cable there tends to fail from moisture, rodents and staples rather than from use. We locate the fault from the jacks first so we only crawl to the point that needs work, and we lift any cable lying on bare soil onto supports.

Can you repair Cat6 in a converted barn or carriage house now used as an office or studio?
+

Yes, and it is one of the most common calls we get north of the city. Conversions often reuse cable that was run while the building was still a barn or garage, rarely with a finished interior in mind. We test what is there, repair the runs that are sound, and replace those that are indoor cable laid outdoors, chewed or too long, routing new cable inside the finished walls or in raceway so it stays protected.

Can you work in a barn with horses or livestock in it?
+

We can, with your staff there to manage the animals. Cable in working barns needs to be out of reach of animals and protected from dust, moisture and chewing, so a repair there often includes moving the run into conduit or raising it out of the way. We agree with you beforehand where the animals will be while the ladder and the tools are out.

Can you come back and retest a repaired link after the next big storm?
+

Yes. Some owners on exposed sites ask for exactly that, particularly for runs that feed gates, outbuildings or yard cameras. A retest compares the link against the saved result from the repair, which shows quickly whether a storm has changed anything. Ask for it when you book and we will note the links on the record.

What should we have ready for a technician arriving at a remote site?
+

Access to every building the run touches, the location of the switch and any network closet, and the name of whoever manages the internet and network equipment. A list or photo of the failing devices and a note of when they fail helps more than anything else, because it lets us bring the right parts and finish in one trip rather than two.
