The Neighborhood

Why Flatbush Properties Need Fire Alarm Installation

Flatbush is older than the Brooklyn street grid around it. Dutch colonists founded it in 1651 and chartered it as Midwout, and the English name comes from the Dutch Vlacke bos, a flat woodland. The City of Brooklyn annexed it in 1894, and in 1898 it became part of Greater New York. It has never had official borders. In the 1980s the Flatbush Development Corporation drew it between Parkside Avenue, Bedford Avenue, Avenue H and Coney Island Avenue, while the New York Times in the 2000s used New York Avenue to Ocean Avenue. Either way it sits in Community District 14, across ZIP codes 11203, 11210, 11225 and 11226. For a fire alarm, what matters is the order in which the buildings arrived. Between 1899 and 1914, the Brooklyn Rapid Transit line to Coney Island let developers turn farmland into the Victorian tracts of Ditmas Park, Fiske Terrace and Beverley Square. After the First World War, a tax exemption that ran until 1932 encouraged six-story Art Deco and Colonial Revival elevator apartment houses on Ocean Avenue and around the edges of the neighborhood. Kings Theatre opened in 1929, Brooklyn College in 1930 and the Sears store at Beverley Road and Bedford Avenue in 1932. From the 1960s and 70s Caribbean immigrants made the neighborhood their own, and the city has formally recognized parts of it as Little Caribbean and Little Haiti. All of those layers are still standing, and each one hands a fire alarm installer a different problem.

Most of the people inside those buildings rent. The Furman Center counted 147,911 residents in Community District 14 in 2024, with 43.4 percent born outside the country, homeownership at 24.4 percent and a rental vacancy rate of 3.1 percent. Its 2010 profile put rent-regulated units at 82.5 percent of rentals; that number is old and includes Midwood, but it describes a district of long-tenured tenants in older buildings. A vacancy rate that low means the buildings are full, and a full building means that a fire in one apartment is a problem for every household sharing the stair, the hall or the elevator lobby. Those common spaces are the owner's to protect and keep working. HPD's guide for tenants adds a rule that matters here: in buildings with three or more apartments, apartment entrance doors and hallway doors must close on their own and must not be propped, disabled or taped. A hall detector and a door that swings shut behind a family leaving a burning apartment are a pair. Take away either one and the other does far less.

The fire reports from 2026 show how often trouble starts in a space that was empty or out of sight. On March 31, Brooklyn Paper reported, heavy fire on the first floor of a two-story rowhouse at 836 Brooklyn Avenue near Linden Boulevard spread into the attached house next door, brought 141 fire and EMS personnel, and ended with both homes vacated by the Department of Buildings. On May 23, a 911 call at 6:49 a.m. reported fire on the second floor of a three-story mixed-occupancy building at 1693 Nostrand Avenue, a building with a vacant storefront; NBC New York reported two people killed, and ABC7 reported residents brought down from windows connected to a fire escape. Sources place that building between Beverly and Cortelyou Roads or between Cortelyou and Clarendon, they split on whether to call it Flatbush or East Flatbush, and the cause was still under investigation. Later in May, hazmat crews removed lithium-ion batteries after a fire at a restaurant on Foster Avenue that took about 90 minutes to bring under control. On July 18, fire began in the basement of a vacant three-story wood-framed house at 1952 Nostrand Avenue, under a full vacate order since a 2023 basement fire, and spread to the first floor and a neighboring building. In September, fire filled the attic of attached private homes on East 46th Street in East Flatbush. In October, a manhole fire outside 2020 Cortelyou Road raised carbon monoxide inside the building enough that firefighters ordered it evacuated. None of these reports says how the alarms in those buildings performed, and we do not suggest they failed.

Two older stories from the same streets explain why we begin every survey by asking who sleeps where. At a house on East 49th Street at Church Avenue, Patch reported, an elderly couple died in a fire the FDNY called accidental and traced to a power strip, and the department said there was no working smoke alarm in the building; the next morning, then–Borough President Eric Adams handed out free smoke detectors in East Flatbush. And releases from the city's Department of Investigation and the Kings County District Attorney in 2016, 2021 and 2022 describe fires in illegally subdivided Flatbush buildings: a 2016 indictment over a fire that killed one person and injured nine, four of them children; a 2021 indictment describing a space-heater fire in a room referred to as the "Dead Man's Room," where the tenant was trapped and jumped from a third-floor window; and a 2022 sentence of six months in jail and five years' probation for a landlord over a 2019 two-alarm fire that killed one tenant and injured six. When rooms are carved out with partitions nobody approved, detectors end up on the wrong side of the walls and the second way out disappears. A plan drawn for the apartment on paper cannot protect the rooms that are really there.

Six Flatbush facts that shape a fire alarm plan before a single device is chosen

  • Three households in four rent. With homeownership at 24.4 percent in Community District 14 in 2024, the person paying for detection is usually not the person it protects, so our written plans are addressed to the owner but explained to the residents.
  • The commercial streets are long and busy. The city's needs assessment, with fieldwork from October 2021 to June 2022, counted 1,662 storefronts on Church, Flatbush, Nostrand and Rogers Avenues and a vacancy rate of 10.6 percent. Every empty store in a building with homes above it is a space nobody watches.
  • The apartment houses are mostly six stories. They came out of the post-war tax exemption that ran to 1932. That puts them far below the 125-foot height at which, according to a DOB bulletin summary from SPARC, one-way voice communication becomes required, so their systems are about halls, stairs and panels, not voice evacuation.
  • Attached houses share their fires. The March fire at 836 Brooklyn Avenue jumped into the attached home next door, and the September fire on East 46th Street burned in the attic of attached homes. Detection near party walls and under roofs matters as much as detection near the kitchen.
  • Carbon monoxide can come from the street. Two buildings were evacuated for high CO in 2026 after utility fires outside them, on East 48th Street at Church Avenue in January and at 2020 Cortelyou Road in October. CO planning cannot stop at the boiler room.
  • Historic districts cover the Victorian blocks. The Landmarks Preservation Commission designated Prospect Park South in February 1979, Ditmas Park in July 1981 and Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park in March 2008, voted Beverley Square West and Ditmas Park West in November 2025 covering 247 freestanding homes built from 1894 to 1910, and also lists Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces. Building Specialists

Fire Alarm Installation for Flatbush's Architecture

Six kinds of building account for nearly every Flatbush fire alarm call. They differ in height, in how many households share a way out, and in which parts of them sit empty for hours or months, and those three things decide the plan before brand or model ever comes up.

The six-story apartment house

Built under the post-war tax exemption that ran to 1932, on Ocean Avenue and the edges of the neighborhood. Many households, one or two stairs, an elevator, and often an existing panel of some age.

The three-story walk-up over a store

A shop or kitchen at street level and apartments above, along Nostrand, Church, Flatbush and Rogers Avenues. The May 23 fire at 1693 Nostrand Avenue was in a building of this height and kind.

The attached two-story rowhouse

Side-street houses that share walls and sometimes roof spaces, like the pair at 836 Brooklyn Avenue and the attached homes on East 46th Street.

The frame house and the house standing empty

Wood-framed houses, including vacant ones such as the three-story house at 1952 Nostrand Avenue, which has burned more than once since 2023.

The freestanding Victorian

Queen Anne and Colonial Revival houses from 1894 to about 1914 in the southern and western tracts, many inside historic districts.

The new mixed-use building

Recent construction such as the residential development with retail at its base at 2263 Nostrand Avenue near Brooklyn College, and the planned redevelopment of the Sears site.

The six-story apartment house: a building's worth of households and a panel that cannot simply be switched off

The elevator apartment houses that went up in the decade before 1932 are the backbone of rental Flatbush. A typical one has dozens of apartments arranged off corridors on each floor, a lobby, a cellar with the boiler and laundry, and stairs that every household depends on in a fire. Many already have a building fire alarm of some vintage, and the first job is usually to test what is there rather than sell something new. The rules around that existing system are specific. Trade summaries from ASM Integrators explain that replacing detectors, pull stations or control panels with identical parts and no change in design counts as a repair, while moving or replacing a fire alarm control panel requires new plans filed with the FDNY. So the line between a straightforward repair and a filed project is often the panel itself. We tell the owner which side of that line the building is on before pricing anything.

The second constraint is that people live there the whole time. The 2022 FDNY Fire Code addresses work that takes a fire alarm system out of service for 8 hours or more in a 24-hour period or 30 hours or more in a month, so an upgrade has to be staged. We work zone by zone or floor by floor, bring each section back before the end of the day, and give the super a written schedule for the halls. Because the halls and stairs are the escape route, they get the most careful detection layout, and each device carries a label with its floor and location that matches the panel. Corridor doors are part of the picture too: HPD expects them to close on their own, and a hall detector protects far less when the door beside it is wedged open.

The three-story walk-up over a store: three floors, one stair, and a ground floor with its own schedule

This is the building that lines Flatbush's commercial avenues: a store, salon, takeout or office at street level and two floors of apartments above. The 1693 Nostrand Avenue fire on May 23 started on the second floor of a building like this, residents came down from windows connected to a fire escape, and the building had a vacant storefront. Whether the ground floor is busy or empty, it has a different schedule from the apartments. A store is open during the day and dark at night; an empty store is dark all the time. Either way the families upstairs are the ones asleep when nobody is downstairs. So the plan covers three areas together: the store and any cellar under it, the stair and the halls that serve the apartments, and each apartment, with smoke detection near bedrooms and CO detection where fuel-burning equipment or the street calls for it.

The store and the apartments are often under separate control. A commercial tenant holds the lease and the keys to the store; the owner holds the building; the families hold their apartments. Before a device goes into a store, we put in writing who maintains it, who gets called when it signals, and who can open the gate for a technician or an inspector. Where household alarms are appropriate, we link the store's detection to the apartments so a fire below wakes the floors above. Where the building's use calls for a filed system, the same goal is met through the panel and its notification devices. Because these buildings have three or more apartments, HPD's self-closing door rule applies to the apartment and hall doors, and we look at those doors on every visit.

The attached two-story rowhouse: the fire next door is your fire too

On the side streets, many houses are two stories, attached to the neighbors on one or both sides. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system for dwelling units in buildings of two stories or fewer when they are separated by one-hour fire partitions and each has a direct exit to a public way, a court or a yard, which is why most of these houses need good household detection rather than a building panel. That exception depends on the conditions being true, and an altered house may not meet them. What the code exception cannot change is that attached houses share fire. At 836 Brooklyn Avenue in March, heavy fire on the first floor spread into the adjoining home, and the Department of Buildings vacated both.

So in an attached rowhouse we plan for a fire that might start on the other side of a wall. Smoke detection goes near every sleeping area and on every level, including the cellar. The alarms are linked so a device that sounds in the cellar or on the ground floor also sounds in the upstairs bedrooms. If the house has an accessible attic or roof space, we look at it, because the September fire on East 46th Street burned in the attic of attached homes, and a fire overhead can be well along before anyone below smells it. Unconditioned attics run hot in summer and cold in winter, so heat detection, which responds to temperature, usually serves better there than smoke detection. When we see gaps that look like they let fire pass from one house to the next, we tell the owner so a contractor or architect can assess them.

The frame house and the house that stands empty

Some of Flatbush's older houses are wood-framed, and some sit empty while an estate is settled, a renovation stalls or a vacate order stays in place. The house at 1952 Nostrand Avenue is the local example everyone on that block knows: Brooklyn Paper described it as a three-story wood-framed home, vacant and under a full vacate order since a 2023 basement fire, with two major fires since 2023. In July, fire started in its basement and spread to the first floor and to a neighboring building, and it left debris hanging over the walkway of the church next door, which DOB documents also show under a full vacate order. An empty house protects nobody inside it, but it can endanger everybody beside it.

For an owner whose house will stand empty for a while, the question is who would ever hear an alarm. A battery detector in an empty house sounds to nobody. Where power stays on, we can install detection with central station monitoring through our partners so a signal reaches a monitoring center and the owner's call list. For an occupied frame house, the plan looks like the rowhouse plan with more attention to concealed spaces, such as the cellar ceiling and the attic, and to any heating equipment. For neighbors of an empty building, the useful work is on their own side of the wall, which we cover in the questions further down.

The freestanding Victorian: interior detection inside a historic district

The freestanding houses of the southern and western tracts were built between 1894 and about 1914 and are now largely inside historic districts: Prospect Park South, Ditmas Park, Fiske Terrace–Midwood Park, Albemarle-Kenmore Terraces, and the Beverley Square West and Ditmas Park West districts voted in November 2025. Fire alarm work in these houses is almost always interior: linked smoke and CO detection across several floors, a finished attic, a cellar and sometimes a carriage-house garage. We found no Landmarks Preservation Commission guidance specific to exterior fire alarm devices, so for anything that would touch the outside of a house in a district, such as an exterior sounder or a conduit, we confirm the address and the requirements first. The houses in these districts get their own detailed treatment on our Ditmas Park, Fiske Terrace and Beverley Square pages.

The new mixed-use building at the Junction: a filed system from the first drawing

New construction near the Flatbush Nostrand Junction, such as the residential development with retail at its base at 2263 Nostrand Avenue, one block from Brooklyn College, and the planned redevelopment of the Sears building into about 650 units, is a different kind of job. These buildings are designed with fire alarm systems from the start. The NYC Business portal's description of the FDNY fire alarm application says the plans must be approved by a New York State registered design professional, an engineer or an architect, with proof of occupancy from DOB, and the FDNY rules say a building core fire alarm system may not be professionally certified. Our part on a project like this is installation to approved drawings, labeling and pre-testing, and coordination with the design team. We do not present ourselves as the designer, and we say so at the first meeting.

Field Notes

Field Notes: Why We Survey a Flatbush Building From the Top Down

These notes describe how our technicians work through a building here. They are our practice, not a story about any one address.

Start under the roof. Smoke and heat rise, and two of this year's local fires involved an attic or a top floor. So the technician asks for access to the attic hatch or bulkhead first and looks at what is up there: wiring, equipment, stored boxes, signs of water, any gap where the space continues over the house next door. In an apartment house, it is the top-floor hall and the roof door.

Then every hall and stair, floor by floor. He counts households, checks each detector's date and test button, notes which hall doors close on their own and which have been wedged, and listens from inside a bedroom with the door shut while a test sounds in the hall.

Then the street level. In a walk-up he looks at the store, the kitchen if there is one, and where batteries are charged. If the store is vacant, he asks who has the key and when anyone last went in.

Then the cellar. Boiler, gas meters, the point where utility lines come in from the street, storage, and anything that suggests somebody sleeps down there.

Last, the paper. Only after the walk does he compare what he saw with the Certificate of Occupancy or Schedule A and any notices. When they match, the plan is simple. When they do not, the owner hears about it before a price is written.

Business Fire Alarm Installation in Flatbush

Flatbush's businesses sit mostly on four corridors the city mapped in its needs assessment: Church Avenue from Coney Island Avenue to East 56th Street, Flatbush Avenue from Parkside Avenue to Avenue H, Nostrand Avenue from Parkside Avenue to Avenue J, and Rogers Avenue from Clarkson Avenue to Church Avenue. The Flatbush Nostrand Junction, where Flatbush and Nostrand meet at Avenue H, is a transfer point for two subway lines and seven bus lines. Seventy-nine percent of the merchants surveyed rented their space. The cards below describe kinds of commercial space on those streets; we do not name any business as a customer.

Restaurants and takeout kitchens

Commercial cooking under apartments, often open late. The May fire at a Foster Avenue restaurant needed hazmat crews to remove lithium-ion batteries, a reminder that delivery equipment lives in these kitchens too.

Stores that sell from more than one floor

Under Title 27, a retail store with a floor above the street floor open to the public, or with 20,000 or more gross square feet, needs a fire alarm system. Opening an upstairs showroom changes the job.

Day care programs

Title 27 requires fire alarm systems in day care agencies with a Board of Health permit for more than thirty children. Smaller programs still need linked detection where children nap.

Houses of worship and community spaces

Churches, mosques and halls in converted buildings, such as the Futa Islamic Center in the 2020 Cortelyou Road building that was evacuated for carbon monoxide in October.

Medical and professional offices

Clinics and offices above or beside stores, like Ditmas Park Medical Care in the same Cortelyou Road building, where patients and visitors do not know the exits.

Schools and the college edge

Title 27 requires fire alarm systems in all public schools and in private schools and university teaching buildings more than one story high, relevant around the Junction and Brooklyn College.

A Flatbush business almost never has a building to itself. The takeout on the ground floor may share a cellar with the building's boiler and an electrical service with the apartments. Its lease may say who maintains detection and who does not. Its owner may live in another borough and have no reason to be on Church Avenue at 3 a.m. So before designing anything commercial, we put three answers in writing: who owns and maintains each part of the system, who is called when it signals and at what hours, and who can open the store when it is closed. Where the Rules Change

Two Stories, Three Stories, Six Stories: The Lines Flatbush Buildings Cross in the Code

Owners lose the most money on fire alarms by buying the wrong category of work: a building panel with pull stations where good household alarms would do, or a box of battery detectors where the building's use calls for a filed system. In Flatbush, the category usually follows the building's height and what happens on its ground floor. This section walks through those lines as we read the public rules. We are installers, not lawyers or code consultants, and this is not legal advice; it is how we decide which path a building is on before we price it.

Two stories: the household path, if the conditions hold. The 2022 NYC Building Code does not require a manual fire alarm system for dwelling units in buildings of two stories or fewer, where units are separated by one-hour fire partitions and each has a direct exit to a public way, court or yard. A similar exception covers R-1-type buildings of two stories or fewer. For most attached two-story rowhouses on the side streets, that means the right answer is linked smoke and CO alarms placed well, not a panel. Our Brooklyn list for a basic residential alarm system starts at $600, and a wireless system runs $600 to $1,200. The conditions matter, though. A rowhouse that has gained a cellar apartment, a rented room or a store at the front may not fit the exception, and that is a conversation to have before buying anything.

Three stories with a store: two sets of rules in one building. The three-story walk-up over a store is where owners most often guess wrong. The apartments upstairs, with three or more households, bring HPD's self-closing door rule and make the halls and stair the owner's responsibility. The store downstairs has rules of its own: a store that opens a floor above the street floor to the public, or that reaches 20,000 gross square feet, is listed in Title 27 as needing a fire alarm system. A store used only at street level, with a stockroom above, is a different case. We look at both halves and tell the owner whether the building needs linked detection across store and apartments, an upgrade to a system already there, or a system with filed plans.

Six stories: the existing system and the outage clock. In the elevator apartment houses, the question is usually not whether a system is needed but what to do with the one that is there. Replacing detectors, pull stations or panels with identical parts and no change to the design is a repair. Moving or replacing the control panel is a change that needs new plans filed with the FDNY. Our Brooklyn list for alarm panel replacement on existing wiring is $800 to $1,500, and that number assumes the cable tests good and the replacement stays within repair. Any upgrade in an occupied building also has to respect the Fire Code's outage thresholds of 8 hours in a day or 30 hours in a month. At six stories these buildings sit well below the 125-foot height at which one-way voice communication is required, so their systems center on detection, notification and the panel.

New buildings and big changes: the filed path. When a building's use calls for a system, or an existing system is redesigned, the plans go to the FDNY for review and approval before installation, approved by a registered design professional, with proof of occupancy. A building core system goes to FDNY inspection rather than professional certification. Prices for this path are set per building after the survey and the design, because the engineering and filing depend on the building.

Occupancy is the hinge for all of it. The FDNY application asks for a Certificate of Occupancy or an approved Schedule A, and the design has to match the legal use. The Flatbush prosecutions over illegally subdivided buildings show what happens when use and paperwork drift apart. If the building is used differently from its records, the honest choices are to change the use back or to legalize it properly, and in the meantime detection has to protect the people who actually live there. Your Questions Answered

Fire Alarm Installation in Flatbush: Questions Answered

These are the situations Flatbush owners, managers and tenants bring to us: empty stores, burned houses next door, attics shared down a row, carbon monoxide from the street, and code lines that switch on when a building's use changes. Each is answered in full here and not repeated in the FAQ further down.

Spaces nobody is watching

The storefront at the bottom of our building has been empty for over a year and the gate stays down. Does a space nobody uses still need fire detection?

It needs it more than an occupied store, because nobody will notice anything there until smoke reaches the floors above. An empty store still has an electrical service, often a gas line, and whatever the last tenant left behind, and the locked gate keeps away the passersby who might otherwise smell smoke. The May 23 fire at 1693 Nostrand Avenue was in a three-story mixed-use building with a vacant storefront; it started on the second floor and the cause was under investigation, so we make no link between the two. The planning point stands anyway. We put detection in the empty store and any cellar under it and arrange for it to sound where people live, through linked household alarms where the building's occupancy allows or through the building system where one is required. The owner also needs a working key, a testing schedule for the devices, and someone on the call list who can open the gate. When a new tenant signs, the detection is reviewed for whatever business moves in.

The house next to ours is vacant, has burned before and is under a vacate order. What can we do on our side of the shared wall?

You cannot make the other owner act, but you can make sure your household hears a fire on the far side of the wall as early as possible. Brooklyn Paper reported that the July 18 fire at 1952 Nostrand Avenue started in the basement of a vacant wood-framed house under a full vacate order since a 2023 basement fire, and spread to the first floor and to a neighboring building. In your situation we look first at the spaces nearest the shared wall on every level, the cellar and any attic or roof space especially, because that is where heat and smoke arrive first. Detection goes there, linked to the alarms by the bedrooms. We also point out anything on your side that would feed a fire coming through, such as storage stacked against the wall. Complaints about the vacant property itself go to the city through 311; the alarm does not replace that, it buys your family time.

Our row of attached houses shares a long open attic under one roof. Should there be a detector up there, and what kind?

If the attic is reachable and holds wiring, equipment or stored things, it should be part of the plan, and the usual choice is a heat detector rather than a smoke detector. In September, Brooklyn Paper reported heavy fire in the attic of attached private homes at 644 East 46th Street, with several people displaced. A fire overhead can burn for a while above the top-floor ceiling before anyone below notices, and in a shared roof space it can travel along the row. Unheated attics get very hot in summer, cold in winter and dusty all year, conditions that make smoke detectors unreliable, while heat detectors respond to temperature and hold up. The attic device is linked to the alarms in the living space so a fire overhead wakes the bedrooms. Whether the attic is actually open between houses is a construction question; if we see openings that look like they let fire pass from one home to the next, we tell you so a contractor or architect can look at closing them.

Air and fire that come from outside your walls

A street fire on our block raised carbon monoxide inside our building. Should CO detection go somewhere besides the boiler room?

Yes. The boiler room does need CO detection, but Flatbush had two reminders in 2026 that carbon monoxide can come in from the street. In January, ABC7 reported, overhead wires caught fire on East 48th Street at Church Avenue and residents of one building were evacuated for high CO readings. In October, Patch reported, firefighters detected elevated CO after a manhole fire in front of 2020 Cortelyou Road and ordered the building evacuated; Con Edison said a weather-related problem with an underground cable caused it. For a building on a commercial street, that means CO detection in the cellar near where utility lines enter, on the ground floor, and in apartments or offices directly above, as well as near fuel-burning equipment. On the survey we find where your services come in from the sidewalk and plan outward from that point, so an event under the street is detected before it reaches the people upstairs.

The people who need the most warning

My parents are in their seventies and live alone in an attached house off Church Avenue. If you could only do a few things first, what would they be?

Working smoke detection outside where they sleep and on every level, alarms linked so one sounding sets off the rest, and a warning they can actually hear from bed. Patch's report on the fire on East 49th Street at Church Avenue, which killed a couple aged 76 and 71, said the FDNY found no working smoke alarm in the building and traced the fire to a power strip. A single detector at the far end of a hall may not wake an older person through a closed door, so we place devices near the bedroom and test the sound from the bed with the door shut. CO detection goes near the bedrooms and the boiler. Then the practical things: no detectors so high they can never change a battery, which usually means hardwired alarms with backup or sealed units, and a walk through the house explaining what each sound means. While we are there, ask us to point out any power strips that look overloaded.

I rent a room in an apartment the landlord has split into more rooms than it was built with. What can I check myself?

Check that there is a working smoke alarm in or right outside your room, press its test button, and look for a way out other than the door into the shared hall. The Flatbush cases announced by the Department of Investigation and the District Attorney show what happens when those things are missing: one described a tenant trapped by a space-heater fire in a room referred to as the "Dead Man's Room," who jumped from a third-floor window, and another involved a fire in an illegally subdivided building that killed one person and injured nine, including four children. If your room has no detector, or the only one is on the far side of a new partition, tell the landlord in writing; you can also report conditions to the city through 311. We are hired by owners and managers, and we will not wire anything that makes an illegal room look like a legal apartment, but when an owner calls, we install detection that protects people where they actually sleep.

The fire escape outside my bedroom window is our second way out. Does that change where the alarm should go?

It changes how much warning you need. Opening a window, climbing out and going down a fire escape takes longer than walking down a stair, and it is harder at night, with children, or for anyone who moves slowly. ABC7 reported that during the May 23 fire at 1693 Nostrand Avenue, residents were brought down from windows connected to a fire escape. In an apartment like yours we put smoke detection close enough to the bedrooms to sound early and loudly with the doors shut, and in a walk-up we look at the hall and stair outside so smoke there is detected before it reaches your door. Two things outside alarm work matter as much: the window has to open without a struggle, and nothing should block the path to it or the landing outside. If there is a gate on that window, make sure everyone in the apartment can open it quickly from inside, in the dark.

When the code changes the job

We want to open a day care on the ground floor of our two-family. At what point does it need a fire alarm system?

Title 27 of the NYC Administrative Code requires a fire alarm system in day care agencies holding a Board of Health permit for more than thirty children, so program size is the first threshold to know. A small home-based program is a different case from a center, and your licensing agency has its own safety requirements, which we do not speak for. What we can do is look at the house as it will actually be used: where children nap, how they would get out, whether the cellar or the upstairs apartment is part of the program, and whether the family upstairs would be warned of a fire below. Even where a full system is not required, linked smoke and CO alarms covering every floor and the program space are the baseline. If you are planning toward a center for more than thirty children, the work becomes a system with approved, filed plans, and it is far cheaper to learn that before a renovation than after one.

Our store on Flatbush Avenue started selling from its second floor. Is that enough to trigger a fire alarm requirement?

It can be. Title 27 requires fire alarm systems in retail establishments with one or more floors above the street floor open to the public, or with 20,000 or more gross square feet. A store that used its upstairs only for stock and now invites customers up has changed in exactly the way that rule describes, as has one that opens a mezzanine or a second-floor showroom. The change may also need to appear in the building's occupancy records, since the FDNY application asks for a Certificate of Occupancy or an approved Schedule A. The NYC Business portal says the plans for an FDNY fire alarm application must be approved by a New York State registered design professional, meaning a professional engineer or a registered architect, and that the filing needs proof of occupancy from DOB. Our Ocean Avenue apartment house needs its fire alarm upgraded, and everyone will be living there throughout. How long can the system be out of service?

As briefly as possible, and the plan has to respect a threshold in the Fire Code. The 2022 FDNY Fire Code addresses work that takes a fire alarm system out of service for 8 hours or more in any 24-hour period or 30 hours or more in a month, and work of that length brings impairment procedures with it. Residents should be told which days we will be in the halls and when tests will sound, so a test is not mistaken for a fire, or a fire for a test.

Tenants in our walk-up keep propping the hallway doors open in summer. Does that affect the fire alarm?

It affects how much the fire alarm can do for them. HPD's guide says that in buildings with three or more apartments, apartment entrance doors and hallway doors must close on their own and must not be propped, disabled or taped. A detector warns people; a closed door slows smoke while they leave. With a hall door wedged open, smoke from one floor reaches the stair faster and cuts the warning time the detection gave everyone above.
